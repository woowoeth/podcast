The More You Try to Be Secure, the Less Secure You Are: Secure Is Being Able to Admit You're Not

Secure isn't the absence of anxiety, avoidance and jealousy — it's being a safe container for those feelings. Pushing the insecure parts away means re-enacting, on yourself, the criticism and neglect that made you insecure in the first place.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

8:12

Pretending to be secure makes you less secure

Franco has observed a popular piece of advice: if you're anxious because someone hasn't texted back, ‘act like a secure person’ and don't be anxious. Her judgment is that this causes a second injury — ‘not only do I feel anxious, now I feel ashamed of being anxious’. It amounts to re-enacting on yourself the criticism, shame and neglect that made you insecure in the first place. Her alternative is to first acknowledge: anxiety is allowed, avoidance is allowed, because ‘we can only heal the parts of ourselves we're willing to acknowledge’.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
10:12

Secure people are the quickest to admit they're insecure

Sarah draws a key distinction: insecure attachment is a pattern, while anxious, secure and insecure are emotional states — the two can't be equated. From this Franco derives the ‘paradox of security’ — the most secure people are the ones most able to admit and acknowledge their own insecurity. So security doesn't depend on what emotions you feel, but on how you respond to them: the anxious type catastrophizes (‘the fact that I feel this means I'm too much, there's something wrong with me’), the avoidant type pushes it away and distracts, and the secure response is ‘it's okay that I'm anxious, what do I need’. She defines worth as ‘treating your internal world as if it matters’.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
14:14

Feel first, heal second, act last

Franco offers a three-step sequence you can use in the moment: feel, heal, do. She gives her own example — a friendship she cares about deeply, one she actively maintains but experiences as one-sided, where the other person cancelled a meetup because of an injury. Of course she wanted to feel only understanding, but what she actually felt was rejected. It was only because she practices ‘scanning her hand across the day to see where the emotional energy lands’ that she noticed the feeling. She first sat with it and named it, then told herself ‘I am loved, I am safe’, and only then replied to the other person, ‘take care of yourself’. If you skip that step and go straight to the nice words, the negative emotion accumulates and eventually distorts the relationship.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
20:19

Memory is self-esteem's database, and it can be rewritten

Franco's core mechanism: memory doesn't just record the past, it predicts the future — ‘I've been abandoned in the past. I think it will happen again.’ That is what an attachment pattern is. She cites memory reconsolidation theory: every time you revisit a memory, you can change its emotional valence. The method is to return to the scene of being picked up late or abandoned by your parents, and have your adult self tell your childhood self ‘I'm here, you're safe, this doesn't mean there's anything negative about you’. Research shows that doing this not only reduces trauma symptoms and raises self-esteem, but also produces changes in the hippocampus. Sarah uses the metaphor of a blueprint: this is pulling up the original blueprint and editing it.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
23:20

Your insecurity is actually trying to protect you

Why is rewriting the blueprint a prerequisite? Franco's explanation: for an anxious type who expects to be abandoned, letting your guard down, letting yourself feel good, actually receiving the other person's love — all of that is threatening, because if you really do get abandoned, you'll be shattered even more thoroughly. So ‘our insecurities are really trying to protect us’. This also explains why simply telling someone to ‘act more secure’ often doesn't work: without addressing the memory first, security itself is a threat. Sarah adds: some people are in fact already in a safer environment, but the old blueprint keeps them from seeing it.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
26:23

Finding secure relationships takes initiation and discernment

Franco gives two things. The first is initiation: people who believe friendships just happen are lonelier over the long run, because they don't put in the effort; you have to show up repeatedly in front of the same group of people, exploiting the mere exposure effect (familiarity breeds liking) — for example, don't just go to one happy hour, sign up for a moms' group. At the same time you have to overcome covert avoidance — being there physically but not mentally, scrolling your phone or only talking to people you already know. The second is discernment: ask yourself what your nervous system feels like around this person, whether you're calm, whether there's reciprocity, whether your values are similar, whether they genuinely want you to succeed. She also mentions mutuality: holding your reality and my reality at the same time.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
29:25

Reflective functioning decides whether attachment passes to your child

Sarah introduces reflective functioning: the capacity to notice and be curious about your own and others' internal states. She says research shows that if you're insecurely attached, your child has a 70% chance of having the same style; and among the portion who don't pass it on, one predictor is how high their reflective functioning is. Franco sees this as consistent with the mechanism described earlier — only once you can perceive your own internal world do you have the spare capacity to recognize another person's, because you're less in a threatened state.

— Dr. Sarah Bren
33:25

‘I was perfectly agreeable’ can be a sign of numbness

Franco interviewed a numb interviewee and asked what numbness looked like; the answer was ‘I was perfectly agreeable’. This woman saved her dark side for her child — because a child won't leave, she no longer had to stay agreeable to maintain the connection; she even once locked her daughter out of the house, and feels deep shame about it. From this Franco rewrote her own definition of a good person: a good person isn't someone who never feels negative emotions, but someone who allows themselves to feel them — otherwise the emotions accumulate and eventually hijack you at some moment, hurting people more vulnerable than you. She also notes that internalizers turn emotions on themselves, showing up as depression and persistent anxiety.

— Dr. Marisa G. Franco
36:27

Your child erupting lava at you is healthy

Sarah uses the metaphor of ‘lava’: we're most likely to lose our temper at the people who are safest and least likely to abandon us — for parents that's often the child — and children do the same thing to us (restraint collapse). But the key difference between the two is asymmetry: it's healthy for a child to erupt lava at a parent, and the parent needs to be able to recognize it as ‘this is lava’ without absorbing it into their own body; conversely, when a parent erupts lava at a child, the child doesn't have the cognitive capacity to say ‘this is mom's lava’. So an occasional eruption is fine, but once it becomes a pattern it damages the attachment relationship.

— Dr. Sarah Bren

Glossary

secureish
A word Franco coined for the state of not chasing perfect security and allowing your own insecurity to exist.
self-verification theory
People seek out others who confirm their existing self-concept, even when that concept is negative.
memory reconsolidation
Every time a memory is revisited its emotional valence can be changed — the method for rewriting your attachment blueprint.
covert avoidance
Being physically present but mentally absent — scrolling your phone or only talking to people you already know.
mere exposure effect
People tend to like those they're familiar with, so showing up repeatedly in the same setting makes connection easier.
reflective functioning
The capacity to notice and be curious about your own and others' internal states; predicts whether attachment gets passed on.
