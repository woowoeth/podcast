Stanford Has a Secret Course That Teaches 12 People How to Extract Value From Other Humans

The course isn't in the catalog, admits only 12 students a year, and is taught by a Silicon Valley CEO on how to extract value from people. The author says students lying isn't an accident — it's what the machine has long rewarded.

5:12

You can raise hundreds of thousands before you have a company

Pre-idea funding was the newest layer of the game when Theo arrived on campus: give an 18-year-old who just started school hundreds of thousands, even millions, of dollars, sign a one-page SAFE check, agree that "when you start a company someday, come back to us" — otherwise the money never has to be repaid. For a VC that's pocket change; they don't really believe in these hyped-up teenagers, and they don't need you personally to succeed — they just need one to blow up overnight. Meanwhile students are encouraged to make enormous promises and then discover again and again that no one follows up: lie, and nothing happens.

— Theo Baker
13:22

The secret course teaches extracting value

Stanford has a course that isn't in the catalog, called How to Rule the World, taught by a Silicon Valley CEO who packages himself as a professor, admitting only 12 students a year — merely knowing it exists is a status symbol. Its central assignment is extracting value from people, with a strong whiff of the ends justifying the means. One woman Theo interviewed said the course taught her how to lie to VCs to raise money — she later actually raised millions and dropped out of Stanford, despite having already been caught cheating in a computer science class; months later she put out a glossy launch video for a technology that didn't exist, and Silicon Valley applauded anyway, she moved into a mansion, and she was invited back to Stanford to give a talk.

— Theo Baker
26:46

Ethics isn't a premise; getting caught is what counts

During ethics week in the intro computer science course, the professor discussed a case where a software defect killed four people in a radiotherapy device, and the TA asked who in the room cared about ethics — Theo was the only one who raised his hand. Weeks later a classmate told him he was taking a business ethics course, and "we're all just there to figure out how they got caught." He didn't sense that these people felt any need to pretend to care; when Sam Bankman-Fried was under house arrest at the house on the Stanford campus where he grew up, students made pilgrimages, threw parties themed around him, and said they wanted to replicate his success minus the final fall. A colleague's summary of the money that mysteriously vanished at Tree Hacks: it's all just a little bit of fraud.

— Theo Baker
38:01

The real reason the president fell wasn't the data

Board members who ousted Marc Tessier-Lavigne later told Theo that one of the heaviest reasons they gave was his posture — a highly aggressive public stance when challenged, deny, deny, deny, plus the threatening letters sent by the lawyer he hired, Steve Neal, which embarrassed the university. Theo found no evidence that Tessier-Lavigne himself falsified data: the problem was in the labs he oversaw, where subordinates committed fraud in papers he co-authored, including the 2009 paper claiming to have found the cause of Alzheimer's, while internal company results before publication showed its central finding was "at best unreliable." He was asked to retract and refused, dragging it out for a decade. What drove him out was image management, not the data.

— Theo Baker
39:01

Four days after being ousted, the mansion deal closed

Four days after the board unanimously removed him, Tessier-Lavigne closed on a 7,600-square-foot mansion in "America's most expensive zip code," valued at over $10 million; eight months later he announced he had raised $1 billion for a new company doing AI biotech. Of the two VCs that led the round, an investor at one messaged Theo two months later to rib him, followed by a winking emoji; an investor at the other offered him money to start a company. Theo says it's the most Silicon Valley thing you could imagine: everything can be monetized, even taking down a university president turns into an invitation.

— Theo Baker
45:44

Duck syndrome: paddling like mad under the surface

Duck syndrome is a term Stanford coined itself: students glide across the surface like ducks, making everything look effortless, while underneath their legs paddle desperately just to stay afloat. Katie Meyer was its best illustration — women's soccer captain, led the team to an NCAA title, headed to law school, didn't drink, didn't smoke, didn't speed. Months before graduation, late on a Friday night, after every student support office had closed, she received a letter: because six months earlier she had spilled coffee on a football player (she said it was an accident, he said it was deliberate), her degree was suspended. That night she took her own life. Twelve schools later paid tribute to her; Stanford was not among them. The women's soccer team she had just captained held a mental health night and didn't mention her name.

— Theo Baker
52:01

A legendary professor's fraud case, and the school says it never heard of it

Stan Cohen was the first geneticist to transplant a gene from one living organism into another, and his 1970s recombinant DNA technique gave rise to the model of universities making money from patents; in its final valid year that patent contributed 62% of all of Stanford's patent revenue. In his freshman year Theo found that Cohen had been found by a court to have committed fraud: he promised investors a drug target that might cure Huntington's disease and said the drug was already approved to treat another condition, without telling them it had been permanently banned by the FDA in 1976 because it was lethal. He was ultimately ordered to pay $29 million and paid it. Theo asked the university, and the university replied that it had never heard of the case and had no involvement whatsoever — even though Stanford owned the relevant intellectual property and had been subpoenaed by the court.

— Theo Baker
55:04

Raising money is easier than an internship, and that itself is a signal

The saying circulating in Stanford's inner circles is that raising money for a startup is easier than getting an internship — Theo says that's absurd, it shouldn't be that way, but that's the reality. He describes the moment as a game of AI musical chairs: everyone is waiting to see who's still standing when the music stops and who's left holding the bag. He isn't worried about cash-flow-positive companies like Meta and Google; he's worried about the companies clinging to the AI narrative that have no technology of their own, no revenue, and no ability to generate revenue — they can't hold on, and they will collapse. But he also cautions that Silicon Valley hasn't held power for long; it's a very immature power system that hasn't grown guardrails, transparency or accountability, and one collapse doesn't necessarily mean a reckoning.

— Theo Baker

Glossary

pre-idea funding
Money investors give before a company exists and before there's even an idea.
SAFE check
A one-page agreement; the money isn't repaid, and it converts into a future investment when you start a company.
Tree Hacks
Stanford's campus hackathon, a weekend project competition that investors treat as a high-signal zone.
duck syndrome
A term Stanford coined: gliding elegantly on the surface while paddling desperately underneath.
wantrepreneur
In the original, people who start companies only because others are doing it, with no real ability.
