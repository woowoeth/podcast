U.S. Bombing Built the Khmer Rouge from Thousands to Two Hundred Thousand

Khmer Rouge membership grew from three thousand in 1969 to two hundred thousand by late 1973, with almost all growth driven by civilian anger triggered by U.S. carpet bombing, not ideological conviction.

3:01

The evacuation order used air-raid prevention as a pretext

When the Khmer Rouge entered Phnom Penh, they announced over loudspeaker that order would return. Hours later, they ordered the entire city evacuated immediately, claiming it was to prevent U.S. bombing—despite the United States having already withdrawn from Southeast Asia. The reason was fabricated. The real goal was to destroy urban life and construct a monetaryless, property-less agrarian society. Approximately two million Phnom Penh residents were driven from the city; many died along the way from hunger, disease, and gunfire.

11:39

Rice smuggling bled the Lon Nol government dry before any military assault

Cambodia grew wealthy by smuggling rice to both North and South Vietnamese armies, each desperate for grain and paying premium prices. These transactions bypassed the state—the regime's primary revenue source, rice-export taxes, was drained away. By the late 1960s, Phnom Penh's government teetered on the edge of financial collapse. This was not a Khmer Rouge victory but the economic spillover of the Vietnam War: the regime's foundations were already hemorrhaging before military attack began.

— Ben Kiernan
20:54

U.S. bombing grew the Khmer Rouge from thousands to two hundred thousand

In 1969, the Khmer Rouge numbered between three and five thousand. By late 1973, they exceeded two hundred thousand. The turning point was Operation Menu, a secret U.S. bombing campaign. From January through August 1973 alone, the U.S. dropped 250,000 tons of B-52 bombs on Cambodia—twice the total tonnage of the previous four years. Nominally targeting North Vietnamese sanctuaries along the border, the bombs killed large numbers of Cambodian civilians. Farmers whose homes were destroyed joined the Khmer Rouge in droves—this army was largely fed by American ordnance.

— Ben Kiernan
24:59

They preached a classless society while engineering new castes

The Khmer Rouge proclaimed the abolition of class and the construction of an equal society, yet institutionalized rigid divisions. Those who had remained in liberated zones until victory were designated "base people"; the 2.5 million evacuated from cities were marked as "new people." The latter received smaller rations, worse treatment, and lower status. Later, the regime formally applied Leninist nomenclature—full and candidate party members—to the entire population. Beneath rhetoric of equality lay systematized hierarchy.

— Ben Kiernan
32:19

The second exodus sent people to starve before the harvest came

After the initial evacuation in April 1975, from September through November the Khmer Rouge relocated hundreds of thousands to what they claimed was the most fertile and sparsely populated northwest zone. Eight hundred thousand outsiders were moved in. But they arrived after planting season and before harvest—too late to sow their own food, forced to subsist on meager local yields stretched impossibly thin. The 1975 harvest fell short. By 1976, the northwest zone was gripped by a massive famine.

— Ben Kiernan
39:50

1.7 million died; ethnic minorities were killed at twice the national rate

Cambodia's population fell from approximately 8 million in 1975 to 5.7 million by early 1979. Historian Ben Kiernan estimates the Khmer Rouge killed 1.7 million over their 3 years and 8 months in power—nearly 20 percent of the total population. Roughly half were directly executed; the rest perished from hunger, disease, or overwork. Ethnic minorities faced steeper death tolls: roughly half of Cambodia's 500,000 ethnic Chinese were killed; the Cham (占族), a Muslim minority, saw death rates approaching 40 percent; the Vietnamese were nearly annihilated, with only single-digit survivors discovered decades later.

— Ben Kiernan
51:29

War on three neighbors at once brought the Khmer Rouge's own reckoning

Beginning in 1977, the Khmer Rouge launched simultaneous military attacks against Vietnam, Thailand, and Laos. Vietnam proposed negotiations multiple times; Pol Pot refused every offer. After enduring sustained Khmer Rouge massacres of civilians, Vietnam invaded in late 1978 and early 1979, overthrowing the regime. China responded by deploying 200,000 troops against northern Vietnam, attempting to relieve pressure on its Khmer Rouge allies. Vietnam repulsed both forces on two fronts.

— Ben Kiernan
58:46

A UN seat protected the Khmer Rouge from trial for nearly three decades

After their ouster, the Khmer Rouge retained Cambodia's UN General Assembly seat for over a decade, propped up by the United States, China, and Thailand. This blockade prevented international prosecutions from commencing. The UN-backed tribunal only began functioning around 2007. By then, Pol Pot and Ta Mok—the military commander responsible for the largest share of killings—had died without facing trial. Only two senior leaders, Nuon Chea and Ieng Sary, were convicted of crimes against humanity in 2014, with genocide charges added for massacres of the Cham and Vietnamese in 2018 and 2019. Many mid-level officials were never prosecuted.

— Ben Kiernan

Glossary

Year Zero
The historical reset the Khmer Rouge imposed, a declared new beginning intended to erase all remnants of the old society and rebuild from nothing.
Killing Fields
Mass execution and burial sites where the Khmer Rouge systematically murdered perceived enemies and class enemies.
Base People and New People
Two populations the Khmer Rouge distinguished by privilege level: those living in liberated zones before 1975 were base people; those evacuated from cities were new people, receiving fewer rations, worse conditions, and lower status.
Democratic Kampuchea
The official state name the Khmer Rouge used for Cambodia from 1975 to 1979.
