A beauty brand sells on mission, but the product is what keeps it alive

Thrive Causemetics first made a false lash for people who lost their lashes to chemo, then used one iPhone ad to push daily sales from $5,000 to $100,000 in seven days. The founder says mission drives repeat purchase, but product decides survival.

18:12

A specific problem comes first, the brand second

Carissa didn't think up a startup and then make a product. Volunteering at Look Good Feel Better, she saw that women who had lost their lashes to chemo had no solution: mascara had nothing to cling to, traditional false lashes used latex and parabens that were too irritating for immunocompromised people, and they wouldn't stick anyway. At the same time she thought about her friend Christy, who after losing her hair still wanted to feel like herself — about dignity. So she went to ophthalmologists and oncologists and used fiber suppliers she had met during her Clarisonic days to make the first false lash that didn't depend on natural lashes, plus Infinity Waterproof Lash Adhesive, free of latex and parabens.

26:36

Scarcity isn't an obstacle, it's a focusing tool

She quotes "scarcity drives focus," and says her scarcity was capital and time. With no money for ads, she sent DMs one by one: an Instagram message to Elena George at Good Morning America, a LinkedIn message to April Franzino at Good Housekeeping. She LinkedIn-messaged more than 20 editors; only one replied, and even after replying it was six months before they met. The first print coverage came out in July 2015 — just a very small slot in Good Housekeeping. Her logic: do it one person at a time, because you never know who will change your life.

— Karissa Bodnar
28:41

During those seven viral days, the company had no employees

Before Black Friday 2016, she shot an ad on an iPhone about Infinity Waterproof Eyeliner, customers started commenting on their own, and the business took off. She called her sister to say they'd do $5,000 that day, the next day $25,000, and within seven days $100,000. At that point the company had not a single employee: her friend Megan, a dental hygienist, came after work to do customer service; her sister did customer service on her lunch break; her mom came too. The first official hires came from Christy's sorority at the University of Washington — women five years younger than Carissa who had never met Christy, but knew Thrive and its mission.

— Karissa Bodnar
32:56

Failing to raise money is what preserved control

From 2013 to 2015 she spent a lot of time fundraising, very unsuccessfully. Only after the company was already profitable, with revenue above $30 million, did she take capital — and she only needed a few hundred thousand dollars for inventory. Because she wasn't desperate for money, her negotiating position was completely different: she and her lawyer wrote the term sheet themselves, keeping majority equity and control of the company, so no one else could decide whether she sold it. Her advice is to raise less, because scarcity sustains that focused mindset. She also says that if she had gotten more money than she could spend early on, she might have lost that focus and never polished the products one at a time.

— Karissa Bodnar
36:31

Mission drives repeat purchase, product decides survival

People always ask her: mission or product? Her answer is product — it has to be product. Mission and product are bound together: because they donate to cancer patients, there are thousands of ingredients they will never use. But she's equally clear that customers don't come for the mission — they come because Liquid Lash Extensions won Allure's best of beauty, the Grammys of beauty. If the formula and the product don't give customers a solution, you're dead. Anyone who wants to donate can just write a check to a local charity; Thrive's value is letting people do two things at once.

— Karissa Bodnar
40:48

Don't own the factory, own the formula

She believes no one needs to own their own manufacturing facility; what matters most is owning your formulas. Ownership comes from creation, so they invest in their own cosmetic chemists. Then you negotiate exclusivity — those two things protect the formula and its integrity. She also stresses not to do deals on a handshake: have contracts, and reinforce them repeatedly, because once a product takes off it attracts competition. She says she likes competition because it keeps the team sharp, but she always keeps one question in view: what do our customers want from us.

— Karissa Bodnar
42:57

Every product has to fund the next one

Thrive is bootstrapped and has always been profitable, so every product has to pay for the next one. She gives a very concrete example: if Empower Shine, a lipstick in development for years, doesn't sell well enough, they can't launch Lasting Mark Lip Stain, which they hoped to bring out within a month. The skincare line, though developed with dermatologists, oncologists and ophthalmologists, is still small relative to the whole business. She sees complexion and sun care as opportunities, but a brand is built one product at a time, and that discipline can't be relaxed.

— Karissa Bodnar
44:00

She says plainly she doesn't want to sell the company

Asked whether she'd be willing to be acquired by a big company the way Tatcha was, she says flatly no, I don't want to sell the company, and they've had the chance. She sees the ending as continuing to show up and keep developing products that change people's lives. She says she now has more money than she needs, so she can give it away; more important is how to leave the world better than she found it, and how to build a sustainable business that outlives them. She believes staying private is the best way to do that, and to grow people from within — for example, someone who started as a cosmetic chemistry intern is now a product development manager, and she hopes one day she'll run the whole company.

Glossary

direct-to-consumer
A brand sells straight to consumers instead of going through retailers.
term sheet
The document in a financing that lays out valuation, equity, control and other core terms.
co-manufacturer
A brand doesn't build its own plant but contracts a third-party factory to make the product.
one for one
For every item sold, one is donated — the model Toms shoes made famous.
