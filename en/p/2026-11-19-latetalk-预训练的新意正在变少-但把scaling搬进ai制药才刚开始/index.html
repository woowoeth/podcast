Pre-training innovations are plateauing, but bringing Scaling to AI drug design is just beginning

ByteDance's protein structure design model trained a 900-trillion-parameter model on just 26.5 million structure data points, while public structure data contains 1.3 billion—compute, not data, is AI drug design's current biggest bottleneck.

Three independent ByteDance teams race to dominate AI drug design

Gu Quanquan leads AI drug design under ByteDance Research; two other independent teams exist elsewhere—one on small molecules, one that shifted from small molecules to proteins, creating the ProteinX series. These teams report to different divisions and operated largely separately until Try2 appeared. Recognizing the need to quickly pivot to stay competitive, Gu's team shifted from SeedFold to SeedPro2 within six months to overtake Try2. The teams' work began overlapping, but ByteDance didn't force a merger—instead letting the fastest-moving team emerge naturally.

— Gu Quanquan
14:04

Try2 transforms protein design from rare wins to consistent success

Try2, an antibody and nanoantibody design model from Try Discovery Lab, achieved a 10%-20% design success rate, dramatically exceeding David Baker's lab's prior 0.1% rate. Though its structural prediction didn't match AlphaFold3, it showed that modest improvements in structure prediction can yield real pharmaceutical value when aimed at design. ByteDance followed with SeedFold (exceeding AlphaFold3) and SeedPro2, achieving 70%-80% success rates with affinity dozens of times higher than Try2.

— Gu Quanquan
28:20

Model scale expands both capability and the cost of failure

‘Training collapse’ (训飞) refers to sudden Loss or gradient swings, or downstream metrics plateauing for days—indicating failure requiring debugging and restart. Gu reports this became common across Chinese tech companies and startups mid-2024 after model scale and training tokens jumped from 10 trillion to tens of trillions. Longer training cycles demand higher optimizer, normalization, and stability standards; any overlooked detail can compound into massive losses. A billion-parameter model failure costs roughly tens of millions of dollars, making this an increasingly capital-intensive game.

— Gu Quanquan
37:00

ByteDance bans distillation to solve problems, not follow solutions

Until at least his departure, ByteDance prohibited training on distilled data. The tradeoff: near-term capability gaps. The payoff: genuinely mastering high-quality data generation, then producing it perpetually rather than depending on others' distilled outputs. Gu likens this to football recruitment—signing foreign players boosts immediate results but doesn't build youth academies and long-term competitiveness. Distillation maintains top-tier status; refusing it pursues world-first leadership.

— Gu Quanquan
46:28

Pre-training feels stale because organizational consensus matters more than raw speed

Gu admits finding pre-training ‘somewhat boring’ (有点无聊) in later stages, but stresses this reflects organizational risk-aversion, not technical plateau. Many proposals had been debated for months; the chosen path was often months-old before execution began. Major decisions require 90% consensus; if 10% (including Gu) spotted the direction sooner, he still couldn't rush ahead—failure costs too high, team cohesion too precious. This is the price of institutional caution, not lack of technical frontier.

— Gu Quanquan
55:04

AI drug discovery is data-rich and compute-starved: Scaling's unrealized frontier

Public protein structure data totals 1.3 billion samples; SeedFold trained on just 26.5 million, reaching 900 trillion parameters. Most AI drug teams operate at single-digit-thousands of GPUs, far below 10,000-card scale. Data still outpaces available compute. This imbalance is Gu's thesis: sufficient hardware investment builds defensible moats—otherwise AI drug companies resemble traditional biotech with hundreds of GPUs, undifferentiated. Compute, not data, is the bottleneck.

— Gu Quanquan
1:02:51

Wet labs must be in-house or GPU clusters sit idle

Wet labs cannot be outsourced to CROs: designed molecules need in-house verification to count. High-throughput validation must keep pace with Scaling's output rate; outsourced dependencies create bottlenecks that idle compute clusters. Hardware costs roughly triple when buying vs. renting; self-building pays off over three years. Building a wet lab base costs roughly millions in the US—far cheaper than compute clusters—yet investors often shock at compute spend, overlooking the lab's true economic advantage.

— Gu Quanquan
1:22:28

Academia's friendliness masks mediocrity; industry competition exposes real gaps

Gu compares academia's discouragement of same-direction competition to wuxia sects claiming superiority while avoiding confrontation. In fast-moving AI, that courtesy breaks: public forums like Arena reveal true capability instantly. Industry thrives on head-to-head competition (all targeting GPT/Claude) and surfaces real gaps faster than academia's ‘doing different things’ (做不一样的东西) approach, which masks universal mediocrity beneath a veneer of differentiation.
