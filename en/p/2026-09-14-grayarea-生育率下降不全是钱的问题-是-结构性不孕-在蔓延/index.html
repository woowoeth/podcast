Falling Fertility Isn't Just About Money — It's "Structural Infertility" Spreading

Infertility used to be a medical problem like blocked fallopian tubes; now it's more often produced by housing prices, childcare costs, slower career advancement and an excess of dating options — what the author calls structural infertility.

5:25

Upward mobility fell from nine in ten to five in ten

The author pins the "age of uncertainty" to one concrete number: in the US, your odds of doing better than your parents have fallen from 90% to about 50%, roughly a coin flip. That means people who want children no longer have a future they can count on — the idea that a child will do better than you has gone from default premise to a bet you have to place. Layer on near-suffocating inequality and steadily deteriorating job quality, and uncertainty isn't a mood, it's a structural change with indicators behind it.

7:33

Childcare is an after-tax cash outlay

The author gives her own math: if you want to keep working, full-time childcare runs $2,500 to $3,600 a month in after-tax income, paid until the child can enter public school — tens of thousands of dollars over three to four years. Housing is another hard cost, especially in big cities with good jobs. But she stresses this isn't purely material — money reshapes how you understand time, how you rank work and relationships, and what you expect "what a child needs to succeed" to be.

— Anna-Louis Sussman
19:22

The pandemic baby bump happened only among white-collar workers

There was indeed a small fertility rebound in 2021, but it broke along class and racial lines. The author explains it through "social reproduction": feeding yourself and those around you, cleaning, tidying — the labor that keeps life running is never counted in GDP. During the pandemic, white-collar workers stayed home, commutes vanished, life got more stable, and men may have been forced to shoulder a bit more, so this group saw a modest baby boom. Blue-collar and frontline workers who had to leave the house had no such conditions, and so had no such rebound.

25:41

Climate anxiety is fear for the child, not fear of emissions

Two scholars estimate 12.5 million childless Americans have given up having children at least partly for climate reasons. The man in the couple in the author's book isn't unwilling to cut emissions for the planet — it's "I really want a child, but I'm too afraid of the world that child will face." His family is Lebanese, migrated to Germany, and has seen far-right anti-immigrant sentiment firsthand, so he doesn't trust the future to provide clean air and water, or humans to respond with compassion. The author also notes that pinning responsibility on individual reproductive choices is a deflection — the very concept of a carbon footprint was invented by a PR firm for BP.

32:00

Infertility went from a medical problem to a structural one

The author proposes "structural infertility": infertility used to be specific medical conditions like blocked fallopian tubes, sperm problems, implantation problems; now it's the first promotion arriving later, buying a home and stopping having roommates arriving later, meeting and falling in love arriving later (because there are too many options) — these larger structural factors functionally cause infertility. She also mentions the environmental dimension: air pollution and particulates are at least correlated with poor pregnancy outcomes, and wildfire smoke and extreme heat are topics she's currently writing about.

— Anna-Louis Sussman
35:05

The fertility industry now has an investment thesis

Assisted reproductive technology has genuinely worked miracles — IVF has produced an estimated at least 10 million babies in the forty-plus years since its invention. But the author points to the other side: some people hold an investment thesis that "more and more people will experience structural infertility and need this help," and with it comes venture capital and private equity money, and consolidation of fertility clinics — older doctors retiring sell their practices to big chains, in some cases pushing prices up. She is especially wary of egg freezing being pitched as "bank your eggs," as if you must invest in your own reproductive capital and get that baby you long for as the return.

— Anna-Louis Sussman
39:11

Every path leads to egg freezing

The author describes running to three to five egg-freezing info sessions in New York in one winter, each with wine and cheese and crackers. The pitch is a closed loop: if your ovarian reserve is low, hurry and grab the last few eggs; if your reserve is high, all the more reason to freeze more early. Either way, the conclusion is freeze now. She names Kind Body for having driven a van to tech campuses to offer free AMH testing, and the founder of Prelude Network for publicly arguing for genetic testing of all embryos to lower US healthcare spending — the author thinks this has slid toward eugenics, and presupposes IVF becoming universal.

— Anna-Louis Sussman
47:37

Nordic welfare didn't change; the reference point did

If the problem were just a lack of money and security, welfare states like Norway and Denmark shouldn't show the same fertility decline. The author's explanation: their safety nets and family policies are indeed stable, but their baseline is their baseline — the world around them changed. Parenting expectations rose, housing prices rose far too much, and one Norwegian demographer said his parents had him and his sister in their twenties, renting a small apartment, and simply had a belief that "we'll always figure something out." Once that belief disappears, life milestones that could once be completed later become more important instead: you have to steady the ground under your feet before you dare to jump.

Glossary

structural infertility
Functional infertility caused by social-structural factors such as housing prices, childcare costs, slower career advancement and an excess of mate choice.
social reproduction
The unpaid labor — feeding, cleaning, caring — that keeps life running but is never counted in GDP.
anxious class
A term the author borrows from economist Robert Reich, referring to people whose rug could be pulled out at any moment and who lack a safety net.
barbell economy
An economic structure with a mass of low-income people and a tiny top tier, with the middle hollowed out.
AMH testing
Testing anti-Müllerian hormone, used as a rough reflection of ovarian reserve.
