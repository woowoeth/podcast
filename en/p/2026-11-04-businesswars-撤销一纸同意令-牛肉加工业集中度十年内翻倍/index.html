One Rescinded Decree, Beef Concentration Doubled in a Decade

After Reagan's administration lifted restrictions in 1981, the Big Four meat companies surged from controlling 30% of beef processing to 80% within a decade, using mergers rather than technological advantage to monopolize the market.

5:23

Beef Prices Rise, But Ranchers Get a Shrinking Share

Over the past forty-five years, American beef prices have risen more than twice as fast as inflation: a pound of sirloin that cost the equivalent of $5.50 in 1981 now costs $13. The ranchers—over 300,000 of them across America—have not seen that money. They're getting a historically low share of profits. Where did the money go? To a handful of giant meat-processing companies—the middlemen who buy from ranchers, do the slaughtering and processing, and keep the spread.

6:24

The Big Four Meat Companies Are More Concentrated Than Ever

JBS, Tyson, Cargill, and National Beef now control 85% of U.S. beef processing, even more concentrated than when ranchers first sued the packers in the 1990s. Critics argue this concentration is not the natural result of economies of scale, but of price manipulation, collusion, bribery, and legal vertical integration—a cycle that has repeated for over a century.

10:26

Boxed Beef Sped Up Production, But at What Cost?

In 1960, Holman and Anderson built a plant in rural Iowa, relocating the slaughterhouse closer to cattle rather than to cities, and replaced skilled butchers with an assembly line. In 1967 they introduced boxed beef—vacuum-sealed, portioned meat packed at the slaughterhouse and shipped directly—faster, cheaper, and fitting more meat per truck. But unions hated it: it replaced skilled butchers with semi-skilled line workers and cut out the traditional six-to-seven-day aging that improved quality. Speed, cost, and quality: you pick two. Iowa Beef picked the first two.

13:49

To Sell Meat in New York, You Had to Pay the Mob

In 1970, Iowa Beef's boxed meat faced a union boycott in New York, and banks threatened to cut off credit. CEO Holman found himself in a dim hotel room with Moe Steinman, the man who actually controlled New York's meat business. Steinman flipped open a phone book and pointed out 14 supermarket chains that could help Holman break in—for a price: 25 cents for every 100 pounds of boxed beef sold within 120 miles of Columbus Circle, a figure Steinman later doubled unilaterally. The narrator makes the point clear: Steinman owned no product, no factory, no store. The only thing he owned was ‘the gate’—and sometimes the gate is the most valuable piece of real estate on the entire supply chain.

20:07

Bribery Conviction, But a Fine That Barely Stung

New York prosecutors had long wanted to break organized crime's grip on the meat trade. Holman was eventually charged. In 1973 he was indicted on conspiracy to bribe charges, and convicted the next year—but his lawyers persuaded the court that Steinman may never have passed the money to union officials; maybe he just pocketed it himself. The charge was downgraded from bribery to conspiracy to bribe, with a maximum fine of $7,000 (less than $50,000 in today's dollars) and no prison time. That same year, Iowa Beef had revenues of $1.5 billion. The judge even said Holman was the victim of extortion and suggested he appeal. A sentence like this sends a message to the industry: if your arm is thick enough, it's the law.

27:24

Rescind One Old Rule, Concentration Doubles in Years

In 1920, the government forced meat packers to sign the Packers Consent Decree, banning them from owning railways, groceries, and other upstream or downstream operations; Congress reinforced it with the Packers and Stockyards Act in 1921. This regime kept the industry relatively competitive for about fifty years—in 1977, the top three firms controlled less than 20% of the market. But in 1981, a rancher's attorney convinced the court to rescind the decree, arguing that times had changed and the free market should rule. Within years, an oil billionaire bought IBP for $800 million, Cargill and ConAgra respectively acquired MBPXL and Swift, and by 1988 three giants controlled over 70% of fed-cattle slaughter—far above the 36% in 1980. The narrator uses a mosquito net as a metaphor: going a whole night without being bitten doesn't mean mosquitoes aren't there. Taking down the net, the consequences usually don't show until you've been bitten.

32:29

Whoever Can Wait Longer Sets the Price

In the mid-1990s, feedlot operator Mike Calicrate had 20 buyers competing to bid on his cattle. Now he might get only one low-ball offer from IBP all week. If he doesn't sell, his cattle keep eating his feed and taking up his space, and he risks missing the optimal market window; the buyer can afford to wait. The narrator points out the mechanism: whoever can outlast the other controls the clock itself—and through it, not just price but the ability to force it down without saying a word. This is how IBP and other buyers extract lower prices without explicitly threatening anyone.

35:34

With an Exit Strategy, One Man Spoke for 30,000

In 1996, Calicrate was invited to testify at a U.S. Department of Agriculture hearing, accusing meat packers of collusion to suppress prices and bypass open auctions by raising their own cattle. It should have been career suicide—opening his mouth could get him blacklisted by all four giants. But he had an escape route: in 1991 he'd invented and patented a bloodless castration technique; that side business meant he could survive even if the packers cut him off. His testimony moved the hearing. The USDA report that followed admitted that regulation had long been absent. Two months later, Sam Britt gathered 200 ranchers in Amarillo; 10 signed on to sue IBP, and the case eventually became a class action representing 30,000 American ranchers—taking on a multinational alone was a losing fight, but 30,000 ranchers' evidence, lawyers, and money is a different story.

Glossary

boxed beef
Beef portioned and vacuum-sealed at the slaughterhouse, then packed in boxes for direct shipment.
vertical integration
A single company controlling multiple stages of production, from livestock raising through processing to retail.
Packers Consent Decree
A 1920 legal agreement forcing meat packers to divest from railways, groceries, and other operations outside their core business.
feedlot
An industrial facility where cattle are fattened to slaughter weight.
class action
A lawsuit brought by many parties with similar claims, combined into a single case.
