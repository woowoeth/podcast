The narcissism epidemic is media hype: the data doesn't support it, and narcissists themselves suffer more

The much-hyped ‘narcissism epidemic’ has little data behind it; narcissism as measured by the NPI has actually been falling in recent years. True NPD affects about 1%, patients often carry depression and anxiety alongside it, and treatment works but takes years.

It took his wife's one word, narcissist, to make him look

Lee Hammock has a diagnosis of narcissistic personality disorder, but what actually made him see the problem was the line his wife threw at him as she slammed the door mid-argument: ‘Living with a narcissist like you is too hard.’ He angrily Googled it, found the DSM diagnostic criteria overlapped heavily with himself, and remembered that as a kid, when his classmate Tony broke his arm, his first reaction was ‘that's what you get for not listening to me, and now you've ruined outdoor time for everyone.’ The example shows that the core of pathological narcissism is not feeling good about yourself, but persistently treating other people as instruments of your own needs.

The narcissism epidemic does not survive a second look at the data

Keith and Jean analyzed NPI scores from 16000 college students between 1979 and 2006, claimed narcissism levels were rising, and paired that with reality TV, cosmetic surgery, and unusual baby names to propose a ‘narcissism epidemic.’ But another psychologist, Collie, used the same-period ‘Monitoring the Future’ tracking survey of high schoolers and found almost no change on the self-evaluation items. The literature went back and forth after that, and newer data from 2024 shows grandiose narcissism as measured by the NPI is declining. Both camps of researchers now accept at least this much: there is no evidence for a narcissism epidemic today.

— Rose Rimler
26:13

Pathological narcissism is three behaviours: exploitation, entitlement, empathy impairment

Unlike everyday attention-seeking, the core of pathological narcissism is an addiction-like need to feel special at any cost. Craig sums it up as the triple E: Exploitation — willing to hurt others for that feeling of being special; Entitlement — believing the world should revolve around you; Empathy impairment — being unable to see other people's feelings and needs. The DSM requires grandiosity, a need for admiration, and lack of empathy all at once before it counts as NPD. Prevalence in the US is about 1%, far below the internet impression that ‘everyone around me is a narcissist.’

— Craig Malkin
30:00

Narcissists are not happy people; they are anxious and depressed

Research finds that about one third of NPD patients also have major depression and one fifth have generalized anxiety, rates significantly higher than in the general population. Lee's account of it is concrete: criticism is like a wound to him, and a single line someone said to him in a club when he was 21 still surfaces and stings today; when he meets a stranger, his first reflex is to look for their weak point, so he can hit back if they insult him. That constant guardedness and repeatedly ruminated anger explains why ‘feeling special’ has not brought happiness, but anxiety and emptiness instead.

— Rose Rimler
32:52

Treatment works by moving the patient from me-mode to we-mode

There is no drug for NPD; the mainstream treatment is talk therapy, aimed at helping patients recognize the signals that trigger narcissistic behaviour and shift from ‘me mode’ to ‘we mode.’ At his first session, Lee was asked ‘what are you doing here at 32,’ and he simply cried, saying he was empty inside and had no emotions. The therapist told him: you're crying, so there is something in there — it's just blocked. Lee says his first reaction is still the narcissistic one after therapy, but he has learned to pause before acting and ask himself, ‘is this narcissism or is this normal?’ Case studies show improvement is possible after sticking with it for 2.5 to 5 years.

— Rose Rimler
36:39

Three tactics let you stay in a relationship with a narcissist

Craig offers three strategies for people who want to keep the relationship going. First, the empathy prompt: lead with ‘you are the most important person in my life,’ then say ‘when you interrupted me like that, it wrecked me’ — lay down empathy before delivering the feedback. Second, catching good: catch the narcissist's occasional kindness, but tie it to their own interest — don't say ‘that made me feel good,’ say ‘you buying me dinner made me more motivated to finish the work.’ Third, gray rocking: if the first two don't work, become a gray rock — no explaining, no defending, giving them no ammunition to attack with. The precondition is confirming first that the relationship is emotionally and physically safe.

— Craig Malkin
42:33

Excessive focus on yourself is harmful even if you score low

Rose says her takeaway from the episode is this: when we focus excessively on ourselves — our legacy, what other people think of us — that self-immersion does not make us happier, it does damage. So the lesson holds even if you score very low on the narcissism inventory. That loops right back to the episode's judgment: the narcissism-epidemic narrative is largely something the media and generational bias amplified, but narcissism as an internal drive really does grind a person down.

Glossary

NPI (Narcissistic Personality Inventory)
A questionnaire measuring grandiose narcissistic traits, with a population average of about 15.
NPD (Narcissistic Personality Disorder)
The personality disorder defined in the DSM, requiring grandiosity, a craving for admiration, and lack of empathy together.
Grandiose narcissism
The class of narcissistic traits marked by overrating yourself, wanting to be admired, and feeling special.
Gray rocking
Acting boring, emotionless, and unresponsive during conflict so the other person loses their ammunition.
Empathy prompt
A phrasing pattern that first states how important the other person is, then delivers how you were hurt.
Triple E
Exploitation, Entitlement, and Empathy impairment — the three E-initial core behaviours of pathological narcissism.
