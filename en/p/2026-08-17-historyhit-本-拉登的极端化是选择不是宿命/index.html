bin Laden Was Not Born Extreme: Three Turns Pushed Him Onto the Jihadist Road

bin Laden was not an extremist as a child but a marginal figure in a wealthy Saudi family; three turning points — the 1979 Mecca siege, the Afghan battlefield and the Gulf War — put him on the road to global jihad, and he always wanted to bring down the Saudi royal family more than he wanted to fight the United States alone.

He had every privilege and was still a marginal figure in his own family

bin Laden was born in 1957. His father was a bricklayer from Yemen who went on to become one of Saudi Arabia's biggest construction contractors, with close ties to the royal family. His mother was Syrian, the father's fourth wife, and was divorced three years later. He and his mother were brought up by an employee of his father's company, living in a semi-detached house in the suburbs of Jeddah — comfortable, but not lavish. That breaks the familiar story that he grew up inside an extremist or royal environment: he attended a private school modelled on an English public school, loved sports cars, and crashed one. The contradiction that matters is that for all his privilege, he was close to marginal inside the enormous family.

What he took from the Mecca siege was admiration for the attackers

In 1979, armed men occupied the Grand Mosque in Mecca for ten days — the same mosque the bin Laden family had helped rebuild. The Saudi royal family used tanks to put the siege down at Islam's holiest site, and afterwards, to shore up its religious legitimacy, doubled down on promoting Wahhabi positions and exporting the ideology. What bin Laden drew from the episode was not the royal narrative but admiration for the attackers. More than a decade later he told a friend explicitly that those men's faith and their willingness to die were the example he meant to follow. Jason treats this as one of the key turning points for understanding him.

— Jason Burke
30:00

He did not rush to Afghanistan the moment the Soviets invaded

After the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979, bin Laden claimed he left for Afghanistan as soon as he heard the news; in fact he did not enter the country for the first time until 1984. Until then he had been in Pakistan distributing the money he raised, and most of it went to hospitals, clinics and refugee projects, not weapons. On his first trip to the front he saw Afghan fighters who were badly equipped and badly dug in, decided that he understood engineering and construction, and concluded both that he should have come sooner and that he could actually be useful — which is when he said, "Now I know what I want to do with my life." This six-foot-four, originally introverted young man came out of his shell from that point on, and gradually formed the belief that a small number of people with faith could defeat a superpower.

35:00

al-Qaeda opened for business with fifteen men in one room

In the year before the Soviet withdrawal, the Arab Afghan volunteers were arguing among themselves about what to do next. bin Laden proposed founding a new organization to serve as a base for continuing the "victorious jihad" elsewhere in the Islamic world. In 1988, in a room in Peshawar, fifteen men formally founded al-Qaeda. It was not a large force: bin Laden had money, contacts and a talent for propaganda, but the outfit was tiny. The name itself means "the base," "the database" or "the vanguard" — a starting point that could grow into something bigger. That origin is a long way from the later impression that al-Qaeda was a huge organization from the beginning.

— Jason Burke
37:01

The royal family chose American troops over his mujahideen

After Saddam invaded Kuwait, bin Laden went to the Saudi royal family and offered to raise tens of thousands of mujahideen to defend the kingdom. They turned him down politely and invited American troops in instead. bin Laden held that the Prophet Muhammad had said plainly that the Arabian Peninsula could have only one religion, and that Saudi Arabia's decision to let in Americans, Christians and Jews carried an extremely high political price. That rejection turned him from a beneficiary of the family's position into an open critic of Saudi Arabia's rulers, and from there, step by step, toward anti-Americanism. Jason stresses that the Gulf War is what really cemented his split with Saudi Arabia, and that it is also where the narrative that led him to call America "the snake" begins.

— Jason Burke
51:29

He kicked the door in without a plan for what came next

Jason rejects the claim that bin Laden was pursuing a personal vendetta because he had been expelled from Sudan. He had long seen the United States as the source of everything wrong in the Middle East, above all its support for Israel and for the Saudi royal family. His plan was not to drag America into a global conflict — he had not thought that far ahead — but to strike first at what he called "the snake," meaning Washington. At the same time he wanted to overthrow the governments of Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Syria and others he regarded as "fake Muslim" regimes. Jason compares him to Hitler in his day: kick the door in and hope something good happens. Terrorism functions to mobilize, radicalize and polarize, not to achieve calculable strategic objectives.

— Jason Burke
58:21

British troops never went up the mountain; chaos let him escape

Jason flatly denies that British forces ever got near bin Laden. He was at Tora Bora himself at the time, and the British never went up the mountain. The supposed encirclement consisted of a small number of American special forces plus a large body of local militia, pushing up the slopes the way one stalks deer in Scotland; the shockwaves from the B-52 bombing nearly knocked people off their feet a mile away. Everyone assumed bin Laden would cross the mountains into Pakistan; instead he took the risk of slipping through the militia's loose cordon, by bribery or by evasion, and fled north in the opposite direction. This was not the product of careful planning — the chaos handed him his opening, and bought him another ten years.

— Jason Burke
1:01:12

Breaking the hard core turbocharged the ideology instead of ending it

Jason splits al-Qaeda into three parts: the ideology, the loosely affiliated groups around the world, and the hard core in Afghanistan. The American invasion in 2001 broke up the hard core and killed many senior members, but it turbocharged the ideology outward across the globe. Over the following decade, attacks such as Madrid and London were mostly carried out by individuals who had received training but operated semi-autonomously; at the same time formal branches appeared, including in the Maghreb, South Asia and Iraq, the last of which eventually became ISIS. bin Laden's death was therefore not the end of the organization: it still has active branches in places such as the African Sahel, and only its top leadership has gone quiet.

— Jason Burke

Glossary

Wahhabi
An ultra-conservative strand of Islamic doctrine originating in the Arabian Peninsula, insisting on a strictly literal reading of the Quran and the hadith.
Mujahideen
Muslim fighters engaged in jihad, referring in particular to the armed men who resisted the Soviets in the Afghan war.
Muslim Brotherhood
An Islamist organization founded in Egypt in 1928 that seeks to reshape the state along the lines of Islamic law, with influence across the Middle East.
Tora Bora
A cave complex in the Spin Ghar mountains of eastern Afghanistan, the last stronghold of al-Qaeda and bin Laden at the end of 2001.
