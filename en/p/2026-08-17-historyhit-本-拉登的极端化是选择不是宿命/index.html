Bin Laden Was Not Born Extreme: Three Turning Points Pushed Him Onto the Jihadist Path

Bin Laden was not extreme from childhood; he was a marginal figure within the Saudi family. The 1979 Mecca siege, the Afghan battlefield and the Gulf War were the three turning points that pushed him onto the global jihadist path — and he always wanted to overthrow the Saudi royal family more than to fight America alone.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:43

He was privileged, yet a marginal figure within his own family

Bin Laden was born in 1957. His father was a bricklayer from Yemen who became one of Saudi Arabia's biggest builders, with close ties to the royal family. His mother was Syrian, the father's fourth wife, divorced three years later. He and his mother were raised by an employee of his father's company, in a semi-detached house on the outskirts of Jeddah — comfortable but not luxurious. This breaks the common narrative that he grew up in an extreme or royal environment: he attended a private school modelled on an English public school, liked sports cars, and once wrecked one. The key contradiction is that although he was privileged, he was nearly a marginal figure within the vast family.

In the Mecca siege, the attackers were who he admired

In 1979, militants occupied the Grand Mosque in Mecca for ten days — the very mosque bin Laden's family had helped rebuild. The Saudi royal family used tanks to put down the takeover in Islam's holiest place, then, to shore up its religious legitimacy, doubled down on promoting the Wahhabi position and ideological export. What bin Laden took from the episode was not the royal narrative but admiration for the attackers. More than a decade later he told friends explicitly that those men's faith and willingness to die were the example he sought to follow. Jason sees this as a key turning point in understanding him.

— Jason Burke
30:00

He did not rush to Afghanistan the moment the Soviets invaded

After the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979, bin Laden claimed he went as soon as he heard the news; in fact he did not first enter Afghanistan until 1984. Until then he had been in Pakistan distributing funds he had raised, money that went mainly to hospitals, clinics and refugee projects, not to buying weapons. On his first trip to the front, he saw Afghan fighters who were terribly equipped and badly entrenched, and felt that, knowing engineering and construction, he should have come earlier and could do something — and so he said, ‘Now I know what I am going to do with my life.’ This six-foot-four young man, previously introverted, came out of his shell from then on, and gradually formed the belief that a small number of people of faith could defeat a superpower.

— Jason Burke
35:00

When al-Qaeda opened, it had only 15 people

A year before the Soviet withdrawal, the Arab Afghan volunteers were debating internally what to do next. Bin Laden proposed creating a new organisation as a base for continuing the ‘victorious jihad’ elsewhere in the Islamic world. In 1988, in a room in Peshawar, 15 people formally founded al-Qaeda. It was not a large force: bin Laden had money, connections and propaganda skills, but the scale was tiny. The name itself means ‘base’, ‘database’ or ‘vanguard’, representing a starting point that could grow into something larger. This origin is a far cry from the later impression that al-Qaeda was a huge organisation from the start.

— Jason Burke
37:01

The royal family chose the US military, not his mujahideen

After Saddam invaded Kuwait, bin Laden met the Saudi royal family and offered to muster tens of thousands of mujahideen to defend the kingdom. The royal family politely refused and instead invited US forces in. Bin Laden held that the Prophet Muhammad had clearly said only one religion could exist in the Arabian Peninsula, and that Saudi Arabia letting in Americans, Christians and Jews carried an extremely high political cost. This rejection turned him from a beneficiary of the family's vested interests into an open critic of Saudi rulers, and from there he moved step by step toward anti-Americanism. Jason stresses that the Gulf War truly cemented his split with Saudi Arabia, and was the starting point of the narrative in which he later called America ‘the snake’.

— Jason Burke
51:29

He kicked the door open without thinking through what lay behind it

Jason rejects the claim that bin Laden launched a personal vendetta because he was expelled from Sudan. He had long seen America as the root of all evil in the Middle East, especially its support for Israel and the Saudi royal family. His plan was not to drag America into a global conflict — he did not think that far ahead — but first to attack what he called ‘the snake’, namely Washington. At the same time he wanted to overthrow what he considered the ‘pseudo-Muslim’ regimes of Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Syria and others. Jason compares him to Hitler: kicking the door open and expecting something good to happen. The function of terrorism is to mobilise, radicalise and polarise, not to achieve calculable strategic goals.

— Jason Burke
58:21

British troops never went up the mountain; chaos let him go

Jason flatly denies that British forces ever got close to bin Laden. He was there at Tora Bora, and British troops never went up the mountain at all. The so-called siege was just a handful of US special forces plus large numbers of local militia, pushing up the mountain like a Scottish deer hunt; the shockwave from B-52 bombing nearly knocked people over a mile away. Everyone assumed bin Laden would cross the mountains into Pakistan, but he took the risk, through bribery or evasion slipping through the militia's loose encirclement, and fled north in the opposite direction. This was not the result of careful planning; chaos gave him the opportunity, and gave him ten more years of life.

— Jason Burke
1:01:12

Breaking up the hard core turbocharged the ideology instead

Jason splits al-Qaeda into three parts: the ideology, the loose global network of affiliates, and the hard core in Afghanistan. The 2001 US invasion broke up the hard core and killed many senior members, but turbocharged the ideology globally. Over the following decade, attacks in Madrid, London and elsewhere were mostly carried out by trained but semi-autonomous individuals; formal branches also appeared, including in the Maghreb, South Asia and Iraq, the last of which eventually evolved into ISIS. Bin Laden's death was therefore not the end of the organisation: it still has active branches in places like the Sahel in Africa, though the top leadership has become more low-profile.

— Jason Burke

Glossary

Wahhabi
An ultra-conservative Islamic doctrine originating in the Arabian Peninsula, advocating strict literal interpretation of the Quran and the hadith.
Mujahideen
Islamic fighters engaged in jihad, specifically the armed men who resisted the Soviets in the Afghan war against the USSR.
Muslim Brotherhood
An Islamic organisation founded in Egypt in 1928, advocating the transformation of the state according to Islamic law, with influence across the Middle East.
Tora Bora
A cave complex in the Spin Ghar mountains of eastern Afghanistan, al-Qaeda and bin Laden's last stronghold in late 2001.
