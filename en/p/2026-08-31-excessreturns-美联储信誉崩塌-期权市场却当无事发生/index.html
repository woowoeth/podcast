The Fed's Credibility Breaks, and the Options Market Shrugs

The Fed's credibility cracked over a single decision not to hike, while the options market still prices Fed day like any other day — the cheapest insurance may be an illusion.

Three pieces of evidence — sticky inflation, the midterms, and Fed credibility — point at the same thing: volatility is too low and cheap protection is a mirage. The frameworks and data are concrete.

3:03

A Fed without forward guidance is flipping a coin

The two hosts open by naming the change: in past FOMC meetings the market knew the odds with near certainty beforehand, but this time the probability of a hike at the next meeting is only 55 to 45 — barely better than a coin flip. Add Warsh's Jackson Hole statement that he wants to fade forward guidance, and every meeting from here can turn into ‘wait for the outcome, then react.’ Rate surprises transmit through debt and leverage into every asset, so volatility has to be repriced. The hosts also assert, based on history, that once the market truly tests them, almost every official eventually folds — so this current commitment to ‘never give the market expectations in advance’ is itself the variable most worth testing.

— Matt / Jack
17:31

Midterm volatility is priced as an ordinary day

Kevin Muir thinks midterm volatility is badly underpriced. During the 2022 midterms, the options market priced a single-day S&P move of ±1.8% for voting day; in 2018 it was ±1.25%; now it's only ±0.9%. And this time Trump's approval is at historic lows, and he just spent an hour in a primetime address talking about ‘election fraud,’ as if paving the way to challenge the result. Muir is careful to say: even if you think he's overthinking it, that's fine — as voting day approaches, the question of whether the election will be disrupted will itself get repriced. With volatility near seasonal lows and implied correlation also at historic lows, the odds on being long volatility are far better than usual.

— Kevin Muir
23:41

The inflation pullback is a supply illusion; demand hasn't cooled

Ahan Manon breaks the PCE components into ‘demand-driven’ and ‘supply-driven.’ Right now the contributions are 3.31 versus 0.38; even if you strip out the energy supply shock from the Iran situation entirely, the remaining demand side alone is enough to keep inflation above the Fed's 2% target. Historically, demand-driven inflation is slow and persistent, while supply-driven inflation tends to spike fast and mean-revert within 6 to 12 months. The breadth is the bigger problem: in recent months, 70% to 80% of PCE components rose more than 2%. June briefly went negative month-over-month and July only rebounded 16 basis points, but that short-term cooling came almost entirely from the supply side; the demand-driven trend was never interrupted.

— Ahan Manon
34:51

The credibility one hike could have fixed, he didn't do

Ben Hunt says Warsh threw a ‘self-inflicted wound’ at the press conference after the July meeting. The new face and the hawkish inflation talk had repaired the ‘Fed credibility’ narrative substantially; but what was actually needed was an immediate 25 basis point hike. Do that one time and the market believes ‘he means what he says,’ and you never need to hike repeatedly afterward. Skip that step and the narrative flips completely: every independent piece that appeared in the financial media that week, in different words, said the same thing — ‘meet the new boss, same as the old boss.’ Once credibility shatters it's hard to glue back; the August gold rally was precisely the market's response to ‘hawkish talk, no action.’

— Ben Hunt
40:13

After credibility is damaged, only verbal financial repression is left

Ben Hunt lists four pressures the Fed and Treasury must manage but absolutely cannot let blow up: the Iran war has no easy exit and oil stays higher for longer; fiscal stimulus effects fade and tariff pressure returns; the insurance sector has a systemic problem of captive insurers being used to warehouse private investments; and 10-year and 30-year long-end yields above all cannot be pushed higher by the market. Any one of them could be the fuse for the next financial crisis. Handling them requires credibility, and credibility has just been damaged, so every policy option left is ‘verbal financial repression’ — using statements and intervention to hold yields down rather than letting the market price them. His judgment: this operation has no maturity date, it only accelerates.

— Ben Hunt
49:25

The options market treats a Fed meeting as an ordinary day

The options term structure Brent Kachuba shows has almost no event risk: the S&P 0DTE straddle on the day of Warsh's speech was worth only 38 index points; SPX options expiring August 31 carried implied volatility of just 7%, the overall term structure sat at a 90-day low, and put open interest was very low. He normally avoids trading Fed events because the event turns options into a kind of ‘tax’ — you collect premium before the event, and time value decays the moment it passes. But what's special this time is that the market has crushed expected volatility to near zero and nobody is buying protection; before Jackson Hole he bought a few ‘lottery tickets’ with extremely cheap short-dated put spreads, because if Warsh says one thing the market doesn't want to hear, naked option sellers get flattened instantly. Once the event passes is the better time to chase directional trends.

— Brent Kachuba
58:42

Outsource the writing and you outsource the thinking

The closing topic is the Druckenmiller column handed to the WSJ: he said publicly it was written with AI, for the simple reason that AI writes better than he does. The two hosts argue the core question is whether the views and the emotion belong to the author; Druckenmiller's piece reads like his usual intuitive judgment, with AI only organizing and expressing it, so it isn't deception. Ben Hunt draws the line at the writing itself: writing is part of the thinking process, ‘I write it down to figure out what I think.’ When the text-generation step is outsourced, readers lose an important clue — whether the author actually thought these words through. Investment blogs are already full of obvious AI output; judging whether an opinion is reliable can't rest on whether the prose is pretty, but on whether the view has a thinking process behind it.

— Ben Hunt / Host

Glossary

forward volatility
The expected volatility for a future period, extracted from the prices of options with two different expiries.
demand-based inflation
Price increases caused by nominal demand exceeding supply capacity, typically slow and persistent.
zero DTE straddle
Buying both a call and a put expiring the same day, with the price reflecting the market's bet on that day's move.
jawboning financial repression
Officials holding Treasury yields down with statements and expectation management rather than rate tools, distorting price signals.
calendarized gamma
Lining up option gamma across expiries by time to see where traders are concentrated long or short.
