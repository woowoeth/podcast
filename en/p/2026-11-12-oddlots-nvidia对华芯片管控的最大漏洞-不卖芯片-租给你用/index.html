The biggest loophole in NVIDIA's China chip controls: not selling chips, but renting them to you

Megaspeed spent $4.6 billion on NVIDIA chips to sublease to Alibaba, and remote access breaks no law; after NVIDIA's own audit, 50,000 chips remain unaccounted for, some of them the most advanced Blackwell.

Megaspeed assembled $4.6 billion in NVIDIA chips through an intricate shell company structure

Megaspeed is Southeast Asia's largest NVIDIA chip buyer and Alibaba's primary compute supplier. It was spun off from a Chinese gaming company, went through a BVI shell company, registered in Singapore, and became an official NVIDIA partner four days later. The company has been under investigation by the US and Singapore for at least a year. According to reporter Mackenzie Hawkins, it is 100% owned by Singaporeans, but its structure is essentially that of a shell company, and the source of its funding remains an unresolved question.

— Mackenzie Hawkins
15:37

After its own audit, NVIDIA still cannot account for 50,000 chips

At the US government's request, NVIDIA tallied the chips Megaspeed had received across three countries, eight companies, and over a dozen data centers. In November 2024, it stated publicly that ‘all shipments to our partners are where they should be.’ But after cross-checking with trade records from Malaysia and Indonesia, the reporter found roughly 50,000 chips unaccounted for, most of them NVIDIA's then-best hardware, Blackwell. NVIDIA later found another batch of previously undisclosed warehouses but has consistently refused to confirm the exact numbers. In December it only vaguely said it had found ‘essentially all’ of the chips, promising further audits—but when the reporter pressed for details more than a dozen times afterward, NVIDIA provided no answers.

— Mackenzie Hawkins
18:44

Megaspeed presented a Shanghai project as an unnamed third country data center to investors

Megaspeed's website lists three data centers, but its 2024 pitch to investors described five, spanning three countries, one located in ‘an unnamed third country’—yet the rendering shown was for a Shanghai project under construction. This was precisely where Megaspeed's ‘corporate twin’ company in China was hiring engineers to use restricted NVIDIA hardware, and the investors behind it have long-standing business dealings with Alice (the executive caught up in Jensen Huang's affair). The company has offered no explanation.

— Mackenzie Hawkins
28:02

Renting NVIDIA chips to Chinese AI companies is legal in nine of ten cases

So long as the data center sits in Southeast Asia, and you remotely rent computing power to cutting-edge Chinese AI companies—even for training the most advanced open-source models—it is legal in the overwhelming majority of cases. The one exception is knowing that the compute will be used by a foreign adversary's military or intelligence service. In its final days, the Biden administration issued a global restriction scheme called the AI diffusion rule in an attempt to close this loophole, originally set to take effect in May 2025, but the Trump administration has not enforced it. Morgan Stanley and others call it a ‘loophole in export controls,’ but legally speaking, it is merely the US government's own policy choice, not a loophole.

— Mackenzie Hawkins
32:09

NVIDIA's general counsel called export control enforcement directly about the investigation

NVIDIA's general counsel Tim Teeter called the head of BIS, the export control enforcement agency, directly in late summer last year, asking whether they had been involved in the investigation into Alice's detention in Singapore—NVIDIA claimed Teeter didn't know at the time that she was Jensen Huang's extramarital partner. Thereafter, if investigators wanted information from NVIDIA, they had to first get approval from or route it through Jeffrey Kessler, a special privilege no other major tech company has enjoyed. This weakened the agency's investigative capability, and ultimately BIS handed the case to DOJ, the Department of Justice.

— Mackenzie Hawkins
38:21

DeepSeek ordered 160,000 Huawei chips but must wait over a year for delivery

DeepSeek placed a large order for 160,000 of Huawei's best AI chips, the 950DT, for the next-generation hyperscale compute cluster it is building in Inner Mongolia—though the US government believes DeepSeek installed NVIDIA's Blackwell chips at this same location. Huawei simply does not have the capacity to deliver this order within a year, and DeepSeek has even lobbied China's government to press Huawei to accelerate delivery. The 950DT was designed by Huawei for training; DeepSeek plans to use it for inference first, not training.

— Mackenzie Hawkins
41:23

Huawei exported its best chips to Egypt using its old telecom playbook

Huawei bid on a tender with Egypt's government to provide AI computing power to Egyptian defense-related enterprises—a small volume, about 2,000 chips, yet Huawei deployed its best hardware. With capacity so tight, every chip exported has to be squeezed out of domestic allocation. After spotting this, the US government hastily convened a consortium of American companies to counter-bid, since almost no one has previously treated Egypt as a key market. This is seen as the same playbook Huawei used to dominate 3G and 4G telecom equipment globally, and the US is trying to prevent history from repeating.

— Mackenzie Hawkins

Glossary

NVIDIA Cloud Partner (NCP)
NVIDIA-certified top-tier cloud computing partner status; Megaspeed holds this designation.
AI diffusion rule
A global NVIDIA chip export licensing system issued in the Biden administration's final days, which the Trump administration has not enforced.
remote access
Chinese companies accessing NVIDIA computing power by leasing overseas data centers rather than buying chips outright.
zero-day inferencing
Optimizing inference for Huawei's chips on the day a model launches, rather than completing pretraining on them.
