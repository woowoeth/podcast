In the Bronze Age, 'sacker of cities' was the highest honor for a hero

Nearly every hero in Homer's epics engaged in piracy: Odysseus's first act after leaving Troy was to raid coastal cities, and the 'sacker of cities' was precisely the Bronze Age's highest praise for a hero.

Piracy was not a sideshow, but the dark twin of trade

Kelder sets the tone at the outset: wherever valuable goods—including people—flow at sea, pirates appear. The more prosperous the trade, the wealthier the coasts, the more rampant the piracy; only when great powers establish secure sea lanes does piracy temporarily recede. This means the Bronze Age Mediterranean's booming trade network wasn't opposed to piracy—it was piracy's breeding ground, a single ecosystem where trade and raiding fed each other.

16:36

The earliest recorded piracy appears in a Hittite king's complaint

Hittite texts from around 1400 BCE, including what's known as the Maduwatta Complaint, record the earliest known piracy: a Hittite vassal king named Maduwatta allied with a Greek named Atarissiya to raid territories belonging to the Hittite king, including Cyprus. The name Atarissiya is almost certainly a Hittite rendering of 'Atreid'—the family designation of Agamemnon and Menelaus in Homer—marking the earliest appearance of Mycenaean Greeks in texts from other civilizations.

34:17

The pottery mistaken for a harvest scene depicts a pirate victory

The famous Harvester Vase was long interpreted as a harvest celebration with peasants parading pitchforks, but its original Italian excavators recognized it was a military column. Kelder endorses this reading—one rejected by mainstream archaeology for nearly a century: the pitchforks are tridents, the 'scale cloaks' are scale armor, and the figure scholars took for a drunken farmer was more likely a captive under guard. The entire scene shows not a harvest celebration but the triumphal return of a pirate fleet.

— Jorrit Kelder
39:31

A gold ring shows a 'hero' with trident hauling captive women

A Mycenaean gold ring unearthed in the Athenian Agora is labeled in the museum as 'Minotaur', but the design actually shows a man in a horned helmet holding a trident, leading two women with ropes around their wrists. Kelder argues that the trident was almost certainly the symbol of a raider, making this ring essentially a Bronze Age embodiment of the 'city-sacker hero' from Homer's epics.

— Jorrit Kelder
45:36

The Trojan horse tale may originate in Egyptian siege deception

We tend to think of Mycenaeans as this era's fiercest maritime raiders, but Kelder argues this is an illusion created because only Greek accounts survived; records from other peoples were mostly lost. Egyptian general Yehuti (耶胡提) besieged Jaffa for ten years without taking it, then feigned retreat, leaving behind 'tribute' jars. The delighted Jaffa residents brought the jars into the city, only to find Egyptian soldiers hidden inside—nearly the prototype of the Trojan horse. This shows that raiding and deception were shared tactics across the eastern Mediterranean, not uniquely Greek heroic narratives.

— Jorrit Kelder
52:53

As kings consolidated power, displaced nobles increasingly turned to piracy

Around 1300-1250 BCE, Mycenaean royal power reached its peak, with massive construction of citadel walls and coastal fortresses. On the surface, this looked like the state gaining control of the sea lanes. But Kelder points out this created a paradox: centralization meant local nobles lost territory, influence, and prestige. For these marginalized nobles to regain status, one of the most effective routes was to become a successful pirate leader and raise their own forces. State-building aimed at suppressing piracy may instead have generated more of it.

— Jorrit Kelder
58:01

Pirates became deadly not through fleet size, but through new technologies

Kelder rejects the idea that late-period sea raiding was won through superfleets and numbers alone. Ugaritic texts show that only a few ships were needed to raid a city. The real turning point came around 1300 BCE with key maritime innovations: faster war galleys, rigging that allowed ships to sail upwind, and mast-mounted observation platforms. These technologies enabled small pirate groups to appear suddenly, withdraw at speed, and inflict destruction and panic far exceeding what came before—through tactical innovation, not fleet size.

— Jorrit Kelder
1:02:09

The 'Sea Peoples' were not barbarian invaders, but network inhabitants

The Sea Peoples are often pictured as mysterious barbarian fleets appearing from nowhere, but Kelder argues that many so-called Sea Peoples likely came from the very regions they had been raiding—like Atarissiya and Maduwatta mentioned earlier. These were simultaneously victims and perpetrators; today's raided communities became tomorrow's raiders. Famine, plague, and the loss of status for nobles due to royal centralization pushed people who had originally lived by trade toward raiding instead. The late Bronze Age collapse emerges not as a stark conflict between civilization and barbarian invasion, but as a gray zone where allegiances and roles blurred.
