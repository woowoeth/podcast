Korean aesthetic medicine is cheap enough that losing the tax refund still beats China; China is stuck on policy ambiguity

Korea has built aesthetic medicine into a mass-market, tourism-facing industry — pricing is opaque but the overall cost is low. China has the demand without the policy, the market is disorderly, and the money is going to Korea.

In Korea, injectables are not plastic surgery — they are dermatology

In Korean usage the boundary between aesthetic medicine and plastic surgery is clearly drawn. Plastic surgery means procedures involving the scalpel and the bone; injections and energy-based devices fall under dermatology. When a Korean says ‘going to the dermatology department at the hospital’, they mean non-surgical treatments — skin boosters, botulinum toxin. The classification shapes how people think about the spending: double-eyelid surgery counts as plastic surgery and requires cutting, while injectables are treated as everyday skincare. Korean society even treats plastic surgery as a summer project after the college entrance exam — hospitals give discounts on presentation of the exam admission ticket, and there are ‘buy one, get one’ promotions.

The concierge clinics cost only 20% to 30% more; the logic is different

Korean aesthetic clinics fall broadly into two types. One is the assembly line: you walk in, name the procedure, and get whichever doctor is on rotation — the fast-food model. The other puts you in a one-on-one consultation with the doctor, who sets the treatment plan while a separate consultant negotiates the price; this is the type people call ‘concierge style’. Quan Xiaoxing went to the second kind this time, three injectables plus one energy-based treatment, at roughly 2,000 to 3,000 yuan. The price gap between the two types is about 20% to 30%, but the service logic is entirely different. The assembly line is built for throughput; the concierge model is built around the consultation and an individualized plan.

14:40

One procedure carries seven or eight prices, and foreigners get the highest

Korean aesthetic pricing is extremely opaque, and many hospitals run multiple price lists at once. Quan Xiaoxing has seen a quote sheet on which a single procedure had seven or eight prices — the filed price, the internet price, the foreign-agent price — with nearly a twofold spread between top and bottom. The most expensive channel is the overseas agent; the cheapest is the internal staff rate. He himself was once offered a commission by a clinic, with a kickback of more than 50%. A price structure like this makes it nearly impossible for a consumer to judge the real cost, and it hands agents and consultants enormous room to negotiate.

Chinese men's appearance anxiety will top out around 30% to 40%

On the market for male aesthetic treatment in China, Quan Xiaoxing expects growth, but bounded growth. He estimates that 70% of young Korean men have appearance anxiety; in China the ceiling is probably 30% to 40%, while the current figure may be under 5%. That gap implies a large amount of headroom, but it is unlikely that China fully replicates the Korean level. Chinese male spending on aesthetic treatment will concentrate in tier-one cities; the lower-tier market does not need attention in the near term. Fan Yuru argues that penetration in tier-one cities is already fairly high, but the market has not yet broken out.

35:24

Korean aesthetic medicine sells for more in Hong Kong than in Korea itself

Korea is exporting these services, and Hong Kong is the clearest example. Korean doctors open clinics there, Korean hair salons keep multiplying, and prices run higher than they do inside Korea, because a ‘Korean face’ carries a premium. Some young Koreans arrive on a working holiday visa, take a job, then convert to a work visa and stay on, working in skin management, hairdressing and similar trades. Mainland China has also opened up short-term practice certification for foreign doctors, but regulation there is far stricter than in Hong Kong, and the Korean model is hard to transplant directly.

41:34

Korea's clinics got professional through sheer volume, not through crackdowns

The Korean aesthetics industry moved from disorder toward professional standards, and the core reason is accumulated ‘volume’. There are plenty of clinics, but only a limited number of universities training doctors; doctors hold high status, the barrier to entry is high, and they must obtain specialist certification. Over the past seven or eight years, hospital signage has been standardized to distinguish ‘dermatology hospital’ from ‘hospital, department of dermatology’, and having an anesthesiologist permanently on site has become a selling point. Even so, problems like ghost surgery remain unsolved, and the tug-of-war over interests between physicians' organizations and nurses continues.

— Quan Xiaoxing
46:48

The tax refund is gone and Korean aesthetic medicine is still cheaper than China

The Korean government places aesthetic medicine within the tourism industry, a sector it actively wants to push. Until the end of last year, aesthetic treatments were still eligible for a tax refund; that was abolished this year. Plenty of people joke that the president is short of money, but even with the tax added, Korea remains cheaper than China. Japan took a different route: its aesthetic sector runs along medical and wellness lines. Japan has high-value-added offerings such as health screenings and stem cell treatments, while Korea draws overseas visitors on price and convenience.

— Quan Xiaoxing
49:56

What China lacks is not technology but an official position

The problem with China's aesthetics industry is not technology or talent, but that officialdom and mainstream ideology neither promote aesthetic medicine nor oppose it — the stance is ambiguous. Demand among the public is strong and many people fly to Korea to spend, but the authorities have not treated aesthetic medicine as an industry to be developed. Quan Xiaoxing argues that if China viewed it as positively as Korea does, it could out-compete on both equipment and raw materials, but as things stand the money all goes to Korea. He expects that over the next 5 to 10 years, as the younger generation gains a voice, attitudes will change — though the policy side may still not open up.
