Death Is the Last Enemy, Not the Final Word: A Former Senator on Borrowed Time

Ben Sasse, a terminal pancreatic cancer patient and former senator, speaks from borrowed time: his tumor has shrunk by more than 80% but may not be curable; he calls death ‘the last enemy, not the final word’ and traces executive overreach back to legislative retreat.

0:52

The tumor shrank 80%, and it still may not be curable

At the time of the interview (July 3, 2026), he had lived roughly 6 months and 3 weeks past the verdict that he had ‘90 days to live.’ Pancreatic cancer has an overall mortality rate of 87%; at stage IV, the 12-month mortality rate is 97%. His tumor has shrunk by more than 80%, but he says it may not be curable. He calls this stretch ‘borrowed time’: every extra week, dinner and July 4th spent with his three children, ages 15 to 25, is an enormous blessing. This premise determines everything discussed afterward — how a man who has been told exactly when he will die thinks about institutions, technology and faith.

— Ben Sasse
6:18

Executive overreach is the effect; legislative retreat is the cause

Sasse sees executive overreach as a reactive phenomenon, with the disease in legislative retreat. He cites interviews with White House chiefs of staff from the 2012 Presidential Transition Project: senior senators from both parties routinely ask presidents to enact popular policies by executive order, because a legislative vote would offend a small number of opponents. Congress thus hands off decision-making power while complaining that the White House has grown too big. He inverts the popular narrative of ‘restraining the president’: what must change first is legislators' habit of refusing to bear the political cost of policy.

— Ben Sasse
14:49

Entitlements are 70% of federal spending and unsustainable

Asked what legislation he would still push, he says first, reforming the welfare state: Medicare, Medicaid, Obamacare, Social Security and debt interest together exceed 70% of federal spending, which is unsustainable, and sticking the next generation with the bill is unfair. Second, dealing with the mid-career job disruption brought by the AI revolution; he argues for rebuilding retraining that actually works. What the two issues share: today's institutional design is not prepared for the next generation or for new technology.

— Ben Sasse
37:09

1.5% of the faculty carries an entire university's research

He uses the University of Florida to illustrate the structural distortion of large research universities: the main campus has about 85,000 people and 6,300 teaching staff; the median professor spends 60% of time on research leave and actually teaches very little; research funding is $1.3 billion, with 80 professors contributing more than 95% of it — 1.5% of the faculty carries the vast majority of the research, while other professors produce nothing and still enjoy research leave. He asks: when research output is concentrated in the hands of a very few, how should a university explain how it spends its money?

— Ben Sasse
51:03

Death shatters false idols and forces attention back to real relationships

He says the core of his faith is sin, grace and gratitude; the diagnosis did not shake his faith but made him calmer. He describes death as ‘the last enemy, not the final word’ — enemy means it should not be cooperated with, not the final word means it does not hold the last decision. Treatment side effects like vomiting and bleeding made him realize that death is a truth injection: it shatters false idols such as workaholism and online flame wars and forces attention back to real relationships.

— Ben Sasse
1:00:27

He hates pancreatic cancer but would not go back to before the diagnosis

He hates pancreatic cancer and would not wish it on anyone; but he would not go back to the stage of not knowing ‘the power of pancreatic cancer prayer.’ For the seven weeks before the diagnosis, he was in severe pain all night, every night, running hot water on his spine in the shower, crying out to God. His prayer practice is not mystified: the family prays aloud daily, and he personally follows a structure of worship, confession, thanksgiving and petition. He says all life carries a terminal diagnosis, and that relationships — with God and with the people you love — are what matter most.

— Ben Sasse
1:09:00

Asked heaven or hell, the right answer is both

He defines the present as an economic revolution set off by a technological revolution: both time and space will be reshaped; looking back a hundred years from now, people will not be talking about today's politics. In his own position he sits between accelerationism and safetyism: Marc Andreessen's answer is too simple, while bureaucratic regulation in Washington will only let the CCP win. Answering whether the digital revolution brings heaven or hell, he says the right answer is ‘yes’ — both, because all human behavior will become faster, cheaper and more widespread.

— Ben Sasse
1:20:18

Death is an evil enemy and should not be accepted lightly

At the end of the episode, Russ quotes Dylan Thomas's poem ‘Do not go gentle into that good night,’ saying Sasse is humble yet still rages against the dying of the light. Sasse takes it up: death is an evil enemy and should not be accepted lightly; late-stage pancreatic cancer often leaves people dispirited and their abdomen frozen, but his choice is to fight as much as he can while keeping the wisdom of ‘numbering his days.’ This passage keeps the whole conversation from sinking into ‘accept reality’ chicken soup, and leaves it resting in the tension of a man facing death.

— Ben Sasse
