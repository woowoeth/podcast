PMs shouldn't write code in the AI era: throughput is no longer the bottleneck, direction is

Atlassian turned PMs and designers into ‘AI builders’ to run an experiment; after a month the team abandoned the model on their own—because AI made output faster, but no one knew which way to row the boat.

4:02

PMs bridge context gaps; they don't own decisions

Sherif emphasizes a counterintuitive idea: PMs don't own decisions—they own decision speed. He recalls complaining early in his career that he was making too many decisions and slowing down his team; his boss replied that he should spend more time on strategy instead. His solution: always give your team a ‘shared brain’. That means continuously filling three context gaps: customer context, product-market context, and business context. The job is not to stand at the center of decisions but to distribute these perspectives. He even recommends PMs ‘stay away from the Jira backlog’—when you think a card is more important, it's usually just because you have context your team doesn't have.

An experiment making PMs and designers into AI builders failed in a month

Atlassian ran a real experiment: they assembled a pure ‘AI builder’ team by extracting PMs and designers from their normal roles and removing those role distinctions. In month one, the team reported ‘unprecedented speed’. But after a month, everyone voluntarily reverted to their traditional roles. The reason: the team realized throughput was never the bottleneck. AI had already made throughput cheap. What was truly scarce was direction—the need for more people to ‘steer the boat’, not more people to build it.

— Sherif
26:26

Everyone can prototype; PMs remain among the worst solution designers

Sherif draws an analogy to the Balsamiq era, when everyone supposedly could make mockups. Atlassian learned that PMs are actually among the worst solution designers in the company—having a tool doesn't substitute for domain expertise. AI will amplify your strengths, but it will equally amplify your weaknesses. The lesson: specialization still matters. Everyone's capability circle is expanding, but there's no more overlap between circles. Professional division of labor hasn't been erased.

— Sherif
32:34

Mature companies should hire more junior staff in the AI era

This is the spiciest claim in the episode. Many mature organizations think the AI era means they don't need to hire junior staff anymore. Sherif pushes back: the biggest problem mature teams face is cultural transformation—learning to work in new ways. And ‘learning’ is easier than ‘forgetting old habits and relearning’. Junior staff have no baggage; they're more willing to try new tools. Internal data show junior staff adopt new tools at significantly higher rates than senior staff (around 38% higher), while senior staff use them more precisely and ship higher-quality output but resist changing how they work. The two groups are complementary; you need both.

— Sherif
44:49

Design hiring plateaus because few see experience as the remaining moat

When Lenny mentions that design hiring is flat and there are only 108 open user research positions globally, Sherif offers a hypothesis he admits is unproven: when everyone can generate the same features using the same AI, experience becomes one of the only remaining differentiators, and the market hasn't realized it yet. At Atlassian, design has never played as important a role in roadmap decisions as it does now. He's ‘completely convinced experience will determine everything’ and half-jokes that now is the time to ‘stock up on designers cheaply’.

— Sherif
46:52

Starting with the problem statement is not always the right way

Sherif challenges PM orthodoxy about always asking the question before proposing a solution. When you're exploring what can be productized in a space, giving customers a ‘canvas’ (like a generic chat box) often reveals the real problem faster than asking questions first. At Confluence, users used the chat box to group sticky notes; that behavior later became a dedicated grouping feature. When setting product strategy, the better approach is to first inventory ‘what unique assets we have’, then reverse-engineer how to win. He cites Amazon reverse-engineering ‘everything from a bookstore’ to its ‘everything store’ strategy.

— Sherif
52:58

Bolting AI onto existing products is often the best strategy

Against the popular wisdom that ‘you should never bolt AI onto legacy products—redesign your whole workflow’, Sherif offers a conditional counterargument: if you already have a mature product with millions of users, the better move is to study the workflows already embedded in the product (even informal, lowercase-w workflows), then inject agents into specific workflow nodes rather than rewrite the entire product from scratch. Jira applied this thinking: customers already modeled tens of billions of business workflows on the platform. Rather than rebuild, bolt agents into existing workflow nodes. Around 10 million agents run monthly in Jira and Confluence.

— Sherif
1:02:42

New manager-PMs most often fail by handing off too quickly

The most common mistake new manager-PMs make is letting go too fast. They think full delegation is good leadership. Actually, the best managers at Atlassian are the ones who roll up their sleeves and dig into product review details with their team, exploring solutions together. Teams want to see their manager leading by example, in the trenches, not coaching from the sidelines.

Glossary

PM Craft Triangle
Categorizes PMs into three natural modes of product exploration: general manager, scientist, and artist.
artist PM
A PM oriented toward vision and experience, often intuiting solutions before reverse-engineering the problem.
AI builder
A role using AI to independently complete most software development work without distinct PM or designer roles.
bolting on AI
Embedding AI or agents into existing product workflow nodes rather than rebuilding the entire product.
