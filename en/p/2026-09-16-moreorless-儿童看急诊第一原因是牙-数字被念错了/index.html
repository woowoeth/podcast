Bad teeth are the number one reason kids go to A&E? The number got misread

A British MP said teeth are the top reason children end up in A&E. The actual figure refers to 5-to-18-year-olds hospitalised for tooth decay — not the same thing, and the decay rate is actually drifting down.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:01

Teeth are the top reason for A&E visits — that claim is wrong

Leila Moran, a Liberal Democrat MP and chair of the Health and Social Care Committee, said on Radio 4's The World at One that the number one reason children go to A&E is teeth. Chris Venatza, a consultant in paediatric dentistry at Newcastle University, flatly rejected it: she very likely conflated two sets of numbers. What actually ranks first is "hospital admission for tooth decay treatment" — the leading cause of hospital admission for children aged 5 to 18. A&E visits, by contrast, have all sorts of causes, and there is simply no data showing whether dentistry ranks high or low among them. Moran's office later confirmed Chris was right, and that she had meant the hospital admission figure, not the A&E figure.

— Chris Venatza
3:09

Lots of hospital tooth extractions, because the rules changed in 2001

When children are hospitalised for teeth, it is usually not for a filling but for an extraction. Extractions are harder for a child to endure, so children who cannot cooperate for an extraction in a dental clinic may need general anaesthetic. There is an institutional turning point here: in 2001 the rules changed, and ordinary dental practices were no longer allowed to administer general anaesthetic — anyone needing it had to go to hospital. So a large share of the hospital admission numbers are general-anaesthetic extractions. This explains why "hospital admission" and "A&E" are two entirely different measures: one is a surgical arrangement pushed into hospitals by the rules, the other is an unplanned visit.

— Chris Venatza
4:10

The admission rate is actually falling, not a crisis

Chris gives the admission rate per 100,000 population: about 320 in 2015, down to 270 before the pandemic, and 250 now. Host Tim Harford's reaction was that this doesn't look like a crisis, and the trend is even rather encouraging. Chris concedes that falling before the pandemic is a good thing, but stresses that even so, a large number of children are still having general anaesthetic and having dental treatment in hospital, which in itself is not good. He adds one more thing: over the past decade, dental causes have consistently been the number one reason for hospital admission, unchanged for ten years — so the "number one" position is stable, but the absolute level is heading down.

— Chris Venatza
5:10

To see the decay rate, look at school surveys, not admissions

Chris says there is a better dataset for understanding the true state of children's tooth decay: the government sends dentists into schools to examine five-year-olds. The latest survey found 22% of five-year-olds already have decay reaching the dentine layer — close to a quarter. A quarter of the population having a disease that needs treatment, he says, is not a good state of affairs. Over time: about 30.9% in 2008, down to 23% by 2017-2018, then a plateau, with the latest survey slightly down again from the 2022 one. Longer-term data comes from a survey run once a decade since 1973: 72% of five-year-olds had decay in 1973, down to 43% in 2003 — roughly halved.

— Chris Venatza
8:15

25% of UK medicines come from Israel — off by two orders of magnitude

US ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee said on the Today programme that 25% of the medicines Britain uses come from Israel. Lizzie McNeill checked the latest ONS import data: in 2025 the UK imported £10 million of medicines from Israel, against total UK medicine imports of about £28 billion — Israel accounts for just 0.04%, one twenty-fifth of 1%, not 25%. She also rules out several possibilities: active pharmaceutical ingredients come mainly from India and China, and the more upstream raw materials mainly from China; medicines supplied by Israeli companies (the largest being Teva) are mostly not made in Israel but in European plants in Hungary, Croatia and elsewhere. Teva estimates it supplies one in eight generic medicine packs in the UK, equivalent to one ninth of all prescriptions, but the other Israeli companies are all too small to push the total to 25%.

— Lizzie McNeill
13:29

Blame Gove for rising NEETs, and Wales dismantles the case

Education Secretary Lucy Powell wrote in The Times that a million young people have been left behind by the education system Michael Gove led. Daisy Christodoulou rebuts with a natural experiment: Wales and Scotland took the opposite path to Gove's reforms. Wales's NEET rate for 16-to-24-year-olds rose from 14% in 2019 to 17% in 2025, while England's rose from 11% to 13% over the same period — similar levels, similar trend, with Wales rising slightly more. If Gove is responsible for England's rise, then Wales must be responsible for Wales's rise, yet the two places had completely opposite policies. She says the NEET rate sits on top of economic structure, minimum wage, tax, regulation and far too many other factors — not just education.

— Daisy Christodoulou
17:51

The teen mental health decline is not unique to England

Against the criticism that Gove's reforms increased academic pressure and damaged mental health, Daisy turns to the WHO's Health Behaviour in School-aged Children survey, which reports England, Scotland and Wales separately. It shows average life satisfaction among teenagers fell in all three places this century (2000 to 2022), but it also fell across Western Europe and many other regions, so it is not specific to England. Then the share of 15-year-olds who "like school a lot": about 10% in England, about 9% in Scotland, about 7.5% in Wales — all low, but basically the same across the three. The share of 15-year-olds overwhelmed by academic pressure is high in all three, with no big difference.

— Daisy Christodoulou
19:59

P-Doom is a subjective probability, and nobody can produce a model

Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigned, warning that Anthropic and OpenAI are irresponsible on AI safety. Anthropic's Evan Hubinger responded on X: he personally thinks the probability that AI kills all of humanity within the next decade is greater than 10%. Alexander Mark Koch, an assistant professor of global risk and resilience at Cambridge, explains that P-Doom is shorthand for "the probability that AI causes human extinction", and is almost always one individual's subjective probability estimate. He says the issue is extraordinarily complex: it involves regulatory capacity, system resilience, where large-model capabilities are heading, autonomous weapons and biotech tools, whether AI can self-improve into an exponential loop, funding, rules, what China will do, and disaster scenarios ranging from science fiction to "a rogue state using a large model to learn to build bioweapons".

— Alexander Mark Koch
24:13

Nobody really believes it, because market prices don't reflect it

Economist Tyler Cowen has repeatedly pointed out that many people talk about human extinction, but no one can point to any market price — interest rates, share prices — reflecting that anyone truly believes it. Asked whether anyone really believes it, Koch says some people genuinely do, and you cannot say they don't; but people differ hugely on the probability and on how a catastrophe would unfold. Asked about his own P-Doom, Koch says he doesn't have one, because he doesn't know how to assign a number to it: he can conceive of many paths to destruction, and he can also think of ways to mitigate each one, but he doesn't know how to assign a probability to any of them.

— Alexander Mark Koch
