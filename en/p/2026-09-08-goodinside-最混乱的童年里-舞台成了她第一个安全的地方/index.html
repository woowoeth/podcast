In her most chaotic childhood, the stage became her first safe place

In a childhood of extreme poverty, constant moving, and rotating stepfathers, the ballet studio was the first place in Misty Copeland's life where each day was the same and no one could hurt her.

Form gave her safety; improvisation on stage gave her freedom

She explains that classical ballet requires doing plié, tendu, and dégagé in the exact same sequence every day—a consistency that gave her the stability she never experienced at home. But on stage, everything changes: the orchestra speeds up or slows down, lights malfunction, partners forget their choreography. She has to improvise instantly. This "freedom within form"—structured foundation plus spontaneous adaptation—became the core ability she now relies on as a mother.

— Misty Copeland
11:31

Working with Prince showed her that creation meant breaking ballet's rules

Before Prince, classical ballet was entirely prescriptive—hundreds of years of choreography that had to be reproduced exactly. She toured France with Prince, arrived a day early, and he told her: interpret these songs however you want. She improvised through the performance that night, returned to American Ballet Theatre with a fundamentally different understanding of what creation and artistry meant, and began bringing that improvisational capacity into her own performances.

— Misty Copeland
15:41

The ballet studio was where her body finally felt like her own

Her parents divorced when she was two; her mother remarried five times. Constant moves, constant new men in the home, uncertainty about shelter and food. At 13, she and five siblings slept on the floor in one motel room. Yet in ballet class and on stage, she felt completely relaxed for the first time—in control of her body, safe from harm. Her severe childhood migraines disappeared inside the dance studio, the opposite of how most people experience ballet.

— Misty Copeland
20:48

A twelve-year-old impulse led her to a mentor who changed her life

At 12, seemingly on impulse, she told her family she wanted to audition for her school's dance team. Everyone objected—she was shy, introverted, not someone who sought the spotlight. She made the team, became captain, and her coach connected her with Cindy Bradley, a community center teacher hunting for diverse ballet students. In her first lesson, conducted on a basketball court, Cindy told her: "I think you're a genius. You can make this a career." Copeland moved in with Cindy and stayed for three years.

— Misty Copeland
25:57

The ballet world defined the ideal dancer in a way that excluded her

Cindy once read Copeland the definition of the "ideal ballerina" from Balanchine, the New York City Ballet founder: long legs, long feet, long arms, small head, long neck, skin the color of a peeled apple. Cindy read it aloud and deliberately skipped the last criterion. After Copeland joined American Ballet Theatre, she was the only Black female dancer in a company of over 90 for an entire decade. When performing white ballets like Swan Lake, she was asked to lighten her skin to match the other dancers.

— Misty Copeland
32:17

She danced the role every night until they erased her from the broadcast

American Ballet Theatre filmed Swan Lake for television. Copeland was first cast for Act II, performing the role nightly. On the day of filming, she alone was replaced, cut from the final broadcast. She later learned that the company's ballet mistresses had privately debated whether she should appear in that act at all. Only after being promoted to principal dancer did she feel emboldened to confront leadership about these unspoken barriers no one wanted to acknowledge.

— Misty Copeland
33:21

Confidence isn't loud self-promotion; it's knowing exactly what you need to dance

She once tried proving herself loudly when colleagues doubted she could make principal or soloist ranks, but that didn't feel like real confidence. True confidence arrived after her promotion—when she stopped needing the title to validate her worth and instead focused on dancing the roles she actually wanted. The satisfaction came from the work itself, not from external labels.

— Misty Copeland
38:32

You raise the confident child, then face them across every boundary

When asked what's hardest about being a mother, Copeland describes raising the "monster you've created yourself." You instill confidence in your children; they internalize it, become confident, full of self-expression and demands. Then you have to pump the brakes and teach them timing and patience—"Wait, not now, be reasonable." This echoes back to her earlier concept of "freedom within form": she wants her son Jackson to have the stability and consistent love she lacked, not her childhood chaos. But she also wants him to struggle a little, to build resilience.

— Misty Copeland

Glossary

principal dancer
The highest rank of dancer in a ballet company, typically performing the leading roles in major productions.
corps de ballet
The ensemble of ballet dancers who perform coordinated, identical choreography as a group rather than as soloists.
soloist
A ballet dancer ranked between corps de ballet and principal dancer, performing featured roles with greater prominence than ensemble members.
