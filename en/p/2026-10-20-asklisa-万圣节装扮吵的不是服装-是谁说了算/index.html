The Halloween Costume Fight Isn't About Clothes—It's About Who Gets to Decide

A seventh-grader's group Halloween costume dispute looks like a wardrobe disagreement on the surface, but is really a fight over who gets left out and who holds social power—the costume is just the container that puts this negotiation on the table.

Costume Fights Are Never Just About Costumes

One seventh-grader and four close friends do a group Halloween costume every year; this year, by September, they're already fighting. Two want to dress as one character group, one says no—not that costume. Screenshots of the group chat circulate. The daughter feels quietly kicked out of the plan even though no one's actually said it. Tori says this isn't a Halloween problem—it's a bigger negotiation over who decides things and who gets included. The costume is just what brings it to the surface.

Why Halloween Night Gets Uniquely Overloaded

They use the psychology term overdetermination to describe Halloween: the night compresses several identity shifts—is she still young enough to knock on doors for candy, or old enough to think of this as freedom to roam without parents? The costume itself splits between "sexy" and "funny." Does she want to join boys in the group? All these adolescent crossroads collide on one night and explode—way more complex than adults realize.

— Dr. Tori Cordiano
9:27

Helping Isn't the Same as Fixing It for Them

Parents' first instinct is often to just opt out—"let's skip group costumes, do your own thing"—but to the kid that reads as "switch schools"–level rejection. It denies what she actually wants: to stay in the group. Tori's frame is "helping without fixing": start with empathy ("this sucks"), not a solution, because there's no answer that satisfies everyone. The goal is support, not to make the conflict disappear.

— Dr. Tori Cordiano
15:45

There Are Two Kinds of Popular—and They Mean Opposite Things

Tori splits "popular" into two: kids who are actually liked, and kids who buy social power by being mean—everyone knows it but no one dares push back. Helping your kid name which kind of "power" is actually running this friend group is the key to understanding what the costume fight is really about. Lisa emphasizes this clinical insight again at the end.

— Dr. Lisa Damour
20:59

Group Chat Groupthink Is Worse Than Individual Judgment

Psychology shows group thinking usually spirals worse than any one person would decide alone—it drifts toward either meaner or dumber. In group chats, no one dares step out or object, so everyone ends up passively part of cruelty they don't actually endorse but can't say out loud.

23:00

A Teen Brain's Brakes Aren't Installed Yet

The part of the brain that asks "should I send this, will it get screenshotted and spread?" isn't done developing during adolescence, so group chat messages go out of control at a word. Real guidance isn't writing the text for them—it's repeatedly asking "if you said that, what do you think would happen?"—letting them rehearse consequences in their head. Even if she doesn't answer, that rehearsal is happening.

— Dr. Tori Cordiano
28:15

When the Group Chat Spirals, a Parent Can Take the Fall

If the chat crosses a line, Tori says set the boundary and let the kid tell her friends "my mom made me leave the chat"—let her blame the parent and use that as a way out of a confrontation she's not ready for. That's not teaching her to lie; it's giving a kid who isn't ready to face the group a face-saving exit. The day she can say "stop, this is wrong" will come naturally—don't force it now.

— Dr. Tori Cordiano
33:26

High School Homecoming and Seventh-Grade Costumes Come from the Same Root

Zoom out to sophomore and junior year homecoming fights: the same social power is still there, just split among more people as friend groups loosen. The parent role shifts from front-line involved to informed but hands-off. This says the costume conflict itself will pass, but the social structure that shaped it stays, just in different forms.

— Dr. Tori Cordiano

Glossary

Overdetermination
A single event shaped by multiple independent causes at once; the Halloween costume conflict layers together power, identity, and exclusion.
Group thinking
In a group, individual judgment yields to collective mood, usually with results more extreme than any single person alone would choose.
Helping without fixing
A parenting stance where adults provide support and guidance but don't solve the friend problem directly for the child.
