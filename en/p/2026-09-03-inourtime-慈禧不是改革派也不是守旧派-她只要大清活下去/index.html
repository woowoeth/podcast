Cixi was neither a reformer nor a reactionary — she just wanted the Qing to survive

She backed the Self-Strengthening Movement, founded the Imperial University, abolished the civil service exams — provided those reforms did not threaten the dynasty or her own power. The moment reform edged toward revolution, she turned on it.

8:12

She climbed up from the sixth rank of concubine

Cixi was Manchu, and her father was a mid-to-lower-ranking official in the Jiangnan region. Because she was Manchu, she could be sent into the palace to take part in the selection of consorts; Han women had no such eligibility. The imperial harem was divided into eight ranks, and she entered at the sixth — a very low starting point. But she bore the Xianfeng Emperor his only son, and her status rose immediately. Yang Wen says her core trait was that she was ‘too good at politics’ — interested in power, interested in personal relationships, and skilled at manipulating people. She was not the empress; the empress had to come from a genuinely aristocratic Manchu family, so her only route upward was to attract the emperor's attention.

— Yang Wen
13:25

She joined with Prince Gong to force the eight regents to death

The day before the Xianfeng Emperor died, he appointed eight regents, charging them with drafting edicts and making decisions collectively, making them the centre of power after the emperor's death. But Cixi was also given the power to seal edicts with the imperial seal, and she was deeply unhappy with this arrangement. So she joined forces with another empress dowager who also held sealing power, Ci'an, and with the key figure Prince Gong, to force the eight regents to commit suicide, ultimately eliminating them. This was the Xinyou Coup of 1861, when Cixi was not yet thirty. Rana Mitter points out that when the coup took place, the Taiping Rebellion would not be put down for another three years, and that through this coup Cixi pushed herself to the very front of power.

— Ronald Po
15:32

Two child emperors gave her room to operate

In the history of Chinese dynasties, a woman actually taking the throne was taboo; the only exception was Wu Zetian of the Tang. So a woman who wanted power had to find a way to operate behind the emperor. Rana Mitter points out that one accidental reason Cixi managed this so successfully at the end of the 19th century was the consecutive appearance of two child emperors — one five years old, the other about eight — neither yet of an age to rule in person. This gave her far more opportunity than she would have had facing a twenty-one-year-old or a forty-one-year-old emperor. Yang Wen adds that being a woman was actually an advantage: when she approached officials and worked for her own interests or the dynasty's, she looked ‘harmless’, and officials did not think a woman could pose any threat.

— Rana Mitter
19:36

She drove her own son to death at nineteen

Cixi was a control freak who tried to control the young Tongzhi Emperor, and the mother-son relationship was extremely turbulent. Tongzhi married at seventeen or eighteen and acquired an empress; by convention Cixi should then have handed over power, stopped ruling from behind the curtain, and let the emperor rule in person. But Tongzhi never learned how to be emperor, because his mother did everything for him. Yang Wen says there were rumours that Cixi wanted to arrange for a woman she favoured to be empress, but Tongzhi chose someone else, and Cixi kept watching whom he slept with. Tongzhi simply chose no one, went out, and contracted syphilis. The official diagnosis was smallpox, but Yang Wen says the real diagnosis was syphilis, and the two look alike in symptoms. To save the dynasty's face, Cixi is said to have ordered the imperial physicians to treat him for smallpox rather than syphilis, and so Tongzhi died.

— Yang Wen
26:49

The defeat in 1895 cannot be blamed on her naval funds alone

Cixi has long been criticised over the First Sino-Japanese War, accused of diverting funds that should have supported the navy to build a new palace. Ronald Po argues this criticism needs a closer look: in the early years of the Self-Strengthening Movement, Cixi strongly supported building a modern navy and other modernisation projects, and only in the few years before the war did she want to retire behind the scenes and build a palace for her old age. So he judges that Cixi bears partial responsibility for losing the war, but that the Qing court also had structural problems — corruption, cronyism, inefficiency — that had existed for a long time, and responsibility cannot simply be pinned on one woman or one leader.

— Ronald Po
30:54

Power had long been flowing from the court to the provinces

Rana Mitter stresses that in the last two or three decades of the 19th century, a great deal of power drained away from the Qing court. Because of the Taiping Rebellion, the court was forced to allow provincial governors like Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang to raise their own provincial armies — and a Chinese province could be the size of a European country. Military power shifted from centralisation to the localities, and the process that in the 20th century would be called warlordism began there. In the north there was a region called Beiyang, whose successive leaders would become an independent force, sometimes cooperating with the court in Beijing and sometimes openly opposing it. Rana says that from this angle, the fact that the court could still retain as much central power as it did while Cixi was in charge is in some sense a credit to her.

— Rana Mitter
36:00

Once reform looked like revolution, she turned on it

During the Hundred Days Reform of 1898, Cixi initially supported or at least tolerated it. Rana Mitter says the specific reforms rolled out that summer and early autumn looked quite progressive, and most crucially, in 1898 the Imperial University was founded — the predecessor of today's Peking University, China's first modern university, teaching languages, science and other modern subjects rather than two thousand years of Confucian classics. Figures like Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao were brought in as a kind of think tank, proposing a constitutional monarchy and reshaping Confucian thought to make it more egalitarian. But when she felt the direction of reform was approaching revolution and making her uncomfortable, she pulled back at once and ordered the reformers arrested or exiled. Yang Wen argues she was neither a reformer nor a reactionary; she had only one goal: for the Qing to survive, and for her own power to continue.

— Rana Mitter
41:26

In her last six years she did what she had refused four years earlier

After the Boxer crisis, the Western powers demanded an indemnity of 333 million US dollars, a devastating blow to Qing finances and one of the factors that helped bring the dynasty down. But in the last six years after 1902, until her death in 1908, Cixi instead supported many of the reforms she had rejected in 1898: a constitutional monarchy, locally elected assemblies, the abolition in 1905 of the civil service examination system that had lasted a thousand years, and the creation of modern educational institutions. Rana Mitter says these were ultimately implemented, and that one can say she did support modernisation in the final stage of her life. The problem was that it was too late — only three years after her death, the revolution in the south in 1911 brought the whole system down.

— Rana Mitter

Glossary

self-strengthening movement
The reform movement from 1861 in which some Qing officials promoted the study of Western mathematics, science and languages to strengthen China.
Hundred Days Reform
The radical reform pushed by Kang Youwei, Liang Qichao and others in the summer and autumn of 1898, later halted by Cixi.
Boxer Rebellion
The 1900 anti-foreign movement in north China in which peasants used martial arts and magic to fight Westerners and Chinese Christians.
Datong
The ideal of ‘Great Harmony’ proposed by Kang Youwei, an attempt to combine traditional Chinese thought with modernisation.
century of humiliations
The narrative advanced by the government of the People's Republic of China referring to the series of humiliating defeats China suffered at the hands of the great powers in the 19th century.
