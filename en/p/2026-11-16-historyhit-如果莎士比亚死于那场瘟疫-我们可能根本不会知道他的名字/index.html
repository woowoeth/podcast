If Shakespeare Died in That Plague, We Might Not Know His Name

When Shakespeare was 30, theaters shut for 18 months due to plague, his patron went bankrupt, and his contemporaries Marlowe and Greene were already dead—he is remembered largely because he survived, not because he showed early genius.

This episode reframes Shakespeare from ‘natural literary genius’ to a young playwright struggling through plague, debt, early marriage, and censorship. The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:24

The year he was born, plague had already killed a fifth of his neighbors.

Shakespeare's mother had already lost two children to childhood illnesses before becoming pregnant with him. During her pregnancy, English soldiers returning from France brought a major plague outbreak. He was born in April 1564, and the plague reached Stratford by July. The parish record notes: ‘the plague begins here’. That wave killed up to a fifth of the local population. Shakespeare's survival through infancy was itself a matter of luck.

— Rory Loughnane
16:35

Marriage at 18 and having three children before 25 closed off university and apprenticeship.

His father's business deteriorated around the time he would have graduated; university was unlikely anyway (only one peer attended Oxford or Cambridge). But marriage sealed his fate: he wed Anne Hathaway at 18 when she was already 26 and pregnant. Two years later they had twins. By his early 20s, he had three children to support. This was a completely different starting point from Marlowe, who was studying at Cambridge at the same time.

— Rory Loughnane
24:55

No one thought him a genius in 1589—he was just a capable craftsman.

His early plays—Two Gentlemen of Verona, Arden of Faversham, Titus Andronicus—were not the works people would later emphasize when discussing Shakespeare. There is no evidence anyone in 1588–89 thought ‘this is the most outstanding writer of our age’. He resembled a journeyman experimenting across genres—comedy, history, tragedy—rather than a recognized talent.

— Rory Loughnane
25:58

Playwrights turned to English history because Elizabeth had no heir and future was uncertain.

Traditionally, English drama consisted of tragedies and comedies; history plays were rare. The turning point came when Elizabeth aged without an heir, leaving her successor uncertain, and society grew anxious about what would follow. Playwrights responded by consulting Holinshed's Chronicles, searching England's past kings for lessons about ‘English-style leadership’. Marlowe and Shakespeare were among the first to write history plays.

— Rory Loughnane
30:14

The 'upstart crow' attack may have accused him of stealing other writers' scripts.

In 1592, A Groatsworth of Wit attacked Shakespeare as a ‘shake-scene’ and ‘upstart crow’. Scholars believe Thomas Nashe wrote this, impersonating the dying Robert Greene. The insult likely targeted Titus Andronicus—George Peele wrote the first 600 lines, and Shakespeare is believed to have completed the rest—implying he ‘took what belonged to others and benefited from it’. Class prejudice about his social origins mixed into the attack as well.

— Rory Loughnane
37:40

Theater closures lasting 18 months forced him to seek patronage through poetry.

Plague forced London theaters to shut for 18 months, eliminating Shakespeare's income from both acting and playwriting. He sought patronage from the young nobleman Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton, and wrote Venus and Adonis. He unusually arranged for its publication and signed his own name—the first time ‘William Shakespeare’ appeared in print as an author. Yet Southampton himself was nearly bankrupt at the time.

— Rory Loughnane
42:46

Had he died in that plague, we would remember his name and little else.

Marlowe died at 29 from murder, leaving a literary legacy substantial enough to be fully traced. But if Shakespeare had died in a plague of the same era, the situation would be entirely different. Most of his early plays were published anonymously. Early works published in 1594 appeared without his name. He didn't sign his name as a playwright in print until 1597. Without a dramatic death to inspire later investigation, he would likely be remembered as having ‘written something outstanding’, yet almost no one would know who he was.

— Rory Loughnane
