One Report Closed Half of America's Medical Schools, and Black Doctors Disappeared for Sixty Years

The Flexner Report cut U.S. medical schools from 155 to fewer than half, and the price of standardization was the near-total elimination of Black medical schools — the next one would not open until 1966.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:09

The report's author was not a doctor but an education reformer

Abraham Flexner never attended medical school. He first wrote a book criticizing American higher education, The American College, A Criticism, which led Carnegie Foundation president Henry Smith Pritchett to ask him to turn the same critical eye on medical schools. He spent two years traveling to 155 medical schools, recording for each one its admission requirements, faculty, funding, laboratories and hospital affiliations. Precisely because he was an outsider to medicine, his assessments carried no professional preconceptions and no mercy — he would name dirty laboratories and lax standards outright, and criticize students being packed into all-day stretches of dull lectures.

At the time, over seventy percent of medical schools required only a high school diploma

When the report was published, 74% of medical schools required only a high school education for admission, and some even accepted a high school equivalency certificate; only one in five required more than two years of college before entry. Many medical schools were essentially for-profit businesses. Meanwhile bacteriology, diagnostic techniques and surgery were all advancing rapidly, but the faculty were mostly part-time practicing physicians teaching the old material they had learned years earlier, without updating it. Flexner argued this directly threatened public health: the doctors being produced gave patients outdated care at best and endangered their lives at worst.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson
18:31

He recommended closing 120 of the 155 schools

Flexner's model of an acceptable school included: college-level biology, chemistry and physics before admission; coursework with both lectures and laboratories in anatomy, pathology, bacteriology and pharmacology; hands-on practice in a teaching hospital; and a faculty that was salaried and full-time at teaching and research rather than part-time practicing physicians. He argued that state licensing boards should refuse licenses to graduates of substandard schools. In the end he recommended closing 120 of the 155. Within a decade, the number of American medical schools was half what it had been when the report was published, and the surviving schools generally reformed their standards and curricula. Only one Canadian school appeared on the closure list, and it ultimately did not close.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson
23:43

Black medical schools were almost all shut down, leaving only two

The report said: "of the seven medical schools for Negroes in the United States, five are at this moment in no position to make any contribution of value." He praised Howard and Meharry as worth developing and judged the rest essentially useless. But that assessment did not account for the fact that Black medical schools charged far lower tuition than white schools, had smaller endowments, and were already running on razor-thin margins. Every Black medical school he named closed: the first four by 1914, the fifth in 1915, and the last held on until 1923. Howard and Meharry survived. The next medical school primarily serving Black students, Charles R. Drew Medical School, did not open in Los Angeles until 1966, and the one after that was Morehouse School of Medicine in Atlanta in 1975.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson
25:48

Fewer medical schools meant a widening care gap in Black communities

Once Black medical schools were squeezed out, aspiring Black physicians were excluded from medical education by geography and lack of opportunity, and care for Black patients often fell to white doctors who, even at best, carried entrenched racial prejudice. In 2005 the AMA's Institute for Ethics convened an expert panel to examine medicine's history of racism, and one of its findings was that the Carnegie Foundation hired Flexner for the project and the AMA was also a sponsor, something not disclosed at the time. Flexner's line about wanting good schools rather than many schools ensured that medicine remained racially segregated, and that the communities most in need of doctors had none.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson
28:53

Did he know he was constraining Black medicine?

Flexner knew he was limiting the scale of Black medicine; he wrote in the report that "the upbuilding of Howard and Meharry will profit the nation much more than the inadequate maintenance of a larger number of schools." In 1933 he wrote to his own institute's board opposing discrimination, saying that in appointments and admissions "no account shall be taken of directly or indirectly, of race, religion, or sex." But medical historians have no consensus on his motives: some see him as a benevolent paternalist, others as a racist to the core, the latter mainly on the basis that he described hygiene as the most important duty of Black doctors rather than life-saving skills. Some scholars also point out that the leaders of Meharry and Howard had used similar language two years before the report.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson
30:53

His sense of equality came from a Louisville childhood

Biographer Thomas Neville Bonner argued that Flexner's progressive views came from growing up in Louisville, Kentucky, where services and opportunities for the Black community were more plentiful than in many places: a dozen or so Black schools, plus vocational training schools for Black teachers, medicine and law. The Flexner family was not wealthy and lived in a neighborhood with Black neighbors, and Abraham later said that he and his siblings often played with the Black children in the community. This may have left him overly idealistic about the reality of Black family life — to him, the children he played with would naturally grow up to do the same work he did, and he did not realize that others did not see it that way. He admitted in his lifetime that only later, when he studied educational institutions, did he understand how disadvantaged the Black students he had known as a child really were.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson
36:01

The AMA did not apologize publicly until 2008

Following the ethics committee's findings, the AMA issued a public apology in 2008 for "its past history of racial inequality toward African-American physicians" and announced a series of programs to encourage minorities to enter medicine. The hosts also note that there are signs that before the Flexner Report was published, many medical schools — Black and white alike — were already in financial trouble, partly because they had been struggling to meet standards the AMA had issued, and the financial burden of meeting those standards was itself unsustainable.

— Holly Frey / Tracy V. Wilson

Glossary

Flexner Report
The 1910 publication "Medical Education in the United States and Canada," which reshaped American medical education standards.
AAMC
Association of American Medical Colleges, founded in 1876, which had set its own uniform standards for medical curricula.
AMA
American Medical Association, founded in 1847, which long refused to admit Black physicians and apologized in 2008 for its history of racial inequality.
hygiene
The subject the Flexner Report recommended Black doctors focus on teaching, criticized as confining Black physicians to public health roles rather than life-saving skills.
