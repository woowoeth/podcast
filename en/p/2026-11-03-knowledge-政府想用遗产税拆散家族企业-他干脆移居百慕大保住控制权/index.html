The government tried to break up his family business with estate tax, so he moved to Bermuda to keep control

K.C. Irving lived by one rule for sixty years: everything that matters must stay under your control. When new tax laws first threatened that 100% ownership, he chose to leave New Brunswick with his entire fortune.

12:30

He buried a gas tank without his father's permission

At nineteen, Irving saw automobiles appearing on country roads where Eaton mail-order catalogs could deliver clothing and tools but not gasoline. He registered his family's general store as an Imperial Oil agent without asking his father. At dawn one summer morning in 1920, before the village woke, he dug a hole and buried a 250-gallon tank. When his father demanded to know what he was doing, Irving answered shovel in hand: "We will absolutely not simply shut our doors." This was his first display of a lifetime pattern: finding positions no one else occupied and claiming them.

16:40

When his supplier cut him off, he found new oil and blended his own

As business boomed, Imperial Oil cut off Irving's supply to force him to sell only cars. He didn't negotiate—he found a new supplier in Tulsa, Oklahoma, had gasoline shipped by rail in tank cars, and ordered a storage tank 40 times the size of his original 250-gallon one. By coincidence, Imperial also ordered the same model tank for its own station, and both arrived in the same small town on the same day. He branded his antifreeze blend Primrose, splicing together the names Premier and White Rose to declare war, and soon captured 95% of the town's gasoline business.

32:29

The railroad choked him off, so he bought a ship

In 1932, a government-owned railroad imposed higher freight rates, arguing that "Imperial Oil has its own refinery and you don't." After two years of negotiation and a final ultimatum that was refused, the railroad counted on him to accept—or at most to buy an oil tanker, which no one would do in the Great Depression. Irving bought the tanker anyway and built his own dock a mile from the railroad's headquarters, then invited the entire town to watch it dock. It ran aground in mud. Irving grabbed a shovel and worked alongside the crew until 2:30 a.m., when they finally freed it.

48:07

Before buying forest land, he put on snowshoes and counted trees himself

Irving's office desk connected to a private radio network that reached his factories, ships, and gas stations directly. Before buying 700,000 acres of forest, he didn't simply sign after hearing the expert report. He put on snowshoes and walked into the woods with a forestry expert to count trees by hand. Back at camp each night, they compared tallies. If the numbers didn't match, they counted again the next day until both sides aligned. Only then did he write the check. He also made a habit of appearing unannounced at his plants in the middle of the night—the best way to understand a business, he said, was to know its beginning and end and every link in between, so you could make sound decisions.

59:34

He took 96% of the deal, then asked about the 4% left behind

Harrison McCain spent eighteen months landing a major client and called Irving in excitement with the news, expecting praise. Irving asked only: "Did you get the lubricant business?" McCain hadn't—it accounted for just 4% of the total deal. Irving cared not about the 96% they'd won, but the 4% they'd lost. McCain later distilled what he'd learned from Irving into a single sentence: Never be satisfied. McCain also said that Irving's name alone was enough to make counterparts concede before negotiations even began.

— Harrison McCain
1:08:58

His refusal of BP's control clause was vindicated by the Suez Crisis

British Petroleum agreed to build a refinery with Irving in Saint John, and after settling the terms, added a condition: BP would have the right to buy out Irving Oil after Irving retired or died. Irving had three sons to pass the business to—he refused. Months later, Standard Oil got blocked by the Suez Crisis; Arab oil couldn't flow, so tankers had to sail around Africa, requiring deep-water ports. Saint John never froze and had the depth needed. This geopolitical event, seemingly unrelated, finally closed the refinery deal Irving had been waiting twenty-three years to make. He called it the best deal he'd ever negotiated.

1:41:52

A Senate question revealed his power exceeded the province itself

A federal senator accused the Irving family of monopolizing all English-language newspapers in New Brunswick and summoned Irving to testify in Ottawa. The senator asked: "If something harms New Brunswick, does it harm your business?" Irving answered: "Yes." The senator pressed: "Then if something harms your business, does it harm New Brunswick?" Irving again: "Yes." Those two affirmations trapped him in the very trap he'd spent forty years building. He'd spent a lifetime freeing himself from dependence on anyone, yet now the entire province had become dependent on him.

— Casey Irving
1:46:04

When the new inheritance tax was announced, he moved to Bermuda

Starting in 1972, Canada would tax capital gains; New Brunswick wanted to add an estate tax on top. Nearly all of Irving's wealth was locked in private company shares—refineries, paper mills, ships—with no public market where his sons could sell shares to pay a bill at that scale. To spare his sons from having to dismantle everything he'd built, on December 23, 1971, he and his wife quietly flew to Bermuda and thereafter strictly limited the days they spent in Canada. His only public statement: "I am no longer a resident of New Brunswick. My sons are managing the operations."

— Casey Irving

Glossary

vertical integration
A strategy of owning and controlling all steps from raw materials through production to retail, eliminating dependence on external suppliers or buyers
Combines Investigation Act
Canada's federal law at the time for investigating and prosecuting monopolistic business behavior
succession duties
A provincial tax on assets transferred to heirs when an owner dies
equal opportunity
A provincial reform initiative aimed at equalizing public services and tax structures across regions
