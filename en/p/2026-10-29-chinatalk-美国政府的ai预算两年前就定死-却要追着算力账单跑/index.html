U.S. government locked AI budgets two years ahead, yet racing compute costs that arrive monthly

Congress budgets on two-year cycles, but LLM subscriptions and compute bills change monthly. The real solution isn't cutting costs, but making budgets fungible across models and use cases.

Software engineering costs have actually collapsed, not just gotten cheaper

Koven describes a State Department project extracting insights from roughly 3,000 daily diplomatic cables using NLP—originally requiring a six-person team over six months. Today, he says, two people could do the same work—or better—in weeks. This isn't hyperbole: he has run the same work with both old and new tools and compares them directly. Software engineering costs have truly collapsed; you see it in how government now processes information.

9:33

Validate diplomatic AI by running it against expert analysts

Koven proposes a concrete evaluation: feed the same prompt to both the model and the actual Russia analyst, then compare conclusions. If they align, both got it right—or both fell into the same mirror imaging (unconsciously assuming the adversary reasons like you do). If they diverge, the divergence itself is the real discovery: it exposes where the model breaks down, or where the human missed something.

— Barnett Koven
14:44

Entity resolution can expose shell companies trying to evade export controls

Export control enforcement isn't about whether to regulate, but how to spot the shell companies actually evading controls among many legitimate subsidiary structures. Koven describes AFS's approach: entity resolution merges Bloomberg, S&P, and government data into one master corporate relationship graph. From that graph, you can identify a previously unknown shell company, trace it through seven layers of intermediaries, and land on a People's Liberation Army procurement office.

— Barnett Koven
22:02

Generative AI wastes money when classical methods would work better

Meta fired people for not burning enough tokens; SemiAnalysis reports an analyst burning $50k/quarter. Koven says enterprises should do the opposite: let proven classical machine learning handle 80–90% (it's certain, it works, it's cheap), and reserve generative and agentic AI only for where classical methods fail. This keeps total cost down, locks determinism where you need it, and—he calls it the secret—gets better results.

— Barnett Koven
24:09

Budgets that lock two years early can't survive monthly compute bills

U.S. federal budgets, in practice, lock in roughly two years before the money spends—but LLM subscriptions, token consumption, and cloud bills move monthly. The only realistic escape, Koven says, is building fungibility inside the budget: tokens from model A swap with tokens from model B; one use case borrows from another. Roll up budget granularity from individual offices to whole departments. Add FinOps to stay accountable. Stop locking down line items in advance.

— Barnett Koven
27:15

Classified AI networks favor attackers because defense costs rise exponentially

Once you're in IL6 or higher, the latest model doesn't drop the same day. Real concern isn't compute—it's whether data leaks or whether the adversary has already poisoned the model. Koven is blunt: if he were on the other side, he could predict what U.S. analysts want the model to do, then plant a trap in advance. He frames this as an asymmetry: attack cost rises linearly over time, defense cost rises exponentially. In classified space, the attacker wins by default.

— Barnett Koven
32:28

Publication bias caused the U.S. government to waste billions on failed strategies

Koven's work in counterinsurgency research: early studies said development aid reduces violence. When he reanalyzed using spatial diffusion models from cancer research, he found the opposite—aid mostly displaced or amplified violence to nearby villages. But he and peers had tried dozens of variants that missed statistical significance and never got published. Other teams duplicated the failed work. The U.S. government kept funding aid blindly. The cost wasn't just dollars; it was military casualties.

— Barnett Koven
37:43

Abandoned research data holds solutions to today's critical supply chain crises

DOE's Genesis mission tackles a blind spot: research failure data is never kept or shared. Example: beryllium can be extracted from many ores; a century ago, certain sources were abandoned because costs were high. Now, with rivals dominating supply chains, those old experiments might be critical. Tailings once dumped as waste could merit re-extraction. RBAC/ABAC is the technical unlock: role- and attribute-based controls let you open data row by row, column by column, between national labs, universities, and partners without losing control.

— Barnett Koven

Glossary

Mirror imaging
Unconsciously projecting your own decision-making logic onto an adversary, leading to misreading their true intentions.
Entity resolution
Linking and merging records across disparate databases that refer to the same real-world entity.
RBAC/ABAC
Role- or attribute-based access control: automatically deciding who sees which data, down to row and column level.
IL6
U.S. government intelligence information security level 6, a classification tier above standard government cloud environments.
FinOps
Cloud financial operations: tracking and accountability for compute and token spending.
Genesis mission
Department of Energy initiative partnering 26 technology companies to advance federal AI capabilities.
