Nvidia can't build the compute futures market—because doing so means abandoning inference revenue

Every player holding computing supply has their own business to protect; only those who completely exit inference and cloud services to become purely neutral brokers can establish pricing everyone trusts.

This episode traces compute trading from futures basics to advanced topics only derivatives professionals ask: how futures curves are shaped, how to measure liquidity, which players can be neutral brokers, and which cannot.

2:10

Compute brokers must become cloud operators to meet buyer expectations

SF Compute began by trying to resell spare cluster capacity. Buyers kept asking for clean infrastructure, customer support, security—services only a full cloud provider could deliver. This taught them what they now call ‘physical settlement’: the buyer doesn't just need a matched price; they need actual runnable compute resources delivered. That means the supply side must look and operate like a cloud provider, not a middleman.

— Evan Conrad
5:45

The brokerage market remains too thin to hedge effectively

SF Compute frankly admits its compute trading market is ‘still immature, shallow, and illiquid’. Only 5% of the company's total capacity appears on order books at any given time, and even that liquidity is highly concentrated. Their core measurement is direct: customers spend $100 to buy compute, then resell it back to the market and recover an average of $25. That number—not market-maker count or other vanity metrics—tells them whether liquidity actually helps customers.

— Evan Conrad
9:18

Abandon unified standards; use multi-dimensional order books instead

Compute capacity lacks agreed grades unlike oil. GPU virtual machines from different clouds vary by up to 40% in performance. Rather than wait for industry standards, SF Compute designed order books with multiple constraint dimensions: buyers specify time windows, storage configurations, and other attributes; the system matches them to sell orders. Liquidity becomes fragmented and spreads widen. But anyone who can arbitrage across compute types can capture the spread between them.

— Evan Conrad
11:41

Early H100 and GB200 batches carried firmware-level defects later production did not

Initial H100 and GB200 units shipped with firmware bugs—sometimes in Nvidia's firmware, sometimes in motherboard vendors' implementations—that required patches or replacement. This means a single chip generation has quality tiers: sophisticated buyers pay more for ‘not the first batch off the line’ the way oil markets price different refining grades. That is another reason compute markets naturally resist reduction to a single standard unit.

— Evan Conrad
20:04

$40-per-hour inference revenue versus $5–6-per-hour acquisition cost forces compression

A B300 running inference at full capacity generates roughly $40/hour in revenue; even at 50% utilization that is $25/hour. Yet the hourly cost to acquire that B300, amortized, is only $5–6/hour. Evan Conrad stated plainly: ‘One of these two numbers has to move’. That gap is the fundamental arbitrage driving capital into compute futures markets—whoever locks in supply earliest captures the difference.

— Evan Conrad
23:44

Cash settlement works only when built on real physical delivery underneath

Conrad argues that financing too much of the derivatives market before physical settlement infrastructure matures creates an easily-manipulated market with severe basis risk—the price you hedge against may completely decouple from the price you can actually transact. He pointed to credit default swaps: at peak, the derivatives market was roughly 12 times the size of the underlying bonds. Cash settlement itself is sound, but only when derived from real, deliverable physical markets, not when it replaces them.

— Evan Conrad
33:20

Neutrality requires abandoning your other lines of business

Nvidia could create a neutral compute exchange; it chooses not to because buyers would distrust its pricing—it has inference revenue to protect. The same logic applies to Crusoe, CoreWeave, Lambda, and the hyperscale clouds: they earn too much from inference and cloud services. The moment they become neutral brokers, they voluntarily abandon that revenue. Conrad's conclusion: this would lower financing costs for the entire industry, but no player is willing to ‘kill its own business’ to make it happen.

— Evan Conrad
34:30

Programmable networks let compute brokers resize clusters in real time

SF Compute's latest breakthrough: they virtualized the physical InfiniBand network topology. Previously, if a customer wanted to repartition InfiniBand segments, they contacted the provider and waited for manual configuration. Now customers dynamically split and recombine segments on the fly, scaling cluster footprint with order book demand in real time. This is what Conrad calls ‘making the data center programmable’—a paradigm shift comparable to cloud computing's emergence.

— Evan Conrad

Glossary

Physical settlement
The buyer receives actual deliverable compute resources; infrastructure must be real and operationally capable.
Cash settlement
Only the contract price difference is paid; no actual compute resources change hands.
Basis risk
The risk that the hedging price and the actual transactable price diverge sharply enough to erase the hedge's value.
Order book
A system where buyers and sellers post bids and asks; a matching engine pairs them into transactions.
Backplane
The switching fabric, data plane units, network cards, and other interconnect infrastructure connecting GPU nodes.
InfiniBand fabric
The high-speed, low-latency network connecting GPU cluster nodes; more specialized than Ethernet, standard in supercomputers and large AI clusters.
