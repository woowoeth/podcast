Returning the Benin Bronzes: Not Whether to Return Them, but to Whom

Restitution of looted artefacts has become the era's correctness, but with the Benin Bronzes the question of "to whom" is harder to answer than "whether": France and Germany are racing, Britain is playing dead, and three factions inside Nigeria are fighting over them.

Whether a regime is likeable does not change the duty to return

Bogans works on antiquities trafficking for the New York County District Attorney's Office in Manhattan. His position is unambiguous: illegally looted artefacts must go back to their countries of origin, even if the regime in that country is unlikeable. He answers the charge of "naivety" with a burglary case: if a man has 19 televisions in his house and 18 are stolen, you don't leave the stolen goods behind just because he doesn't need that many televisions. He says he has been in three wars and six combat deployments, and has seen mass graves in Kosovo and Iraq. He asks receiving countries to sign a letter of commitment at the restitution ceremony, guaranteeing public display and maintenance; the document has no legal force, but it is enforced through a chain of relationships: the next time you want my help on a case, don't break your word.

— Matthew Bogans
7:56

The Pergamon Altar has no one to return it to

Frum uses the Pergamon Altar to show that ancient loot has no clear restitution counterpart. The altar was excavated by German archaeologists, but its creators were Greeks, its commissioner a king of mixed Greek and Macedonian blood, and its taxpayers peoples speaking the various languages of the Asian continent, including Semites. Is it Turkey, Greece, Macedonia, Syria, or the heirs of a vanished world? Unlike the traceable looting in the Russia-Ukraine war, looting from 100 to 500 years ago cracks the very concept of "theft" open. He concludes: "That world is gone, so there is no one to return it to."

— David Frum
15:00

The Benin Bronzes are not a collection, they are the spoils of a massacre

Dan Hicks pulls the story back to the 1884 Berlin Conference — European powers sitting down to divide the map of Africa. The Royal Niger Company was Britain's commercial-military arm, controlling the palm oil and rubber trade. In 1892 the company signed a treaty with the King of Benin; a few years later the British, finding the king uncooperative, sent an expedition that was killed, and then launched a punitive expedition: 10 Royal Navy vessels, 1,400 soldiers, equipped with the newly invented Maxim gun; Benin's defenders had only machetes, pistols and muskets. The British slaughtered residents, burned the city, exiled the king, and carried off thousands of objects. Within weeks the Benin Bronzes were already on display in museums in London, Oxford and Berlin, placed in Assyrian galleries alongside ancient Egyptian artefacts, implying that this culture had been knocked back to the Bronze Age.

— Dan Hicks
26:07

Restitution is soft diplomacy, not a moral awakening

Savoy says France, Britain, Belgium and Germany today are like competitors in a "restitution Olympics", vying to be the first to return important pieces. In 2017 Macron gave a speech in Burkina Faso, saying it was unacceptable that so much of Africa's cultural heritage remained in France, and promptly appointed Savoy and Felwine Sarr to write a report, promising to return thousands of artefacts and to provide loans for building museums. But critics say the report is too academic and too idealistic; French law forbids removing artefacts from public collections. Savoy argues restitution is also soft diplomacy: France's reputation in its former colonies is dire, and Macron wants to repair relations. She also punctures the black box of European museum storage: one museum in Paris holds 69,000 African artefacts and displays only 1,000.

— Benedicte Savoy
33:42

The 1963 act is not a law of nature; Parliament can change it any time

New director Cullinan says the 1963 act of Parliament explicitly forbids the British Museum from removing anything from its collection; objects acquired for the nation belong to the British people, and the museum has no right to sell them. Frum replies that this is just an ordinary law passed by Parliament at a particular moment, and Parliament can pass a new one; treating "legally impossible" as a law of physics is laughable. Cullinan concedes he cannot spend his whole tenure fighting and that parliamentary action is needed. He also notes that early in the pandemic people feared small museums would be forced to sell their only painting, so the prohibition was seen as a safety valve. The strategy the British Museum ended up adopting: change the subject abroad with the Parthenon sculptures and Easter Island.

— Nicholas Cullinan / David Frum
37:18

"Return them to Nigeria" conceals a three-way contest

After going to Nigeria, Frum found that the phrase "return them to Nigeria" conceals an internal contest. Three claimants: Edo State's planned Museum of West African Art (a public-private partnership with an independent board); the National Commission for Museums and Monuments; and the current Oba, Ewaise II, descendant of the king exiled in 1897. He warns that when you choose a side, you have made a moral decision and must bear the consequences. The museum project was initially favoured by Western donors, but ran into the historical feud between the Edo State governor and the Oba's two families. Once the governor's term ended, the project stalled. In 2023 the Nigerian government announced that all artefacts must be handed to the Oba, and the museum plan has stalled.

— David Frum
43:05

Something 500 years old can wait another 50

Frum says this is not a good moment to decide; an object 500 years old can wait 50 or 100 years, until the grandchildren's generation gives a better answer. Faced with the rebuttal that "loot should just go back", he provokes: if the goal is merely to erase guilt, you might as well burn and melt down the bronzes, or sell them to a Russian oligarch to fund British schools. He thinks people doing good are apt to admire themselves in the mirror, and that in this guilty world no one holding power can exercise it in perfect innocence. You have inherited something with an unclean provenance, and wherever it came from, you bear the responsibility of continuing it — so you should not choose a bad answer under the pressure of "it must be dealt with as soon as possible".

— David Frum
52:24

Breaking up the plaques on the wall is like tearing Shakespeare apart

Ehikhamenor recalls seeing the Benin Bronzes for the first time in the British Museum basement in 2017. He could not understand why a burial mask was in a glass case — it was meant to go into the ground with the dead. Benin had no written language; the bronze plaques record the kingdom's history, like the pages of a picture book. Before the looting there were more than 1,000 plaques; the British Museum holds nearly 200, taken apart one by one and hung on the wall. His analogy: it is as if you tore Act I out of Macbeth, tore the last act out of Hamlet, pasted them together, and called it Shakespeare. The melody is broken, the story is gone, and history, once taken, becomes decoration.

— Victor Ehikhamenor

Glossary

Benin Bronzes
Thousands of brass, bronze, ivory and other objects looted from the Kingdom of Benin by the British expedition of 1897, now scattered across more than 160 museums worldwide.
Deaccession
The institutional act by which a museum formally removes an object from its collection and disposes of it (by sale, transfer or restitution).
Oba
The title of the king of the Kingdom of Benin; the current heir of the line claims ownership of the Benin Bronzes.
Punitive expedition
A military action launched by a colonial power to punish a local force that attacked it or refused to submit, often accompanied by looting and destruction.
