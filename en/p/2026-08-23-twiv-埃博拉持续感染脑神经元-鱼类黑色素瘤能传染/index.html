Ebola Infects Neurons and Sheds Virus for 120 Days; Fish Cancers Spread Between Fish

Brain organoids show Ebola can infect neurons and release infectious virus for up to 120 days; melanomas in brown bullhead catfish populations descend from a single clonal ancestor and transmit between fish. The boundaries of viral persistence and contagious cancer are rewritten at the same time.

Three-tier scoring gives political appointees more room to pick projects

The NIH proposes replacing the numeric scoring of grant review with a three-tier ‘good, medium, poor’ system, on the grounds that current numeric scores are severely compressed, with large numbers of grants clustered together and very low discrimination; the three-tier system is already used at NSF. Rich does not oppose tiering itself, but says the real problem is that the three of them now do not trust NIH leadership: vague tiers would make it easier for political appointees to pick projects on ideological grounds. He also recalls that NSF still gave him partial funding back when his first grant was rejected.

— Rich Condit
22:34

Detecting RNA is not persistent infection; you have to see virus released

A team at the Bernhard Nocht Institute in Hamburg used human brain organoids to study Ebola's long-term persistence, and distinguished two kinds of persistence: productive persistence is continuous release of infectious particles; nonproductive persistence is when only RNA can be detected. Microglia remained persistently infected for 91 days across 13 passages at MOI 1 or 10; more than 90% of the whole organoid was infected, and it continued producing virus for 120 days. Once the definition is clear, ‘persistent infection’ can no longer be waved through with a single RT-PCR positive — you have to demonstrate release of intact virus.

— Alan Dove
37:55

Ebola can directly infect neurons, which had never been reported before

The team watched infection spread through the organoid: the virus advanced from the periphery toward the center, infecting neurons, astrocytes and microglia. Neuronal infection had never been reported before, rewriting the prior belief that Ebola is not neurotropic. Microglia clustered around infected cells, suggesting innate immunity was attempting a response; Lassa virus, used as a control, disappeared from the organoids within 48 days, while Ebola remained. The difference suggests viral replication in the brain is not incidental contamination.

— Alan Dove
45:04

Immunity both attacks and brakes, which helps the virus stay longer

It is not only destructive inflammation that infects the organoids. In infected brain organoids, researchers detected pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and anti-inflammatory mediators at the same time. This shows the host inflammatory response is a self-balancing system, with both attack and brakes. For viral persistence, a moderately suppressed immune environment is more conducive to long-term coexistence than a one-sided inflammatory storm.

49:08

Mutations in organoids have never been seen in survivors

The authors acknowledge that the model is isolated from the in vivo environment and lacks contributions such as the blood-brain barrier to immune privilege; more critically, the single-nucleotide variants that appeared in the organoids have never been reported in Ebola virus disease survivors, indicating a fundamental difference between the two. Many mutations are thought to reduce or block viral replication, and low-level replication may instead allow the virus to persist in the cell population. Persistent infection need not rely on high titers; it may also be maintained by replication low enough not to trigger clearance.

51:16

Pollution is not the culprit; this cancer transmits between fish

Since 2012, fishermen on Lake Memphremagog (between Maine and Quebec) kept catching brown bullhead catfish (Amiurus nebulosus) with black raised lesions, histologically identified as malignant melanoma. In samples taken between 2014 and 2017, 23%–37% of the fish had lesions. Initially people suspected runoff pollution from Tropical Storm Irene in 2011 as the trigger, but subsequent data pointed toward a rarer direction — the tumor itself could transmit between fish. The lake became a natural site for observing how cancer becomes a pathogen.

1:00:43

The lake's tumors are one clone, and it came from elsewhere

Two genomic analyses corroborate each other. On the mitochondrial genome tree, all tumor samples form a monophyletic group, meaning they come from a single ancestor; and the tumor sequences resemble neither the normal tissue of the same fish nor the normal tissue of other fish in the lake, whose sequences are scattered across the tree. Single-nucleotide variant analysis of the nuclear genome also yields a monophyletic group, and these tumor genomes are closer to unaffected reference fish from New Hampshire and Maine than to other fish in the lake — so the clone causing the transmission did not originate in the lake, but was introduced from elsewhere.

1:03:54

Human cancers each have distinct mutations; over half are shared in fish cancers

Harder evidence comes from variant-sharing statistics. Across 16 tumor-normal tissue pairs, 245,000 tumor-specific SNVs and 61,000 brain-specific SNVs were identified; 59% of tumor-specific SNVs were shared by at least 14 fish, while 83% of brain-tissue-specific SNVs appeared in only one fish. For reference, in the human melanoma database, 94% of SNVs are unique to a single cancer, and only 0.008% are shared by more than 10 cancers. The authors argue that such a high proportion of shared variants in fish tumors further supports that these cancers come from the same clone and are contagious.

Glossary

productive persistence
Continuous release of infectious viral particles, rather than merely residual nucleic acid.
nonproductive persistence
A state in which viral RNA can be detected but infectious particles are no longer released.
BSL-4
The highest-level containment facility for handling the most dangerous pathogens.
MOI
The ratio of virus particles to cells added when infecting cells.
