Even Legal-Cannabis States Can't Escape: A Federal Ban Is About to Empty the THC Shelves

A loophole in the 2018 farm bill let hemp-derived THC grow into a $28 billion industry, and now Congress wants to close it — even legal-cannabis states will feel it when they lose the cheap alternative.

0:00

Legal-cannabis states can't escape a federal ban either

Half of US states have legal recreational cannabis, but the reason THC drinks, vapes and gummies can be sold openly in states that ban cannabis is a loophole left by the 2018 farm bill: it allows low-concentration THC from hemp. After lab processing, that low concentration is enough to mimic a cannabis high, which is how you get Delta 8, Delta 9 — and, as the show jokes, by now Delta 11. Congress now wants to close the loophole, and it is using a stricter ban to do it. The key is that this is a federal ban, so even if your state has legal cannabis, you get hit.

— Adrian Ma
1:00

What's being banned is a $28 billion industry

The show puts the size at $28 billion and says the industry could disappear before the end of this year. Tennessee has already gone first: since July it has banned most THC hemp products, and a local reporter describes herself as already living in a ‘post-hemp world’. State legislators even beat Congress to it, banning full-spectrum CBD and smokable flower first. For retailers, farmers and processors, this isn't a policy debate — it's a choice that has to be made before year-end.

— Mariana Bacayau
3:07

Farmers are going back from hemp to blackberries and corn

Tennessee farmer Lee Crabtree's farm used to grow mostly CBD-variety hemp, and the new state law makes selling those products illegal, so he has gone back to traditional seasonal crops like blackberries and corn. The family still has the industrial kitchen from its CBD food days, and now his wife Stacy uses that big stainless-steel counter to cut grapes for chicken salad and sell it at the roadside farmers market. Lee says income is down, and if the traditional crops don't sell, he'll have to go find a job. This is what the ban looks like landing on one specific household.

— Mariana Bacayau
5:08

The regulator's reason: consumers don't know what they're drinking

Tennessee Republican Representative William Lamberth uses a drinks analogy: someone buying a Sprite expects a Sprite, not a beer; someone buying a beer expects a beer, not a tequila; someone buying a tequila expects not to get a Sprite. He says the goal is for consumers to know exactly what they're buying. But even farmers who agree with the logic worry the law overcorrects. The show gives the other side too: big companies are laying people off, and some are simply moving across state lines — Emily Schaeferich's company Burning Acre moved from Columbia, Tennessee, to North Carolina to keep growing, and she admits ripping up the entire retail, manufacturing and wholesale supply chain ‘definitely costs a lot of money’.

— William Lamberth
6:10

Moving to another state may be a wasted move

Hemp economist Beau Whitney calls the nationwide ban taking effect in December an ‘extinction-level event’. His mechanism: cannabis can only be grown in certain states, while hemp can be grown and shipped anywhere, so hemp businesses can build supersized capacity, push costs down and ship nationwide; cannabis, by contrast, needs a separate supply chain per state and can't get hemp's economies of scale. The result is that hemp-derived foods are far cheaper than their cannabis equivalents. So even in states with legal recreational cannabis, consumers will feel the squeeze when they lose the cheap alternative.

— Beau Whitney
7:13

The ban also takes out state tax revenue

The show pulls out economies of scale on its own to explain: the more of the same good you produce, the lower the unit cost, and the savings can be passed on to consumers. That also explains why hemp-derived foods are cheaper than cannabis products. But consumers aren't the only ones affected. For many state budgets, losing the hemp industry means losing a sizable source of tax revenue — in Tennessee alone, a $54 million budget hole. Beau Whitney also says it could put hundreds of thousands of jobs at risk.

— Adrian Ma
7:13

The delay just moves it from November to December

The White House has urged Congress to postpone or amend the ban, the Senate currently supports it, and the House voted earlier this month to delay it too. But those moves only push the effective date from November to December — a one-month delay. For companies that have already moved across state lines and rebuilt supply chains, a month changes nothing — they may have just moved the whole business only to find the industry destroyed in December.

— Adrian Ma
8:14

Those staying behind switch to fiber hemp

Tennessee farmer Bill Corbin, in Springfield, is preparing the first fiber hemp crop for the French company Hemp-It; this kind of hemp is used for rope, packaging and auto parts. He says there isn't a single person in the industry who didn't know this day was coming, that he thinks the industry was unsustainable to begin with, and he agrees with legislators' efforts to suppress recreational use of hemp derivatives. But he worries about medical consumers: he himself is older and has arthritis, relies heavily on full-spectrum CBD, and the products left after the ban may not be strong enough for older people or those trying to avoid opioid painkillers.

— Bill Corbin
