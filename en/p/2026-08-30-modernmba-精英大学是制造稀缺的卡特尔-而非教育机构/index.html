Elite Universities Are a Cartel: They Sell Status, Not Education

America's elite universities are not charitable educational institutions but a cartel that maintains its brand by freezing enrollment, running alumni donations, and absorbing federal research grants; they sell status, not education.

University FinanceEndowmentsElite EducationAdmissionsUS Innovation
It dissects elite university finances through an RTS-game framework, wiring endowments, admissions, tenure, and federal grants into a single closed loop — a novel and counterintuitive angle.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:03

High selectivity is not an educational advantage, it's a status business

The logic of the American free market is that privatization brings competition, competition produces meritocracy, and meritocracy drives innovation and lower prices. Universities are the counterexample: they were never treated as a market, yet they have pushed prices to the highest in the world. Harvard, Stanford, Princeton, and MIT charge six figures a year, applications have exploded while class sizes have been frozen for decades — deliberately manufacturing scarcity, just like Hermès or Ferrari. The host admits from his own workplace experience: Ivy League graduates are not smarter, and often talk more than they do; these schools have never produced data proving better teaching or better employment outcomes, so high selectivity has no educational basis — it is a status business.

6:04

Alumni are not a community, they're the school's sales pipeline

The advancement office is a sales organization wearing campus-spirit clothing, and its goal is to extract as much new money as possible. Every graduate is a potential harvest, and the wealthier you are, the more closely you are tracked. Alumni reunions, dinners, and annual giving campaigns are essentially free parties that let the school record your career, financial status, and probability of donating — no different from a sales team managing leads in Salesforce. Princeton alumni volunteers solicit classmates for free, giving it the highest donation participation rate in the country; the Harvard Red Book is a database of alumni self-reported income and occupation. Schools even hire people full-time to watch for liquidity events like IPOs, company sales, and inheritances, because those are the best moments to harvest.

11:06

Money lost on teams and dorms is a donation decades later

Elite universities manufacture membership identity through layer upon layer of small groups: Yale's 14 residential colleges, Harvard's 12 houses, Princeton's 11 eating clubs, plus fraternities, sororities, and societies. Over 90% of Harvard and Princeton undergraduates live on campus for four years, and there is one varsity team for every 150 students; the Ivy League bans athletic scholarships, so sports programs run at a total loss and exist only to manufacture emotional stickiness. These facilities are like the "happiness buildings" in an RTS: not cheap, and their purpose is to bind students' formative years, ages 18 to 22, to the alma mater in exchange for donations decades later. Dorms, dining halls, and gyms are all loss-making investments, but only elite schools can afford them, because they have few students and concentrate their investment.

15:09

In the longest bull market in history, endowments underperformed an index fund

The endowment office's only job is to make the money grow; its fund managers are the highest-paid people at the university, often poached from Wall Street, investing in private equity and venture capital in pursuit of unicorns. About 5% of the endowment's value is drawn each year to fund operations, spent on schedule regardless of whether markets rise or fall, to smooth out bad years. But the biggest irony is this: even in the longest bull market in history, after fees nearly every school underperformed an ordinary Vanguard index fund, and Harvard lost the most. Because undergraduate enrollment is frozen, tuition is loss-leading customer acquisition, and the endowment covers about two-thirds of these universities' annual operating budgets. Most of the money in the endowment is restricted; only small annual gifts are unrestricted, which is why schools still work so hard to maintain alumni relations.

18:11

When the provost moves one faculty slot, he sentences a department to death

The person actually running the university is the provost, who decides each department's budget and staffing. The strongest unit is the professors: after a six-year term they face tenure review, and once approved, the school pays them permanently and can hardly ever fire them; the long-term cost of a single tenured position can reach tens of millions of dollars, so schools allocate headcount as cautiously as an RTS player watching the population cap. The provost's most important decision is moving a faculty slot from one department to another; the department that loses the slot is sentenced to death, its talent drains away, while the winner enters a virtuous cycle. This is why CS has exploded while the humanities have shrunk. Nuclear engineering is the cautionary case: celebrated in the 1970s, it spent decades after Chernobyl paying salaries to an obsolete department.

27:13

Universities are not sanctuaries of thought, they're outposts of federal industrial policy

Doing research at a university almost inevitably loses money, but it is the core of the American innovation system. Corporations long ago abandoned long-horizon research; universities are the only institutions willing to wait 20 years rather than for a financial return. The technology behind the COVID vaccines came from UPenn, PageRank and optogenetics from Stanford, open-source cryptography from MIT. The federal government reimburses research costs with tax dollars, universities publish for free, and private companies then productize it and sell it back to taxpayers at a high price. The government uses grants to steer universities toward national priorities — after the NIH budget doubled in the late 1990s, schools went on a frenzy of building and poaching. So universities are not sanctuaries of independent thought but outposts of federal industrial policy, and cutting federal funding would instantly destroy the entire business model.

34:18

Harvard has the most money, but twelve separate schools keep it from moving

Princeton takes the narrow path: no medical school, no law school, no business school, entering biomedicine through quantitative theory, with research spending only one-fifth of Harvard's yet not falling behind in any field. Its buildings were almost all donated by alumni, such as the $100 million energy center and the $180 million neuroscience institute. MIT is the opposite, so flush that it spent $400 million building a nanofabrication facility first and sold the naming rights afterward, filling new buildings with its own Nobel laureates, and in 2025 spun federally funded fusion research into a for-profit company. Harvard, by contrast, has twelve schools each controlling its own budget and endowment, and the money legally cannot be moved between them; its $1.4 billion cross-river campus stalled for six years during the financial crisis. The decentralized structure makes Harvard's strategy advance extremely slowly.

42:25

Challengers can't fight a total war, so they bet on a single discipline

The top brand sits above all departments: when a new field appears, stars, donors, and applicants rush to Harvard and Stanford on day one. Lower-ranked schools cannot fight a total war and can only bet on a single field to buy reputation: Babson bets on entrepreneurship, Johns Hopkins on medicine, Carnegie Mellon on CS and robotics, NYU on Stern and Tisch. Meanwhile, undergraduate scarcity is the fuel of the brand, and the admissions office's only goal is to maintain the scarcity of "you can't get in," with admissions officers guarding an empty counter like an Hermès salesperson. Early Decision forces students to commit to enrolling, artificially inflating yield and letting the school win the "first choice" rankings.

Glossary

endowment
A university's permanent investment pool, from which about 5% is drawn each year for operations while the rest keeps compounding.
advancement
The university's sales team for soliciting alumni donations, tracking careers and wealth and looking for moments to harvest.
provost
The top academic officer actually responsible for the whole university's budget, departmental headcount, and academic direction.
tenure
A permanent position a professor earns after review, which the school can hardly ever revoke, creating a fixed cost for decades.
yield
The share of admitted students who choose to enroll, artificially inflated by Early Decision (ED).
tech tree
The upgrade path in an RTS game, used here as a metaphor for a university's disciplinary layout and investment direction.
