He threw away the hard drive with 8,000 Bitcoin; thirteen years of litigation got him nowhere

In 2013, James Howells accidentally threw away a hard drive holding his private keys. Over the following decade-plus—through excavation requests, lawsuits against the city, even a cryptocurrency launch—he could never accept the core tragedy: the loss was theoretically reversible but practically permanent.

6:42

Eight thousand Bitcoin cost just ten pounds in electricity to mine

In February 2009, Howells connected a Dell laptop—used for work and gaming—to the Bitcoin network just after Nakamoto launched it, becoming one of only five nodes on the entire network. He mined continuously for ten weeks, accumulating approximately 8,000 Bitcoin; the electricity cost was roughly ten pounds. Bitcoin had no trading value at the time. He pitched this "peer-to-peer currency" to friends about its future, but nobody took him seriously. He voluntarily stopped mining after the fan noise disturbed his partner's sleep.

9:50

The private-key file never got copied because the wallet software wasn't available for Mac

After his laptop was damaged by cola, he disassembled it and sold the working parts, keeping only the hard drive. He backed up films and photos to a new iMac, but Nakamoto's wallet software only ran on Windows. Rather than copy over the wallet.dat file containing the private keys, he casually tossed the hard drive into an office desk drawer—where it was later joined by another hard drive that looked nearly identical but was completely blank and 20GB.

12:55

He planned to verify the drive in the morning, but overslept

In August 2013, while cleaning out the desk drawer, he tossed a hard drive into a garbage bag, planning to have his partner Havina take it to the landfill the next morning. As he was going to bed, he suddenly thought, "As a systems engineer, I should double-check before throwing it out." He decided to wake up early and verify which drive it was. But when the front door closing sound woke him up, it was already 9 AM—Havina had already taken the bag to the landfill. She thought she was helping.

17:08

Finding the drive would be hard, but the manager said it was technically possible

After learning the truth, Howells visited the landfill to survey the site. What confronted him was garbage piled across several football fields. But the manager explained that the landfill wasn't haphazardly dumped; instead, waste was buried by date in separate cells, allowing the search area to be narrowed by the hard drive's disposal date and the site's cell system records. This detail gave Howells hope. However, police search protocols showed that one professional excavation required 15–20 personnel in full protective gear, excavators, and search dogs—far beyond what he could afford at the time.

20:16

The council rejected excavation, citing asbestos, leachate, and toxic gases as the reason

After the story made headlines, the Newport council's response was to refuse negotiation. They invoked strict landfill licensing regulations, arguing that disturbing thousands of tons of waste could release asbestos, leachate, and toxic gas, posing environmental risks. They refused to meet with him. This refusal lasted nearly eight years, until 2021 when the two sides met face-to-face for the first time—Howells offered to donate 25% of any recovered assets to Newport as a pandemic relief fund. The council rejected the offer on the spot, saying they had "no interest in this project."

25:32

He sued the council for 495 million pounds after a decade of failed negotiation

In 2023, Howells announced to the public: "I tried being polite for ten years; now we're going to war." He assembled a legal team and sued Newport council, seeking damages equal to the full value of his Bitcoin—£495 million (approximately $650 million). His argument was straightforward: either let me keep excavating, or pay me the value of the property you're withholding.

31:46

The judge ruled ownership transferred the instant the drive entered the landfill intake

In January 2025, the judge dismissed all claims outright on grounds of "no reasonable prospect of success." Under English law, the moment the hard drive entered the landfill's intake receptacle, ownership transferred to the council. Moreover, the judge highlighted a critical legal-technical mismatch: the council had never technically "seized" his Bitcoin, since Bitcoin consists only of records on the blockchain; all the council possessed was a piece of metal waste holding a private key. Howells was also ordered to pay the council's legal costs of £117,000. His subsequent appeal was also rejected.

33:52

He launched a new coin, theoretically backed by Bitcoin he can never actually access

In summer 2025, when his Bitcoin holdings neared $1 billion, Howells launched Keenog Coin (Welsh for "pence"), conceived as a digital gold standard backed by his buried Bitcoin—even though that asset remained inaccessible beneath tons of garbage. He likened the design to the nested-layers structure in the film Inception. But whether the market would accept Keenog was an open question.
