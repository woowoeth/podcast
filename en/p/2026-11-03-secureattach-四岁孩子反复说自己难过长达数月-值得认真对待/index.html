Months of sadness is the key signal

This mother's four-year-old son has spent months repeatedly saying he is sad, sometimes the moment he wakes up. Rebecca Hershberg notes that even four-year-olds—and younger children—can experience depression. While young children rarely meet the formal diagnostic criteria for depression, a duration of ‘months’ is already a long span for a four-year-old. Add the mother's own long struggle with anxiety and depression: that family-level genetic loading is a variable beyond parenting style's control. With both factors present, Hershberg recommends direct professional evaluation rather than waiting for the podcast to air before offering guidance.

Depression in young children is rare but treatable

Host Sarah Bren cautions against panic at the word ‘depression’—it is genuinely rare in young children, and even if evaluation confirms it, it is entirely treatable. She frames the process as elimination, not crisis, aiming for parents to neither dismiss the signal nor fear seeking help. Her view: failing to screen is the riskier choice.

— Sarah Bren
8:18

Appetite, sleep, and peer relationships tell the story

There is no single formula for whether to intervene, but Rebecca gives concrete dimensions to observe: the child's appetite, sleep, and how they interact with peers. She even recommends ruling out seemingly unrelated physical factors—children with prolonged low mood sometimes need thyroid screening. The pediatrician is the easiest first stop, though most are not mental-health experts and can only make preliminary judgments before deciding on referral.

— Rebecca Hershberg
9:20

The change from baseline matters more than the emotion itself

Sarah adds that what matters is not whether a child says ‘sad’ but whether this represents a departure from their baseline—a shift in how they engage with the world, versus a temperamental constant. Typical departure signals are changes in relationships, loss of interest, and giving up things they once loved. The mother's description partly fits this pattern.

— Sarah Bren
11:28

Age thresholds matter when finding a child therapist

Rebecca cautions that many who claim to do child therapy only accept ages six and up. Therapists who actually treat preschoolers should explicitly state expertise with young and toddler-age children. For this age group, therapy is nearly always play-based—stories, stuffed animals, puppets, not sitting down to talk. Parents must actively confirm the therapist's experience covers this age.

— Rebecca Hershberg
16:42

Play therapy works for internalizing problems, not externalizing ones

Rebecca distinguishes two types: children who internalize emotions—sadness, anxiety, trauma—the ‘internalizing’ problems play therapy excels at; and those with ‘externalizing’ behavior problems like aggression or impulsivity, where play therapy struggles to transfer learning from the room to the world, often requiring family-system intervention. She urges parents to be ‘informed consumers’ because some therapists claim to handle all types.

— Rebecca Hershberg
19:50

Parental presence is the safety net that makes therapy work

For children this young, Rebecca more often uses joint parent-child play therapy rather than having the child meet alone. She calls parental presence a ‘warm handoff’: the child experiences the therapist as safe, with the parent as a reference point. When discussing painful material, knowing both mother and therapist are ‘there’ lets the young child enter genuine emotional work with a stranger.

— Rebecca Hershberg

Glossary

internalizing issue
Emotions directed inward—such as sadness, anxiety, or trauma—which play therapy addresses well.
externalizing
Behavior problems such as aggression or impulsivity, which typically require family-system intervention.
warm handoff
The transition in which parental presence lets a child view an unfamiliar therapist as a safe figure.
genetic loading
Familial predisposition to mental illness, which increases a child's risk.
