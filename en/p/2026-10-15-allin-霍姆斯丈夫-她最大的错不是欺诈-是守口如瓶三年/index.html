His Greatest Mistake Wasn't Fraud—It Was Three Years of Silence

Billy Evans recounts how the judge required fighting "in court, not in media," and Holmes obeyed so scrupulously she said nothing to press throughout; he contends this silence was her greatest mistake—her power has always come from telling her story.

4:11

He met a person first, then found out who she was

They connected at a San Francisco party in October 2017, when the Carreyrou series was everywhere and Holmes was widely viewed as a fraud. What Billy saw was a girl in a fishing hat with a bandage on her face—someone whose focus and curiosity made him feel "completely seen." Only when they started talking about work did he realize who she was. This shapes his core disagreement with the outside world: everyone else read the story first and judged the person; he knew the person first and was forced to contend with the narrative later.

— Billy Evans
8:17

She was calm as a cucumber because she genuinely believed she was innocent

Three weeks after meeting, Holmes was indicted. Billy describes her response as "calm as a cucumber"—not detachment, but genuine conviction that she and the company had done nothing wrong. She'd been repeatedly told prosecution wasn't coming before it actually happened. Their mutual reaction to the cascade of bad news wasn't "we knew this was coming" but "this shouldn't be unfolding like this."

— Billy Evans
15:33

The revenue gap wasn't fabricated—it was Walgreens' broken contract

One widely cited piece of "fraud evidence" is the vast gap between Theranos's revenue forecasts and what it actually brought in. Billy presents a different chain of causation: Theranos had a contract with Walgreens for approximately 800 stores, which should have delivered corresponding revenue. After negative press created public backlash, Walgreens unilaterally stopped performing the contract. Theranos couldn't collect because Walgreens didn't fulfill its end. The shortfall wasn't fiction—it was an actual obligation that went unmet.

— Billy Evans
19:34

Prosecutors targeted four patients out of 12.5 million; the jury acquitted all four

Holmes was indicted on 13 counts. The government spent years investigating approximately 12.5 million patient tests Theranos conducted over 18 months and ultimately singled out just four patients to accuse of receiving false results. These "patient fraud" charges were all acquitted by the jury. Billy emphasizes that the diagnostic industry tolerates a certain error rate; four cases in 12.5 million have no statistical meaning. What she was actually convicted of was defrauding three specific investors and conspiring with her former business partner—and that conspiracy conviction gave the judge the basis to substantially increase her sentence.

— Billy Evans
23:40

Silence imposed by the judge became her greatest error

The judge's pretrial order: fight this case "in court, not in media." Holmes observed that instruction with what Billy calls "obsessive" obedience, staying silent to the press from investigation through the appeal decision last April, even as coverage, books, and podcasts piled up everywhere. Billy says this was one of her greatest mistakes. Her actual power has always been the ability to tell her story; by honoring the judge's order, she surrendered the only tool that could have reshaped the narrative.

— Billy Evans
27:44

The minimum-security camp wasn't minimum in any way

She's serving time at Bryan federal prison camp in College Station, Texas. Everyone says "camp" is the cushiest prison category. Billy says otherwise. As of the interview, she'd been inside for 1,205 days, and every single one has been brutal. On visiting days, out of 800 female inmates, typically only about 30 have any visitors on a given weekend. He calls that scene "heartbreaking." Billy notes he's not alone in this—130 million Americans have or will have a family member who's been incarcerated.

— Billy Evans
35:57

The sentence math: trading time served for early release, 70% deducted for restitution

Holmes was ordered to pay $435 million in restitution. Billy explains that less than $100 million of this is officially recognized as actual economic harm; the remainder came from prosecutors adding the Walton and DeVos families plus eight other major investors to the restitution claims, which pushed the sentence from an initial 4.5 to 5 years up to 135 months. Any money deposited in her prison account gets 70% deducted to go toward restitution. Under the First Step Act, signed during Trump's first term, she earns 16 days of sentence reduction for every 30 days of completed programming, putting her projected release into a halfway house in Q2 of next year.

— Billy Evans
39:05

His new company uses light to feed data to AI, not to diagnose patients

Billy previously worked on Luminar's lidar division. He's now founded Hamanthus, which applies Raman spectroscopy—shining light on biological samples and analyzing scattered-light color shifts to reverse-engineer chemical composition—to create cheaper, smaller sensors. These devices currently cost millions and take up huge space; his goal is making them affordable and accessible. But the output feeds AI for pattern analysis, not diagnostic conclusions sent directly to doctors the way Theranos attempted. He jokes that media prefer portraying him as "El Chapo running a diagnostics cartel from prison" rather than "Billy, who started a nerdy company."

— Billy Evans
