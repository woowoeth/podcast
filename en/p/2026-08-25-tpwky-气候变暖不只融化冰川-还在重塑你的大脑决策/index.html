Warming Doesn't Just Melt Glaciers — It Reshapes Your Brain's Decisions

The brain is a statistical model of the environment, and climate is the human-set baseline for normal. When the baseline itself moves, forgetting becomes an adaptation mechanism — you slide into treating the abnormal as normal without noticing.

5:56

The brain is a statistical model of the environment, not a hard drive

Aldern defines the brain as ‘a statistical model of the environment’ — a generative model that lets an organism represent the external world and navigate it successfully. The model's core function is to measure the gap between expectation and actual perception. When a gap appears, the organism has only two paths: update the model to match perception, or take action to change perception. This ‘action-perception loop’ is the foundation for understanding every climate effect that follows. Climate anxiety is only the surface; the real problem is that the model itself is being reshaped by environmental change.

— Clayton Page Aldern
11:01

Climate doesn't exist — it's just a cultural construct

Aldern makes a counterintuitive claim: climate is not an entity you can reach out and touch. A raindrop landing on your hand is real, but that's weather. Climate is ‘the definition of normality for any given point in time and space’, a human-constructed average — the World Meteorological Organization defines it as a 30-year, 10-kilometer-by-10-kilometer average. In other words, climate is expectation itself. If the brain's core function is to calibrate expectation against perception, and climate as expectation is changing, then your nervous system must change along with it.

— Clayton Page Aldern
13:05

Forgetting is an adaptation mechanism, not a cognitive defect

If the brain is a model and climate is expectation, what happens when the expectation itself is moving? Aldern's answer: forgetting. If you remembered every climatic past, you couldn't adapt to the new world. So ‘we ought to forget what the world currently is not, in order to successfully navigate the new world’. This produces a paradox: a voice in your ear whispering ‘it's always been like this, this is the definition of normal’. The battle over climate therefore has two fronts — the external physical world, and the external world's remodeling of your internal representation.

— Clayton Page Aldern
15:10

Heat unbalances the prefrontal cortex, and vmPFC takes over decisions

From the heated telegraph operators in a 1940s Cambridge lab to the psychology labs of the 1980s, research has consistently found that under high heat people are more impulsive, less rational, and less capable of critical thinking. The mechanism is that brain tissue is extremely sensitive to temperature and must burn extra metabolic energy to cool down. That crowds out a fixed pool of attentional resources. Concretely in neuroscience terms: the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) handles critical thinking, while the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) receives emotional input from the amygdala and elsewhere. In a healthy environment the two are balanced; under extreme heat the DLPFC overloads, the vmPFC takes over, and decisions become impulsive.

— Clayton Page Aldern
20:22

Heat increases violence: from honking to retaliatory hit-by-pitches

Researchers in the 1980s hid in bushes to observe how the car behind reacted when the car in front didn't move after the light turned green. In hot weather, especially in vehicles with open windows (suggesting no air conditioning), honking increased, horn presses lasted longer, and drivers were more likely to swear. The effect extends into professional domains: baseball pitchers are more likely to throw retaliatory pitches in high heat; a Harvard economist's study of harassment complaints in the postal system found that the hotter it was, the more harassment occurred, for both indoor and outdoor workers. Online hate speech, domestic violence and interpersonal violence all rise with relative temperature deviation.

— Clayton Page Aldern
23:30

Blue-green algal neurotoxins may travel through the air

Cyanobacteria produce the neurotoxin BMAA, known to be associated with ALS, Parkinson's and Alzheimer's. The new finding is that BMAA may spread via aerosols, so people living around lakes can be exposed too. Warming drives cyanobacterial blooms from three directions: synthetic fertilizer runoff balances the nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio, warmer water temperatures, and extreme storms washing more fertilizer into rivers. Globally, cyanobacterial blooms are increasing in both scale and frequency. Aldern warns: if cyanobacteria are everywhere on a global scale, they could cause serious damage in the coming decades.

— Clayton Page Aldern
31:44

In utero through a hurricane, tenfold depression risk at age six

Research on the Paradise fire shows PTSD risk stratification: people who neither lost property nor directly experienced the fire were basically normal; those who knew someone who lost property or witnessed the event had about triple the PTSD rate; those who themselves lost property or family members had triple that again. After Hurricane Katrina, roughly half of low-income parents had PTSD symptoms. More striking still is Yoko Nomura's research on Hurricane Sandy: children who experienced the hurricane in utero were ten to twenty times more likely than the unexposed group to show anxiety, depression, OCD, ADHD and other cognitive-behavioral deficits by age six. Aldern believes this hints at epigenetic mechanisms at work.

— Clayton Page Aldern
39:53

All neural climate effects are still sitting on the shelf, outside policy

Aldern points to a key gap: a few intergovernmental bodies now seriously consider climate change and health, but large climate policy cost-benefit analyses rarely incorporate those organizations' recommendations. No climate-and-health literature has attempted to price the presenteeism and absenteeism caused by rising temperatures in the workplace. All the neural effects discussed ‘are sitting on the shelf, existing in conversations like this one, but they haven't yet become part of the policy-making infrastructure’. He argues that awareness at the societal level means integrating well-understood research into the actual decision protocols for governing climate change.

Glossary

BMAA / β-methylamino-L-alanine
A neurotoxin produced by cyanobacteria, associated with ALS, Parkinson's and Alzheimer's.
DLPFC / dorsolateral prefrontal cortex
The brain region responsible for critical thinking and rational decision-making, prone to overload under high heat.
vmPFC / ventromedial prefrontal cortex
The brain region receiving emotional and impulsive input, which takes over decision-making when the DLPFC overloads.
Naegleria fowleri
Commonly known as the brain-eating amoeba; it enters the brain through the nasal cavity, and the death rate after infection is extremely high.
presenteeism
The phenomenon of employees being physically at work but underperforming because of health or cognitive problems.
