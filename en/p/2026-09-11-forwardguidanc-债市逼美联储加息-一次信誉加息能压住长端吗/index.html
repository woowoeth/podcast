The Bond Market Is Trapping the Fed: Can One Credibility Hike Hold Down the Long End?

When the long end is driven higher by term premium, what the market wants is a single credibility hike; after that kind of hike, long-bond yields fall rather than keep climbing.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:07

Bessent says he's the house; the bond market isn't buying it

The traditional size for long-end buybacks is $2 billion; Bessent telegraphed at least $4 billion and then announced $6 billion, above his own initial guidance. But the long end kept getting sold and yields kept climbing anyway. The hosts' read: the bond market is saying that's not enough ammunition — if you say you're the house, you have to act like the house. He also notes that Bessent, back when he worked under Soros, was the guy standing opposite the Bank of England, and now he's sitting in the chair he once shorted.

6:56

Regressed on nominal GDP, the 10-year should be at 5.8%

The host ran a regression of the 10-year yield on nominal GDP and concludes that if 6.6% nominal GDP growth persists, fair value on the 10-year is around 5.8% — the 100 basis point spread floor policymakers want to defend. He also points out the yen leg is actually underrated: formal intervention started from the 160 area and it's now at 153, so that side has worked, partly thanks to a hawkish BOJ. But on the bond side, the market is calling his bluff fairly aggressively.

11:01

The part of PPI that flows into PCE is actually tame

August headline PPI came in at 0.4% month over month, in line; core was 0.2%, slightly below the 0.3% consensus. The host cites Guy Lebas's breakdown: PPI doesn't measure producer input costs the way many assume, and the portion flowing into PCE is quite tame, with portfolio management services at negative 1.6%, the most notable. What really sent the market the wrong way was the transportation and warehousing line, driven by a wholesale diesel price breakout — that has the smell of energy price pass-through. Overall a neutral reading with a touch of energy pass-through.

13:10

Don't use portfolio management services to say inflation is fine

The host goes straight at the placebo-style reading that the PCE-flow portion is tame: portfolio management services isn't in most people's inflation basket at all, because assets sit with the top 1%. His logic, in his own words: you can say there was no inflation this month because portfolio management services was negative 1.6%, but 80% of people don't have a portfolio. His conclusion: month by month you can always find a thread to pull, but zoom out to the long-term chart and inflation is reaccelerating.

14:11

Both roads are bad: can't hike, can't keep stimulating

The host frames the current position as two bad roads at a fork. Historically, when central banks hiked into an energy supply shock, it ended badly — usually reversed within 12 months, as happened in several European countries, because a rate hike doesn't conjure more oil; it raises financing costs, suppresses production, and often plants the seeds of recession. But the other road is just as unusable: pushing the dollar down and stimulating commodities while suppressing bond yields and intervening in the bond market to prop up asset prices amounts to pouring more demand into a supply shock, and that gets you COVID-level inflation. His judgment: the only thing to do now is sit still and let the war and the strait issue work themselves out.

20:05

Hike odds went from a coin flip to 70/30

After this hot PPI print, the market's pricing of a pause fell to about 30% and hike pricing rose to about 70%; a week ago it was close to a coin flip. The host personally thinks it should still be near 50/50 going into the meeting, and tomorrow's CPI is the decider — especially because of Waller's dogmatic guidance style, where a single data point determines his vote. His assessment of Waller's reaction function is that it's ‘a bit much,’ and he half-jokingly suggests Waller may be a little sore about not getting the chair.

26:08

On a term-premium-driven first hike, the long end falls

The host ran an analysis of long-end performance around first hikes, with the key variable being what's driving the rise in long-end yields: inflation expectations or term premium. In the 2022 case, dominated by inflation expectations, long-bond yields kept climbing after the hike; when term premium is the dominant term, historical precedent shows the market is effectively shouting ‘we need a credibility hike,’ and once that one-off credibility hike lands, long-end yields fall instead. He thinks the current setup is term-premium driven, with inflation expectations only slightly elevated.

30:29

Growth is peaking and AI capex growth is slowing

The host's call is that growth is peaking: the growth rate of AI infrastructure investment is slowing — decelerating at least, not declining near term, but the rate of change is falling, and it's the main driver of the economy. Add real rates that have sat above 2 for a long time, high-yield credit spreads widening, and financial conditions outside equities tightening, and these usually work with a lag. Fiscal stimulus also fades after the midterms, and after the election come the debt ceiling and budget fights, divided government, plus bipartisan political backlash against data centers and the AI trade.

37:42

The AI trade looks like 2021 crypto; leverage has to clear first

The host draws an analogy between the AI trade and 2021 crypto: money comes too easily, it breeds untouchable arrogance, and that ‘cloak of invincibility’ eventually gets tested. His trading discipline: once a trade gets hot enough to see tens of billions of dollars in 3x leveraged ETF volume, a $4 billion fund running 4x leverage, and that fund gets wiped out, don't touch that direction for at least six months, maybe a year. He stresses this doesn't mean it keeps falling — it means it becomes dead money, just like the day Leopold was liquidated and they said ‘the bottom is probably in,’ but the bottom being in doesn't make it a good trade.

Glossary

term premium
The extra compensation investors demand for holding long bonds instead of rolling short ones; together with inflation expectations it makes up the long-end yield.
credibility hike
A one-off rate hike meant to prove the central bank's anti-inflation resolve to the market, rather than the start of a sustained hiking cycle.
debasement trade
A bet on currency debasement and fiscal-monetary accommodation, expressed as longs in hard assets like gold, silver and bitcoin.
SOFR curve
The interest rate futures curve benchmarked to the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, reflecting the market's pricing of the policy rate path.
