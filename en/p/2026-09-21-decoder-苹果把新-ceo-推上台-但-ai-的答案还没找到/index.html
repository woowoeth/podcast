Apple Puts a New CEO on Stage, but the AI Answer Isn't There Yet

The iPhone Duo is the coolest Apple product in a decade, and it was unveiled by a CEO two weeks into the job — a deliberate arrangement so the new CEO gets credit for an entire new product cycle, rather than letting Cook pick the fruit at the end of his tenure.

5:00

Ternus and Cook have completely different management styles

Gurman's comparison, from people inside: you bring an idea to Tim Cook and he keeps probing, and in many cases won't give you a single decision — it's collective, collaborative decision-making; you go to John Ternus and he makes a decision, he has an opinion — you may hate the opinion, you may completely disagree with it, but he has one. That's the biggest difference between the two. The first thing Ternus did on taking over was put out his own view on AI: the intelligent personal hub. Gurman says whether or not you agree with the view, at least they finally have a clear narrative, whereas Apple's AI has been a mishmash with no strong narrative since Apple Intelligence launched at WWDC 2024.

— Mark Gurman
8:08

The September 2026 event shipped no new AI features at all

Gurman points out a sharp fact: Apple held a major product event in September 2026 and did not introduce a single notable new AI feature. The first 15 minutes of the keynote were just a recap and re-narrativization of the past two years of Apple Intelligence. Apple has no AI-centric device, so it has to prop up the iPhone as their AI device, because there is no alternative. But once there's a killer device, the whole narrative changes. Gurman says this isn't a criticism — if he ran the company he'd sell what he had too — but Apple is a narrative-, VR- and marketing-driven company, and this time they played it very safe.

— Mark Gurman
14:14

Cook could have closed out with the iPhone Duo, but he didn't

Gurman makes the key point: Apple's typical CEO transitions happen in mid-October, mid-April or on January 1. Letting Ternus launch the iPhone Duo, and launch the entire new product roadmap, is a specific decision. This hardware has been in the oven for five to ten years because of development cycles and R&D. Cook could have waited until after the iPhone Duo launch to hand over, keeping the credit for himself, but he chose to make Ternus the face of this new product era. Gurman's read: if Cook was going to step down within three years anyway, better to do it now, let Ternus take all the credit, and draw a clear line between the old era and the new.

— Mark Gurman
19:28

Apple's executive overhaul was staged step by step over five years

Gurman reconstructs the timeline: in 2023 Cook started saying at every executive meeting that ‘we have to take succession seriously.’ At that point the top ranks either had $500 million in the bank or were billionaires, and this era was going to age out fast. So they made a plan and went one by one: at the end of 2024 CFO Luca Maestri retired, Kevin Parekh took over; in 2025 COO Jeff Williams retired, his deputy Sabih Khan took over; in 2026 general counsel Kate Adams retired, and Jennifer Newstead was poached from Meta; at the end of 2026, Cook handed over to Ternus. Gurman calls this retention strategy ‘Hotel California’ — you never want to leave. Cook stays on as executive chairman, the general counsel stays six to nine more months to handle government affairs, and after stepping down as COO Jeff Williams stayed three or four more months overseeing design, watch and health teams.

— Mark Gurman
23:33

Schiller left because he didn't want to squeeze developers for more money

Gurman's reporting shows Phil Schiller gave up his two remaining responsibilities, the App Store and Apple events, partly because of age and family, but also because of a roadmap split in the new era: Eddy Cue now firmly controls all of Apple's services, and Ternus is close to Eddy Cue, and they're looking for new ways to make more money from the App Store — more ad slots, more subscription opportunities, and over the next few years the App Store will be redesigned around AI and Siri AI. Schiller has always thought Apple squeezes developers too hard, and he thinks regulators are still watching Apple closely and developers are still unhappy, and he wanted to keep a bit of distance in this new era. Gurman stresses there was no big blowup between Schiller and Cue or Ternus — it was just a stack of considerations that naturally arrived at the moment to exit.

— Mark Gurman
31:52

Failing to keep young people is why Apple didn't see AI

Gurman draws a direct line from talent retention to the AI misstep: Eddy Cue has been at Apple nearly forty years, the people under him want to succeed him but know he'll never leave, so they go elsewhere for bigger opportunities, and Apple keeps losing good people at the VP level. Gurman argues that without a bench of younger replacements with fresh, disruptive ideas, Apple in some ways failed to see what AI was. He brings up the Cook Doctrine — Tim Cook often says Apple has to own and control every foundational technology that goes into the phone, which is Apple's paranoia about being tied to Intel for so long. But they can't see a path to owning and controlling AI, because the investment is too big, and they'd just burned a pile of money on the car.

— Mark Gurman
33:55

Apple says it will build its own models, but Gurman sees no evidence

Gurman says the thinking inside Apple has always been: the Google partnership is just a stopgap until we can build our own models from scratch. But he also says he has seen no evidence that Apple is pushing Plan A again — no evidence they're heads-down building new Apple first-party models from scratch, no evidence they're out doing big AI acquisitions, no evidence they're willing to pay AI researchers the kind of massive packages Meta, Google, Anthropic and OpenAI pay. Right now it feels stalled: they want to see how the Google deal and third-party partnerships play out over the next few months, see the Anthropic and OpenAI IPOs, see the news on AI safety, and maybe decide a direction in six months.

— Mark Gurman
48:19

The watch's all-day listening feature is a problem Apple created itself

Gurman reacted strongly to the watchOS 27 all-day listening transcription feature: ‘I can't believe they did this.’ The feature just listens, and a double tap shows a transcript of the last 15 seconds of what someone said to you, without their permission. Gurman says Apple knows the next generation of AI-driven user interfaces needs some amount of ambient capture — whether it's hearing you talk or seeing the world around you — but they haven't solved the social privacy layer. They have white papers on how to solve technical database privacy, they have private cloud compute, they encrypt data and don't store it, but Gurman thinks none of that overcomes the social privacy problem. Apple didn't spend enough time in the keynote unpacking this feature, and he thinks it was launched badly.

Glossary

Hotel California
A term coined by Gurman for Apple's retention strategy of keeping retired executives on in various capacities so they never really leave.
Cook Doctrine
The principle Tim Cook repeatedly stresses: Apple must own and control every foundational technology that goes into the phone.
Intelligent Personal Hub
The AI narrative Ternus put forward at the event, putting intelligence at the center with devices around it, the counterpart to the 1998 iMac digital hub.
private cloud compute
Apple's privacy architecture, which claims not to store user data when processing AI requests in the cloud.
