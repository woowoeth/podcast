Nasser's single speech ignited the entire Middle East

On July 26, 1956, Nasser addressed 250,000 people in Alexandria, announcing the nationalization of the Suez Canal Company. The speech was broadcast live across North Africa and the Middle East on Cairo's ‘Voice of the Arabs’ radio station. This single address triggered the Suez Crisis—a British-French-Israeli invasion of Egypt aimed at overthrowing Nasser, which ultimately collapsed under American pressure from President Eisenhower and became a defining symbol of British imperial decline.

17:37

Churchill's meddling in surgery nearly cost Eden his life

In April 1953, Eden came close to death on the operating table during gallbladder surgery—allegedly because Churchill, insisting on applying his ‘experience’ from Gallipoli, personally directed the surgeons, and a surgeon's hand shook under the pressure, severing Eden's bile duct. Eden underwent two rescue surgeries, with the third in Boston giving him fifty-fifty odds of survival. He lived, but ‘was never the same man again.’ This near-fatal experience set the stage for the emotional instability that later colored his handling of the Suez crisis.

— Dominic
36:21

An arms deal left Eden unable to tell who the enemy really was

Britain refused to sell weapons to Nasser because he would not join the Baghdad Pact, a Western anti-Soviet alliance. Nasser turned instead to Czechoslovakia, signing an arms agreement in September 1955. The West read this as a Cold War red flag, and Eden wrote to Eisenhower expressing suspicion that Nasser's ties to the Soviet Union ran ‘far deeper than he admits.’ Yet Eden himself could not decide if Nasser was a fascist or a communist—only that he was the enemy.

— Dominic
41:27

‘I'm going to destroy him’: Eden's breakdown over the telephone

In March 1956, when a subordinate named Nutting suggested ‘isolating’ Nasser, Eden erupted over the telephone: not isolation—total destruction, even if Egypt descended into anarchy. Nutting recalled that from that moment forward Eden became like a rogue elephant, charging at enemies that did not exist. The hosts pinpoint this call as the moment Eden completely lost rational judgment.

— Dominic
43:33

The Aswan Dam funding withdrawal stung for its tone, not its cost

Nasser had borrowed $270 million from the U.S., UK, and World Bank to build the Aswan Dam, then the most ambitious engineering project in the world. In July 1956, the U.S. and UK withdrew the loans, using language laden with contempt—implying Egyptians lacked the competence to complete it. Nasser said later that the money did not hurt; it was the attitude of the refusal. A week after the withdrawal, he announced the nationalization of the canal.

— Dominic
55:17

The nationalization was entirely lawful—which was Eden's real problem

Nasser compensated the canal company's shareholders at the Paris stock exchange closing price for that day, making the act indistinguishable in international law from the British Labour government's own nationalizations of the railroads and water utilities. The hosts emphasize that Eden's invasion was not grounded in law or morality, but was a pure political choice: partly Eden's own hunger to escape Churchill's shadow and prove himself, and partly fear that appearing weak to Nasser would signal to all Britain's departing colonies that empire was finished.

— Tom
1:13:00

In his televised address, Eden peddled anti-fascist memory

On August 8, 1956, Eden took to television in a rare national broadcast, comparing Nasser to a fascist dictator and warning citizens that ‘we all know how fascist governments work.’ Yet the cabinet papers reveal almost no moral or ideological language in private—only naked calculations of geopolitical interest. The Mussolini comparison was packaging for the public; in closed-door discussions, the hosts note, it barely came up.

— Dominic
1:19:12

A clandestine meeting at a country manor exposed the Britain-France-Israel plot

On October 14, two Frenchmen arrived secretly at Eden's country house with an audacious proposal: France had identified an Egyptian ship ferrying weapons to Algerian insurgents and was resolved to eliminate Nasser; France would supply Israel with hundreds of tanks and armored vehicles; Israel would launch a massive assault on the Sinai heading for the canal; Britain and France would then invade ‘to protect canal traffic’ and seize control before marching on Cairo. The hosts leave Eden's response for the next episode.

— Dominic

Glossary

Suez Canal Zone
A 120-mile stretch of British military bases along the canal, the world's largest military installation at the time.
Free Officers
The group of young Egyptian military officers who mounted the 1952 coup against King Farouk, with Nasser at its core.
Baghdad Pact
Britain's NATO-equivalent alliance in the Middle East, directed against the Soviet Union, which Nasser refused to join.
Operation Musketeer
The code name for the British-French military operation to retake the canal and overthrow Nasser.
Pan-Arabism
The ideology of Arab unity across the Arab world, with Nasser as its leading advocate.
FLN
The Algerian National Liberation Front, a guerrilla organization fighting for Algerian independence, which France blamed Nasser for supporting.
