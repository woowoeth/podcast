Cruise giants grew by acquisition; the one that bought a shipyard to build its ship went bankrupt

Over decades Carnival bought up storied names like White Star and Cunard one by one, at its peak holding around 100 ships; Genting bought a German shipyard to build a 200,000-ton giant, got its capital trapped inside, and went bankrupt.

The "post" in cruise ship comes from a postal contract

Cruise ships were not originally built for pleasure. In the 18th century the North Atlantic route was mainly about carrying cargo, taking passengers along the way, and only gradually adding functions beyond food and lodging. White Star Line, founded in 1845, won a contract with the British Royal Mail to carry letters and parcels for the government under contract, and only then did ocean liners that had carried just cargo and passengers become mail-carrying ships flying signal flags — hence the term "ocean liner." White Star built Olympic in 1908 and Titanic the following year, and was Titanic's parent company. In 1912 Titanic set out from Southampton on its maiden voyage to New York, displacing 46,000 tons, carrying more than 2,200 passengers, at a speed of about 26 knots.

15:07

Carnival grew big by buying companies

Carnival Group has only about 40 years of history, but nearly all of its dozen-plus brands were acquired: White Star was bought by Cunard, Cunard was bought by Carnival, and Princess Cruises and Costa, which operates in China, are also under Carnival. The guest says buying White Star and buying Cunard were considered "huge sums" at the time, but looking back they may have been just a few million to a few tens of millions of dollars — whereas today building one ship can cost several hundred million dollars: prices have risen, and ships are built ever larger, so the cost of buying a ship keeps climbing. On scale, Carnival at its peak had roughly 100 cruise ships, Royal Caribbean about 50, Norwegian about 25, MSC more than 20, and Genting a dozen or so.

— Liu Jianbing
17:08

It bought a shipyard to build a giant, and went bankrupt

Genting Cruise Lines had bought shares in Norwegian, later sold them at a profit, and used that money to buy a German shipyard and build ships in Germany. It wanted to build a 200,000-ton "World Dream" planned to operate in China, but to build this giant to its own design requirements it first had to acquire the shipyard — otherwise the yard and the cruise line are just client and contractor. The German government also required it to pay certain shipyard costs, and a large amount of capital got stuck in shipbuilding. Meanwhile the Chinese market showed no movement for three years, and even though overseas markets had reopened it could not get in. Cash flow and financing were squeezed from both sides. The guest's judgment is that the pandemic effectively dragged this cruise giant down, and the big five became the big four.

— Liu Jianbing
23:11

When a new ship launches, the champagne bottle must break

When navigation technology was underdeveloped, shipwrecks were frequent, and people believed shipwrecks were the sea god's anger, so a new ship's launch required a bottle-smashing ceremony, asking the captain's wife or a noblewoman of high standing to throw a bottle of champagne at the bow. The bottle must break, for the sake of "smashed to safety"; if it does not break, everyone's heart is shadowed. This woman is the ship's godmother. Queen Elizabeth was named by Queen Elizabeth, and Queen Elizabeth 2 by Elizabeth II; Audrey Hepburn, Margaret Thatcher and Kate Middleton have all served as godmothers. Today the scope has widened to outstanding women in all fields, and Sophia Loren has been MSC's godmother for more than 20 years. The ceremony has changed too: a big screen projection, the godmother presses a button to cut the bottle and then cuts the rope, and the livestream shows it more clearly.

— Liu Jianbing
26:16

Queen Mary 2 is the only ship that can bring dogs

Ordinary cruise ships hug the coastline; even China to Japan is not really ocean-going. Queen Mary 2 runs from Southampton to New York with no stops in between, a true ocean liner, and it follows this old route precisely to give guests a sense of nostalgia. It follows an aristocratic tradition from over a century ago: the lamp posts and oil drums on the top deck are there so dogs can pee somewhere familiar to them. Very few cruise ships allow pets, and pets must buy a ticket too, starting at several thousand dollars, only a little cheaper than a person. The dress code is also the strictest, with men basically required to wear tuxedos, and casual wear possibly barred from the dining room. Most people on this ship are old money.

— Liu Jianbing
32:19

The dividing line for high-end ships is two ratios

On small luxury cruise ships, the steakhouse hangs an authentic Picasso, the Asian restaurant has an entire gilded copper wall, caviar is all-you-can-eat, and on Ponant's ships even the butter served with bread is the French court supplier's. But the guest says none of this is the point: doing high-end is not about how good, how big or how new the facilities are, but about the service-to-guest ratio and the space-to-guest ratio. Mass-market cruise ships run about 3 to 1 or a bit over 2 to 1 crew to guests, while small luxury can reach 1 to 1, with someone ready to serve at any moment during meals; rooms start at 30 to 40 square meters, while a large cruise ship's base cabin is only 17 square meters. On price, mass-market cruises run one to two hundred up to two to three hundred dollars per person per night, while small luxury runs 500 to over 1,000 dollars per person per night.

— Liu Jianbing
45:28

China's cruise market went from 2.9 million to zero

The first cruise ship entered China in 2006, and since then the number of cruise passengers rose from tens of thousands a year to over a hundred thousand, then several hundred thousand, with the peak year seeing 2.9 million Chinese take a cruise. But 2.9 million is still a small number against a population of over a billion; many people neither know what a cruise is nor how to spend time on board; cruise culture is only a dozen-plus years old in China, while abroad it is already 100 or even 200 years old. Then the number came to an abrupt halt. The guest expects cruises to resume sailing starting around June or July this year, river cruises to resume in March, and outbound trips to Japan and Korea to still take time; the domestically built large cruise ship Adora Cruises already has two ships, Adora Magic City and Adora Mediterranea, with plans to operate over the months of May, June and July.

— Liu Jianbing
56:38

Trading photos for income, replacing the hunting rifle

The guest believes the next opportunity in the travel industry is ecological travel: Chinese tourism has already passed the stage of chasing only luxury, and next comes protecting the environment. His example is the ecological tourism protection work done by teacher Xi Zhilong in Naren village at the foot of Baima Snow Mountain in Shangri-La — the village became famous 20 years ago because of a photograph by teacher Xi Zhilong titled "the last pure land on earth," which later became the cover of a National Geographic book, making the village well known internationally, while in China maybe 99 out of 100 people have never heard of it. What teacher Xi did was hand out cameras to people in the reserve and teach them to take photographs, so they could trade photos for income rather than, as before, trading hunting rifles for income — in effect changing the income structure of the reserve's residents.
