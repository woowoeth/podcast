Fresh Air 1996-02-22

Steve Jobs' 1996 Prediction: All Software Will Move Online for Download Within 24 Months

After leaving Apple, Jobs reflected: Next's focus on hardware was treading the same successful path without seeing the world had changed—what's truly valuable isn't the graphical interface itself, but networking and object-oriented programming, the two things he didn't understand at Xerox in '79.

AppleNextPixarInternet commercializationObject-oriented programmingSteve Jobs
A rare interview before Apple's peak: Jobs himself breaks down why he was ousted from Apple, the root of Next's failure, and his judgment on the network and Apple's fate—dense with information and almost no filler.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:01

Static web pages can't serve different things to different people

Jobs pointed out that web pages at the time were written manually—every visitor saw the same page and couldn't get answers to personalized questions, like where a package was. FedEx's solution was to spend months developing software that dynamically generated web pages based on tracking numbers, pulling data from the host in real time; about 10,000 people used this service per day instead of calling. This was his starting point for explaining why Next had to build WebObjects and make web pages dynamic and interactive.

— Steven Jobs
4:04

The internet makes small companies look as powerful as giants

Jobs believed the internet was becoming the ultimate channel for companies to bypass intermediaries and reach consumers directly, and this channel had a leveling effect: as long as a small company invested enough in its website, it could appear just as professional and powerful as a large company online; some small companies even embraced the internet earlier and more sharply than large ones, making them look more advanced.

— Steven Jobs
6:07

Software going entirely digital is inevitable, just a matter of time

When asked whether buying software would become downloading from the internet, Jobs answered without hesitation that it would definitely happen, and would happen within the next 24 months—it's just that the network bandwidth reaching consumers' homes at the time (compared to the high-speed lines corporations had) wasn't sufficient yet, so large-volume software like CD-ROM games would have to rely on physical media for now, until things like cable modems caught consumer bandwidth up.

— Steven Jobs
8:08

He understood only one of three things when he visited Xerox in '79

Jobs said that when he visited Xerox PARC in 1979, they had actually done three groundbreaking things: graphical user interface, fully electronic networked office with over 100 machines, and object-oriented programming (Smalltalk). But he was dazzled by the graphical interface's brilliance and completely missed the other two; as a result, with Macintosh he only achieved the graphical interface, without addressing networking and object-oriented technology. This gap in understanding later became the starting point for Next.

— Steven Jobs
10:11

Next failed by replicating what worked before, blind to how the world had changed

Jobs acknowledged that Next's initial focus on hardware was a common mistake: after leaving a place of success, he replicated the previous success formula exactly, without realizing the world had shifted beneath them. The window for a new hardware company had actually already closed before they started—they just didn't know it. Fortunately, the software was advanced enough that even after exiting hardware and pivoting to pure software, they were still five years ahead of competitors.

— Steven Jobs
17:18

When he left Apple, he held a ten-year technological lead

Jobs said that the day he left Apple, the company had a ten-year technical lead over Microsoft—a lead that appears only once every few decades in the tech industry. But Apple stopped innovating; ten years after he left, the Mac was only 25% different from when he departed, nowhere near enough to justify ten years and billions in research spending. Microsoft caught up not because they were smarter, but because Mac for a decade was ‘a sitting duck’.

— Steven Jobs
18:19

Apple must be 50 to 100 percent better than competitors to win

Jobs explained how Apple differed from Compaq and other Wintel players: Compaq only needed to be 5% better than competitors to win, because buyers wanted the mainstream standard; but Apple was a non-mainstream choice, so buyers bore extra risk and Apple needed to be 50 to 100 percent better to make people willing to take that chance. He asserted that cutting costs wouldn't save Apple; the only way out was to innovate again.

— Steven Jobs
28:42

With Pixar, Jobs became a consumer of computing rather than a vendor

Jobs said that in 1986, after meeting Ed Catmull, he invested in and founded Pixar, spending ten years to realize his dream of making the first computer-animated feature film. In Toy Story, each frame on average required 300 megabytes of data, the film had 114,000 frames total, and even the fastest computers took an average of three hours per frame to render. For the first time in his life, he shifted from ‘worrying about supplying computers to others’ to ‘being a consumer of computing power’; faster computers for him were now good news, not a threat.

— Steven Jobs

Glossary

object-oriented programming
Building programs from reusable software components rather than custom code written line by line.
graphical user interface (GUI)
Operating a computer using icons, menus, and a mouse rather than typing command-line text.
blue box
An illegal electronic device that Jobs and Wozniak built early on to make free long-distance calls.
