Rack radiation exceeded limits twice with no fix, until an unrelated conference handed them the answer

Radiation at 26.565GHz exceeded limits at the rear of the rack. Two rounds of debugging went nowhere. The fix came from a paper at an unrelated conference: add absorber material at the connector. This isn't black magic, it's high-frequency physics.

7:40

EMC looks like witchcraft, but it's geometry

Robert explains EMC: electronic devices run signals at different frequencies and can become radiators that interfere with nearby equipment. Unintentional antennas are the main problem — not the pull-out antenna on a phone, but slot antennas and cavities. For example, a quarter wavelength at 26.565GHz is only about 3 millimeters, so if an opening in a metal cavity happens to be a few millimeters, it becomes an accidental resonant antenna. Bryan says this is the only engineering field where the words ‘witchcraft’ and ‘voodoo’ come up regularly, because it looks like black magic but is actually geometry.

— Robert "RFK" Keith
20:30

The drive caddy spring fingers aren't there to hold the drive

Bryan uses the spring fingers on a drive caddy as an example: those tabs that look like they secure the drive actually break up the continuous slot of the connector, splitting one long slot into several short ones, which pushes the radiated energy to higher frequencies with less energy. It's the cheapest and most common trick in EMC. Bryan remarks that this isn't first-principles thinking so much as ‘last-principles’ — you're forced to deal with it right before shipping. But physics is physics, not black magic.

— Bryan Cantrill
29:40

If you sell a whole rack, you can't just test one server

Robert explains the background for this return to the chamber: the Cosmo rack went from 24 slots to 32 because the power constraint was lifted. But 32 Gen5 PCIe links running at higher frequencies means radiation adds up, so the whole rack-level compliance test had to be redone. He contrasts this with server vendors who test a single unit on a separate 12V supply, whereas Oxide sells a whole rack, so the entire rack has to go into the chamber — every switch and sled cable, backplane and cavity is a potential radiator. That's why the object of this test is the complete system.

— Robert "RFK" Keith
38:20

Two rounds of debugging all failed, with no next step

Stu describes the first trip into the chamber: Rack model 0.5 powered up for the first time, rectifier fans louder and shriller. Unexpectedly, radiation over the limit appeared at 26.565GHz. They first checked the spring-finger contact points on the QSFP cage, found the paint masking was imperfect, sanded it down — no effect. Then they tried tidying the internal flying cables — no use. Local shielding with aluminum foil — still no effect. After two rounds of testing there was no progress at all; they left with the problem unsolved and not even one viable next step.

— Stu Donnan
49:00

Only the worst case the product can't achieve flushes out the problem

After leaving, Joe offered a lead: the transceivers used this time were not the same batch as last time. Same spec, but each year's new version updates the DSP, and the emission characteristics may shift slightly. Robert wasn't impressed at first, because even so it isn't an actionable fix — the product has to support these transceivers. But it was an important direction to investigate: the configuration was an artificially constructed worst case, 64 identical 200G transceivers all running, which is impossible in a real product because the side panel has no 200G backplane. This ‘fake worst case’ is what exposed the hidden problem.

— Robert "RFK" Keith
55:40

The fix came from an unrelated conference, not luck

On the third trip into the chamber, Dan had heard a paper at an SI conference in Texas on finite-difference time-domain simulation of electromagnetic fields, and one of the cases happened to be an optical transceiver, with the fix being to apply absorber material around the connector. Dan immediately sent it to Robert, and Robert and Stu were just about to start hand-cutting the same material. The paper gave them the precise method: the material goes on top of the connector, below the QSFP cage, as close to the transceiver pins as possible. This wasn't luck — it was the value of information sharing in the technical community.

— Dan Chirpich
1:03:00

Hand-verifying it is easy; getting the line to apply it identically is hard

Robert describes applying the absorber: you have to stuff a small 10mm×5mm strip into the tight space below the QSFP cage, using tweezers and a ruler, like doing handicrafts. The good news is the material does work, but they only tested the top row and had to extrapolate by power addition to the effect of applying it to all four rows. The real difficulty is the factory: this has to be applied before the connector is press-fit, using die-cut small squares so line workers can apply them like stickers. This solution made the factory want to kill them, but it's the only fix they have.

— Robert "RFK" Keith
1:07:00

The absorber isn't mitigation, it's a fix

Bryan stresses this shouldn't be called mitigation, it should be called a fix. Dan adds that this energy leaking from the connector is almost an inherent property of the transceiver driver design, not an Oxide design error. Adding an absorber at the point where the energy goes off track is working with the laws of physics, not against them. Stu laughs that throughout the testing he had to forcibly suppress his optimism, because once you expect a change to work, it usually doesn't. In the end they left with two decibels of margin, and in two weeks they have to go back into the chamber for final confirmation.

— Bryan Cantrill

Glossary

EMC / electromagnetic compatibility
The ability of equipment to work properly in an electromagnetic environment without producing unacceptable interference.
Slot antenna
An accidental antenna formed by a slot in a metal body, whose length determines the resonant frequency.
Grounded coplanar waveguide
A routing structure that suppresses radiation with nearby ground vias, at the cost of high insertion loss.
QSFP cage
A metal shielding enclosure that wraps a pluggable optical module and connects to chassis ground.
Line (EMC chamber)
The chamber's power input filter, whose current rating limits the power of the equipment that can be tested.
