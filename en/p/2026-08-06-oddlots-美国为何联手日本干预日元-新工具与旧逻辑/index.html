The US Joins Japan to Intervene in the Yen: Selling Euros, Not Dollars, and Betting on a BOJ Hike

The US Treasury has unusually joined Japan in intervening in the yen, selling euros rather than dollars and drawing on the FIMA repo facility. Brad Setser argues the intervention works as long as the Bank of Japan raises rates, and that Japan's fiscal position is far better than America's.

What the BOJ fears isn't inflation, it's a return to deflation

The Bank of Japan is raising rates slowly, with its short-term policy rate around 1%, while inflation has long been above that level. Its concerns include: not wanting to end the hard-won 2% inflation target prematurely and slip back into a zero-rate deflationary economy; and, on the technical side, that rate hikes push up bank deposit costs while banks hold large amounts of low-yielding assets, potentially hitting the banking system. There is also a market view that rate hikes raise government financing costs, though Brad thinks that worry is somewhat overstated.

— Brad Setser
6:10

East Asia piles up global surpluses, yet its currencies weaken

Global trade surpluses are concentrated in East Asia. South Korea's current account surplus is set to rise from $100 billion to $300-400 billion, and Taiwan's surplus will reach 25%-30% of GDP, yet their currencies are broadly weakening. Taiwan's central bank has guided the New Taiwan dollar lower through regulatory loosening, while in South Korea a stock market rally triggered foreign ownership concentration limits, causing foreign investors to sell Korean equities and capital to flow out, compounded by pension outflows — a "good news turns into bad news" trap. The yen's weakness stems more from extremely low interest rates.

— Brad Setser
12:20

The US is stepping in to save the yen, but what it really wants to save is Treasuries

Traditionally, weak East Asian currencies hurt US manufacturing, especially autos, but Bessent did not stress that. What concerns him is the overall pressure that yen weakness puts on Asia's currency system and the potential shock to the Treasury market. If the BOJ intervenes by selling Treasuries, it would add to pressure on Treasuries. A joint US intervention both supports the yen and reduces the need for Japan to sell Treasuries, thereby protecting the Treasury market.

— Brad Setser
15:23

Selling euros, not dollars, is a way of saying this has nothing to do with the dollar

The US sold euros rather than dollars partly because Bessent, a former FX trader, wanted to "have some fun," but the more important signal is that this is not a view on the dollar's exchange rate but a purely yen-focused operation. By selling euros, the US shows it still supports a strong dollar while avoiding a direct dollar sell-off that markets could misread. It also makes the operation more flexible — for instance, conducting rate checks in the euro-yen market to create a surprise effect.

— Brad Setser
19:29

Japan would rather borrow against Treasuries than sell them, but the facility is too small

The FIMA repo facility lets foreign central banks use Treasuries as collateral to obtain dollars from the Fed, avoiding outright Treasury sales. Brad was a proponent of this tool, arguing it can prevent the Treasury market from falling into a selling spiral during stress. But its rate is above market, and its size cap of about $60 billion may limit its effectiveness. Still, for Japan's Ministry of Finance, which holds high-coupon old bonds, using FIMA may be more advantageous than selling, because it keeps the bonds and earns the spread.

— Brad Setser
23:30

America's intervention ammunition can't sustain an exchange rate target

The US Treasury's own ammunition is limited: the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) holds about $20 billion in foreign currency and $20 billion in cash, plus roughly $160-170 billion in SDRs, but it is not unlimited. Setting a yen exchange rate target would require cooperation from Japan's Ministry of Finance and institutions like the GPIF — for example, adjusting pension investment guidelines so they hedge FX risk. But setting a fixed target is very risky: once the world changes (an oil price shock, a Fed rate hike), it may have to be abandoned.

— Brad Setser
26:33

Whether the intervention works all depends on a September hike

Brad argues that if the Bank of Japan raises rates in September, the intervention will work; otherwise it will face a test. He rebuts Adam Posen's view that intervention is ineffective, pointing out that the yen is already severely oversold and fundamentals are improving: oil prices are not high, Japan's current account surplus is 5% of GDP, and long-term rate differentials have narrowed. The key is changing market expectations, and the Japanese government, as the largest holder of foreign exchange assets, has an enormous influence on the exchange rate through its behavior.

— Brad Setser
32:41

Shorting the yen means going long the worse fiscal position

Japan's fiscal position is far better than the market perceives: its primary balance (excluding interest) is already balanced and even trending toward surplus, better than the US, UK, France and Germany. Although rising rates increase the interest burden, the Japanese government holds huge overseas assets whose returns can offset part of the cost. Net debt has been falling for five years, while US net debt will soon catch up. Brad argues that shorting the yen and going long the dollar is effectively going long US fiscal policy — and the US fiscal position is the worse one.

— Brad Setser

Glossary

FIMA Repo Facility
A Fed facility that lets foreign central banks use Treasuries as collateral to obtain dollars, avoiding outright Treasury sales.
ESF
The Exchange Stabilization Fund, the US Treasury's pool of money for FX intervention, limited in size.
Primary Balance
Government revenue minus spending (excluding interest), a measure of fiscal health.
Carry Trade
Borrowing in a low-rate currency (such as the yen) to invest in higher-yielding assets and earn the spread.
Big Mac Index
A purchasing power parity comparison based on the price of a McDonald's Big Mac, used to gauge currency valuation.
