Happiness comes not from desires or self-cultivation, but from the between with others

After reconsidering Stoicism and Buddhism, Jonathan Haidt proposes a third happiness theory: happiness is neither getting what you want nor inward cultivation, but rather establishing the right embeddedness—in relationships with others, in work, and in something beyond yourself.

Getting what you want doesn't make you happier

Haidt explains that his book's original working title contrasted two happiness theories. First: 'happiness comes from getting what you want.' But psychology quickly disproves this. The left prefrontal cortex manages circuits for pursuing, tracking, and achieving goals—making the pursuit itself intensely pleasurable. Yet once you achieve, you don't experience a burst of happiness, only relief or a lifting of burden, which quickly fades as you adapt. This is where the episode's happiness argument begins, and why Haidt rejects the 'achievement above all' narrative.

— Jonathan Haidt
6:40

Happiness comes from the between, not from outside or inside

Haidt presents what he considers his original contribution to happiness scholarship: happiness comes neither from external achievement nor from Stoic self-discipline alone, but from the between—embeddedness in three relations: with others through trust and love, with work through deep engagement, and with something transcendent through moral community. Perfect these three, and you reach the upper limit your genes allow.

— Jonathan Haidt
18:30

Stoicism fits the modern person better than Buddhism

Haidt admits he originally believed Buddhism would be psychology's 'optimal' tradition, but reconsidered during writing. Stoicism permits deep love for spouse and children and attachment to them—requiring only that you practice beforehand to prepare for potential loss. Some Buddhist branches, by contrast, demand complete emotional indifference about gain and loss. He's candid: if his twenty-year-old son chose meditation for peace, he'd be 'very disappointed,' because he wants his son to experience risk, heartbreak, and love.

— Jonathan Haidt
42:00

Phone bans in schools advance like a swift military victory

Haidt breaks social media legislation progress over the past few years into several battlefronts. Banning phones from schools is advancing rapidly—he calls it 'Operation Desert Storm'—facing almost no real opposition, with teachers and students both supportive. Raising the social media age limit to sixteen is a harder fight, but Australia has legislated and implemented it, with the EU following suit. While a ban on smartphones for children has no legislative pathway, he sees social norms shifting because 'iPad babies' display obvious language delays and neuro-developmental problems.

— Jonathan Haidt
58:00

Age verification isn't identity verification, but Britain got it wrong

Mounk questions whether limiting social media to those over 16 would isolate minors from broad internet content and potentially force users to surrender identity information to government. Haidt responds that Australia's law doesn't restrict content access—minors can still search anything on YouTube—and explicitly forbids platforms from using government ID alone for age verification. Apple and Google can now verify age without exposing identity. However, when Mounk's producer describes her own experience in Britain of being asked to upload her ID after accessing Substack messages, Haidt concedes the implementation is flawed. He explicitly rejects content-based censorship-style regulation and supports only age restrictions.

1:01:30

Status quo is generational destruction; inaction is not an option

On the policy question of whether incomplete evidence justifies legislation, Haidt sidesteps proof-of-concept arguments entirely. The bottom line: PISA tests show global reading ability declining, and the status quo already inflicts destructive harm on this generation's mental health and education. No one can defend 'maintaining the status quo' as a viable option. If someone proposes a better way to address social media's core problems than age limits, he'll engage—but major change is necessary.

— Jonathan Haidt

Glossary

embeddedness
The deep connections an individual establishes between themselves and others, work, and things larger than themselves—core to Haidt's 'between' theory of happiness.
set point / set range
The baseline range of happiness an individual reaches, determined by genetics, not a fixed unchanging point.
antifragility
The capacity to grow stronger after experiencing moderate hardship or stress.
