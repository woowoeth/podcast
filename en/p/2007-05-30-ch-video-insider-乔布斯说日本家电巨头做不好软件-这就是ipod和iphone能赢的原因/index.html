Video Insider 2007-05-30

Software, not hardware, is Apple's real moat—where Japanese electronics makers failed

Jobs argues Apple's real moat isn't hardware but the software capability gap—Japanese consumer electronics and phone makers can't build decent software, which is why iPod and iPhone ultimately win.

AppleiPhoneProduct StrategyApple TViTunesBusiness History
A rare inside look just before iPhone's launch: pricing logic, the trust deal with Cingular, why Apple TV only dares call itself a ‘hobby’—granular detail throughout.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:00

Apple TV is only a hobby—Mac and music are Apple's real businesses

Jobs said Apple had two businesses at the time, each doing roughly $10 billion in annual revenue: Mac and music (iPod). The company was about to add a third: phones. But Apple TV he deliberately called a ‘hobby’, not a business. His reason: everyone from Microsoft to every consumer electronics company tried moving content from computers to living room TVs—and failed. It's a known hard problem, and Apple hadn't yet scaled it into a real business.

— Steve Jobs
8:08

Cingular trusted Apple enough to sign an iPhone deal without seeing it

Jobs reveals that Cingular signed a partnership agreement with Apple without ever seeing a physical iPhone, breaking the carrier's standard procedures. He compares this trust to venture capital logic: top VCs invest in people, not ideas. Cingular was betting on Apple as a company, not on a specific product design.

— Steve Jobs
9:09

Carriers betting on iPhone wanted to recover their 3G network investments

Jobs explains why Cingular was willing to break protocol: carriers had already spent heavily on 3G networks, but users couldn't afford to use them. Mobile internet was too slow; video downloads were out of reach. They hoped iPhone would finally put real internet on phones, so their existing network investments could pay off.

— Steve Jobs
14:21

Lock-in doesn't explain iTunes success: most iPod songs come from elsewhere

When pressed on the claim that DRM locked users into buying Apple devices, Jobs offers a counterintuitive fact: the average iPod held fewer than 25 songs from the iTunes Store. Most music came from elsewhere—ripped CDs and so on. Lock-in fails both ways, he argues: iPod plays any MP3 from any source, and iTunes music plays on rival devices. Apple wins because the music player and store are simply better, not because of lock-in.

— Steve Jobs
22:36

Software superiority, not hardware, is what makes iPod and iPhone win

Jobs makes his core argument: an iPod is fundamentally ‘software wrapped in beautiful hardware.’ Japanese consumer electronics giants excel at hardware but have never bridged the software gap. Phone makers have the same problem—great hardware, can't do software. iPhone's software is at least five years ahead of rivals. Competitors can quickly copy the shape; they cannot duplicate the software capability.

— Steve Jobs
26:51

Apple TV abandoned the set-top box strategy for the internet-era DVD player

Jobs explains that Apple spent years considering how to replace set-top boxes. But entering that market means dealing with CableCARD and OCAP standards, integrating with carrier billing systems—work Apple didn't want to do. No viable entry point. The breakthrough came when he realized: more DVD players sit in homes than set-top boxes. Apple doesn't need to replace set-tops. It can be ‘the DVD player for the internet age.’ That sidesteps the entire industry's barriers.

— Steve Jobs
33:02

iTunes installations far outnumber iPods and live almost entirely on Windows

Jobs reveals iTunes had been installed around 300 million times—several multiples of the 100 million iPods sold. Almost all were on Windows PCs. Apple had become a surprisingly successful Windows software company. He sums up the irony with self-deprecation: putting good software on Windows is like handing someone in hell a glass of ice water.

— Steve Jobs
34:06

Jobs admits he was wrong about demand for mobile video

Jobs acknowledges he once publicly said he didn't believe users would watch video on small screens. Even after iPod added video support, he remained privately skeptical. Users proved him wrong with their actual behavior. Video became a first or second reason for buying iPods, used frequently. This pushed Apple to keep investing heavily in portable video.

— Steve Jobs
42:30

iPhone stays closed to third-party apps because of a safety-openness tradeoff

Developers asked why they couldn't write apps for iPhone. Jobs calls it a ‘very important tradeoff between safety and openness’—Apple wants both. The problem: many smartphones crash multiple times a day, and each additional third-party app increases crashes. Apple won't accept that, so it's holding third-party development—for now. But he plans to find a way within the year to open it up without sacrificing safety.

— Steve Jobs
