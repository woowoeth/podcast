Insects Are Disappearing, but Pesticides Aren't the Biggest Killer

Insect declines keep getting dismissed as exaggerated, but global data still points the same way; the real underrated culprit isn't agriculture, it's the pesticides ordinary people buy and spray themselves.

The 70% decline was challenged, but the data keeps piling up

A decade ago, a European study covering 30 years of data showed insect total mass in one place had fallen 70%. Jessica herself raised doubts at the time: you can't directly compare heavy beetles and light mosquitoes, maybe it's just a change in composition. But since then scientists have sampled repeatedly around the world, north to south, from the roots to the tips of the insect evolutionary tree, and seen the same pattern — a rate of decline never seen before. She concedes some pest cockroaches and some bee species are doing better, but in the other column the insects in decline are ‘still astonishingly high in number, and increasing.’

— Dr. Jessica Ware
19:02

Pesticides aren't an agriculture problem, they're a your-backyard problem

The host assumed the answer would be agricultural chemicals, but Jessica turned the blame toward ordinary people. Agricultural pesticide use is tightly regulated, with rules on volume and type, and farmers don't want to spend more than they have to. But ‘you and I’ can go buy any pesticide tonight and spray it wherever we like, with no regulation at all. She mentions the host ordering a pesticide from the US that is banned in Australia, and her response is ‘wear gloves and a mask.’ She calls the overall picture what her colleague David Wagner calls ‘death by a thousand cuts’ — drought, floods, fire, nitrogen deposition, land-use change, noise, light pollution, chemical pollution, invasive species, with no single cure.

— Dr. Jessica Ware
22:24

Colony collapse was only ever the story of domesticated honeybees

The colony collapse disorder that caused panic a decade ago was actually only about Apis mellifera, the European domesticated honeybee, not a North American native species. They get trucked around the country in huge trailers, live under heavy stress, and on top of that face Varroa mites and neonicotinoid pesticide residues. Jessica's criticism: worrying only about this one domesticated, managed bee is like not worrying about any mammal going extinct except the cow we like to eat. What's actually needed is holistic bee health, which is also why pollinator gardens have become popular — plant native species for all the pollinators in the environment.

— Dr. Jessica Ware
24:13

America's biggest irrigated crop is lawn

NASA researchers estimated in 2005 that the US has more than 30 million acres of lawn, golf courses included, and by surface area lawn is the single largest irrigated crop in the country. Jessica's advice is concrete: shrink the lawn, keep the part you play on, stop mowing the edges, let it go wild; or just plant a pollinator garden. She did exactly that on the slope in front of her house, and got ‘garden shamed’ by neighbors on the community social media, because a pollinator garden needs native plants of different heights and bloom times, and looks ugly for the first few years.

— Dr. Jessica Ware
27:16

Spotted lanternfly honeydew invites mold

The spotted lanternfly is a Hemiptera, its mouthparts a suction pump plus a straw that stabs into plants to drink sap, and an all-liquid diet means its waste is basically sugar water, known as honeydew. This sticky honeydew rains down on the environment, and certain molds and fungi grow on it, potentially harming plants. They have more than 70 host plants, their preferred host being the equally invasive tree of heaven, but they'll also take maples, apples, and unfortunately grapes. As they spread west toward California's big grape-growing regions, that's the real worry.

— Dr. Jessica Ware
30:28

The giant northern hornet was completely eradicated

The so-called murder hornet has been renamed the giant northern hornet, and Jessica finds the old name a bit stigmatizing. She calls this story a success case for human collaboration: a homeowner saw an unfamiliar insect in their tree and, instead of ignoring it, reported it; the specimen reached a colleague at the Smithsonian and was identified; the government put up money, people on the West Coast mobilized immediately, found the location and cleared it. A piece of wood from that tree is still on display at the Smithsonian, because it held a batch of individuals. In December 2024, the USDA announced the hornet had been eradicated.

— Dr. Jessica Ware
33:46

Cockroach milk is secreted through modified spiracles

Most cockroaches lay egg cases called oothika, which look like little coffee beans, and when they hatch all the larvae take a breath at once and pop the ‘little box’ open. But cockroaches like diploptera punctata are ovoviviparous, and the mother feeds the embryos a milk-like secretion. Insects aren't mammals and have no nipples, so they modified their spiracles to secrete this milk. Compositionally it's high in protein, not all that different from mammalian milk, and cloudy white in color. A few years ago European scientists tried to pitch it as the next superfood. The current problem: extracting the milk usually requires sacrificing the mother.

— Dr. Jessica Ware
37:36

Fear of bugs is learned from your mother

Jessica says fear of insects is learned, not innate. A colleague at Rutgers ran an experiment: put babies and tarantulas together, and the babies aren't afraid on their own; but if the mother or primary caregiver shows disgust, the babies show disgust too. Same with snakes and other animals. She uses her own two kids as an example: her daughter didn't start daycare until nearly one and was never afraid of bugs; her son started daycare at six weeks, and the daycare teacher was afraid of bugs and would scream and try to swat them, so the son grew up afraid of bugs — even though his own mother isn't. Once this learned behavior forms, it's very hard to change.

— Dr. Jessica Ware

Glossary

colony collapse disorder
The phenomenon of sudden mass death in domesticated honeybee colonies, mainly affecting European honeybees.
Varroa mite
An invasive mite that parasitizes honeybees and harms colonies.
neonicotinoid
A class of pesticides with negative effects on bees and other pollinating insects.
pollinator garden
A garden planted with native species to provide habitat for all kinds of pollinating insects.
oothika
The egg cases laid by cockroaches, shaped like little coffee beans.
spiracles
The openings on an insect's body surface used for breathing, which certain cockroaches modify to secrete milk.
