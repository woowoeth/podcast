Data centers became one of the biggest losers in America's midterm elections

Congress's approval power hasn't changed, but the execution layer has been infiltrated by political screening; even with Democrats controlling both chambers, they cannot shake Trump's actual control over science—voters ultimately decided based on electricity costs and noise from AI data centers.

Funding totals unchanged, but money's destination has shifted

One-and-a-half years ago, NIH already terminated over a thousand projects related to climate change, vaccine uptake, and health issues. American aid cuts are predicted to cause 25 million deaths over the next fifteen years. Now it's becoming more opaque: these cuts have been institutionalized as routine screening procedures.

— Max Kozlov
5:17

Scientific approval isn't the final gate; politics comes next

Even after expert panels score a project as "scientific excellence," the funds must pass through political review by NIH leadership and even RFK Jr.'s office before release, ensuring alignment with government priorities. This screening targets keywords; research touching women's health, LGBTQ rights, or vaccines gets held up—timing that coincides with the recent spike in measles cases.

— Max Kozlov
8:30

Officials claim no cuts, but entire research fields have disappeared

NIH and NSF distribute similar total amounts as before, letting administrators say "no cuts." But fields explicitly required by Congress to receive funding—like women's health and health disparities research—have had their money redirected elsewhere, making some research topics nearly impossible to pursue.

— Max Kozlov
11:35

RFK Jr. remains one of the more popular cabinet members

Democratic candidate Annie Andrews publicly called RFK Jr. a "liar and conspiracy theorist" and pushed for impeachment. But Max notes that despite scientists' disdain, RFK Jr. is actually fairly popular among cabinet members, and he had ambitions to run for president in 2028—meaning he may not leave easily or be forced out.

— Max Kozlov
12:35

Candidates weaponized MAHA's language against MAHA itself

Michigan's Abdul El-Sayed didn't attack RFK Jr.'s MAHA movement head-on. Instead he launched his own Baha campaign—Building a Healthier America—borrowing MAHA-style language about "removing toxins from food" but directing "toxins" specifically at pesticides with scientific evidence behind them, reframing the rhetoric through a scientific lens.

— Wendy Zuckerman / Max Kozlov
16:50

Majorities named AI data centers a key reason to vote

NBC exit polls found 56% of voters thought AI and data centers were a major or decisive factor in how they voted. Of over 40 ballot measures restricting data center construction, nearly all passed—interpreted as anger over rising electricity costs, noise, and water use, not necessarily opposition to AI itself.

— Max Kozlov
17:55

Data centers have become symbols of giants crushing ordinary people

Many towns report having almost no say in data center decisions, with the process opaque and feeling like big tech crushing small people once again. Silicon Valley and Big Tech bet heavily on Republicans and Trump; as data center controversy builds, public distrust of their motives rises noticeably, especially in Texas where Elon Musk poured in large sums.

— Max Kozlov
21:00

Democrats control Congress but cannot override vetoes or Senate rules

Even with Democrats controlling both chambers, Trump can veto bills, and Democrats lack the two-thirds majority in both houses to override. Non-budget bills must also clear the Senate's 60-vote filibuster threshold; budget bills require Trump's signature. This means even if Democrats wanted to double NSF funding, it would likely fail on Trump's desk.

— Max Kozlov
