Credit scores intended to eliminate bias, but encoded historical inequality into the score

FICO algorithm stopped lenders from refusing you based on gender or race, but it absorbed the entire wealth gap created by centuries of racial discrimination and locked it into a three-digit score.

6:11

Credit bureaus' founder actually aimed to make lending safer

The 1837 Panic left countless commercial lenders wiped out. Merchant Louis Tappan saw the problem and founded Mercantile Agency in 1841—the world's first large-scale credit reporting service. It had lawyers across the country gather merchant information for free, then sold those reports to lenders in New York. The lawyers' reward was referrals for future debt collection work. The system's core product wasn't a number but a written report answering: ‘Is this person trustworthy?’

— Josh Lauer
7:14

Assessing someone relied on three factors: character, capacity, capital

The standard for assessing credit was three C's: character, capacity, capital. Capital meant property, capacity meant work history, but character—the fuzziest standard—often carried the most weight. Was this person lazy, a drunk, a gambler, well-liked? These judgments were recorded in thick ledgers. Subscribing lenders had to visit Mercantile Agency's office and hear staff read the contents aloud from behind a screen—the person themselves never saw it.

— Josh Lauer
14:26

The 1900s credit man opened accounts almost exclusively for white men

As department stores scaled charge-account shopping, credit assessment professionalized into the ‘credit man’role: sitting across the desk, personally interrogating and evaluating customers to judge whether their character warranted a credit limit. The system assumed women shouldn't have their own credit accounts—they had to be under men's names. Non-white customers could essentially not get credit accounts at major department stores in 1900. As long as humans made these character judgments, bias persisted.

— Josh Lauer
14:26

FICO algorithm wasn't designed to fight discrimination, yet became an anti-discrimination tool

In 1956, William Fair and mathematician Earl Isaac founded Fair Isaac to replace manual review with computer statistical models. Scores looked only at repayment history, debt levels, credit history length—nothing to do with gender or race. After the 1974 Equal Credit Opportunity Act, lenders realized algorithm scoring could serve as legal cover for ‘we don't discriminate.’ That's when FICO was broadly adopted, and in 1989 it became the standard scoring model across all major credit bureaus.

18:51

Removing subjective bias didn't eliminate the underlying unfairness

Credit scores did solve one unfairness: you won't be rejected because a credit man disliked your face. But they can't solve another unfairness—scores show only that you missed a payment, not why: illness, accident, job loss. America's 400 years of slavery and decades of Jim Crow laws left real wealth and credit disadvantages for Black and Latino communities. Credit scores measure everyone on the same ruler without adjusting for these starting-point differences.

— Chi-Chi Wu
20:58

Once scores become gatekeepers, algorithms amplify inequality

Chi-Chi Wu points out that if credit scores were merely a socioeconomic indicator, they'd work fine. The problem is they're used in reverse as a ‘gatekeeper’ for getting credit, housing, insurance, even jobs. Credit scores absorb the entire effect of centuries of unequal credit access and historical discrimination into a single number, then lenders use that number to decide—whether intentionally or not, they may embed that inequality deeper and make it worse.

— Chi-Chi Wu
22:01

The three credit bureaus don't serve you, they sell you to others

After over a century of mergers and acquisitions, thousands of local credit bureaus consolidated into three: Equifax, Experian, TransUnion. Consumers have no choice—this is called ‘functional monopoly.’ More importantly, you're not these companies' customer. Lenders and businesses pay for your data. One in five consumer credit reports contain verified errors; one in twenty errors are serious enough to cause denial or higher interest rates. The three bureaus lack incentive to help you fix them because you have no alternative.

— Chi-Chi Wu
25:06

Companies that breach your data turn around and sell you identity protection

In 2017, Equifax suffered one of America's largest data breaches, affecting nearly half of Americans. But hackers had already targeted these systems back in the 1980s, as soon as bureaus stored files in databases—breaches happened then too. More perversely: these companies that can't keep your data safe then turn around and sell consumers identity theft protection—the problem-maker selling the solution.

— Chi-Chi Wu
31:29

Free credit freezes are the actual useful tool for self-protection

Starting in 2018, the three bureaus were legally required to offer all consumers free credit freezes—largely forced by the 2017 Equifax breach. Freezing only blocks new lenders from viewing your report and doesn't affect existing credit cards or auto loans. You can freeze and unfreeze unlimited times for free, for instance unfreezing at the dealership when buying a car, then refreezing right away. Each time your report is pulled (hard inquiry) lowers your score; freezing protects you from that damage too.

Glossary

credit freeze
Locks your credit report to prevent new lenders from accessing it without your consent.
hard inquiry
A lender's request for your credit report; temporarily lowers your score.
FICO score
Credit score calculated by Fair Isaac's algorithm and the standard model across the U.S.
