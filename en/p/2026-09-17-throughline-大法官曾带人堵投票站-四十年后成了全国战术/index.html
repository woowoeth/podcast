A justice once blocked a polling place; forty years later it was a national tactic

Rehnquist challenged Black voters' eligibility in Phoenix in 1962, and two years later the playbook became a nationwide "Operation Eagle Eye"; meanwhile, Arab autoworkers in Detroit built political power out of a wildcat strike.

0:00

The future chief justice stopped people at the polls

At Rehnquist's 1986 confirmation hearings, the most dramatic part was not his 14 years on the bench but what he had done in Phoenix in the 1960s. Witnesses said he stood outside a polling place, walked up to a Black voter and asked, "Are you a qualified voter?", demanding the person show identification and read aloud a passage of the Constitution. Rehnquist denied harassing or intimidating voters at the hearings, but acknowledged he had given legal advice to Republicans challenging voters' eligibility at polling places, and sometimes showed up in person. He called it an "election day project"; others called it a "ballot security program." That project later became a national strategy.

8:12

His conservatism took shape in high school

Journalist John A. Jenkins was doing interviews for a Rehnquist biography when his editor told him to dig into "the origins of his conservatism." Rehnquist was born in 1924 in Shorewood, Wisconsin, an all-white community, and went to an all-white high school. He was a high school senior when Pearl Harbor was attacked; he organized a school assembly called "Wake Up America," joined patriotic groups, and signed up as a block captain to report subversive activity to the FBI. After the war he entered Stanford Law School, finished first in his class, and then clerked for Supreme Court Justice Robert Jackson. Five months later, in 1952, the 28-year-old wrote a memo telling Jackson: you need not vote with the majority in Brown v. Board; there is a more principled position.

13:46

He saw integration as a personal choice, not a federal matter

Rehnquist's position was that integration was a question of personal choice, not something to be imposed on people, and that it should be decided by the states, not the federal government. This was a classic conservative stance, and it helped establish him among rising Republicans as an ideological purist. He carried that philosophy with him to Phoenix, which was still grappling with Jim Crow laws and an emerging civil rights movement. When the city council passed an anti-discrimination ordinance, he wrote letters to the editor and spoke at meetings, was noticed, and was encouraged to enter politics. That meant extra scrutiny of voters in Democratic-leaning precincts — Republicans believed many of them were voting illegally.

16:50

The voter-challenge playbook: returned mail means sending people to the polls

Tova Wang, research director of the Ash Center at Harvard Kennedy School, read FBI background-check excerpts: two white Republican challengers, one of them allegedly Rehnquist, at a polling place in an elementary school named for civil rights leader Mary McLeod Bethune on November 6, 1962, facing Black and minority voters, holding up a card with a passage from the Constitution and asking them to read it to prove literacy. The method was: first mail letters into Democratic precincts, and if the mail came back, send challengers to the polls to question eligibility. According to one FBI interview, the plan was to send large numbers of challengers into minority Democratic precincts at peak hours to clog the polling places, intimidate voters, or stall them until they gave up. Arizona law allowed each party to send representatives to challenge suspect voters, but eligibility was ultimately decided by election officials.

24:10

Operation Eagle Eye: 1.25 million voters challenged nationwide

In the 1964 election, Republicans rolled out "Operation Eagle Eye." Its director, Charles Barr, told media including The New York Times that it expected to challenge or deter 1.25 million voters nationwide. The blueprint was the same as Phoenix's: mail into Democratic precincts, and if the mail came back, challenge at the polls. On election day, The New York Times reported that Republican officials said 100,000 trained Republican workers were deployed across 35 key cities. The Miami Herald wrote that "hundreds upon hundreds of voters were swept up in partisan poll-watching, confused and frightened." The New York Times said the operation was launched in nearly every state. The FBI was asked to investigate leaflets distributed in Black precincts, whose purpose was to create delay, confusion and fear.

26:12

Goldwater lost; Rehnquist was playing a long game

Rehnquist persuaded Goldwater to oppose and vote against the Civil Rights Act, which became a symbol of Goldwater's extreme conservatism, and even some in his own party began to abandon him. But Rehnquist did not. Goldwater was crushed, and Rehnquist wrote his concession speech. The judgment in the episode is: Rehnquist did not care how others saw him, would rather be defeated on an issue than compromise, and believed he could win it back later — he was playing a long game. In 1971, at 47, he was nominated to the Supreme Court, and the voter-suppression allegations at his confirmation hearings did not hold up for lack of evidence. At his 1986 confirmation as chief justice, new witnesses appeared, including former federal assistant attorney general James Brosnahan, but it was still one side's word against the other. He served on the Supreme Court for 33 years, until his death in 2005.

35:30

Next to the Ford plant, a neighborhood held off bulldozers with a human chain

Ismael Ahmed's father came from Egypt to New York at age 10 on a cargo ship, and no one asked him any questions when he disembarked. In the 1950s the family moved to Detroit, settling in the South End of Dearborn — a narrow strip between the Ford Rouge plant and the city of Detroit. The Rouge plant had 90,000 workers at its peak. In the 1960s, the Edward C. Levy company used Ford waste to make asphalt, and big trucks drove through the neighborhood, spilling waste off their open beds. After residents protested, the city decided to rezone the neighborhood as heavy industrial — satisfying Ford and Levy while driving out the complainers. Arab residents began to fight back: when bulldozers came to tear down houses, women and children formed a human chain in the street. They held the line block by block, house by house.

44:58

Wildcat strike: the union bought Israel bonds, and the workers walked

The Arab Workers Caucus discovered that their local UAW had bought more than $300,000 in Israel bonds, and the national union had bought far more. That meant roughly 15,000 Arab members of the UAW were spending money to support what they saw as a violent occupation. They ran an ad in the Detroit Free Press: these workers viewed the purchase of Israel bonds the way Black workers viewed UAW investments in apartheid South Africa. They petitioned the UAW with no result, then learned that UAW president Leonard Woodcock was to receive an award at a ceremony, and decided to organize a wildcat strike — a strike without union authorization. After more than a month of organizing, on November 28, 1973, about 2,000 workers walked out of the Dodge main plant, not only Arabs but also Black workers. That evening about 1,000 Arabs picketed outside the awards ceremony, and Woodcock slipped in through a back door. The UAW later sold off some of the bonds, though not all of them.

Glossary

Operation Eagle Eye
The Republicans' nationwide polling-place challenge program in the 1964 election, aiming to challenge or deter 1.25 million voters.
wildcat strike
A strike launched by rank-and-file members on their own, without the approval of union leadership.
Arab Workers Caucus
An organization of Arab autoworkers in Detroit plants that launched the 1973 wildcat strike.
UAW
The main union in the American auto industry, protested by its Arab members in 1973 over its purchase of Israel bonds.
