Mantle plume sources started dry, water was later introduced by subduction

Noble gas isotope models show that the mantle plume source had 4 to 250 times less water than the upper mantle during Earth's accretion, but today exceeds the upper mantle by 20 times—supplied by subduction-zone water.

Ocean ridge and plume basalts come from separate mantle reservoirs

Ocean ridge magma rises from shallow mantle during plate separation, while hotspot volcanoes like Hawaii and the Galápagos draw from deep mantle plumes confirmed by seismic imaging. Their chemical differences fall into two categories: internal variation in hotspot basalts far exceeds that in ocean ridge basalts; additionally, hotspot basalts retain signals from radioactive isotopes that became extinct within the first billion years of Earth's formation. This indicates that the two reservoirs remained separate throughout most of Earth's history, never fully mixed by convection.

— Rita Parai
9:17

Radiogenic-to-primordial isotope ratios measure how much mantle has degassed

Every noble gas has a ‘primordial isotope’ (inherited from primordial gases at Earth's accretion) and at least one ‘radiogenic isotope’ (continuously produced by decay of uranium, thorium, and similar radioactive elements). If a reservoir has degassed little and retained most of its primordial gas, the ratio of radiogenic to primordial isotopes becomes diluted and appears low; if it has degassed heavily, primordial gases escape while non-volatile parent elements like uranium and thorium remain in minerals, continuing to produce new gas through decay, making this ratio very high.

— Rita Parai
11:21

Helium, neon, and argon show mantle plumes have undergone less degassing

Comparing helium, neon, and argon isotopes between ocean ridge and plume basalts, plume samples consistently show lower radiogenic-to-primordial isotope ratios. Under this framework, this means the portion of mantle sampled by plumes has retained more primordial gas and has been less ‘processed’ by melting and plate tectonics—a relatively primitive reservoir that has not been thoroughly stirred.

— Rita Parai
15:38

Late veneer contributed more to the upper mantle's initial composition

Solar nebula gas, volatile-rich silicate materials (similar to carbonaceous chondrites), and comets each carry distinct neon isotope fingerprints from the three accretion sources. Using these fingerprints to reconstruct the initial mixing proportions of the two reservoirs, results show the upper mantle required a higher fraction of volatile-rich silicate material—consistent with the hypothesis that late veneer material, delivered after the Giant Impact forming the moon, preferentially mixed into the upper mantle.

— Rita Parai
17:44

Counterintuitively, mantle plume sources started poor in volatile gases

‘Primitive’ does not mean ‘gas-rich.’ Plume sources have retained a larger proportion of initial gases, but this does not mean they were absolutely rich in gases—that depends on how much they started with. Forward modeling fitted simultaneously to today's helium, neon, and xenon isotope ratios reveals that ‘less degassing’ alone cannot explain the data; it also requires assuming that plume sources had lower initial gas concentrations than the upper mantle. The result unexpectedly shows that plume sources began volatile-poor.

— Rita Parai
20:52

Today's mantle plume sources contain roughly 20 times more water than the upper mantle

At the end of Earth's accretion, plume sources had 4 to 250 times less water than the upper mantle. This dry, rigid early mantle plume may have resisted mixing, perhaps explaining why the Giant Impact did not homogenize the early Earth, and might account for why plume sources retained more early isotopic heterogeneity. But today's plume sources contain roughly 400–1000 ppm water, actually wetter than ocean ridge sources' 50–200 ppm, indicating that most water in today's plumes is not primordial but was added later.

— Rita Parai
24:04

Subduction has delivered surface water deep into the mantle for only 2.5 billion years

Water reaches plume sources through subduction zones either when descending slabs carry hydrous minerals deeper than the magma generation zone, or when rising plumes pass through the transition zone and entrain water-rich material. Using the 15-year-accumulated record of ancient atmospheric xenon isotope evolution as a ‘fingerprint’ to work backward, researchers found that around 2.5 billion years ago, the mantle would have been unable to absorb such large quantities of atmospheric xenon without leaving an isotopic anomaly visible today—which we do not observe. This indicates that subduction zones became sufficiently cold to retain volatiles in the slab and transport them to depth only within the last 2.5 billion years, though it does not necessarily mean plate tectonics itself began then.

— Rita Parai
27:15

The upper mantle and plume sources have reversed their water balance over Earth's history

The upper mantle began relatively water-rich but has lost most of its original volatiles through melting and degassing over Earth's history, now maintaining rough equilibrium between degassing and atmospheric input. Plume sources show the opposite: initially dry, their current water content derives mostly from the surface—introduced through subduction zone cycling or entrainment as plumes rise through the transition zone—completely reversing the positions of the two reservoirs.

— Rita Parai

Glossary

mantle plume
A column of hot material rising vertically from deep within the mantle, driving hotspot volcanism (such as Hawaii).
primordial isotope
An isotope present since Earth's accretion, not produced by internal nuclear reactions; used as a fingerprint for material origins.
radiogenic isotope
An isotope continuously produced by the decay of radioactive elements such as uranium and thorium; used to measure degassing degree.
late veneer
Material added to Earth after the Giant Impact that formed the moon, believed to have preferentially mixed into the upper mantle.
