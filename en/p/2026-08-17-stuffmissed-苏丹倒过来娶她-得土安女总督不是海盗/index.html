The Sultan Came to Her: Tetouan's Woman Governor Was Not a Pirate

Sayyida al-Hurra ruled Tetouan for more than twenty years, built a shipyard, and worked with Ottoman privateers against Spain and Portugal; European sources call her a pirate, recent papers treat her as an early example of decolonial practice.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:03

Her honorific means noble, powerful, independent — not pirate

The opening gives the most important judgment of the whole episode: European sources sum up Sayyida al-Hurra's life as "pirate," but that label cannot tell the whole story. Sayyida al-Hurra is an honorific, literally carrying meanings like "noble, powerful, independent," and it was also used for other Muslim women such as the twelfth-century Yemeni ruler Arwa al-Sulaihi. Her birth year may fall between 1485 and 1495; her father was Ali ibn Rashid, a prince of Sharifian descent, and she grew up in the multiethnic community of Chefchaouen, fluent in Arabic, Spanish, and Portuguese.

— Tracy V. Wilson
4:09

The city she governed was a fortification and a refugee camp

Chefchaouen was built in the Rif mountains, about 60 kilometers from the coast, precisely to defend against Portuguese invasion after Portugal took Ceuta (1415). In the same period, Spain conquered Granada in 1492, ending the Reconquista, and issued the Alhambra Decree expelling Jews that same year; Muslims were then required to convert or leave in 1502 and 1526. Chefchaouen and Tetouan thus took in Muslims, Jews, and converted Muslims (Moriscos) fleeing the Iberian Peninsula, forming a diverse but not entirely equal refugee community.

— Tracy V. Wilson
10:20

Her governorship was appointed by her brother, not inherited

In 1510 she married Mulay al-Mandri, governor of Tetouan, a political marriage linking the two great families and factions of Chefchaouen and Tetouan. Because she was educated, she could help her husband govern from the start, and her co-rule became formalized when he traveled. Her husband died around 1519, and his brother tried to seize power, but her brother Ibrahim, then vizier in Fez, appointed her as successor. It was not common for a woman to hold such power, but she seems to have won the people's trust and was accepted as governor.

— Tracy V. Wilson
14:28

Her ships guarded the coast and captured Spanish and Portuguese merchant vessels

Facing the constant threat from Portugal and Spain, and an economic decline in Morocco as European sea routes around Africa opened, she cooperated with the Ottoman privateers Oruç Reis and Hızır Reis (often lumped together as the "Barbarossa brothers"). The two brothers were sons of a potter from Lesbos; they first served a sultan's son, then were hired by Algiers, and ultimately served the Ottoman Empire. She built a shipyard for the Ottomans to build and dock ships, and expanded her late husband's fleet; these ships both defended Morocco and attacked and captured Spanish and Portuguese vessels, with captives held in Tetouan awaiting ransom.

— Tracy V. Wilson
18:34

If holding people for ransom makes her a pirate, Elizabeth I was one too

Holding captives and demanding ransom from their home country cannot by itself be the line that makes her "a pirate," because it was quite common at the time. The episode notes that Oruç was once captured by the Knights and his brother Hızır paid the ransom to free him; Spain and Portugal also kidnapped and enslaved people in Africa. The scholar Jason Idris Sparks's paper goes further and compares her actions to Elizabeth I, who likewise employed the privateer Francis Drake and eventually knighted him. For them and for the Barbarossa brothers, the motive was more about defending Muslim territory and striking back at attacks on Muslims in the Iberian Peninsula.

— Tracy V. Wilson
22:39

She would not go to Fez, so the Sultan came to marry her

In 1541, the Sultan of Fez, Ahmad al-Wattasi, proposed to her. By convention, the Sultan's bride should go to Fez to marry and stay in the capital, but Sayyida al-Hurra refused to leave Tetouan; the Sultan had to come to Tetouan himself to marry, and after the wedding she continued to rule. The episode's original wording says "this was completely not the usual practice." The marriage was political, similar to Ferdinand and Isabella unifying Aragon and Castile through marriage, and it forged a link between the Sultan and her natal Chefchaouen.

— Tracy V. Wilson
24:40

She was overthrown by her son-in-law's coup, then nearly vanishes from the record

In the later years of her rule, Ceuta cut off trade with Tetouan and prosperity declined somewhat. In 1542, her son-in-law Mulay Ahmed al-Hassan al-Mandri launched a coup and overthrew her; she lost her property and returned to Chefchaouen. Sources record little about her life afterward, only that she may have retired to a Sufi lodge and died on July 14, 1561, with no children. The hosts note that later rulers of Tetouan may have found her legacy threatening and been unwilling to acknowledge how a city prospered under a woman, which may be one reason the archives are so sparse.

— Tracy V. Wilson
27:45

Her chroniclers were European, so a pirate is all she could be

Contemporary or near-contemporary accounts come mostly from Europeans, which is why she is always written as a "pirate" rather than a city-state leader or a sultan's queen. Recent research is rewriting that frame: Jason Idris Sparks's 2025 paper in the journal Religions treats her as an example of "early decolonial thought"; Amal Elhaimeur, who grew up in Morocco, has translated primary and secondary materials that previously had no English version. In 2016 Moroccan television even aired a series about her.

— Tracy V. Wilson

Glossary

Sayyida al-Hurra
An Arabic honorific literally meaning "free/noble lady," here referring to the sixteenth-century woman ruler of Tetouan.
Sharif
An Arabic honorific for someone whose lineage can be traced to the family of the Prophet Muhammad.
Mulay
An Arabic honorific roughly equivalent to "prince," used for men of Sharifian descent.
Reconquista
The long Christian military campaign to retake the Iberian Peninsula, which ended after the fall of Granada in 1492.
Privateer
A person authorized by a state or ruler to attack enemy ships, somewhere between a navy and a pirate.
Morisco
Muslims forced to convert to Christianity and their descendants, the group that remained in the Iberian Peninsula at the time.
