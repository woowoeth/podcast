The sultan came to her: Tétouan's woman governor was no pirate

Sayyida al-Hurra governed Tétouan for more than twenty years, built shipyards and worked with Ottoman privateers against Spain and Portugal; European sources call her a pirate, while recent scholarship treats her as an example of early decolonial practice.

1:03

Her title meant noble, powerful, independent — not pirate

The episode opens with its most important judgment: European sources compress Sayyida al-Hurra's whole life into the word "pirate," but the label cannot account for the full picture. Sayyida al-Hurra is an honorific, literally carrying senses like "noble, powerful, independent," and it was used for other Muslim women as well, among them the twelfth-century Yemeni ruler Arwa al-Sulaihi. She was probably born somewhere between 1485 and 1495; her father was Ali ibn Rashid, a prince of sharifian descent, and she grew up in the multiethnic community of Chefchaouen, fluent in Arabic, Spanish and Portuguese.

4:09

The city she governed was a fortification and a refugee camp at once

Chefchaouen was built in the Rif mountains, roughly 60 kilometers from the coast, precisely to defend against Portuguese incursion after Portugal took Ceuta in 1415. In the same era, Spain conquered Granada in 1492, ending the Reconquista, and issued the Alhambra Decree that same year expelling Jews; Muslims were then required to convert or leave in 1502 and 1526. Chefchaouen and Tétouan consequently filled with Muslims, Jews and converted Muslims (Moriscos) fleeing the Iberian Peninsula, forming refugee communities that were diverse but not entirely equal.

— Tracy V. Wilson
10:20

Her governorship came from her brother's appointment, not from inheritance

In 1510 she married Mulay al-Mandri, the governor of Tétouan — a political marriage joining two families and two parts of the region, Chefchaouen and Tétouan. Because she was educated, she was able to assist her husband in governing from the start, and when he traveled her share of the rule was gradually formalized. Her husband died around 1519 and his brother wanted to seize power, but her own brother Ibrahim, serving as vizier in Fez, named her the successor. It was not common for a woman to hold that kind of power, yet she appears to have won the trust of the population and was accepted as governor.

— Tracy V. Wilson
14:28

Her ships guarded the coast and also captured Spanish and Portuguese vessels

Facing continuous pressure from Portugal and Spain, and a Moroccan economy in decline as European shipping routed around Africa, she worked with the Ottoman privateers Oruç Reis and Hızır Reis (often lumped together as "the Barbarossa brothers"). The two were sons of a potter from Lesbos, first served a sultan's son, later were hired by Algiers, and ultimately served the Ottoman Empire. She built shipyards where the Ottomans could construct and berth ships, and she also expanded her late husband's fleet; those ships both defended Morocco and attacked and captured Spanish and Portuguese ships, with captives held at Tétouan awaiting ransom.

— Tracy V. Wilson
18:34

If holding captives for ransom counts, Elizabeth I was a pirate too

Holding captives and demanding ransom from their home country cannot on its own be the line that makes her a pirate, because the practice was fairly common at the time. The episode notes that Oruç was once captured by a knightly order and freed when his brother Hızır paid the ransom; Spain and Portugal were also kidnapping and enslaving people in Africa. The scholar Jason Idris Sparks goes further in his paper and draws an analogy between her and Elizabeth I: Elizabeth likewise employed the privateer Francis Drake and eventually knighted him. For these rulers and for the Barbarossa brothers, the motive was largely to defend Muslim territory and to strike back at the attacks on Muslims on the Iberian Peninsula.

— Tracy V. Wilson
22:39

She would not go to Fez, so the sultan came to marry her

In 1541 the sultan of Fez, Ahmad al-Wattasi, proposed marriage to her. By convention a sultan's bride would travel to Fez for the wedding and remain in the capital, but Sayyida al-Hurra refused to leave Tétouan; the sultan had to go to Tétouan himself for the wedding, and afterward she stayed on as ruler. As the show puts it, "this was not at all how it was usually done." The marriage was political, comparable to Ferdinand and Isabella uniting Aragon and Castile through marriage, and it established a link between the sultan and her family's Chefchaouen.

— Tracy V. Wilson
24:40

A coup by her son-in-law removed her, and the record nearly stops

Late in her rule, Ceuta cut off trade with Tétouan and the city's prosperity slipped somewhat. In 1542 her son-in-law Mulay Ahmed al-Hassan al-Mandri staged a coup and overthrew her; she lost her property and returned to Chefchaouen. The sources say very little about her life after that — only that she may have retired to a Sufi lodge, and that she died on 14 July 1561 with no children. The hosts note that later powerholders in Tétouan may have found her legacy threatening and been unwilling to acknowledge how a city prospered under a woman, which may be one reason the archive is so thin.

— Tracy V. Wilson
27:45

Europeans wrote her down, so she could only be a pirate

Most contemporary or near-contemporary accounts come from Europeans, which is why she is consistently written as a "pirate" rather than as a city-state leader or a sultana. Recent research is rewriting that frame: Jason Idris Sparks published a paper in the journal Religions in 2025 presenting her as an example of "early decolonial thought"; Amal Elhaimeur, who grew up in Morocco, has translated primary and secondary sources that previously had no English version. In 2016 a series about her also aired on Moroccan television.

— Tracy V. Wilson

Glossary

Sayyida al-Hurra
An Arabic honorific meaning roughly "the free/noble lady"; here it refers to the sixteenth-century woman ruler of Tétouan.
Sharif
An Arabic honorific for someone whose lineage traces back to the family of the Prophet Muhammad.
Mulay
An Arabic honorific roughly equivalent to "prince," used for men of sharifian descent.
Reconquista
The long Christian military campaign to retake the Iberian Peninsula, ending with the fall of Granada in 1492.
Privateer
Someone authorized by a state or ruler to attack enemy ships — between a navy and a pirate.
Morisco
Muslims forced to convert to Christianity and their descendants, the population that remained on the Iberian Peninsula at the time.
