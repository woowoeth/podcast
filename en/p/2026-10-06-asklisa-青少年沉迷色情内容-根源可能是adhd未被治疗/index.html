Teenagers' pornography addiction may stem from untreated ADHD

Adolescents with ADHD have weaker impulse control and emotional regulation, are prone to hyperfocus, and process dopamine differently—making it harder for them to disengage from pornography than other teens. Parents should first confirm that ADHD itself is being treated effectively.

Over three-quarters of adolescents have encountered pornography by age 17

The episode opens with prevalence data: more than three-quarters of teens have seen pornography by 17; over half of boys and over one-third of girls actively search for it; and more than half of those who search do so regularly. These figures frame pornography exposure not as aberration but as a common adolescent experience.

4:08

Following expert advice on parenting doesn't guarantee success

A parent in the audience had done nearly everything experts recommend: honest conversations about curiosity and pornography's risks, app-based blocking, and monitoring of viewing history. Her 15-year-old still found workarounds using his phone and laptop. She knew the right moves and couldn't execute them—what Tori calls the ‘helpless city’ of solid parenting meeting an immovable problem.

5:09

Poor impulse control is where ADHD and pornography engagement overlap

Tori draws a Venn diagram linking ADHD to pornography, gaming, and YouTube—all the ‘can't stop’ habits. The core is not curiosity itself but the ADHD brain's difficulty with impulse control and emotional regulation. ADHD teens use visual content to self-soothe; hyperfocus makes them lose time awareness; and their brains' dopamine regulation is weaker or less efficient, driving them to seek external stimulation. Together, these make stopping extraordinarily hard.

12:32

No adolescent should keep devices in their bedroom overnight

Tori's first intervention step: confirm the teen's ADHD is being treated effectively—breaking the problem apart at its roots. But device management matters too. Leaving devices in the bedroom overnight is not advisable for any adolescent. Lisa cites Jason Nagata's research: half of teens use devices between midnight and 4 a.m.—‘and they're definitely not doing calculus.’

14:35

Pornography depicts bodies that are anatomical outliers, not typical human variation

Parents need to be explicit: pornography shows bodies with atypical sizes and proportions that are not representative of human anatomy. For a 15-year-old in the midst of puberty, already uncomfortable in their own body and with limited exposure to real nudity, pornography becomes dangerously easy to accept as a template for what an adult body should look like.

16:38

Early exposure desensitizes adolescents to the stimulation of healthy relationships

Tori explains desensitization: when someone's sexual exposure is graphic, violent, and anatomically distorted from the start, ordinary intimacy becomes unstimulating by comparison. The more extreme the initial exposure, the higher the arousal threshold climbs. Starting exposure earlier compounds the effect, making it harder to build and sustain healthy romantic and sexual relationships.

21:50

Activating a child's values replaces the futility of device blocking

A key pivot: instead of positioning yourself as an interceptor—a role teens will simply circumvent, and which vanishes entirely once they leave home—activate their existing values around kindness and desire for genuine connection. Tell them directly: ‘You are a thoughtful and decent person, and this content conflicts with the kind of intimate life you want.’

27:59

Adolescents are developmentally wired to seek novelty and test boundaries

Lisa reframes the closing insight: teens are neurologically designed to seek novelty and adventure—that's how the species survives. Parents shouldn't treat boundary-testing as pathology. Instead, deliberately create safe outlets for it: learning to drive, getting a job, meeting unfamiliar people. A closed door to one outlet without an open door elsewhere is not a solution.

Glossary

ADHD
A neurodevelopmental condition whose core features are impulse control and emotional regulation difficulties, not merely inattention or hyperactivity.
Hyperfocus
An ADHD characteristic in which a person becomes completely absorbed in an activity and loses all sense of time.
