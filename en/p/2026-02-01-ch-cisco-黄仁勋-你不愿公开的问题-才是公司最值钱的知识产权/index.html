Jensen Huang: The Questions You Won't Disclose Are Your Company's Most Valuable IP

NVIDIA keeps its conversational systems on-premises not because answers are sensitive, but because ‘what I'm asking’ is more valuable than ‘what the AI answered’—this logic counters the default assumption of moving everything to the cloud.

The first half is product-launch-style banter with low information density; the second half contains arguments about compute mindset, physical AI, and self-built infrastructure worth hearing separately.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:02

The entire compute stack is being rewritten away from explicit programming

Huang defines this transformation as the first time in 60 years that computing has been reinvented: previously ‘explicit programming’ meant writing explicit code and passing explicit variables; now you state intent and the machine plans how to solve the problem (‘implicit programming’). He emphasizes this isn't just adding a layer of AI, but rewriting the entire stack—processing, storage, network, security. This is why NVIDIA is integrating AI networking technology directly at the Nexus control layer with Cisco rather than starting from scratch.

— Jensen Huang
7:11

Don't use ROI spreadsheets to decide whether to adopt AI

Huang says companies now most commonly ask ‘what's the ROI?’ but he believes early-stage new technology cannot possibly fit into a spreadsheet to prove it's worthwhile. His approach is ‘say yes first, then ask why’—not ‘prove it to me before I approve.’ The company simultaneously uses multiple tools—Anthropic, Codex, Gemini—letting a thousand flowers bloom until some point calls for ‘pruning the garden,’ consolidating resources on the most effective option; closing off too early is betting on the wrong direction.

— Jensen Huang
11:12

Compute progress makes Moore's Law look like a snail

He describes Moore's Law's 18-month doubling as ‘slow as a snail’; the current pace is 1 million-fold improvement in 10 years. This scale of change breeds an engineering mindset of ‘assume tools are infinitely fast, weight is zero’—when facing a problem, first ask: if speed were infinite and weight zero, how would I redesign this? rather than continuing the old ‘batch processing’ logic (like feeding an entire trillion-node graph to the model in one shot).

— Jensen Huang
15:14

Only the application layer of the stack is real money

He divides this technology cycle into five layers—power, chips, infrastructure (hardware + software), models, applications—and flatly states that everything except applications is ‘infrastructure stuff.’ His advice: don't stay in infrastructure discussion; apply this logic to your company's most core, most important work. At NVIDIA that is chip design, software engineering, system engineering; for this he has opened all technical access he can provide to Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens, Dassault.

— Jensen Huang
27:34

Robots should use existing tools, not reinvent them

Huang uses a thought experiment against the claim that ‘AI will replace software tools’: if you're a general-purpose humanoid robot, would you use an existing screwdriver, hammer, saw, or reinvent one? Obviously use what's there. Similarly, general AI should invoke existing tools—ServiceNow, SAP, Cadence—not rebuild a calculator. This is why ‘tool use’ has become a recent focus of AI breakthroughs. He also notes large language models still can't understand ‘domino’ causality (a concept a child grasps), a problem physical AI hasn't yet solved.

— Jensen Huang
31:39

The digital driver is worth more than the car itself

Autonomous vehicles are essentially a ‘digital driver’ whose economic value across its lifetime far exceeds the vehicle itself. Huang's math: IT is roughly $1 trillion in size, global economy roughly $100 trillion—AI lets every company for the first time compete for that $100 trillion, not just the $1 trillion. He cites Disney wanting to become Netflix, Mercedes to become Tesla, Walmart to become Amazon. Core advice: ‘technology first, your industry is the application’ not the reverse.

— Jensen Huang
38:47

Protect what you're asking, not what the AI answers

When asked whether to fully rent cloud or build machines, he answered like teaching a child—even though PCs are mature and ubiquitous, build one yourself, understand how each component works. More crucial: NVIDIA chooses to build a system on-premises not because information is sensitive, but because his most valuable IP is not the answers (answers are already ‘commodities’) but ‘what problems am I asking’—that he doesn't want anyone to see.

— Jensen Huang
41:48

AI in the loop beats humans in the loop

He rejects the idea that ‘AI should always have human in the loop,’ saying the direction is backwards: every company should keep AI continuously in the loop because companies should never regress or stagnate. In the future multiple AIs will continuously capture each employee's work experience; these AIs themselves become the company's IP accumulation—this is his definition of the company's future form.

— Jensen Huang

Glossary

AI factory
Huang's formulation for next-generation computing infrastructure, emphasizing integrated reconstruction of compute, network, storage, and security.
agentic AI
AI systems with tool invocation, research, and memory capabilities that autonomously plan tasks.
self-supervised learning
Models learn without human-annotated data; the key breakthrough enabling deep learning to scale.
physical AI
AI that understands physical-world causality (gravity, contact, cascades); foundational for humanoid robots and similar applications.
TAM
Total addressable market; the theoretical maximum market size a technology or product can reach.
