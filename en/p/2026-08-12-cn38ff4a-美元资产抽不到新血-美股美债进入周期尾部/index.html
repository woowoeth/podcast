Dollar Assets Can't Draw Fresh Blood: US Stocks and Treasuries Enter the Tail of the Cycle

The rise of dollar assets (US stocks + Treasuries) rests on a continuous inflow of incremental global capital; now that Treasury yields are high and Japan moves the whole board, the US can find almost no path to drawing enough new money, and major asset classes enter the tail of the cycle: a high-volatility, stock-game endgame.

4:19

The policy bottleneck in services consumption is the form of employment

The Politburo readout singled out ‘services consumption’, which the market usually reads as consumer stimulus; the signal this episode teases out is the form of employment. China's service sector was digital and platform-based from the start, and AI-ization lies ahead; delivery, couriers and livestreaming all happen on platforms. Platforms raise the output efficiency of the individual worker, partly offsetting the Baumol effect by which service-sector labor productivity trails manufacturing; the price is the social insurance of flexible workers, stuck on whether ‘a platform counts as an employer’—with orders taken across multiple platforms, serving three or four platforms a day, the old employer-plus-individual cost-sharing framework breaks down. The readout's separate mention of flexible-employment social insurance points precisely at this emerging socioeconomic problem.

17:01

Repairing household balance sheets is a one-shot clearing

The June Qiushi article formally discussed ‘repairing household balance sheets’ for the first time, and this episode splits it into two symmetrical halves. The liability side: from August 1, internet multi-platform lending is banned and borrowing-new-to-repay-old is sealed off, so the debts of the weakest-credit cohort will collectively turn bad at a very fast pace, and banks' short-term personal loan NPL ratios will jump—this is a deliberate one-time clearing, similar to the wholesale剥离 of the Big Four banks' bad debt in 2000. The asset side: the Politburo wrote ‘stabilize the real estate market’ directly, with no modifier like ‘strive to’, in order to halt the continued shrinkage of housing prices, the largest asset, and thereby stabilize consumption expectations.

27:23

The hardest-hit old small flats become yield assets first

In the ten-odd tier-one and tier-two cities Sanlian Lifeweek surveyed, the ‘old small flats’ in the districts that fell the most over the past three years stabilized first. The reason is a yield passively beaten out by the decline: after two and a half consecutive rounds of falls, the rent-to-price ratio reached about 2.6, already above long-term bank deposit yields. In cities where purchase restrictions can still be worked around, investment demand beyond owner-occupancy is starting to appear; because government purchases of subsidized rental housing must satisfy three conditions at once—interior floor area, location and total price—the acquisition targets are also almost all old small flats. The judgment: price stabilization does not start with new homes but with the old small flats that have already ‘fallen into yielding’.

49:17

The yen is the gate on dollar-asset liquidity

Why must global markets watch the yen? Japan is simultaneously a steady buyer of Treasuries (FX reserves plus central bank), a major allocator of overseas assets (corporate plus pension money), and the exporter of the world's low-cost funding (the carry trade of borrowing yen at zero rates to buy dollar assets). All three channels top up dollar assets. But after the yen fell to a 40-year low, Japan's stop-loss actions all happen to tighten the top-up tap: to steady the exchange rate the central bank must sell Treasuries for dollars, and once it hikes, the carry trade must unwind on a large scale, selling dollar assets and coming home. Both stop-loss actions depress dollar-asset liquidity, while letting the yen keep falling makes imports unaffordable—this is the unsolvable yen death loop.

58:18

Before the dangerous triangle tips, black swans always come from outside

Treasuries, the yen and US stocks form a dangerous triangle: the US needs to issue debt on a large scale in the coming months, and the winning bid rate cannot be too high or the cost of rolling over the debt spirals; at the same time Japan cannot cut back its purchases or sell Treasuries, or the demand side loses a piece. Stability holds only if all three constraints hold at once; if any one loosens, dollar assets go from broad gains into a stock game. The candidate black swans this episode names are all external: the yen (the master liquidity gate), the private debt accumulated by tech giants issuing bonds to build data centers (Oracle being the archetype), and the Strait of Hormuz—where commodities' capacity to absorb shocks is far worse than in the last crisis. The reason for a decline is never earnings themselves.

1:10:25

Why Chinese assets don't feed on external liquidity

Why can't the same liquidity framework directly explain Chinese assets? Three reasons. Foreign investors were already underweight Hong Kong and A-shares, so outflow pressure is small—foreign capital is an increment to us, not a burden; stabilization funds had already sold more than 90% of their positions and recovered their bullets before early July, and since then have traded in reverse intraday: buying broad-based ETFs on overshoots and selling back on rebounds, automatically smoothing the swings; add capital controls, and Chinese assets are hard to blow up instantly by dollar liquidity. The real uncertainty is internal: households still hold over ten trillion to over 30 trillion in excess savings, and wherever that activates and flows, sustained incremental capital will follow. Don't transplant the logic of shorting dollar assets onto China as-is.

1:18:56

The dollar cycle's final reckoning: from flooding to no blood left to draw

The 23-25 macro cycle recaps into one complete chain: 20-21 saw the largest money flood in human history, multiplying the base money several times over; then a strong-dollar expectation was maintained; when the rate hikes began to deliver, dollar assets attracted net global inflows; add regional conflict (Russia-Ukraine) drawing in safe-haven money, and the回流 accelerated. Now, with the increment exhausted, we are at the tail of the cycle. The numbers: US stocks from 30 trillion to 70 trillion, Treasuries from 21 trillion to 40 trillion, global debt from 250 trillion to over 350 trillion. The last wave of money is the hardest to make: in a broad decline, almost no sub-category keeps rising.

1:29:45

The narrower and hotter the primary market, the more it looks like the cycle's end

The primary and secondary markets share the same tail logic: not a broad advance, but capital crowded into the few intersections of AI × policy—robotics, embodied intelligence, world models, quantum computing, controlled nuclear fusion—with hot spots rotating very fast. What's hot now is the narrowest AI: data centers, chips, semiconductor equipment. The judgment can actually be inverted: if US assets see a sharp selloff and the AI bubble is passively deflated, the primary market may spread from narrow AI to broad AI—consumer, services, industrial and manufacturing directions that ‘can use AI and can make money’ will get funded before the ‘most imaginative’ directions.

Glossary

Baumol effect
Service-sector labor productivity rises more slowly than manufacturing, but wages converge, pushing up overall costs.
Multi-platform lending
The same borrower simultaneously taking high-interest loans on multiple internet platforms, the main target of the August regulatory ban.
Stabilization funds
State-team money that enters the market to steady the index, buying broad-based ETFs on declines and selling back on rebounds to suppress volatility.
