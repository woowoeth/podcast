Tracing Yang Li's overnight fame backlash: when nearly every male comedian was trafficking in hackneyed jokes about wives and girlfriends

On China's stand-up stages in 2021, most male comedians recycled the same tired material about wives and girlfriends. This industry-wide cliché is the real context that made Yang Li's performance spark the fiercest backlash—and the most misunderstood.

Thirty shows in, exhaustion turns to addiction

She breaks her 50+ shows in four-plus months into stages: the first 20-odd felt like adjustment, not exhaustion; around 30 shows was the low point, when the material had solidified into pure repetition; but beyond that, having internalized every line, she began consciously fine-tuning timing and tempo until she experienced pure performance joy for the first time. As she says, ‘one pleasure faded, and the joy of another thing appeared’.

— Yang Li
1:16:43

One line from Zhou Qimo rewrote her creative method

Early on, she kept writing jokes that felt off, tearing them apart and starting over. She asked Zhou Qimo (周奇墨) why she wasn't funny; his answer wasn't a technique but a root cause: ‘you might be missing exaggeration, metaphor, and rhetoric.’ She took this to the stage—performing larger, using metaphor and rhetoric to describe feelings—and quickly found her voice. She says this insight still shapes her work today.

— Yang Li
1:32:54

She refuses to defend herself against controversy

Faced with repeated misreadings, Yang Li's answer is silence. She sees these disputes as not simply misunderstandings but something more fundamental: stand-up is a public performance, and different audiences feel different things about the same joke. ‘This controversy isn't just a misunderstanding—or not only a misunderstanding. It comes from different people feeling differently about the same thing.’ Once you admit that ‘some people are extreme’, that label can later attach to people she doesn't mean to call extreme. She's also decided: when a joke dies, ignore the people who won't laugh anyway. Controversy works the same way—some people can't be reached.

— Yang Li
1:40:00

Fame and her father's terminal illness arrived the same week

Before recording the season-three special that made her an overnight celebrity, her father was diagnosed with terminal cancer. ‘I think fate just didn't give me a chance to celebrate,’ she says. ‘The best and worst things happening in my life at the same time—you can't feel proud when that's the case.’ There was no happiness to spare, no space to face the online abuse that followed. When her final tour show started, her father's condition had stabilized enough to attend, but he took a turn just before curtain. By the time she finished and rushed back, she couldn't say goodbye.

— Yang Li
1:53:09

The context that made the controversy legible has vanished

Luo Yonghao (罗永浩) reconstructs 2021: nearly every male stand-up comedian on stage talking about girlfriends or wives recycled the same tired clichés. ‘Back then in 2021, whenever male comedians talked about girlfriends or wives, it was always the same tired clichés—'my wife's so dumb,' 'my wife's ridiculous.'’ The crowd was so sick of it that even male comics in the audience were done. When Yang Li appeared fresh and cutting in that moment, the applause and laughter were genuine relief, not the ‘taking a side’ that later reinterpretation imagined. Four years on, the roles flipped: now almost no male comedian dares touch the topic because the cost is too high.

— Luo Yonghao
2:08:23

Reason cannot stop extremist communities from attacking

Luo Yonghao offers evidence: his peer Zhang Jun (张骏) delivered a sincere, objective segment on patriarchy; it landed well. That night, Zhang sent him a message asking if he'd been attacked by extremists—‘almost not at all’, came the reply. Yonghao wasn't reassured. He checked the most extreme online communities and found people still cursing him out, even recruiting others to dig up ‘dirt’ that turned out to be pure fabrication. The lesson: no matter how generous or reasonable the words, extremist communities operate on logic unrelated to the actual content.

— Luo Yonghao
2:31:36

She treats her detractors and admirers with the same indifference

‘In my eyes, an audience is an audience,’ she says—today they like you, tomorrow they might not, and that's exactly what she wants because they're still deciding for themselves. She ignores those who rage against her because it's their right, and she has no control over it. But she's equally deliberate about not enabling zealous fans. ‘I can't control someone who likes me just because they like me—that wouldn't be fair to them either. I treat everyone the same: I can't control any of them.’ All people are equal in her calculus—none to be controlled, none to control back.

— Yang Li
3:11:55

Closing with a song was her farewell to a chapter

At the final show in the gymnasium, she sang ‘I love the country, and I love beauty’—a traditional Chinese song. Simple reason: stadium seats aren't comfortable, and she wanted to give the audience something special. Deeper: that song was playing when she was eliminated from the original stand-up competition. Singing it again at her sendoff felt like formally closing that circuit era. ‘For me, that song was really a goodbye to my stand-up circuit career. It felt a bit mournful, but I wanted to fill that moment with some joy—to end it in a state of fullness.’ She admits she sang off-key but doesn't believe you need perfect pitch to sing—and that comfort with imperfection matches her intention this year: to console her younger audience.

— Yang Li
