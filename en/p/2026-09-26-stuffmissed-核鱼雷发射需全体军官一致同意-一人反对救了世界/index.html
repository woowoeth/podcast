Nuclear Torpedo Launch Requires Unanimous Officer Consent, One Dissenting Vote Saved the World

Four Soviet nuclear submarines en route to Cuba, the flagship B-59 required three military officers' unanimous consent for nuclear torpedo launch—Fleet Commander Arkhipov's dissenting vote made that 1962 moment the closest the world came to nuclear war.

Four lost nuclear submarines relied on Miami radio to judge war or peace

Four Soviet Foxtrot-class submarines, each carrying a nuclear torpedo, were en route as part of the Cuban garrison force but had been without new instructions from Moscow for over a week. The submarines were designed for cold waters; air conditioning failed in the warm water near Cuba, with cabin temperatures rising above 100 degrees Fahrenheit, and food and water running out. Having lost contact with Moscow, their only way to judge whether the US and Soviet Union had already gone to war was by listening to civilian radio broadcasts from Miami.

8:09

US signal charges sounded like attacks to exhausted, oxygen-deprived Soviet crews

To force Soviet submarines to surface, the US blockade fleet began dropping signal explosives into the sea. Accounts from those who lived through it vary: some say these were officially agreed-upon surfacing signals, while others say they were deliberately harassing the submarine to force it up. Meanwhile, the Soviet crew aboard B-59, deprived of oxygen, dehydrated, and extremely exhausted, had no idea whether these explosions meant their ship was about to be destroyed or whether fighting had already broken out outside.

9:13

One dissenting vote prevented a nuclear torpedo launch that nearly started WWIII

Captain Savitsky of B-59, believing they were under attack, ordered preparation of the nuclear torpedo. Since B-59 was the flagship of the fleet en route to Cuba and carried Fleet Commander Arkhipov, launch required his consent too—Arkhipov refused and persuaded Savitsky to surface instead. This remained nearly unknown until declassified in 1992, and Arkhipov faced criticism back home for not ‘going down with the ship,’ only to be regarded years later as the man who saved the world.

16:20

A new Soviet satellite mistook clouds for five American missiles

In the early hours of September 26, 1983, the Soviet Union's recently activated—and, by Petrov's account, still very ‘raw’—early warning satellite system mistook sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds for missile launches, with the reported count climbing rapidly from one to five, all marked as originating from the US. Alarms blared in the command center, giant red warning text flashed across screens, the scene like a disaster movie.

17:20

Logic prevented disaster when an officer doubted a flawed early-warning system

Petrov's suspicion stemmed half from the system being too new and hastily deployed, and half from logical reasoning—if you were launching a first-strike nuclear war, you wouldn't use only five missiles causing limited damage. So instead of following procedure to confirm the missile attack with his superiors, he reported it as a false alarm, even though he himself was only about fifty-fifty on it at the time. In retrospect, he believed that if someone else had been on duty, the outcome might have been different. Ironically, this incident brought him a reprimand for incomplete logging, not public acknowledgment until fifteen years later.

21:29

Russia's nuclear retaliation window is ten minutes—sometimes five is too much

The nuclear briefcase (weighing roughly 45 pounds) accompanies the head of state; once missile launch indicators are detected, Russia's established protocol is: first send a signal to the president's briefcase, then assess the situation, and decide whether to retaliate within ten minutes—per defense protocol, no need to wait for the opponent's missiles to actually land before ordering a counterattack. In the 1995 Barents Sea incident, radar mistook the separation of a Norwegian sounding rocket for the separation of a Trident missile; about five minutes into the ten-minute window, submarine commanders in the affected sea area were ordered to enter alert status.

23:32

Scientists notified all nations except the radar operator about the rocket launch

The Norwegian and American teams responsible for the sounding rocket (used to study the aurora) had notified multiple nations in advance, including Russia, but the message never reached the satellite operator who actually sounded the alarm, and nobody connected ‘the launch report from the coast direction’ with ‘the science rocket launch everyone knew about.’ This incident, nearly causing disaster through communication breakdown, also marked the only time in human history that a head of state actually activated a nuclear briefcase.
