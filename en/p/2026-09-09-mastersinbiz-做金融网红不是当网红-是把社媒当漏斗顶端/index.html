Being a finfluencer isn't being an influencer — it's treating social media as the top of the funnel

Tyler Gardner went from teacher to financial advisor to financial content creator, reaching 6 million followers. His judgment: short videos are just the top of the funnel; the real asset is the email list and long-form content you own yourself.

What clients want isn't education, it's convenience

Gardner originally thought being a financial advisor meant going back to his home state of Vermont to teach people how to manage money. The biggest surprise was that most clients don't want to be educated at all. They just want to hand over their assets, say "don't call me, I'll call you," and pay for convenience. This directly changed his career path — since clients didn't want a classroom, he went to social media to teach everyone. This is a reminder for anyone doing to-C financial services: the educational product you're selling, users may just want the result.

5:12

The Roth IRA video with 2 million views was wrong

Gardner's turning point came when his wife came across a TikTok about Roth IRAs. It was funny and engaging, but the person had no idea what a Roth IRA was, and the video had 2 million views. He dug into this thread and found that a large number of people on the platform who were articulate, confident, and charismatic were saying things that were completely wrong; while people who actually had the data, who had been PMs or advisors for 20 years, didn't know how to attract an audience. What he saw wasn't a content opportunity — it was a market gap: people who both know the subject and can compress complex concepts into 30 seconds are almost nonexistent.

9:15

The compliance line is: don't name specific buys or sells

When Gardner was studying for the Series 65 and CFA, the conclusion in the textbooks about social media was always "we haven't figured out how to regulate it yet." The small RIA he was at chose to be extremely conservative: no naming specific funds, no naming specific drawdown rates, only pure educational content, avoiding anything that could be interpreted as investment advice. He cited another practitioner's experience as more relaxed — as long as you don't say "buy Microsoft" or "sell Dell," you can talk about anything else. The difference between the two approaches isn't courage; it's the compliance bottom line of the institution you're at.

14:20

First you have to get through the cringe phase when no one watches

Two years after his career change, Gardner learned that almost everyone thought he wouldn't make it, believing the attention economy was too cyclical — you might be the internet's favorite for a week, then crash. He says every content creator has to go through the so-called cringe phase — producing terrible content because you're not good yet, and can only learn by trial and error. Friends will laugh, and this phase is when people are most likely to give up. And actually starting to get business deals usually takes six months to a year of daily grinding. He compares it to New Year's resolutions at the gym in January: if you don't hit a million followers in two weeks, you quit.

— Tyler Gardner
17:21

Follower count no longer equals reach

Gardner says that on most platforms now, how many followers you have doesn't really matter for your reach potential. A TikTok account opened tomorrow has the same chance of going viral as his account with millions of followers. He calls this the best instant feedback system in the world: if the content isn't good, there's no reach, regardless of whether you have a million followers. This is crucial for judging the value of "follower assets" — follower count isn't a moat; whether you can earn someone's attention in 30 to 60 seconds is.

— Tyler Gardner
19:22

Short videos deliberately skip due diligence; long-form fills the gap

Gardner admits that some of his videos knowingly didn't go as deep in due diligence as they should have. There are two reasons: first, 30 to 60 seconds is a scarce resource; second, he treats TikTok, Facebook, and YouTube Shorts all as the top of the funnel, with the goal of driving people to the newsletter. In the newsletter he doesn't cut corners, because readers are self-selected and willing to read details. He quotes a line about politics from "Yellowstone": before you get the position, you have to poke people, alienate people, be a version of yourself with lower moral standards. His self-defense is: first earn attention, then repay it with real information.

— Tyler Gardner
22:26

TikTok shut down for a day, and two years of platform presence went to zero

Gardner once wrote in a monthly reflection that "never do a newsletter, that's a joke," but later read Tim Ferriss saying the opposite — the newsletter is the only asset where you can accumulate email addresses that no one can take away. What really changed his mind was TikTok's brief 24-hour shutdown in the US: within a day, the platform reach he'd built over two years went directly to zero, and he had no control over it. His conclusion is that not diversifying your digital assets is as stupid as not diversifying the money in your IRA or 401k. You must own your content and platform, and use other people's platforms to drive traffic to what you own.

— Tyler Gardner
26:32

The biggest risk is doing one stable job for twenty years

Gardner pushes back on the default assumption that "a stable job is safer": the biggest risk is staying in the same job for 20 years as a safe W-2 employee, with your upside sealed off. He believes people don't hate work, they hate being ordered around and working with people who don't respect them; once you have autonomy, your energy goes up. His own experience is that with autonomy, on good days he can go into the woods and shoot 20 videos, and on bad days he doesn't appear on camera and writes instead. He also says that if you try for a year or two and it doesn't work out, you can most likely return to the same type of position you had before.
