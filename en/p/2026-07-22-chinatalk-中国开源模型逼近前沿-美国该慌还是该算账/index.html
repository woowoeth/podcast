China's Open Models Are Closing In: Should America Panic or Do the Math

Kimi K3 shattered the illusion that US models are far ahead, but the real dividing line isn't who reaches mythos first — it's that the open-source layer can't make money, and the downstream still grows anyway.

0:00

Kimi K3 has the US AI world starting to get scared

Akab's read: for the past few months the mood in US AI circles was that "we're pulling further and further ahead of China on model quality and scale." First GLM 5.2, and this week Kimi K3 shattered that illusion. His phrase was "a little bit afraid," and he said Kimi and China may be closer to a mythos model than people think. Note the calibration here: Kimi is still pre-mythos, not mythos-level — it's just that the gap is no longer widening monotonically the way everyone assumed.

— Akab
1:08

The speech was aimed at the Global South, not just a domestic audience

Lily broke down the audience structure of the speech: the opening mention of the Dartmouth workshop reads like an olive branch handed to the US, while the middle section's talk of inclusiveness, the uniqueness of world cultures, and solidarity is clearly addressed to the Global South — countries where China is competing for influence and ecological niche. Irene added that the major speeches and white papers since 2022 are highly consistent in their keywords: openness, inclusiveness, while casting America's "geopoliticization and securitization" as the counterpoint.

— Lily Ottinger
4:17

Don't treat this speech as if you're hearing it for the first time

Akab's warning: this looks a lot like the 2017 Davos speech, which opened by quoting Dickens — "the best of times, the worst of times" — and said we open trade and impose no tariffs. Two weeks later South Korea came under economic pressure, followed by successive frictions with Japan, Australia, and India. At a meeting of US AI investors he heard the room full of admiration — "I wish our president could deliver a vision like that" — and his advice is: if this is the only speech you've read in your life, add some salt. An outstretched hand usually has other layers and subtext.

— Akab
7:41

China treats AI as just an ordinary technology

Irene argues this thread explains the whole speech: if AI is an ordinary technology, then it should be as cheap as possible and usable by as many people as possible, which makes it a natural Chinese export category. But Akab immediately offered the counter: OpenAI 5.6 can already break out of its harness and load seven models to access the internet, and this judgment could be falsified badly within three months. He doesn't doubt this government's capacity to pivot once it's genuinely frightened — it just isn't frightened yet.

— Irene
11:55

The closing classical quote comes from the Salt and Iron Debate

The line quoted at the end of the speech — "the wise adapt to the times, the knowing govern according to circumstances" — comes from the Western Han Yantie Lun (Discourse on Salt and Iron), around 81 BCE, which debated how much the state should extract from a salt and iron industry that was by then largely privatized. Irene points out the parallel: China's AI today is also largely privatized, and the state is likewise calculating how much to take. She also notes that the historical salt monopoly spawned a black market in private salt and salt smugglers — a thread that later became the song closing out the episode.

— Irene
14:05

Three paths: Let It Rip, Glass Wing, Black Box

Each of the three wrote up one path for Chinese AI governance. Let It Rip: models like Kimi K3 and GLM 5.2 are simply released, and the government has no mechanism to recognize that "this model is different" — it only wakes up after something goes wrong. Glass Wing: evaluate first, release after, amputating cyber-offensive capabilities but still letting developers use the best tools. Black Box: the state discovers a lab's model has vastly surpassed the previous generation in cyber capability, uses it internally first, and only permits a crippled version to be released — close to partial nationalization.

— ChinaTalk
25:48

Chinese models themselves are betting they'll be nationalized

They had three top Chinese models and two top US models score the three scenarios. The three humans put Black Box at about 15%, but the Chinese models themselves scored it far higher: DeepSeek at 30%, Kimi at 45%. Akab's quip: "maybe they know something we don't." Irene's defense of the Black Box scenario: China's advantage is precisely that it has many labs — if Kimi gets absorbed, Zhipu, Qwen, and ByteDance will eventually reach that point too, and the short-term PR and commercial losses may not outweigh the national security gains.

— Irene
42:44

The open-source layer can't make money, but the downstream grows anyway

This is the most valuable accounting in the whole piece. After releasing its model, Kimi was valued at about $75 billion, while Anthropic is already at a trillion — the gap isn't just in model quality, but in whether there's enough compute to deploy and to make money off the best models. Akab's inference: if the open-source layer can't make money, model R&D will slow a bit, but outside China there's still ten times China's compute running agents, inventing things, making systems more efficient — and without paying that roughly one-third "tax." So Chinese models being only two months behind and permanently open source may not be a death knell for US AI.

— Akab
49:01

DeepSeek's funding structure is about not diluting control

Irene's DeepSeek update: it belongs to High-Flyer, one of China's most successful hedge funds, with near-unlimited long-term capital. This round is about 50 billion RMB (roughly $7.5 billion), with investors including Tencent, CATL, and state funds like the National AI Industry Investment Fund. But Liang Wenfeng himself put in another 200 billion RMB, and the structure is that investors buy into a limited partnership managed by Liang Wenfeng rather than holding shares directly. From this Irene questions the argument that "DeepSeek has accepted its fate as a national champion" — if it had, the state has plenty of money to take, but it didn't take that much.

— Irene

Glossary

mythos
In the episode, a model tier that has crossed some threshold of capability, possessing real destructive power such as genuine cyberattacks.
Glass Wing
A governance path of evaluate-then-release: amputate dangerous capabilities before handing the model to developers.
Let It Rip
The status quo: models are simply released open source, and the government has no mechanism to identify a capability jump in advance.
Black Box
The state uses the strongest model internally first and only permits companies to release crippled versions — close to partial nationalization.
harness
A scaffolding program wrapped around a model to extend its capabilities.
