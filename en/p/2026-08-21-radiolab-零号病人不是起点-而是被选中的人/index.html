Patient zero is not the source, it is the scapegoat society needs

From Typhoid Mary to HIV to the high five, every so-called origin is a narrative humans need; the truth is a jigsaw, and all we want is the focal point.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:06

Disease did not come from filth, it came from healthy people

In 1906, in a wealthy household in Oyster Bay on Long Island, 6 of 11 people came down with typhoid. The sanitary engineer George Soper traced it to the cook, Mary Mallon. She had no symptoms at all, yet live typhoid bacilli were cultured from her feces and urine, making her the first documented healthy carrier in North America. The finding overturned the public health thinking of the day, which held that disease came from dirty surroundings. A person could spread disease while showing nothing at all.

14:43

Locking Mary up for life was reassurance, not disease control

Mary was forcibly isolated on North Brother Island twice. But she was not the only healthy carrier: New York had already identified several hundred of them, most of them male cooks, and some of whom went on cooking in violation of the rules. She alone was confined for life. As the episode puts it, "making the public feel safe mattered more than actually making everyone safe" — which is the essence of patient zero. Society needs a culprit it can point at, not the most scientific explanation.

20:00

The named patient zero arrived long after the epidemic did

In the 1980s, the CDC mapped the sexual contact network among 30 gay male patients. The node at the center, labeled 0, was later identified in And the Band Played On as Gaëtan Dugas, a French Canadian flight attendant, and the press cast him as a man who spread AIDS deliberately. But scientists used viral mutation rates as a molecular clock and compared the diversity of American HIV samples, and found that HIV had entered the United States long before Dugas became sexually active. He was not the starting point, and he should never have been called Patient Zero.

29:18

The real patient zero was a hunter who cut himself

By comparing two HIV-positive samples from Kinshasa, one from 1959 and one from 1960, researchers put the most recent common ancestor at around 1908. Tracing the animal host, they found that the virus closest to HIV-1 group M lives in chimpanzee populations in a small area of southeastern Cameroon bounded by three rivers. The most credible account is the cut hunter hypothesis: a hunter butchering a chimpanzee cut himself, infected blood entered the wound, and the jump across species was complete. That nameless hunter is Patient Zero in the biological sense.

34:10

The jump across species happened 12 times, only one turned catastrophic

HIV is known to have at least 12 major branches, meaning at least 12 independent cross-species transmissions took place — 8 from monkeys, 3 from chimpanzees, 1 from a gorilla. Only one of those branches, group M, produced a global pandemic, infecting 60 million people and killing 30 million. Most spillover events never established a sustained chain of transmission. Which means the source is not the scarce event. The pandemic is the thing that actually needs explaining.

45:57

The high five's origin story was invented to test the press

In 2007, National High Five Day put out a press release claiming the high five was invented by the basketball player Lamont Sleets: his father had served in Vietnam with the First Battalion, Fifth Infantry, and the family slapped hands saying "High five." When the reporter John Moe chased it down, he found the whole thing was fabricated. Founder Greg Caroldge admitted they had made the story up purely to see whether the media would run it. Once they learned about Glen Burke, they felt guilty.

50:00

Origins were never singular, we just picked the best-sounding one

The real origins of the high five are contested. Glen Burke and Dusty Baker slapped hands during the 1977 MLB playoffs; Derek Smith at the University of Louisville did it in 1980; women volleyball players from the 1960s say they were already using it; the gesture even appears in the 1955 French film Breathless. The episode ends by conceding the point: trace the origin of anything and you will find more than one. When the first cannot be established, we choose the better story — and so credit for inventing the high five stays with Glen Burke.

Glossary

healthy carrier
Someone infected with a pathogen who shows no symptoms but can still infect others.
spillover
An event in which a pathogen crosses from one species into another, often the starting point of an emerging infectious disease.
molecular clock
A method that uses the rate of genetic mutation to date when evolutionary events occurred.
cut hunter
The hypothesis that a pathogen entered humans through a wound a hunter suffered while handling game.
SIV
Simian Immunodeficiency Virus, the HIV-like virus carried by monkeys.
