A black hole is a 100%-efficient power plant; fusion only reaches one percent

Lower a brick slowly to just above the horizon and let go, and its rest mass can become work at a distance with 100% efficiency; chemical burning gets only one part in ten billion and fusion only one percent, because nuclear reactions never touch the rest mass of protons and neutrons — only gravity does.

The equivalence principle is the only clue

In electromagnetism, charge and mass have nothing to do with each other — a neutron is heavy but uncharged, an electron is light but fully charged. Gravity is not like that: the inertial mass that resists acceleration in Newton's second law and the gravitational mass that gets pulled in the law of universal gravitation are the same number. In Newton's system this is a pure coincidence, but Newton himself verified it to one part in a thousand, Einstein's era to one part in a billion, and today to one part in 10^15. Einstein treated this coincidence as the only clue: a force that can be explained as an inertial force must have inertial mass as its "charge"; conversely, only a force whose charge equals inertial mass is even eligible to be an inertial force. Electromagnetism never has that eligibility; gravity does.

The price is redefining what a straight line is

Swing a bucket in a circle and the water doesn't fall out; someone sitting in the bucket would say that's centrifugal force. The charge of an inertial force like centrifugal force must be inertial mass, because its source is just mass's tendency to travel in a straight line. From this Einstein makes the leap: gravity itself is a kind of inertial force. The price is enormous — you must accept that the parabola of free fall is the straight line, and that you, sitting still, are not. This is no more absurd than a flight map on which San Francisco to London has to curve over Greenland: draw something curved as if it were flat and you will inevitably get wrong which line is straight. So matter curves spacetime, curved spacetime defines what a straight line is, and treating curved spacetime as if it were flat makes you see a fictitious force called gravity.

The infinite-energy paradox forces out black holes

Lower a brick of mass m slowly on a pulley from far away to radius r and release it, and the fraction of its rest mass energy you can extract is GM/(c²r). Lowered to Earth's surface it's only 7×10^-10; to the Sun's surface, 2×10^-6; to a white dwarf like Sirius B, larger still. But the formula has no upper bound: once r is less than GM/c², the fraction exceeds 1 — you extract more energy than the brick's rest mass energy, and using that energy to make another brick and lower it again gives you a machine that creates energy from nothing. Adam says explicitly that this is a purely Newtonian calculation and only suggestive, but it shows that something must break down near that radius.

— Adam Brown
45:54

Gravity's resolution is to get stronger, not weaker

In electromagnetism the analogous paradox is resolved by the force getting weaker: two charges brought too close smear out quantum-mechanically and stop attracting so hard. General relativity goes the other way — the acceleration needed to stay at rest carries a factor of 1/√(1-2GM/c²r), which diverges at 2GM/c², and the brick is torn straight out of your hand. The same square root also gives time dilation and redshift, and gives the exact extraction fraction 1-√(1-2GM/c²r): lowered to just above the horizon it is exactly 1, no more and no less, 100%. So a black hole is a power plant at the efficiency limit: chemical burning 10^-10, fission 10^-3, fusion 10^-2, because neither fission nor fusion changes the total number of nucleons — 99% of the energy is locked in the rest mass of protons and neutrons.

Doomed, but not yet dead

I watch you fall toward the black hole from far away: first faster and faster, then slower and slower, your clock running ever slower, your light ever redder, and after a last photon you fade to black — I never see you cross the horizon. Early physicists therefore thought something violent must happen at the moment of crossing; that is wrong. You cross with nothing unusual happening to you; tidal forces would hurt for a solar-mass black hole, but the bigger the black hole the gentler, and at galactic-mass scale you wouldn't notice at all. Once you cross the horizon you are doomed, but not dead — only on hitting the singularity are you spaghettified by tidal forces. The horizon is not something locally measurable; it is a teleological fact: inside a sufficiently large black hole you can live out a whole life and leave several generations of descendants.

Three kinds of evidence for black holes

For half a century after the Schwarzschild solution was written down, people thought it was a mathematical monster that required finely tuned initial conditions, and Einstein himself wrote mistaken things like objects bouncing back from the horizon. The theoretical reversal was Penrose, and later Hawking and Penrose, proving that black hole formation is a generic property of general relativity, not a finely tuned artifact. Experimentally there are three lines: decades of stellar orbits around Sagittarius A* at the galactic center, which imply something extremely heavy, extremely dark and extremely compact; LIGO, which picked up spacetime jitters right after turning on in late 2015, with multiple sites shaking in exactly the same way, ruling out trucks and earthquakes; and the Event Horizon Telescope, using a global radio array to see the radiation of infalling matter. Seen, felt, and calculated from orbits.

Two failed eclipses actually saved him

Mercury's orbit came out right, but that was a known answer and only weakly persuasive; what really made general relativity consensus was the bending of light. Plug the speed of light into Newtonian mechanics and you also get a deflection value; general relativity gives twice that. The 1911 eclipse expedition to Argentina was clouded out; the next was a German team funded by Krupp to Crimea, and war broke out before the eclipse, with the whole team interned until the war ended. These two failures were good for Einstein: his early prediction based on the equivalence principle was wrong and happened to equal the Newtonian value, and it was precisely in the wartime window when no one was observing that he changed it to twice. In 1919 Eddington's British expedition reported exactly twice — a British experiment confirming a German theory, which also became part of postwar reconciliation.

— Adam Brown
1:27:41

What string theory bets on is uniqueness

Dwarkesh's provocation is: the most beautiful theory barely needs experiment, so why spend tens of billions building machines. Adam's answer is that general relativity is an extreme case — you need only that the speed of light is finite, plus the symmetry that protects it, plus the empirical fact of the equivalence principle, and after that the available options are limited: it is feasible to have a large number of models lay out the whole tree, with each "Einstein" taking one set of options (say, giving up simultaneity). But the branching ratio differs by field. String theory bets entirely on this path: it believes there is only one consistent quantum theory of gravity, reachable by consistency checks alone, because seeing it would require a galaxy-scale collider. Condensed matter physics, by contrast, often has no choice but to do experiments.

Glossary

equivalence principle
Inertial mass and gravitational mass are exactly equal — the reason a feather and a brick land at the same time.
fictitious / inertial force
A force that arises from the reference frame, whose charge must be inertial mass, such as centrifugal force.
Schwarzschild radius / event horizon
2GM/c²; past it, no matter how much thrust you have, you must fall toward the singularity.
gravitational time dilation
Clocks deeper in a potential well run slower, independent of relative motion; GPS must subtract it out.
spaghettification
Tidal forces stretch and shred an object radially; fatal only near the singularity.
