US Economic Resilience Comes From Consumers Borrowing From the Future

Barkin breaks down why consumption stays strong despite high prices: consumers cut savings, fall behind on bills, and defer loan payments to keep spending; AI investment so far is more price hikes and crowding out than productivity dividend; the Fed may need to lean against the wind for a long time.

3:05

You can't dig forward guidance out of a man who doesn't give forward guidance

Asked how he viewed Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks, Barkin said he listened carefully to the speech but explicitly refused to derive September FOMC action from it. "He personally doesn't like forward guidance, so you shouldn't look for forward guidance from a man who doesn't give forward guidance." Barkin stressed that you can have a good discussion about the economy, but if the choice is not to do forward guidance, then you don't do it. The line was effectively also a response to the market's tendency to always try to extract a rate path from Fed officials' remarks. He described that impulse as "we can't help but want to know," but said it should be restrained.

— Tom Barkin
4:06

The consumer's money is borrowed from the future

Barkin's observation: unlike in the Great Recession, during the pandemic people accumulated a lot of cash, stocks and housing wealth, and formed a mindset of "I insist on spending." The key is that low-income consumers are also "creatively finding money": shifting to Walmart and dollar stores, cutting insurance, moving kids back home, not paying the gas bill in summer, stretching auto loans to 60 days rather than 120 days past due — in essence "borrowing from the future." His conclusion: as long as employment and asset markets are healthy, this spending sustained by lower savings and delinquent bills will continue. This micro mechanism explains the economy's "resilience" better than macro data does.

— Tom Barkin
6:08

Data centers pushed ordinary buildings out of the construction cycle

In one week early in the year, $700 billion of investment was announced, and transformers, switchgear and electricians were all in short supply. Barkin believes the construction cycle is shifting from offices and multifamily housing toward data centers and industrial, and that multifamily housing "doesn't pencil out" can't be blamed on interest rates alone, because in 2004-05 rates were equally high and buildings were still going up in large numbers. He acknowledges that data center construction has indeed pushed up the cost of other construction, creating a "chicken-and-egg" crowding-out effect, but says it's hard to pin down exactly how much. The answer is a restrained display of the real disagreement inside the institution over the spillover effects of AI capex.

— Tom Barkin
8:10

AI is hitting hiring first, not output

The AI question Barkin hears at every chamber of commerce and town hall is political: jobs, water, data centers. Economically, AI's productivity shock is still concentrated in a few replaceable use cases like call centers, coding and clerical work; current productivity improvement comes more from the automation and process reengineering driven by the 2022 labor shortage. On the hiring side, employers are already using the logic of "can we let AI do it first, then decide whether to hire" to hold down hiring. Enthusiasm for AI is asymmetric between firms and workers: bosses are keen, employees are lukewarm. In other words, AI is currently affecting the economy by reducing labor demand rather than by raising output.

— Tom Barkin
16:17

Choosing which inflation narrative is choosing which rate path

Barkin sums up today's inflation into two readings. One is "65 months without hitting target, don't give excuses," corresponding to Warsh's hawkish stance: inflation has been above target for a long time and rates may not be tight enough. The other is "47 months up plus 18 months down": post-pandemic inflation appeared, and after rate hikes it had fallen back to 2.3%-2.4% by March 2025, and it was the later AI, tariff and oil price shocks that pushed it back up. He explicitly says the second is "entirely defensible," and that "we will push it back down again later." Which narrative prevails determines whether the Fed keeps adding tightening or waits for the shocks to fade.

— Tom Barkin
17:20

Tariffs are now stimulus, because the refunds have landed

The tariff topic is quieter than half a year ago, not because trade no longer matters, but because refunds landed after the Supreme Court ruling. Barkin says that over the past three or four months firms received refunds rather than paying extra tariffs. Steel and aluminum producers benefited by treating tariffs as a "price umbrella"; foreign manufacturers that ship European components to the US for assembly were instead hurt by the tariffs. Refunds are "very positive" for corporate profits, and firms will use them for marketing, store renovations and keeping people employed, so there is a stimulus effect, but the pass-through to prices is "very targeted," not systemic. That explains why people aren't complaining much about tariffs right now.

— Tom Barkin
21:25

The tax base data centers provide doesn't buy political acceptance

Local residents dislike data centers because, unlike factories, they don't come with workers' kids playing ball with local kids, and the tax base benefits touted by economic developers lack political resonance. Barkin half-jokingly says you should name an elementary school after Microsoft or Google to win hearts and minds. He admits he doesn't know how large the data center buildout will be, and that the AI footprint, data center footprint and energy demand could all be substantially underestimated or overestimated. If political backlash leads to underbuilding, it will drag on growth; if there is overbuilding, it will worsen inflation. This is an uncertainty not yet incorporated into the policy framework.

— Tom Barkin
24:30

AI could be inflationary or deflationary, so don't bet in advance

On whether AI brings a productivity boom and lifts the neutral rate R-star, Barkin is cautious. He says the AI path one, two, three years out has an "extremely wide range," and could show up as either inflation or deflation, with 18 versions in between. Until he has enough confidence, policy cannot be set on assumptions. He also responds to Warsh's view: different AI models point to completely opposite rate directions. So he hints the Fed should wait for data rather than putting AI productivity into the dot plot in advance. That statement is a warning to those betting on a higher R-star.

— Tom Barkin

Glossary

SEP / Summary of Economic Projections
The Fed's quarterly economic and rate forecast document, of which the dot plot is a part.
dot plot
A scatter chart of Fed officials' anonymous forecasts for the future rate path, easily misread by markets as a commitment.
R-star / neutral rate
The nominal rate level that keeps the economy neither too hot nor too cold; rising AI productivity could push it higher.
forward guidance
A central bank's steering of markets about the future policy path.
private label
Retailer-owned brand goods, usually cheaper, whose sales grow when consumers trade down.
