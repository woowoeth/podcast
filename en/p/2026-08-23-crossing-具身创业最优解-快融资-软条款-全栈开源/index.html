A 22-year-old CEO, five rounds, over $100M: the first round's terms decide life or death

In a year and a half, 22-year-old Huang Yi built RoboParty to nearly 150 people: five rounds and over $100M raised, RPO selling about 100 units a month. His judgments: the first round's terms decide life or death, open source is a moat that lowers the friction of collaboration, and the body is the embodied entry point.

What you should fear isn't a board seat, it's personal joint liability

You want to take more chips in the Seed Round, but what matters more is the terms of the first round's agreement — they will basically carry over verbatim to the Pre-IPO. Huang Yi stresses that the company to date has no buyback and no VAM, and that this rests on the first round having been negotiated friendly enough. He is not afraid to give up board seats: as long as the shareholder is genuinely helpful (Xiaomi, for instance), giving a seat actually makes collaboration smoother. What really needs guarding against are hidden pits like personal joint liability and buybacks at simple or compound interest, so you must find a lawyer who has negotiated a large number of investment terms.

— Huang Yi
7:07

You don't need to dodge the bubble; it's the industry's momentum

Huang Yi openly admits ‘of course there's a bubble’, but he sees the bubble as the industry's momentum: suppliers take a second look because you're in the embodied track, and talent floods in from other industries. Shunwei taught him to go with the tide — no company can change an era, you can only ride the heat to build the company, and the counterexamples are solar and real estate when nobody would look at them. He also explains where the bubble's money comes from: the secondary market has money, an extra bag of money becomes an LP entering a GP, then becomes ammunition for startups, and the sentiment cycle is the fundraising window.

— Huang Yi
12:14

Take as much as you can, but not all money should be taken

RoboParty raises a round every one or two months, which looks crazy, but behind it are clear milestones: the RP1 launch plus mass production is the Series A standard, and a commercial closed loop is the Series B standard. Huang Yi's logic is that in an industry upcycle you take as much as you can, because missing the window may mean waiting another year. But he also stresses that not all money should be taken: USD institutions value technological leadership, RMB cares more about commercialization, and the later the stage, the lower the professionalism of the investors you meet.

— Huang Yi
21:22

What's valuable isn't the body, it's the empty layer between model and body

Asked ‘how do you make money if you open-source the whole stack’, Huang Yi gives two concepts: the threshold is how much fixed assets and manpower you put in, while the moat is where you make money. He positions RoboParty in the missing layer between model companies and body makers with commercial products — model companies have no body, existing body makers won't open up the low level, and the motor layer, URDF snapshots and data-collection interfaces are all absent. Open source lowers the friction of collaboration and expands the brand; body sales are the base, but the user compounding is bigger: a CMU professor buying a body is equivalent to bringing in two PhD engineers at million-yuan salaries who keep publishing papers.

— Huang Yi
37:38

The hive suits only the Lab; manufacturing must have process

A company of nearly 150 people uses a ‘hive structure’: each person has at most 6 effective connection surfaces, driven by talent density rather than hierarchy, an idea inspired by Anthropic. But Huang Yi states plainly that the hive suits only the Lab — if the whole-machine department also ran on talent-driven logic, you'd get chaos where ‘nobody even knows who to buy parts from’. So the company's approach is to split off a pure Lab running on hive logic, while the manufacturing department must have process, and the knowhow on both sides is absorbed from each other through a set of communication-style meeting rituals.

— Huang Yi
46:43

Embodied gross margin is twice an automaker's; the giants will definitely enter

Huang Yi gives a read on the landscape: there are about 300 embodied companies now, and by 2030 maybe only 10-15 will make it to the next round. He uses gross margin as a ruler: embodied is about 40%, automakers only 20%, so the next wave of players will be companies with hundreds of billions in scale like Wei Xiaoli, Honor and Geely, and only when BYD, Huawei, CATL, BAT and Xiaomi finally enter does the ‘big era’ truly begin. He is also bullish on model companies like OpenAI and Zhipu, believing that once they fill in robotics cognition, their scaling capability far exceeds that of startups.

— Huang Yi
55:45

China doesn't reward explorers, only those who make it

Asked which embodied companies he most admires in China and the US, Huang Yi names Tesla, Figure, and companies like Song Shuran's team Generalyst — they dare to explore paradigms nobody has done, and in the US they can raise the most money. But he says outright that Chinese culture doesn't reward explorers, it rewards people who ‘can build the company up’. He actively chooses the latter path, and gives an extremely concrete success criterion: his parents, without knowing he made this robot, would be willing to buy one and take it home.

— Huang Yi
1:01:51

The body is halfway; the brain doesn't even know where the finish line is

Using a marathon as a metaphor for embodied progress, Huang Yi splits it into segments: the body is already at one-half, with the rest being iterative convergence; the cerebellum (whole-body coordination) is also about half, and behavior foundation models can already do interactions like ‘carry something and return to position’; the brain he is most candid about — ‘I don't know how much’, because nobody has seen the endgame. He thinks the endgame is ‘an agent that has sedimented all of history and reacts exactly as you would’, and by that definition today it has run only one or two kilometers. This is the only place in the whole conversation where he admits a gap in his understanding.

— Huang Yi

Glossary

missing layer
The open platform layer lacking between model companies and body makers, able to reach down into the low level such as motors and URDF.
deep pocket
An institutional investor with abundant capital that can keep betting in subsequent rounds.
BFM / Behavior Foundation Model
A behavior foundation model in the cerebellum direction, used for whole-body motion generation and object interaction.
sim2real
The technical path of transferring policies trained in simulation to real robots.
hive structure
An organizational form Huang Yi invented: each person manages at most about 6 people, driven by talent density rather than hierarchy.
