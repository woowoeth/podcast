DNA viruses' weapon unexpectedly targets RNA sensors, hitting two immune pathways at once

Adenovirus protein 7—itself a DNA-binding protein—suppresses RNA sensor RIG-I by hijacking TRIM25, and probably blocks DNA sensing too.

Hosts and viruses maintain perpetual immune competition

Cells contain multiple proteins recognizing viral components like nucleic acids; when bound, they trigger innate immune responses including inflammation, fever, and muscle aches. This reflects long evolutionary arms competition: hosts evolve detectors to intercept viruses, viruses evolve evasion mechanisms, repeating endlessly. RIG-I is one such sensor specialized in recognizing viral RNA, especially RNA with structures different from human RNA.

— Briana
15:39

RNA immunity suppression is essential for DNA viral evasion

The paper title saying adenovirus protein 7 inhibits RNA sensor RIG-I initially surprised Vincent—adenovirus is a DNA virus, so why attack the RNA detection pathway? He expected DNA viruses' obstacles to be DNA sensing mechanisms. After careful reading, he realized this is actually the virus's clever innate immunity evasion strategy; nearly all surviving viruses must have similar antagonistic mechanisms, otherwise they'd be cleared by innate immunity during early infection.

— Vincent
24:10

RNA sensing is the real target, DNA sensing is not

The research team initially stimulated DNA sensing with the small molecule 2'-3'-cGAMP, finding protein 7 reduced interferon levels, seemingly confirming DNA pathway suppression. But when they repeated the experiment using cell lines completely lacking DNA sensing capability, interferon suppression persisted, showing this effect was actually unrelated to DNA sensing. Switching to RNA stimulants like poly IC, they confirmed the truly suppressed pathway was RNA sensing.

— Briana
34:38

Protein 7 works through TRIM25, not against RIG-I directly

The team found protein 7 blocks critical K63 ubiquitination of RIG-I, but protein 7 doesn't directly bind RIG-I; instead it binds TRIM25, the E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for ubiquitinating RIG-I. When TRIM25 was deleted, protein 7's suppression effect vanished, confirming this indirect mechanism. Crucially, TRIM25 ubiquitinates not just RIG-I but other proteins (including NoNo, involved in DNA sensing), meaning protein 7's hijacking of TRIM25 is a one-arrow-two-targets scenario—suppressing both RNA and DNA sensing pathways.

— Briana
38:41

Proteins confined to the nucleus still block cytoplasmic immunity

Protein 7 is predominantly nuclear, while TRIM25 and RIG-I are cytoplasmic; theoretically they should never meet. The research team used a clever trick: they engineered protein 7's amino acid sequence to retain more protein 7 in the cytoplasm. They found that the more cytoplasmic protein 7, the more frequently it bound TRIM25, and the stronger the interferon suppression—proving even small amounts of mislocalized protein can exert important immune evasion functions.

— Briana
43:49

This evasion mechanism appears across every adenovirus lineage tested

The team tested multiple adenovirus serotypes and human clinical isolates, finding protein 7's RNA sensing inhibition function was universal, not specific to one strain. The two hosts noted this connects to adenoviruses being used as gene therapy and vaccine vectors (Johnson & Johnson, Oxford-AstraZeneca, and Russia's Sputnik vaccine); whether removing this immune suppression function could yield better vaccine vectors remains unknown but is considered a worthwhile next research direction.

— Briana
1:04:29

DNA viruses encode vast genomes, RNA viruses cannot

DNA virus genomes can exceed 2 million base pairs; DNA replication has proofreading, low mutation rates, allowing more genetic cargo—more proteins, more antagonistic factors, broader tissue tropism. RNA viruses (coronaviruses excepted) lack proofreading, max genome size approximately 140,000 base pairs, but mutate faster and jump species more readily. Historically, nearly all pandemic viruses are RNA viruses; DNA virus pandemics require ancient cases like smallpox.

— Vincent
1:10:39

Major findings often hide as dismissed experimental artifacts

Vincent shared a doctoral experience: analyzing influenza-infected cell RNA electrophoresis gels, viral RNA was always at the top, the bottom always had small fragments everyone thought were degradation products and ignored. Later they learned those bottom-fragment small RNAs (small interfering RNAs) were actually the biologically meaningful part. Many major scientific discoveries were initially missed by being treated as ‘garbage.’

— Vincent
