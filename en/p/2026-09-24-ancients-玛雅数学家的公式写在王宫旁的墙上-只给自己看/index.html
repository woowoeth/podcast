A Maya mathematician wrote his formulas on a wall next to the palace, for his own eyes only

In 2010 at Xultun in Guatemala, a small room was uncovered by accident, its walls covered in calendrical calculations and signed Saktanwash — not a monumental inscription carved for the public, but a Maya mathematician's private notes, kept for his own reference.

Deciphering Maya writing took nearly a century, not one flash of insight

Cracking Maya script was not a single Rosetta Stone-style eureka moment. Individual signs were recognized as early as the 19th century, and words or phrases were deciphered nearly a hundred years ago, but genuine understanding of how the writing system works only arrived in the 1980s. Stuart describes it as a hundred years of small breakthroughs accumulated by many people — a sign identified here, a substitution pattern spotted there — until systematic understanding finally pulled it all together.

— David Stuart
7:11

Scribes loved to show off, so Maya writing was never standardized

Unlike Egyptian hieroglyphs, which stayed conservative and legible over thousands of years, Maya scribes actively sought variation. The same king's name appears again and again in the inscriptions, and it is written differently every time, because the scribe was showing off his own inventiveness, picking different sign variants to spell the same syllable. That deliberate artistry and arbitrariness is one reason decipherment was so unusually hard.

— David Stuart
10:22

Maya script was a court language reserved for the elite

The "Classic Mayan" of the inscriptions functions more like a courtly lingua franca across city-states than like ordinary people's everyday speech — roughly five million people still speak Mayan languages today, but the inscriptions correspond to a specific, carefully trained official written language. Whichever Maya city-state you were in, if you were a noble or a priest you were trained in this same language and script, which is why inscriptions from different cities can be read against one another.

— David Stuart
12:24

What survives is epic; what is missing is the ledgers

Maya writing records almost nothing but ritual, dynastic history, and wartime alliances — no economic records survive, no tribute lists, no merchants' market dealings. The host draws a contrast with Mycenaean Linear B: there, scholars have a mass of dull bureaucratic records but can barely name a single verifiable king, and they envy the Maya their epic narrative; Maya scholars, meanwhile, lack exactly the everyday accounts the other side dreams of.

— David Stuart
19:46

Tikal and Calakmul: the Maya world's two superpowers

The Classic period Maya world (roughly AD 200 to 900) consisted of many city-states, and by the 6th and 7th centuries Tikal (the Mutul dynasty) and Calakmul (the Kanul dynasty) had risen as the two centers of power, often called the superpowers of Maya politics. The two were not far apart, yet they were mortal enemies for centuries; inscriptions record repeated alliance maneuvering and multiple wars between them, with victory changing hands and neither able to crush the other outright.

— David Stuart
26:01

A noblewoman whose family defected is remembered better than her husband

Maya royal women were often used as marriage-alliance currency, but they were never mere accessories — many queens were powerful figures who led armies themselves. Stuart tells one specific case: in the 7th century a member of the Mutul royal house defected for some reason to the mortal enemy Kanul, and his daughter was later used by the Kanul royal house to weave further alliances, becoming the mother of a powerful ruler at Naranjo. She appears repeatedly in inscriptions and stelae at multiple sites, while her husband's identity was only confirmed a few years ago — because the inscriptions barely mention him.

— David Stuart
33:31

Gaps in the dynastic lines are the fingerprints of political upheaval

Maya dynasties typically ran about 400 years, but the inscriptions often show gaps in the succession of 50 to 100 years, usually corresponding to a conquest or an interruption of rule. The wars between Mutul and Kanul were exactly this kind of back-and-forth: Kanul wins this round, Mutul turns the tables the next. Toward the end of the Classic period, in the decades before the so-called great collapse, the conflict escalated markedly, to the point of records of kings being driven out and fleeing from place to place.

— David Stuart
42:06

The private notes beside the palace: formulas written for the author himself

In 2010 at the site of Xultun (舒尔顿), a looters' trench accidentally exposed a small painted chamber, its walls densely covered in thumbnail-sized miniature inscriptions. The team spent years on digital scanning and restoration, and the latest paper confirms that one passage is a mathematical formula integrating the calendrical cycles of the solar year, Venus, Mars and others, signed at the end "Chehen Saktanwash" (thus says Saktanwash). The room sits right next to the palace and looks more like a Maya mathematician's own whiteboard than anything written for the public.

— David Stuart
