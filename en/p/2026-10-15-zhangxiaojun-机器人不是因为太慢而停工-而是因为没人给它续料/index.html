Robots don't stall because they're slow—they stall because no one refills them

Dyna's napkin-folding robot achieved 99%-plus success rates with paying customers, but the company halted scaling—completing a task isn't managing a workflow, and robots need constant human resupply like a babysitter.

1:01

Founder called herself a cockroach to survive a death spiral

Kaber, Linden's previous venture, built autonomous shopping carts. At Y Combinator graduation, she ranked as the cohort's weakest fundraiser with only $300,000 raised. She and three cofounders then moved into shared housing on zero salary, spending just $7,000 monthly for two and a half years. Pitching over 1,000 angel investors yielded only two commitments, each for roughly $50,000. She sustained the team with a survival mantra: ‘我们是蟑螂, 我们死不了’ (we are cockroaches, we cannot die). This lean period eventually led to a $2 million seed round.

— Linden
40:37

Team split over research vs. commercialization—workflow became the bridge

Last year-end, Dyna's team fractured over a strategic identity crisis: some members pushed for commercialization and revenue, while others advocated continuing pure research. Rather than choosing sides, Linden extended the timeline to find a path that could do both. The solution was reframing the product around ‘workflow’—unifying all steps from resupply through transport, folding, and storage. This goal simultaneously forced capability improvements and created a genuine business closure.

— Linden
58:57

Ninety-nine percent success isn't enough: robots need a babysitter that doesn't exist

Dyna One, the napkin-folding robot, achieved over 99% success rates and secured more than 10 paying customers. Yet Linden chose not to scale. The core issue: after folding 3-to-4 buckets of napkins—roughly 30 to 60 minutes—the robot stops because it has no fresh material to process. Staff members must set phone alarms to manually refill it. This dependency Linden defines as the fundamental, unsolved product problem—the robot needs a ‘机器人保姆’ (robot babysitter). Scaling would only multiply this defect across all deployed units.

— Linden
1:04:00

Product roadmap upgraded from task completion to entire workflow management

Dyna redefined its product goal from completing a single task to managing an entire workflow. A complete cycle requires the robot to: locate material stock, retrieve supplies, transport them to the work station, complete folding, place finished goods on the designated shelf, retrieve the empty container, and begin again. Each step carries its own success rate; the combined chain is exponentially harder. Linden set a hard metric: one robot's 24-hour output must match one person's 8-hour output. The team has already achieved and surpassed this target.

— Linden
1:28:38

Egocentric data revealed Scaling Laws the industry thought didn't exist

The industry long believed that scarce high-quality data was the ceiling for robot intelligence, so Dyna initially pursued that path. But while developing the workflow, the team shifted focus to egocentric—first-person—data from human operators. Using roughly 1 million hours of pretraining footage, they discovered remarkably strong Scaling Laws. This suggested robot data ceilings are far higher than previously assumed. Linden challenges the field's consensus that high-quality data should come first, arguing it's not a first-principles position.

— Linden
1:40:55

Model leaps will obsolete some optimizations—but not if you stay maximally general

When asked whether a leap in model capabilities might render Dyna's scene-specific fine-tuning obsolete—as happened with many Agent applications this year—Linden acknowledged yes, it could. But she views this as compatible with her approach. She points to Anthropic's experience: after the Coding Agent's breakthrough, the same training framework scaled directly to Excel and other domains without rework. Dyna maintains maximum generality in pretraining, with workflow-specific tuning only in the fine-tune stage. This means scene-specific experience survives model leaps and can be reapplied.

— Linden
2:00:12

The brain sets whether it's possible; the body sets how good it gets

From day one, Linden insisted on building a 40-to-50-person hardware and supply-chain team in China—reasoning that embodied AI cannot produce world-class robots without supply chains. Her calculus inverts common thinking: the brain (AI) sets the lower bound—what's theoretically possible; the body (hardware and supply chains) sets the upper bound—how good the robot actually becomes. If the field moves toward a cost-and-quality competition within a decade, supply chains become more critical than AI capabilities. Dyna's China team is already operating at 100-unit production scale.

— Linden
2:37:51

Dropping the perfectionist burden revealed the path she'd been too anxious to see

During last year's strategic confusion, Linden discovered her deepest constraint: the burden of ‘不能犯错’ (cannot make mistakes). Because she had already sold one company, she felt pressure to prove the success wasn't luck, and that burden pushed her toward non-first-principles decisions. Once she set it down and clarified direction, her first strong emotion was gratitude—for the cofounders, team, and family who had carried her through. She frames entrepreneurship as a marathon with no visible finish line, where 修心 (cultivating the heart-mind) is what sustains you.

— Linden

Glossary

product research
Guiding research choices by whether they form a complete product cycle, rather than pursuing research without limits.
egocentric data
First-person video of human operations, used to train robots to replicate human movements and actions.
Scaling Law
The predictable relationship where model capability scales proportionally with increases in data or compute.
