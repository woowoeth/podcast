AI's Frontrunners Are Just Refineries, Not the Native-App Winners

AI has entered its agent phase: the first-stage leaders are just refineries, not the native-app winners; new platforms don't come from old platforms; organizations must shift from jobs to tasks; and in the end what people compete on is the creativity to conjure something from nothing.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

16:28

Building agents today is like building websites in 1992

Zeng Ming divides the industrialization of a general-purpose technology into three stages: first it becomes social infrastructure, then applications explode, and finally native applications appear. In 2026, token as a unit of measure reaching consensus shows the first stage has matured; the agent events around Chinese New Year (known colloquially as "lobster") mark AI's formal entry into the agent stage. He draws the analogy to the 1992 website-building movement: back then people built websites to share information, today they build agents to share capabilities. The browser hasn't appeared yet, and whoever can establish the agent standard and seize the user entry point may be the biggest opportunity of the next two years.

— Zeng Ming
27:49

A ten-trillion-dollar company will appear, but not these two

Zeng Ming says plainly that OpenAI and Anthropic are remarkable technological and commercial achievements, but business history shows the first wave of breakout companies is most likely not the big winners of the native-application stage. Yahoo reached a $120 billion market cap five years after founding, AOL was valued at $220 billion, and neither became the Google of the next era. Model companies are more like AI cloud companies, refineries that refine the base oil — the real application value belongs to chemical plants and car companies, and cars were not built by oil companies. He believes a ten-trillion-dollar company will certainly emerge, but not necessarily the two currently leading. Model companies will evolve into a mature business of oligopoly plus heavy government regulation.

— Zeng Ming
1:10:29

The unit of an AI-native organization is the task, not the job

The company corresponds to the hierarchical system of the industrial age; AI overturns the industrial age, and the corporate system will wither. The basic unit of an AI-native organization is not the job but the task: people are not organized by reporting lines but flow around "what needs to be solved," with humans and AI coordinating around tasks. So well-designed jobs and hierarchies will disappear, and the traditional middle layer will die out too; future founders must first define the organization's task network. Silicon Valley's NewLab is a prototype of the new organizational form, and OpenAI itself is more like a Lab than a traditional company. The culture of the new organization is more like a sports team, emphasizing transparency, sharing, and co-creation, no longer relying on commands and performance reviews.

— Zeng Ming
1:31:32

Excellence is only judged in hindsight; being good is visible on the spot

Zeng Ming argues that "good ≠ great." Being good is growth that keeps getting positive feedback, while greatness requires overcoming negative feedback again and again, persisting when everyone disapproves, and finally proving through enormous success that you were the era's prophet — which is why "greatness is judged in hindsight." Good people easily fall into a loop of proving themselves, which makes their strategies conservative. Great people have a small ego, tend to attribute success to the times and their partners, and are driven by mission rather than external valuation. When identifying founders he asks, "Ten years from now, what kind of self would you be satisfied with?" If the answer is "build a ten-billion-dollar company," that's mainly ego-driven, not an inner mission. Truly great founders tend to be altruistic and empathetic.

— Zeng Ming
1:47:24

Strategy in the AI era can't be planned, only generated

Strategy in the AI era is not planning but generation. Zeng Ming proposes a "strategy generation system": the organization must build an environment and network in which strategic insight emerges naturally. Because decision frequency is higher and quality demands are higher, a CEO cannot plan by linear extrapolation, nor can strategy be outsourced to McKinsey — McKinsey's explosion was a product of the slow maturation of the industrial age. His methodology is "look ten years out, think three years out, act one year out," with the core being "think three years out": you must at least see two or three milestones ahead, creating tension between short-term action and the mid-term picture. Organization-building must ensure a large enough context rather than control, letting the right people make the right decisions at the right time.

— Zeng Ming
2:01:39

The right analogy for robots is appliances, not cars

Zeng Ming believes robots are still in a strategic exploration phase, far from convergence. Of the current two paths — build a generalized brain first or close the scenario loop first — both are logically valid, and it's impossible to judge in advance which is faster. By historical analogy, the auto industry had thousands of companies from 1900 to 1920, and only entered scale when Ford built the assembly line in 1913; robots haven't yet reached their Model T moment, and whoever actually sells ten thousand robots first is already remarkable. But the better analogy for robots is appliances rather than cars: after electricity was invented came countless categories — refrigerators, washing machines, air conditioners — and robots interacting in the physical world will have N kinds of big scenarios, with home, companionship, and industrial all potentially producing their own native giants.

— Zeng Ming
2:03:39

The incumbents who feel safe are the least safe

Zeng Ming says that in the face of a giant wave no one is safe, and feeling safe is the most unsafe thing of all. In business history, no era's companies have ever transitioned smoothly into the next era; IBM and Microsoft are the only two possible exceptions, and both are full of uncertainty. AI is a productivity revolution that overturns the industrial revolution, not a continuation-style innovation like mobile internet, so old giants' technology reserves and organizational culture are negative assets. Google can live on as an AI cloud company but may not capture the consumer entry point; ByteDance has an AI cloud opportunity, but Doubao is not the future application; Tencent's social relationships will be restructured; Alibaba is likewise not naturally safe. The new platform will most likely be created by a new company.

— Zeng Ming
2:20:56

Human value lies in conjuring from nothing, not in writing songs or painting

Borrowing Drucker's framework, Zeng Ming defines the AI era as the era of creativity: all knowledge work that can be structured will be taken over by AI, and people are on one hand forced and on the other finally liberated to develop new potential. That potential is called creativity — not writing songs or painting, but the ability to originally define complex problems and conjure something from nothing. AI has already taken away all existing knowledge, and human value lies in creating what does not yet exist. That's why he calls his own mission "to be a happy researcher," and believes civilization's next step will reorganize around creativity, with even the education system forced to shift from "knowledge instillation" to "open exploration," because young people already know the old path leads nowhere.

— Zeng Ming

Glossary

Token factory
Treating AI compute as infrastructure for producing tokens, symbolizing intelligence's entry into a stage of standardized pricing.
Agentic OS
An OS that can directly orchestrate all agents to complete tasks by intent; Zeng Ming considers it a third-stage thing, currently not deployable.
New Lab
A new organizational form for people dissatisfied with the corporate system, centered on researchers; OpenAI is seen as its earliest example.
High agency
A Silicon Valley buzzword meaning taking charge of one's own fate and actively seeking tasks and growth, replacing hierarchy- and command-driven work.
Context not control
A management philosophy: give ample shared context and information so members can make the right decisions on their own.
