3:07

Chronotype is biological, not a choice of willpower

Angela and Stephen discuss the concept of chronotype — whether a person naturally becomes alert in the morning or the evening. Angela calls herself a lark, often waking before the alarm goes off. Stephen stresses that the 2017 Nobel Prize in Medicine went to circadian rhythm research, which shows this is biological — even plants have biological clocks, because the Earth rotates on its axis, there is day and night, and organisms must predict and adapt to that rotation. So the answer to "why don't you just become a morning person" is that this trait is extremely hardwired.

— Stephen Dubner
8:12

Teenagers sleeping late has biological evidence, it isn't laziness

Angela cites a 2017 PLOS ONE paper that used 12 years of diary data from the American Time Use Survey and found that chronotype is near-normally distributed both overall and within every age group, with the mean shifting systematically with age: it moves later after puberty, peaks at its latest around age 19, then moves earlier. In other words, there is evidence behind teenagers' bodies tending toward late sleep. The paper also found that before age 40 men are usually later chronotypes than women, and after 40 it reverses. This supports the recommendation to push back school start times.

— Angela Duckworth
9:17

Later school start times have measurable benefits

In 2017 the Seattle school district pushed middle and high school start times from 7:50 to 8:45, nearly an hour later. Researchers from the University of Washington and the Salk Institute used this to measure sleep-wake cycles and found students' median sleep duration increased by 34 minutes, with measurable benefits. Based on these studies, the RAND think tank estimated that delaying middle and high school start times alone would generate $8.6 billion in economic productivity gains. This shows sleep schedule isn't an individual problem but a system design problem.

— Angela Duckworth
11:23

Early risers earn more, but it's only a correlation

Danish economist Jens Banke published a 2012 paper, "Do Morning-type People Earn More Than Evening-type People? How Chronotypes Influence Income," whose analysis showed early risers earn significantly more than late sleepers, and that being a morning type especially benefits men. But Stephen stresses this is correlational data and cannot be called causal. Being an early riser correlates not only with income but also with happiness, productivity and health. So you can't distinguish: whether early risers are simply more productive people to begin with (a spurious correlation), or whether the social structure favors early risers.

— Stephen Dubner
13:26

COVID was a forced experiment, and people generally pushed back their wake times

Stephen says one thing learned from COVID is that many people are able to and prefer to work in very different ways. Angela adds that a big silver lining of COVID was a forced experiment, and research showed people really did change their sleep patterns: when they didn't have to be in the office at 9 and had more flexibility, people generally shifted their wake times later, more like weekends than weekdays. This proves the rigid nine-to-five system isn't unchangeable — it's just that before 2020 no one was forced to test it.

— Angela Duckworth
20:00

The email signature solves an empathy problem, not an efficiency problem

Stephen says he's an early riser and often sends work emails at 5 a.m.; he thinks email doesn't demand an instant reply, so it's fine. But some people who get an early-morning email think, "He sent it at 5, I only woke at 7, I have to reply right away." So Angela added a line to the end of every email: "my work hours may not be your work hours, reply if you want, when you want." She says it's a tribute to her late friend Seagal Barsade, a scientist who studied emotion and empathy. Barsade was a famous night owl who wrote on every email, "my work hours may not be your work hours, please don't feel obligated to reply outside normal working hours."

— Angela Duckworth
24:05

The most effective lever for shifting your schedule is controlling light

Angela learned from Stanford neuroscientist Andrew Huberman's podcast that you can adjust your natural circadian rhythm by controlling light. Huberman recommends getting daylight within 15 minutes of waking, especially important in the low-sunlight winter months. Angela moved her laptop from her basement office to an upstairs spot by a window. Huberman also recommends minimizing light later in the day, i.e. dimming the lights at home after dark. Angela says she's also more careful about not playing on her phone, and instead reads on an old black-and-white-only Kindle, which she finds helps.

— Angela Duckworth
27:18

From early bird to night owl is the body clock shifting with age

At 17, Angela wrote a college application essay titled "Confessions of a Teenage Owl," in which she called sleep a "needless waste of time," said she slept only four hours a night, and claimed she was "just as healthy and twice as happy for it." Stephen says anyone in medicine or science today would want to delete that sentence. Angela is now 52, says she tries to sleep more, and likens herself to "the wise owl is to the early morning lark" — she went from early bird to night owl. This confirms the paper's finding that chronotype shifts with age.

— Angela Duckworth

Glossary

chronotype
A person's biological rhythm type — whether they naturally tend to sleep and wake early or late.
lark
A person who habitually rises early and is energetic in the morning.
owl
A person who habitually stays up late and is energetic at night.
near-normal distribution
A bell-curve-like distribution shape, high in the middle and low at both ends.
crepuscular
The habit of animals that are active at dusk and dawn.
