Had he known the suffering ahead, he would never have founded NVIDIA again

He says that if he had to endure the same humiliation and losses again, he would not start over—but that does not mean he regrets what NVIDIA is today.

2:03

He would refuse to build NVIDIA again, knowing the cost

He says if someone compressed the suffering of 2007 onward into a single sentence and told his 30-year-old self—stock in freefall, every quarter delivering the worst results, hated by everyone, forced to lay off employees—he would never start again. But this is not regret: he emphasizes that he never talks about the past because founders must delete yesterday's pain every single day to push forward, an ability that requires deliberate practice, not innate optimism.

— Jensen Huang
13:33

At nine, his parents sent him to scrub toilets in Kentucky

In 1973, a coup in Thailand prompted his parents to send him and his brother to the cheapest American boarding school they could find—Oneida Baptist Institute in Clay County, Kentucky, a town of 600 with no traffic lights. The school had no janitors; students cleaned it themselves. He was assigned to the bathrooms and was the youngest student in the school's history. For two years he never called home, only sent recorded cassettes.

— Jensen Huang
24:52

He left a secure job with only $30,000 in the company's bank account

He held a stable, well-paying position at LSI Logic, tracking toward management, with his wife Lori raising their two children and his parents newly immigrated to America. When Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem approached him about founding a company, he was initially content, but the conversation built his excitement—when they started the company, they had only $30,000. He says he never contemplated failure; once the idea formed in his mind, it became as real as anything else.

— Jensen Huang
35:08

He turned Sega's cancellation into an equity investment by flying to Japan

The NV1 architecture proved catastrophically flawed, but the company had signed a $12 million contract with Sega to deliver NV2 and NV3 using the same broken architecture. He flew to Japan and told Sega's CEO directly that the architecture was wrong and they should find another partner—then made a request: convert the remaining contract funds into an investment in NVIDIA. Sega's board agreed. That investment saved the company. The price was laying off two-thirds of the staff.

— Jensen Huang
40:11

With no prototype, he bet half the remaining cash on a single chip tape out

The company had only six months of cash left; there was no time for the traditional iterative spin cycles. They bought a used chip simulator from creditors of a failed company and wagered half of the remaining corporate cash—no prototype, no room for error. ‘There was no such thing as making a prototype first.’ The chip had to work on the first attempt, or the company was finished.

— Jensen Huang
57:21

NVIDIA gave away CUDA for nearly two decades, embedded in every GeForce card sold

CUDA faced the classic chicken-and-egg problem from the start: without enough installed base there were no developers, without developers there were no killer applications. His solution was to send every GeForce gaming graphics card into millions of homes carrying CUDA with it, at the cost of gross margins that went ‘from poor to abysmal’—and this investment ran for nearly twenty years, until the Toronto team won ImageNet training neural networks on GPUs and finally made it pay off.

— Jensen Huang
1:15:11

He rejected the consensus that AI will displace workers wholesale

He did not sign the 2023 open letter on AI extinction risk. He uses radiology as his example: a decade ago, people predicted AI would eliminate radiologists entirely. AI did make reading scans faster and more accurate. But because it was cheaper and faster, hospitals did more scans, admitted more patients, and demand for radiologists actually rose—work has tasks, yes, but also purpose; AI replaces tasks. He believes exaggerating job-loss anxiety only frightens young people away from fields that genuinely need talent. This pessimistic narrative is worse in America than almost anywhere else; in China, ‘they simply don't buy it’.

— Jensen Huang
1:24:36

Beyond luck lies knowing whom to trust for the next decision

Looking back over 34 years, he attributes the outcome to a mix of judgment, values, friends, information, and some luck—there were hundreds of forks in the road, and he picked the right one most of the time. He emphasizes that the company built a system of self-reflection that never treats one bad decision as fatal, so mistakes never become catastrophic. The person who helps him make the right choice at the next fork was probably not the one who helped him at the last one—the key is identifying who they are.

— Jensen Huang
