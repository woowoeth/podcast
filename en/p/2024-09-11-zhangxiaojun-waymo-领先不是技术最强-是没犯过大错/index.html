Waymo's lead isn't the strongest tech — it's that it never made a fatal mistake

Waymo started earliest, cycled through four leaders, and was written off by the whole industry — yet it ended up the only L4 company still leading. Because it survived, and it didn't make the fatal mistakes Cruise and Uber made.

The ragtag team the DARPA Challenge buried

At the first DARPA Grand Challenge in 2004, the prize was $1 million and the course was 240 kilometers. The winner, CMU, died after just 11 kilometers; not one team finished. The second edition raised the prize to $2 million, drew 20-plus teams, and 5 finished the whole course. The most overlooked was the fourth-place team: a squad from a Louisiana insurance company. Its two founders read about the event in a magazine and signed up, tapped the person at the company who knew computers best — the one who had originally built their website — as lead, and gave him $650,000. No one on the team had a PhD, no one understood autonomous driving. They built the equipment in three months and spent six weeks learning AI while passing DARPA review, all while Hurricane Katrina flattened their school. Stanford finished in 7 hours; they finished in 7 and a half. Mengxing says this story tends to get buried.

The history of autonomy is data-driven methods being stuffed in, block by block

Mengxing lays out a clear thread of technical evolution: in 2009 the industry used traditional robotics rule systems; in 2012 AlexNet appeared, end-to-end perception became feasible, and Waymo brought it into perception in 2014; in 2017 and 2018 the prediction module shifted from rules to data-driven, because they found people don't drive according to lane-line ratios; in 2021 the hardest part, planning and control, also went from hard code to collecting human driving data and using imitation learning. The whole process went from the most mature parts to the least, from partial adoption to full. The product Waymo ships today is not fully end-to-end, but the senior technical people on the team hold extremely high ideals about end-to-end, believing it is the simplest, most direct, cleanest and most elegant approach — it's just that L4 has too high a bar for the floor, and end-to-end floor control is inherently unstable.

— Meng Xing
21:38

Four months in Phoenix: treating the driverless car like an Uber

Mengxing went to investigate Waymo in Phoenix in the second year of its public testing. The area was 7 miles by 10 miles, about 70 square miles. At midnight he drove to the Costco entrance and hunted for the hardest parking spot, hailing Waymo purely as a user, watching the car come from afar with his initials on its lights. The impact came from three things solved at once: high safety, no slowness, and unlimited stops — no fixed stations, only a negative list, so it could stop anywhere except where stopping is forbidden. He also tested the two hardest scenarios: in the Costco parking lot, people backing up, pushing shopping carts, kids playing, with no concept of right of way; and in a villa neighborhood on weekly trash day, bins all over the street, the car weaving around them like obstacles. He also strapped down the lidar, splashed water on it, and opened the door while moving for extreme tests, finding Waymo could self-diagnose and keep driving, whereas Cruise in the same situation had to send someone out in person.

28:40

Waymo's home base looks like a sci-fi movie mothership

Mengxing followed the car to Waymo's operations center, in a Phoenix suburb, a big warehouse of about five or six thousand square meters. The entrance looked like an abandoned car factory, with broken cars from the previous generation parked outside, lidar hanging off, unguarded. When the new-generation car arrived, an automatic door opened, and in the distance you could see charging piles and automated equipment. His metaphor is the sci-fi movies he watched as a kid: aliens attack Earth, the mothership door opens, out come very small spacecraft, and in a while they return to the mothership to charge and refuel. He explains the information asymmetry: in a 45-degree Phoenix summer, few people are willing to trek out to the suburbs to do this, and there are security guards patrolling. His motive for doing this research was intense curiosity — he didn't want second-hand information, and the best first-hand information is usually what others won't let you get.

— Meng Xing
43:50

Cruise's fall: the dragging incident and dishonesty

Cruise had just obtained its 24×7 paid-operations permit in August or September last year when it had an accident at an intersection: a person was crossing the street, the Cruise car didn't move, but a human-driven car on its left knocked the pedestrian in front of the Cruise car, and the pedestrian fell under the car and got caught. By human driving behavior, the best thing would be to stop and not move, but Cruise's mechanism is to pull over when it encounters a safety scenario, so it dragged the person a few more meters and rolled them under the car. Mengxing points to two problems: first, the industry had only ever considered avoiding accidents, rarely how to handle an accident once it has happened, or how to know there was one; second, Cruise didn't mention the dragging at its briefing, and when it later came out, it was seen as dishonest, which is the bigger problem. After that, testing was halted for investigation, Kyle and essentially all the executives left, OpenAI hired many of them, and the original team is basically gone.

— Meng Xing
47:53

Cruise was aggressive because it had to prove itself to GM

Mengxing speculates on the mechanism behind the two companies' different styles: Cruise was a company acquired into GM, and its incentive mechanism was tied to performance; it was also one of the best-paying companies in the industry, and GM, as a traditional automaker, needed great courage to sustain a team more expensive than a tech company long-term, so it had to have presentable results to keep proving its rationale. That was pressure on Kyle, and pressure on GM CEO Mary Barra, who championed the effort. The upside is that GM's profits during the pandemic years were all above $10 billion, so spending over $2 billion a year was affordable. Waymo, by contrast, was incubated by Google itself, and the people it hired were initially on Google's payroll, so it didn't need to prove itself extra, and preferred to follow the laws of technical progress to set its operational pace, with a bit less pressure.

— Meng Xing
55:58

In 2020 everyone thought Waymo had fallen behind

Mengxing tells a small story: during the pandemic in 2020 he had breakfast with Sebastian Thrun in Silicon Valley, and in conversation found that not just Thrun but many people at the time rated Waymo very poorly, thinking it was too slow, that its iteration speed represented the older generation's autonomous driving capability, that it didn't use the latest technology, and that it hadn't done anything remarkable in the past three or four years; Tesla, by contrast, was updating and iterating every year. This was the industry's common perception in 2020 and 2021, and even the most authoritative people said similar things. This only reversed recently, and reversed very thoroughly — within L4, Waymo is absolutely the best. Mengxing believes Waymo's model is not the core reason it does well or poorly; the real reason is that not many companies survive, surviving is what matters most, and among the survivors it didn't make the fatal mistakes Uber or Cruise made.

— Meng Xing
57:59

Uber's exit: lawsuits, the founder's ouster, a fatal accident

Uber's exit from autonomous driving came from three overlapping reasons: first, the lawsuit with Waymo, with Anthony installed as Uber's lead, creating internal questions over whether he was really in charge and whether to cut ties; second, founder Travis Kalanick's ouster, turning Uber into a company run by professional managers, with Dara an excellent CEO but unwilling to make big bets on a direction with no visible bottom; third, and most directly, Uber ATG was the only company in the US to have a fatal accident during testing, was suspended for over a year, was badly weakened, and was judged to be at fault. Mengxing also mentions cost control: where an ordinary US company spends three dollars, a Chinese company gets by on one; where Waymo spends three dollars, Uber might spend five.

— Meng Xing
1:05:02

L4 has no ChatGPT moment, only a commercial moment

Mengxing gives two answers. The first: this industry has no ChatGPT moment, because L4's eventual realization is a gradual process, not just a technical breakthrough but a business and product operation, a linear curve offered bit by bit to more people, not a hockey stick. If you look only at technology, the moment when one car is offered to one person may already have happened, for example Waymo running a 24×7 experience in San Francisco. The second answer is a limit he artificially constructs: when there are 10,000 cars, whether deployed in one city or several, running 24×7, with everyone hailing them just like a ride-hailing car, with orders filling capacity, and with unit economics that work, that can be called the commercial ChatGPT moment. He stresses that what Waymo has validated so far is that single-vehicle technology passes; mass production is being tested; unit economics and scaled operations are not yet validated.

Glossary

DARPA Grand Challenge
An autonomous driving competition funded by the US Defense Department's DARPA; no team finished the first edition in 2004.
AlexNet
A deep-learning-based neural network that won the 2012 ImageNet competition, abandoning traditional expert rules.
End-to-end
Using a single deep-learning approach to go directly from perception to planning and control, replacing a modular rule-based system.
Moon Shot Project
Google X's name for seemingly impossible projects that take a very long time to realize.
Robotaxi
Providing mobility operations with autonomous vehicles, replacing human drivers.
