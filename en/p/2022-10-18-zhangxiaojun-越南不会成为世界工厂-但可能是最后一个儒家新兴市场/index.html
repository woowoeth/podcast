Vietnam Won't Become the World's Factory, But It May Be the Last Confucian Emerging Market

Vietnam's GDP per capita is only $3,700, roughly where China was in 2003. Its opportunity isn't to replicate China's full industrial chain, but to pick a few industries and reach developed-country levels, the way the Four Asian Tigers did.

6:52

Vietnam is the last untapped Confucian market

The core logic behind Yang Fan's judgment on Vietnam's opportunity: most markets shaped by Confucian culture — Japan, the Four Asian Tigers, mainland China — are already well developed, and Vietnam is the last emerging market that hasn't yet developed. He sums up this cultural sphere as ‘eating with chopsticks’, underpinned by Confucianism, Buddhism and Daoism. The key to this judgment isn't the culture itself but the behavioral traits the culture produces: a hardworking and pragmatic people, emphasis on education, and a pursuit of worldly success and standing out. Those traits are not common in other Southeast Asian countries — Indonesia, for instance, is the largest Muslim country, and Thailand is Theravada Buddhist, where the pursuit is more about religious fulfillment.

— Yang Fan Francis
18:31

Vietnam will not become the world's factory

Yang Fan says plainly that Vietnam will not become the world's factory, because its size decides it. Vietnam's total population is just over 98 million, more than Germany, more than South Korea, with an area about the same as Japan. China could develop a full industrial chain because of its land area, population size and earlier accumulation; Vietnam doesn't have those conditions. He thinks Vietnam is more likely to develop in the direction of the Four Asian Tigers, picking a few industries the way South Korea did — Samsung's electronics, Hyundai Motors, shipbuilding, high-precision chemicals — and if it can develop several of those well, it could become a developed country. But he also stresses this requires the right timing, the right place and the right people, a kind of historical accident.

— Yang Fan Francis
22:37

The right people matter more than timing or place

Yang Fan breaks Vietnam's development over the past decade into timing, place and people, and argues people matter most — meaning Confucian culture. He offers several concrete supports: Vietnam ranks in the top 12 globally for engineering graduates; Vietnam is the world's second-largest IT outsourcing center after India; Vietnam's female employment rate is 10 points higher than China's; and the median age is just over 30, very young. On the high female employment rate, he guesses it may be related to war — male mortality during the war was very high, women had to do everything in the villages, and they became the backbone of economic development.

— Yang Fan Francis
25:25

Men do big things, and fighting a war is a big thing

There's a Vietnamese proverb: men are the ones who do big things. Yang Fan explains: what is a big thing? Fighting a war is a big thing. This has left Vietnamese men today somewhat more languid, while women, besides working, also take care of the family, and are extremely hardworking. He also mentions another feature of Vietnamese culture: it only extends to China's Ming dynasty, and never experienced the Qing dynasty's suppression of Han culture, so there were no restrictions on women's social status like foot binding. At the same time he stresses that Vietnamese men are very resilient — the only people in the world who beat the US military are China's Han and Vietnam's ethnic Kinh majority.

— Yang Fan Francis
36:39

Vietnam has four slims, and land is permanent-use rights

Yang Fan says Vietnam has four slims: a slim country, slim pho, slim women in ao dai, and slim houses. Houses are slim because the tax system is like the Netherlands', taxing by the width of the house's facade, so people like to build narrow and deep, and build upward. The key difference is that land is permanent-use rights: when a new family member arrives you can add another floor, and the ground floor can be rented to a shop. So for locals life is quite easy, and there's no need to buy another house. Foreigners have been able to buy since July 2015, but only apartments, not land — and what appreciates fastest in Vietnam is land.

— Yang Fan Francis
40:09

Vietnam is safe, but low-level officials skim benefits

Yang Fan says Vietnam is a very safe country, which is tied to its Confucian cultural foundation. He gives the example of Hanoi: if someone on the street dares to do wrong to anyone, a bunch of Hanoi ‘Chaoyang aunties’ rush over and beat that person — the moral demands are very high. Ho Chi Minh City has phone snatching from motorbikes, but Hanoi doesn't. On the business environment, Vietnam is very similar to China: the government welcomes foreign investment, but low-level officials hold approval power and want to skim benefits from companies. Compared with the West, Vietnam is not so much a rule-of-law society; it's still officialdom-centered, and also a society of personal connections — to do business well you have to cultivate relationships with all kinds of people.

— Yang Fan Francis
42:44

The China model hits a size ceiling in Vietnam

Yang Fan gives a concrete example: a Chinese luxury brand contacted his friend in Vietnam, planning to open 500 stores in Vietnam in five years, and asked whether it was possible. His friend thought about it and said, 500 — you'd be doing well to open two: Vietnam has one north and one south, one Hanoi and one Ho Chi Minh City. Because of its size, China can make any vertical very big, but Vietnam's size is much smaller. He also mentions that southern Vietnam is more Southeast Asian — people won't work overtime, and things get done surprisingly slowly. This is the pit Chinese companies most easily fall into when they go to Vietnam with China's habitual thinking.

— Yang Fan Francis
49:33

Don't only go to Ho Chi Minh City — you have to pay respects in Hanoi

Yang Fan's advice for Chinese companies: manufacturing firms need to find good industrial parks — Vietnam has roughly 500 industrial parks from north to south, but only about half may be operating, and quality varies. For other companies, including fairly large Chinese internet firms, the mistake is that they all only come to Ho Chi Minh City, because it's the economic center, was once called Saigon, and is more famous — but they neglect Hanoi. Hanoi is the capital, equivalent to China's Beijing, and many matters of paying respects have to be done in Hanoi. He also warns that many people come over, take one look and feel it's very much like China, but in actual operations you must absolutely not be held hostage by that ‘very much like’ feeling — there are many subtle places that are very different.

— Yang Fan Francis

Glossary

Confucian society
East and Southeast Asian societies shaped by Confucian culture, marked by the chopstick-eating sphere.
Four Asian Tigers
Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore and South Korea, which achieved economic takeoff one after another after World War II.
Ao dai
Traditional Vietnamese women's dress, similar to the qipao, slit on both sides and worn with trousers.
Chaoyang aunties
Middle-aged and older women in Beijing's Chaoyang district who eagerly watch over neighborhood security; here a metaphor for Hanoi's nosy citizens.
