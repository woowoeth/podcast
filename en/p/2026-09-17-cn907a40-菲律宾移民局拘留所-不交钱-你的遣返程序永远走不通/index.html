Philippine Immigration Detention: If You Don't Pay, Your Deportation Never Moves

A Chinese surfer was held in the Philippines for 41 days. What he saw wasn't a prison — it was a business with prices posted on the wall: beds, boxed meals, phones, lawyer fees, every one of them bought with cash. Those who don't pay stay locked up for years, even decades.

The Immigration Bureau quoted 1.5 million pesos to walk free on the spot

The night Feili was arrested, an Immigration Bureau officer pulled him aside and said quietly, "I can help you." After talking to a lawyer, the word came back: hand over 1.5 million pesos, roughly 160,000-170,000 RMB, and you can be released immediately, no problem at all. Of the 12 to 13 people arrested in the same batch, nobody agreed to pay. Everyone thought it was impossible that such a thing could still exist in 2026, and wanted to resolve it through normal legal procedure. But the price of refusing was this: they were transferred from the holding cells of the General Santos police station to the Immigration Bureau detention center in Manila, where Feili ultimately spent 41 days.

18:21

When his phone was confiscated, the money in Gcash was transferred out

When Feili was detained his phone was taken, and they asked for his unlock code. He assumed it was standard procedure and gave it. His phone unlock code was the same as his Gcash payment password — the Philippine equivalent of Alipay. For the next four or five days he applied repeatedly and could not get the phone back, until he kept pressing the Chinese consulate and his lawyer, at which point it was returned. When he turned it on, the interface had all switched to English, and the Gcash transfer records showed money had been sent out — but then sent back the night before he got the phone. There was only about 1,000 RMB in there. Feili says it isn't about the amount; it's that someone touched it while it wasn't in his hands. His read: many Chinese in Manila doing gray-industry scams who get arrested go through this same layer of skimming, but they don't dare approach the Chinese consulate, because their phones are full of criminal evidence.

— Fei Li
24:26

The air conditioning and takeout the Immigration Bureau promised exist — in another form

For those unwilling to buy their own plane ticket to transfer, the Immigration Bureau works on them in shifts, saying conditions at the Manila bureau are excellent: air conditioning, plenty of freedom, you can work out, play basketball, order takeout, use your phone. Feili says they weren't lying — there really is air conditioning, you really can order takeout. It's just another form. A completely different form. They arrived at the main detention center past three in the morning. What he saw after getting out of the vehicle was a square area wrapped in a layer of iron grating, a sheet-metal roof overhead, and all around it iron cages like dog kennels, with long communal platforms inside, hundreds of people. Past three in the morning, many weren't sleeping — bare-chested, in nothing but underwear, clinging to the cage bars to stare at the newcomers. Feili says he and the other foreigners didn't say a word for several minutes after getting out, and then it hit them: the Immigration Bureau had lied to them.

— Fei Li
32:38

The detention center is a business invested in by a Chinese boss

The story Feili heard inside was that this Immigration Bureau detention center was invested in by a Chinese boss, in cooperation with the Immigration Bureau, and that the beds, toilets and other facilities were all newly built by that Chinese boss. Foreigners just stay there, but Chinese who come in have to pay. The cheapest tier, a spot on the communal platform, is 10,000 pesos a month, about 1,100-1,200 RMB. The wealthy panzong and gambling bosses can pay 50,000 pesos a month, over 6,000 RMB, for a so-called "air-conditioned room" — in reality the bed is the same, just wrapped in tin foil and wooden boards, with a styrofoam box of ice beside it, a hole cut in the box, and an exhaust fan pulling the cold air into the bed. That is where the Immigration Bureau's promise of "air conditioning inside" came from.

— Fei Li
34:38

Chinese gangs collect protection money: you want out without paying?

On Feili's first day inside, the head of the Chinese gang came to collect a "rumor fee," saying the facilities were all their investment and there was no way he'd stay for free. Feili said he had no money, that he was here to serve time — how could he be expected to pay on top of that? The gang boss, in a threatening tone, said: you're already in here and you don't want to spend money, and you still think you're getting out? Feili learned that the place is run through gangs, and the Chinese gang monopolizes everything for Chinese inmates — food, clothing, daily life — running a Chinese restaurant and a Chinese supermarket. The whole detention center holds about 200 people; when Feili arrived it was the off season, and there were still over 100 Chinese, more than half of them gambling and gray industry. The Chinese gang's staff draw wages: the head chef gets 10,000 pesos a month, the kitchen hands 5,000 pesos. The pay is very low, but many people can't get out precisely because they have no money for a lawyer, so they can only work to earn.

— Fei Li
43:48

A young man from Yunnan held over a year; his family thought he died in Southeast Asia

A young man from Yunnan asked Feili for help. Before the pandemic he'd been lured to the Philippines by a high-salary recruitment ad; the moment he landed his phone and passport were taken and he was dragged into a compound to run telecom fraud. With poor numbers he was shocked with an electric baton by his supervisor every day. After the company was busted he served a few months, then was transferred to the Immigration Bureau detention center. The normal deportation procedure is simple — a few departments sign off, you buy your own plane ticket, and you're gone. But the Immigration Bureau, in order to extract money, keeps you locked up a long time; without paying, the procedure doesn't move. He'd already been held over a year, with not a cent, no phone, eating only prison food every day, unable to reach his family, who thought he had died in Southeast Asia. Feili helped him write a letter in Chinese, translated it into English, and through an introduction from a Japanese man got to see the warden. The warden had a guard bring Feili's phone, and let the young man add his father on WeChat and send a selfie and a voice message. Afterwards the Immigration Bureau bought a basic phone specifically to solve this problem.

— Fei Li
51:29

Scam company ransom: 3 million pesos per person

The only Chinese Feili talked with at any length came from a scam company. The company's 70 or 80 people had been surrounded by the Immigration Bureau together with the police and the NBI — three agencies. The first wave of skimming was the NBI and police locking each person in a toilet, because there are no cameras in the toilets, and stripping them of everything valuable. The second wave was the Immigration Bureau negotiating with the company boss for the release of people, with different quotes per person — 1 million, 2 million, 3 million pesos — depending on the mood of whoever was in charge. That company's deal was 3 million pesos per person, over 300,000 RMB. The boss ultimately ransomed 30 people, exactly the 30 held on one floor; the rest were promised food deliveries until they got out. This young man ran an operation targeting the Japanese market, scamming middle-aged Japanese women. In his best month he made 2 million RMB, and bought a house and a car in his small home county. Later he was kidnapped by a friend, had several hundred thousand transferred out, and was hit with a trumped-up illegal gun-possession charge by a corrupt cop — which is why he couldn't leave.

— Fei Li
57:32

A Philippine lawyer raises his price the moment he sees a Chinese client

In Cebu, through an introduction from a Lithuanian, Feili met a Philippine lawyer who quoted 200,000 pesos, over 20,000 RMB, to get it done in four weeks. After Feili got to Manila the lawyer came to visit, saw he was Chinese, and immediately raised the price, saying the earlier figure was the old price and the new price required another 120,000 pesos, over 10,000 RMB. Feili decided not to use him and wrote off the deposit, turning instead to a Chinese middleman. The Chinese middleman quoted over 30,000 RMB, four to six weeks, no more than six. Feili says the genuinely reliable Chinese middlemen know this procedural pipeline extremely well, because everyone locked up in there is Chinese. In the end he was held inside for over 30 days, 41 days counting Cebu. The Lithuanian who introduced the Philippine lawyer still hadn't gotten out when Feili sent him a message.

Glossary

Gcash
The Philippines' mainstream electronic payment app; Feili had set its payment password identical to his phone unlock code.
POGO
Philippine Offshore Gaming Operator — licenses issued in large numbers after 2016, allowing companies to run online gambling operations aimed at customers abroad.

A Jewish house of worship; Jewish travelers must find one to resolve their dietary needs.
