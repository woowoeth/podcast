Philippine Immigration Detention: If You Don't Pay, Your Deportation Never Moves

A Chinese surfer held for 41 days in the Philippines saw not a prison but a business with prices on everything: beds, boxed meals, phones, lawyer fees — all bought with cash, and those who don't pay are locked up for years or even decades.

10:05

Immigration offered to release him on the spot for 1.5 million pesos

The night Feili was arrested, immigration officials pulled him aside and whispered, "I can help you." After talking with a lawyer, the feedback was: give 1.5 million pesos, about 160,000-170,000 RMB, and you can be released immediately with no problems. Among the 12 to 13 people arrested in the same batch, no one agreed to pay. Everyone thought such a thing couldn't possibly still exist in 2026 and wanted to resolve it through normal legal procedures. But the price of refusal: they were transferred from the detention room at the General City police station to the Manila Immigration headquarters detention center, where Feili ultimately stayed for 41 days.

18:21

When his phone was confiscated, money in Gcash was transferred out

When Feili was detained, his phone was taken. They asked for his unlock password, and he thought it was normal procedure so he gave it. His phone unlock password was the same as his Gcash payment password. For the next four or five days, no matter how he applied, he couldn't get the phone back. Only after constantly pressuring the Chinese consulate and a lawyer was the phone returned. When he turned it on, the interface was all in English, and the Gcash transfer records showed money had been sent out, but it had been transferred back the night before he got the phone. There was only about 1,000 RMB in it. Feili said it wasn't about the amount, but that the phone had been tampered with while not in his hands. He analyzed that many Chinese doing gray-industry scams in Manila who get arrested go through this layer of fleecing, but they don't dare go to the Chinese consulate because their phones contain a lot of criminal evidence.

— Fei Li
24:26

The air conditioning and takeout the immigration bureau promised were another form

For those unwilling to buy their own tickets to transfer, the immigration bureau would repeatedly work on them, saying the Manila immigration bureau had good conditions, air conditioning, freedom, and you could work out, play basketball, order takeout, use your phone. Feili said they weren't lying — there really was air conditioning, you really could order takeout, it was just another form, a completely different form. Arriving at the headquarters detention center was after 3 a.m. Getting off the vehicle, he saw a square area, with an outer layer of iron bars, a tin roof above, and around it iron cages like dog kennels, inside were long communal beds, hundreds of people. Many people weren't sleeping at 3 a.m., shirtless in just underwear, clinging to the iron cages watching the newcomers. Feili said he and the other foreigners didn't speak for a few minutes after getting off, then it hit them: they'd been fooled by the immigration bureau.

— Fei Li
32:38

The detention center is a business invested in by a Chinese boss

What Feili heard inside was that this immigration detention center was invested in by a Chinese boss, in cooperation with the immigration bureau, and the beds, toilets and other facilities were newly built by the Chinese boss. Foreigners could just stay, but Chinese coming in had to pay. The lowest-tier communal bed was 10,000 pesos a month, about 1,100-1,200 RMB. Wealthy bosses and gambling tycoons could pay 50,000 pesos a month, about 6,000+ RMB, to stay in a so-called "air-conditioned room" — actually the bed was the same, just wrapped in tin foil and wooden boards, with a foam box of ice next to it, a hole cut in the box, and an exhaust fan drawing cold air into the bed. That's where the immigration bureau's earlier claim of "air conditioning inside" came from.

— Fei Li
34:38

Chinese gangs collect protection money: you don't want to pay but still want to get out?

On his first day inside, the head of the Chinese gang came to Feili to collect a "rumor fee," saying the facilities were all their investment and it couldn't be free. Feili said he had no money, he was here to serve time, how could he still have to pay. The gang boss said with a threatening tone: you're already in and don't want to spend money, you still think you can get out? Feili learned that inside it was managed by gangs, and the Chinese gang monopolized all food, clothing, and daily life for Chinese, running a Chinese restaurant and a Chinese supermarket. The whole detention center had about 200 people; when Feili entered it was off-season, but there were still over 100 Chinese, with gambling and gray industries making up more than half. The Chinese gang employees were paid — the head chef 10,000 pesos a month, the assistant cook 5,000 pesos — very low wages, but many people couldn't get out because they had no money for a lawyer, so they could only work to earn money.

— Fei Li
43:48

A Yunnan man held for over a year, his family thought he died in Southeast Asia

A man from Yunnan asked Feili for help. Before the pandemic, he was lured to the Philippines by a high-salary recruitment ad. As soon as he landed, his phone and passport were taken, and he was pulled into a park to do telecom fraud. For poor performance, he was shocked daily with an electric baton by his supervisor. After the company was busted, he served a few months, then was transferred to the immigration detention center. The normal deportation procedure is simple: a few departments sign, you buy your own ticket, and you can leave. But the immigration bureau, to make money, will keep you locked up for a long time; without paying, the procedure doesn't move. He had already been held for over a year, had not a cent, no phone, could only eat prison food every day, couldn't contact his family, and his family thought he had died in Southeast Asia. Feili helped him write a Chinese letter and translated it into English, and through an introduction by a Japanese uncle, got to see the warden. The warden had a guard bring Feili's phone, and let the young man add his father on WeChat and send a selfie and voice message. Later, the immigration bureau bought a basic phone specifically to solve this problem.

— Fei Li
51:29

Scam company ransom: 3 million pesos per person

The only Chinese person Feili talked with much was from a scam company. The company's 70-80 people were surrounded by the immigration bureau together with the police and the NBI in a three-department raid. The first wave of fleecing was the NBI and police locking each person in a toilet — because there were no cameras in the toilets — and taking all valuables. The second wave was the immigration bureau negotiating with the company boss for ransoms. The price per person varied: 1 million, 2 million, 3 million pesos, depending on the mood of the person in charge. That company negotiated at 3 million pesos per person, about 300,000+ RMB. The boss ultimately ransomed 30 people, exactly the 30 people held on one floor. The rest were promised food would be sent until they got out. This young man worked on a scheme targeting the Japanese market, scamming middle-aged Japanese women. In his best month he earned 2 million RMB, bought a house and car in his small hometown county. Later he was kidnapped by a friend, had several hundred thousand transferred away, and was framed by a corrupt cop with illegal gun possession, so he couldn't leave.

— Fei Li
57:32

A Philippine lawyer raised the price on the spot when he saw he was Chinese

In Cebu, Feili was introduced by a Lithuanian to a Philippine lawyer, quoted 200,000 pesos, about 20,000+ RMB, done in four weeks. After arriving in Manila, the lawyer came to visit, saw he was Chinese and immediately raised the price, saying that was the old price, the new price required an additional 120,000 pesos, about 10,000+ RMB. Feili decided not to use him, forfeiting the deposit, and turned to a Chinese intermediary. The Chinese intermediary quoted 30,000+ RMB, four to six weeks, no more than six weeks. Feili said truly reliable Chinese intermediaries are very familiar with this procedure because everyone locked up inside is Chinese. In the end he was held inside for over 30 days, plus Cebu, a total of 41 days. The Lithuanian who introduced the Philippine lawyer — when Feili messaged him, he still hadn't gotten out.
