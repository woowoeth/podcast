The Philippine Immigration Detention Center: If You Don't Pay, Your Deportation Never Moves

A Chinese surfer was locked up in the Philippines for 41 days. What he saw wasn't a prison — it was a business with prices posted on the wall: beds, boxed meals, phones, lawyer fees, every one of them bought with cash. Those who don't pay stay locked up for years, even decades.

The Immigration Bureau quoted 1.5 million pesos to walk free on the spot

The night Feili was arrested, an immigration officer pulled him aside and said quietly, "I can help you." After the lawyer communicated, the word came back: give 1.5 million pesos, about 160,000 to 170,000 RMB, and you can be released immediately, no problem at all. Of the 12 to 13 people arrested in the same batch, no one agreed to pay. Everyone thought it was impossible that such a thing could still exist in 2026, and wanted to resolve it through normal legal procedure. But the price of refusing was this: they were transferred from the holding cells of the General City police station to the Immigration Bureau's main detention center in Manila, where Feili ultimately spent 41 days.

18:21

When his phone was confiscated, the money in Gcash was transferred out

When Feili was detained his phone was taken, and they asked for his unlock code. He assumed it was normal procedure and gave it. His phone unlock code was the same as his Gcash payment password — the Philippine equivalent of Alipay. For the next four or five days he applied however he could and still couldn't get the phone back, until he kept pressing the Chinese consulate and his lawyer, and only then was it returned. When he turned it on, the interface had all switched to English, and the Gcash transfer records showed money had been sent out — but the night before he got the phone back, it had been transferred back in. There was only about 1,000 RMB in there. Feili said it wasn't about the amount; it was that the phone had been touched while it wasn't in his hands. He reasoned that many Chinese doing gray-market scams in Manila go through this same layer of fleecing after being arrested, but they don't dare go to the Chinese consulate, because their phones are full of criminal evidence.

— Fei Li
24:26

The air conditioning and takeout the Immigration Bureau promised were just another form

For those unwilling to buy their own plane ticket to transfer, the Immigration Bureau would take turns working on them, saying the Manila bureau had great conditions — air conditioning, lots of freedom, you could work out, play basketball, order takeout, use your phone. Feili said they weren't lying: there really was air conditioning, you really could order takeout — it was just another form, a completely different form. Arriving at the main detention center was past three in the morning. What he saw after getting out of the vehicle was a square area, an outer layer of iron grating, a sheet-metal roof above, iron cages like dog kennels all around, and inside them communal sleeping platforms, several hundred people. Past three in the morning many people weren't asleep, bare-chested in nothing but underwear, hanging on the iron cages watching the newcomers. Feili said the few foreigners didn't say a word for several minutes after getting out, then it hit them: they'd been lied to by the Immigration Bureau.

— Fei Li
32:38

The detention center is a business invested in by a Chinese boss

What Feili heard inside was that this immigration detention center was invested in by a Chinese boss, in cooperation with the Immigration Bureau, and that the beds, toilets and other facilities had all been newly built by the Chinese boss. Foreigners could just stay, but Chinese who came in had to pay. The lowest-tier communal platform bed was 10,000 pesos a month, about 1,100 to 1,200 RMB. Wealthy pan zong and gambling bosses could pay 50,000 pesos a month, about 6,000-plus RMB, for a so-called "air-conditioned room" — actually the same bed, just wrapped in tin foil and wooden boards, with a foam box of ice beside it, a hole cut in the box, and an exhaust fan drawing the cold air into the bed. That's where the Immigration Bureau's original claim that "there's air conditioning inside" came from.

— Fei Li
34:38

Chinese gangs collect protection money: you want out without paying?

On Feili's first day inside, the head of the Chinese gang came to him for the "rumor fee," saying the facilities inside were all their investment and it was impossible to stay for free. Feili said he had no money, that he'd come to serve time, so how could he be expected to pay on top of that. The gang boss said in a threatening tone: you're already in here and you don't want to spend money, and you still think you're getting out? Feili learned that the place is run by gangs, and the Chinese gang monopolizes all the food, clothing and daily life of the Chinese, running a Chinese restaurant and a Chinese supermarket. The whole detention center held about 200 people; when Feili went in it was the off season, and there were still over 100 Chinese, more than half of them gambling and gray-market. The Chinese gang's employees drew wages — the head chef 10,000 pesos a month, the kitchen helper 5,000 pesos — very low wages, but many people couldn't get out precisely because they had no money for a lawyer, so they could only work to earn.

— Fei Li
43:48

A young man from Yunnan was held over a year; his family thought he'd died in Southeast Asia

A young man from Yunnan asked Feili for help. Before the pandemic he'd been lured to the Philippines by a high-salary recruitment ad; the moment he landed his phone and passport were taken, and he was dragged into a compound to do telecom fraud. With poor performance he was shocked with an electric baton by his supervisor every day. After the company was busted he served a few months, then was transferred to the immigration detention center. The normal deportation procedure is simple — a few departments sign off, you buy your own plane ticket, and you can go — but the Immigration Bureau, in order to extract money, will hold you for a very long time, and without paying the procedure doesn't move. He'd already been held over a year, with not a cent, no phone, eating only prison food every day, unable to reach his family, who thought he'd died in Southeast Asia. Feili helped him write a letter in Chinese, translated it into English, and through an introduction from a Japanese man got to see the warden. The warden had a guard bring Feili's phone, and let the young man add his father on WeChat and send a selfie and a voice message. Afterwards the Immigration Bureau bought a basic phone specifically to solve this problem.

— Fei Li
51:29

Scam company ransom: 3 million pesos per person

The only Chinese Feili talked with at any length came from a scam company. The company's seventy or eighty people were surrounded by the Immigration Bureau together with the police and the NBI, three agencies. The first wave of fleecing was the NBI and police locking each person in a toilet — because there are no cameras in the toilets — and taking everything valuable off them. The second wave was the Immigration Bureau negotiating with the company boss for the people, with different quotes per person: 1 million, 2 million, 3 million pesos, depending on the person in charge's mood. That company negotiated at 3 million pesos per person, about 300,000-plus RMB. The boss ultimately ransomed 30 people, exactly the 30 held on one floor, and the rest were promised food deliveries until they got out. This young man ran an operation targeting the Japanese market, scamming middle-aged Japanese women; in his best month he made 2 million RMB, and bought a house and a car in his home county town. Later he was kidnapped by a friend, several hundred thousand was transferred out of his accounts, and a corrupt cop pinned an illegal gun-possession charge on him, so he couldn't leave.

— Fei Li
57:32

The Philippine lawyer raised his price on the spot when he saw he was Chinese

In Cebu, through an introduction from a Lithuanian, Feili met a Philippine lawyer who quoted 200,000 pesos, about 20,000-plus RMB, done in four weeks. After he got to Manila the lawyer came to visit, saw he was Chinese, and immediately raised the price, saying that was the old price and the new price required another 120,000 pesos, about 10,000-plus RMB. Feili decided not to use him and forfeited the deposit, turning instead to a Chinese middleman. The Chinese middleman quoted 30,000-plus RMB, four to six weeks, no more than six weeks. Feili said the truly reliable Chinese middlemen know this procedure inside out, because everyone locked up in there is Chinese. In the end he was held inside for thirty-some days, 41 days counting Cebu. The Lithuanian who'd introduced the Philippine lawyer still hadn't gotten out when Feili messaged him.
