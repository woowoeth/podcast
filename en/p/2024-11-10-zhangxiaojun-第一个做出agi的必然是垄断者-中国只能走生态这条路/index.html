The first to achieve AGI will inevitably be a monopolist; China can only take the ecosystem path

Chasing the first AGI that crushes all rivals is, at its core, an ambition for commercial hegemony—it will use that brain to invent the means to maintain monopoly. Chinese companies can't win this money-burning game, so they must first drive down inference costs and use the ecosystem as a moat.

If inference costs don't fall, the application ecosystem can't take off

01.AI's most important technical decision in 2024 was to unreservedly pursue the Mixture of Experts (MoE) route, creating models that are highly capable, fast, and cheap. Li Kaifu's insight: the entire industry's explosion will definitely happen on the application side, and the application side needs extremely cheap inference to ignite. He did the math—applications will get harder one after another. A chatbot can tolerate spitting out one character at a time; a search engine needs to be faster; replacing something like Douyin means users scroll for three to five hours. Usage intensity and model difficulty keep escalating. Multiply those together, and even a tenfold annual drop in inference cost isn't enough.

— Kai-Fu Lee
19:19

Vertical integration is the way to drive costs down

Li Kaifu doesn't buy the idea of lying flat and waiting for GPU and memory prices to fall—enjoying a tenfold drop in a year, then jumping out to do applications at the right time. He cites the iPhone: Apple didn't wait for capacitive screens, multi-touch, software keyboards, and music APIs to become industry standards; it folded them together one by one, because waiting might take five to seven years. So 01.AI chose vertical integration: optimizing everything from hardware to software, from models to applications, in combination. The specific approach is converting computation into memory, designing a three-tier memory architecture of HBM next to the GPU, CPU RAM, and SSD. Spending 10% more on memory can cut GPU computation by 40% to 50%.

— Kai-Fu Lee
27:36

Building the world's best model and building applications don't connect

Li Kaifu uses Character.AI as an example: founder Noam Shazeer was single-mindedly pursuing AGI, so he first built a character.ai product, but the two things conflict—an AGI model is a sledgehammer to crack a nut, not suited for characters. The company struggled to find its place and was eventually acquired by Google. Those who wanted to do AGI returned to Google; those who wanted to build apps stayed at Character. He explains the mechanism: people pursuing the world's best AGI have a mix of idealism and arrogance, believing that once AGI is achieved it will crush all competitors, that there may not necessarily be an ecosystem above it, that every app is just a thin shell—in English, a veneer—and all value lies in the AGI. Li Kaifu says he is not that kind of person.

— Kai-Fu Lee
41:25

The first to achieve AGI will inevitably be a commercial hegemony monopolist

Li Kaifu reasons: suppose AGI has thinking and reasoning, creativity and invention, and independent thinking and self-learning abilities far beyond humans. Then it can help you invent new things—invent a business model to defeat other large-model companies, design a PR strategy to make everyone trust you, even design cyberattacks to paralyze competitors. So the first to achieve AGI and crush rivals is, of course, a technological ideal, but also a commercial hegemony monopolist, and will become an ambition for an ultimate monopolist. This is far bigger than past claims of Microsoft monopoly or Google monopoly, because in the past Microsoft could hardly make Windows eliminate Mac, but this is a brain.

— Kai-Fu Lee
45:44

Today's AI ecosystem is an inverted triangle

Li Kaifu drew an ecosystem diagram: a healthy ecosystem should have chips earning the least, platforms earning quite a lot, and applications earning the most—all applications combined earning more than platforms. That's how PC, internet, mobile internet, and cloud all worked. But today's AI ecosystem has chips/GPUs at $75 billion, cloud vendors at $10 billion, and application providers (like ChatGPT) at only $5 billion—a complete inverted triangle. If the inverted triangle persists, applications won't spring up like mushrooms, users won't get benefits, and app builders will struggle to quickly achieve PMF, make money, and raise funds. The positive cycle won't turn.

— Kai-Fu Lee
1:14:18

OpenAI still has many cards hidden; don't underestimate it

Li Kaifu just returned from Silicon Valley, and a consensus among many people he met is: OpenAI still has many good things hidden that it hasn't released. GPT5 training isn't going smoothly, but to raise funds it dropped an O1. It still has many cards in hand and isn't in a hurry to play them—because each time it plays a card, global tech companies including China's will look at what it played, guess, and try to replicate, and in the end even if they can't match it they can get 80-90% there. So it wants to save them until AGI is within reach. Li Kaifu thinks O1 itself didn't bring that big an improvement in reasoning and understanding; the most impressive thing is the scaling law at inference time—the straight line it drew opened up another gold mine.

— Kai-Fu Lee
1:26:55

Nvidia's advantage may waver as training shifts to inference

Li Kaifu comments on overseas giants one by one: Nvidia is currently the biggest beneficiary, but the problem it may face later is—when most GPUs are no longer doing training but inference, can its advantage persist? Meta is the biggest disruptor; whatever it can't win at, it open-sources. This time it seems quite successful: making money from ads, using open source to stake a position. Although technically behind, open-sourcing when you can't beat them has already succeeded twice (the first was TensorFlow vs. PyTorch). Microsoft is in the best position, able to attack and defend, but its challenge is that its own models have never been good. Long-term, if it can't build models and falls out with OpenAI, it will face challenges.

— Kai-Fu Lee
1:30:22

Google's search faces enemies on two fronts

Li Kaifu says Google is rather sad: theoretically it should be the strongest—the best large-model papers were done by Google, the best reinforcement learning by DeepMind—but putting them together doesn't seem to produce much杀伤力. Search faces challenges on all sides—large models make users ask ChatGPT first when they have questions, taking away some volume; more seriously, many users who want to buy things go directly to Amazon, so commercial search isn't done on Google anymore. Plus a dilemma: should it put large models into search? Replacing search would dismantle the ad business; making two entrances is self-deception; having both coexist makes it a neither-fish-nor-fowl—giving an overview but not an answer, plus a pile of links and ads.

— Kai-Fu Lee

Glossary

veneer
Li Kaifu uses this to describe how each app above AGI is just a thin shell, with all value in the underlying model.
Service as Software
A new Silicon Valley term for providing digital employees via software access, charging by one person's workload.
TCPMF
Two words Li Kaifu added to PMF, meaning you also need to judge how strong the technology needs to be, who can build it when, and when costs will be low enough.
HBM
The fastest tier of memory next to the GPU, the most commonly used data storage in Li Kaifu's three-tier memory architecture.
