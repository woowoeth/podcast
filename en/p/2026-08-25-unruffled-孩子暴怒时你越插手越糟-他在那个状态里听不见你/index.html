When Your Kid Explodes, Stepping In Makes It Worse — He Can't Hear You in That State

A child's rage isn't an attack on you; it's a loss of control while the prefrontal cortex is still under construction — in that state he can't hear any reasoning. The adult rushing to soothe and explain is really managing his own discomfort; what helps is staying nearby and letting the child ride it out himself.

Not every piece of parenting advice applies

Loryn's daughter dysregulated extremely easily; they read every book and saw psychologists and all kinds of experts. The turning point was advice from one expert — that was when they realized not all the advice they were handed applied to their child, that they had to respond to the child they actually had, trust their instincts, and tailor things to her. The specific situation was refusing to stay in her own bed at night, known professionally as Jack in the Box Syndrome, and it happened to coincide with a newborn brother crying all night, leaving both adults unable to sleep.

— Loryn Brantz
5:06

PDA: asked to do something, the system shorts out

PDA is pathological demand avoidance. You tell your child to put on shoes, and it isn't just the power struggle every kid has — it's his nervous system shorting out. He may feel he can't do it, or be anxious about the thing itself. Kids don't explode for no reason: when they're little it's usually an unmet need (tired, hungry), and when they're older it's because they genuinely can't do it, and you have to meet them where they are. So they reshaped their lives around their daughter: she couldn't stand washing her hands, which was a sensory issue, so they switched to wet wipes. As a result she actually developed faster and more calmly.

— Loryn Brantz
6:07

The anger is actually coming from shame

Loryn says people don't realize how much kids want to succeed, how much they want to please their parents, and that the moment they feel they've failed they're flooded with shame — but they won't say the shame out loud, they just explode. Knowing this emotion comes from embarrassment and shame rather than hostility aimed at the parent changes how you handle it entirely. Janet adds another layer: a child's brain and body are developing at enormous speed, permanently in a state of imbalance, and self-regulation hasn't grown in yet, so they live inside their emotions and have never once stood outside them — something adults can do and they can't.

— Loryn Brantz
7:12

Don't treat your child as an equal opponent

Loryn's reminder to parents is: your child is not on the same starting line as you, they're a sweet little spicy nugget, the prefrontal cortex isn't developed yet, they're still learning and growing. Janet calls this the key to understanding everything — if what you see in front of you is an angry adult, that may summon someone from your own life who was perhaps even abusive, and so your reaction can't possibly help the tiny person in front of you who is nothing like that. So train yourself to see this, and then to see each child's particular sensitivities.

— Janet Lansbury
10:19

She uses meditation to watch her emotions from the side

Loryn has severe OCD and anxiety. What she needs isn't to bottle herself up like a soda bottle but real inner calm, because her daughter can tell whether she's genuinely calm. She read a stack of books, among them Michael Singer's The Untethered Soul, which she calls her best parenting book, teaching her to be an observer of her own thoughts and feelings rather than being swept away by them. In practice, that means meditating 10 minutes every morning after dropping the kids off, or using Gabby Bernstein's app for a guided meditation before bed.

— Loryn Brantz
13:34

Intervening usually makes it worse

Writing the book made one thing clearer to her: adults stepping in tends to worsen the situation, and doing less is better. In the book the adult doesn't try to help the little spicy nugget calm down — she calms down on her own, because they always do calm down. That's when you debrief, and then try again next time. Janet puts it more bluntly: our urge to speak, to take action, is essentially our own discomfort and our wish for this to stop, and that's why it doesn't work.

— Loryn Brantz
19:45

Humor has to beat the explosion to the punch

The tactic that works best at home is imaginative play and humor, provided you get there before the explosion. Heading out, she'd open the door and pretend an invisible animal had run out, shouting ‘Are you going to race the cheetah? The elephant's coming’, and the kids would bolt out the door; bedtime ran on the conga, and once the parents started dancing the kids couldn't help joining in. The phrase ‘spicy nugget’ itself came from this: when her daughter started to heat up, she'd say ‘Are you about to turn into a spicy nugget? I'm going to dunk you in cold milk to cool you down’, then pick her up and dip her gently up and down on the couch a few times, and they'd both laugh and the temperature would drop.

— Loryn Brantz
21:51

They don't even remember why they got angry

At the end of the book, the little girl says ‘Wait, what was I even mad about?’ — and Loryn thinks that's the key to the whole book: the child has no idea what happened or how they got there, they weren't in their own body at the time, and they weren't in the relationship with you either. So how do you blame them, how do you punish them? So don't burn your energy inside the storm, don't hurt yourself trying to help your child, because by the time they've long since moved on and gone off to play, you're too exhausted to keep them company. Questions and reasoning are useless — they can't hear you. Don't go surfing; sit on the beach, or be an anchor — I'm here, I'm not going anywhere.

— Loryn Brantz

Glossary

spicy nugget
Comparing a child mid-tantrum to a crispy-outside, tender-inside spicy nugget — a self-deprecating way to cool things down.
PDA (pathological demand avoidance)
When being asked to do something shorts out the nervous system, rather than being an ordinary parent-child power struggle.
Jack in the Box Syndrome
You put the child to bed and he pops up again and again all night like a jack-in-the-box.
radical acceptance
Fully accepting what you can't control, including your child's emotions and your own situation.
