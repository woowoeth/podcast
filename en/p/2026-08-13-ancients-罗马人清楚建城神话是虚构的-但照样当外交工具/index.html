The Romans knew their founding myth was false, and used it as a diplomatic tool

Rome's origin myth was not history carved in stone but a flexible narrative the Romans used to negotiate, absorb foreign peoples and digest civil war; the archaeological evidence even directly conflicts with the 753 BC founding date.

In the early tradition, Aeneas never reached Rome

In Homer, Aeneas is just a minor prince in the Trojan camp; after the city falls he carries his father out of the city, and the Greeks let him go out of respect for that piety. He then wanders the Mediterranean, and many city-states claimed he had been to their own place, but few traditions have him arrive at Rome. Even in Virgil's Aeneid he only takes a look at the site of Rome, finds communities already there and turns around and leaves. What he is really tied to is Lavinium and Alba Longa, linked to Rome through the bloodline of his descendants. Jeremy stresses that this thread is "very rough, very vague," far less direct than what the Romans later claimed.

17:10

Roman foreign policy was stitched together from invented figures

The function of these origin stories is not to record history but to serve as "connection points." Aeneas wandered many places, so many communities could claim a link to him. By the third century BC, Segesta in Sicily was cozying up to Rome through Aeneas: "we're both connected to this guy." Jeremy says the local elites mostly knew this was fiction, but it was "useful and pleasant," letting them negotiate real political treaties on that basis. Odysseus in Greek myth was likewise used by various places as a diplomatic intermediary. In other words, Roman foreign policy was stitched together from this kind of invented figure.

— Jeremy Armstrong
22:33

The image of the wolf suckling the twins predates the written accounts

The Romulus and Remus story as we have it comes mainly from Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Plutarch, all of them centuries after the events. But the core image of "twins suckled by a she-wolf" had already appeared around 300 BC in a statue group in the city of Rome, dedicated by the Ogulnius brothers, and also on early silver coins. This shows that by at least the late fourth century BC the story had taken fixed form in elite culture. Jeremy also points out that some details in the story — nomadic raiding, Indo-European mythic motifs — feel archaic, not like something someone in the middle Republic would have made up, so the oral tradition should be very old.

— Jeremy Armstrong
32:06

The lupa is either a metaphor for a prostitute or just a real wolf

"Lupa" means both she-wolf and prostitute; later some explained it as the wife of the shepherd Faustulus. Jeremy's view is the opposite: in the early images it is a wolf, and a she-wolf that has just given birth is one of the most ferocious animals on the Italian peninsula. Wolves appear widely in the origin stories of Italian peoples such as the Samnites, representing migration, settlement and ferocity. By choosing the she-wolf as a symbol, the Romans wanted to write that extreme violence into their own origins. The so-called "prostitute" explanation looks more like a later Roman version that humanised it in hindsight; he states clearly that this is just his intuition, not a settled conclusion.

— Jeremy Armstrong
43:14

The fratricide story is about Rome absorbing its Latin kin

Remus is not just a supporting character in the Roman story; he is himself a Latin hero, and his name appears in other Latin communities; the name Romulus literally means "Roman." The killing of brother by brother thus corresponds to the fourth-century BC process by which Rome conquered and absorbed the other Latin city-states: same origin, yet they had to be killed and then integrated. When Livy wrote this passage in the first century BC, he may also have been using it to respond to the trauma of civil war. Jeremy's reading: the story admits that "brother killing brother" is terrible, but the key is what comes after — everyone regroups into a single community. This is also the narrative template by which the Romans digested civil war.

— Jeremy Armstrong
47:15

Rome expanded by seizing people and recruiting, not only by fighting

After Remus's death Romulus quickly turns into a cool-headed founder: he finishes the wall, establishes religious rites, divides the population into 30 curiae, all of which later ages saw as the prototype of the Roman constitution. He also makes Rome an "open asylum," anyone willing could join; but those who came were mostly men, and women were lacking. So he used a trick to invite the neighbouring Sabines to a festival and had his men seize the most beautiful women. Jeremy compares the early city-state to a shopping mall, having to find ways to attract all kinds of "customers." For the Romans, expansion relied on three mechanisms used together to absorb outsiders: force, intermarriage and attraction.

— Jeremy Armstrong
49:30

The Romans did not need a single ending for their founding father

Romulus's end is "extremely messy." He disappeared while reviewing the army in the Campus Martius and never came back. Two mainstream versions: one, he was wrapped in lightning and became a god; two, he was torn apart alive by the senators — which easily brings Caesar's death to mind. What Jeremy finds most intriguing is that authors like Livy place the two endings side by side, both as acceptable accounts. This founding father could be a god ascended to heaven, or a tyrant executed by his own people. The Romans did not need a single verdict, and this contradiction is precisely what gave later political figures room to take from it what they wanted.

— Jeremy Armstrong
57:07

The founding year was cobbled together to align with Greek chronology

Jeremy cites drilling data from Andrea Brock and others: the Tiber changed course around 600 BC, and Rome's geological layout changed drastically with it. From this, there is a clear rupture between the traditional founding year of 753 BC and the later Rome that served as the city centre. But he says the Romans would not have been troubled by this — in their eyes the city was more a gathering place, a set of ideas and relationships, than a physical entity. They used 753 to align with Greek chronology (the first Olympics were in 776 BC), and beyond that they could only rely on "reasonable guesses." The gap between myth and history was not a problem for the ancient Romans.
