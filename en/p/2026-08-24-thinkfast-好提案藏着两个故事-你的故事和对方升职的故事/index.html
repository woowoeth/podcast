Every Pitch Needs Two Stories: One About the Project, One About Their Promotion

Hollywood screenwriter Rhett Reese reveals the essence of pitching: on the surface you tell the project's story, on the hidden line you tell the story of the other person's success. Make them the protagonist, lower the risk, amplify the reward, and your pitch lands far more often.

2:03

One extra word after the punchline and the laugh is gone

Comedy, like all communication, works best in short sentences. When writing comedy, we find the rhythm and put the funniest word at the end of the sentence. If there are words after the punchline, the brain hears the joke and wants to laugh, but it still has to process what comes next, which weakens the comedy. So we trim the line down to the minimum and end on the funniest word. The strategy works.

— Rhett Reese
4:04

The protagonist of a pitch isn't the project, it's the executive across the table

We're all selling, and when we pitch we're actually telling two stories. The first is the story of the project itself — like a campfire gathering, consider the audience's needs, keep it short and entertaining, give it a climax. The second story is subtler: cast the executive across the table as the protagonist, and tell the narrative of how they succeed after buying this project. The executive's problem is a limited budget and the need to buy the right project and deliver results. We convey to them the vision that ‘we're professional, you're safe, and buying this project will make you successful.’

— Rhett Reese
7:09

They don't want the best option, they want the safe one

When pitching, lower the risk the other side perceives and amplify the reward. Once the pitch begins, they are your audience — like sitting in a dark theater or by a campfire — and you have to guide them to see the picture, to visualize it. This applies to any setting: to a VC, to your boss when asking for a raise, even a plumber pitching a client. The core is making the other person believe you are the safe choice, that your experience and credibility let them take the risk with confidence.

— Rhett Reese
9:12

Disagreements aren't settled by persuasion, but by who cares more

My writing partner Paul and I never fight; the rule is ‘whoever cares most wins.’ When we disagree, we each state our case, and if we can't persuade the other, we see who is more passionate. This defuses conflict, because once one side realizes the other really cares, they back down. Next time it may be their turn. The rule works, and it keeps the collaboration smooth.

— Rhett Reese
10:12

Put criticism in the document, don't say it to their face

We don't criticize face to face. Instead we write scenes separately, send them by email, and edit each other's work on our own computers. Edits are bolded, and the bolding stays until both sides are satisfied. This reduces emotion and territoriality. When you get the revised draft, what you see is changes in a document, not criticism delivered in person. If the other person changed your material, you can change it again, but usually you won't revert it to the original unless you have a strong reason.

— Rhett Reese
13:16

The next Chris Nolan won't come out of Hollywood

My feelings about AI are contradictory: 85% fear, 15% excitement. The fear is that AI can produce Hollywood-quality content, studios will find cheaper ways to make things, and a large number of jobs will disappear. The excitement is that within three years, someone will be able to make a Hollywood-caliber film alone with AI tools, bypassing Hollywood's gatekeepers. That will let young creators who were shut out emerge, and among them will be new Chris Nolans and Charlie Chaplins.

— Rhett Reese
18:23

When changing lanes, only take half a step at a time

Hollywood typecasts easily. We deliberately take only half a step at a time — say, from horror comedy to action comedy, then to pure action, then to romantic comedy. That way we gradually broaden the résumé and prove we can handle many genres. At the same time, having a sellable brand is an advantage, but you have to expand the boundary consciously to avoid being boxed in.

— Rhett Reese
21:30

Honesty is the foundation of communication; once you're caught, it's all over

The first element of successful communication is honesty — that's the foundation. If the audience catches you lying, your credibility is destroyed. The second is considering the audience: every communication targets specific people and needs to be tailored. The third is concision and clarity — avoid beating around the bush, repeating yourself, and going off on tangents. In an age of information overload, concision is respect for your audience's time.

— Rhett Reese
