A Pitch Tells Two Stories: The Project, And The Buyer's Promotion

Hollywood screenwriter Rhett Reese on what a pitch actually is: the project story runs on the surface, the story of the other person's success runs underneath. Make them the protagonist, lower their risk, enlarge their reward, and your pitch lands far more often.

2:03

One word after the punchline and the laugh is gone

Comedy, like all communication, works best in short sentences. When we write comedy we find the rhythm and put the funniest word at the end of the line. If words come after the punchline, the brain hears the joke and wants to laugh but still has to process what follows, and that weakens the comic effect. So we cut a line down to the fewest possible words and end on the funniest one. The strategy works.

— Rhett Reese
4:04

The protagonist of a pitch is not the project but the executive across the table

We are all selling something, and a pitch is really two stories at once. The first is the story of the project itself — told like a tale around a campfire: think about what the audience needs, keep it short and entertaining, give it a climax. The second story is subtler: make the executive across the table the protagonist, and tell the narrative of how they succeed after buying this project. The executive's problem is a limited budget — they need to buy the right project and produce results. So what we transmit to them is a vision: we are professionals, you are safe, buying this project will make you successful.

— Rhett Reese
7:09

The other side does not want the optimal option, it wants the safe one

In a pitch you lower the risk the other side perceives and you enlarge the reward. From the moment the pitch begins they are your audience, as if they were sitting in a dark theater or beside a campfire; your job is to lead them to see the picture, to let them visualize it. This applies in any setting: pitching a VC, asking your boss for a raise, even a plumber talking to a customer. The core of it is making them believe you are the safe choice — that your experience and credibility let them take the risk with confidence.

— Rhett Reese
9:12

Disagreements are settled by who cares more, not by who persuades

My writing partner Paul and I never fight. The rule is "whoever cares most wins." When we disagree, we each state our case, and if neither of us can persuade the other, we look at who feels more strongly about it. This defuses conflict, because once one side realizes the other genuinely cares, that side gives way. Next time it may be your turn. The rule works well, and it keeps the collaboration running smoothly.

— Rhett Reese
10:12

Criticism belongs in the document, never delivered to someone's face

We don't criticize each other face to face. Instead we write scenes separately, send them by email, and edit each other's work on our own computers. Edits go in bold, and they stay bold until both of us are satisfied, at which point the bold comes off. This takes the emotion and the territoriality out of it. When a revised draft arrives, what you are looking at is a change in a document, not criticism aimed at you in person. If the other person has changed something of yours, you can change it again — but usually you don't put it back the way it was unless you have a good reason.

— Rhett Reese
13:16

The next Chris Nolan will not come out of Hollywood

My feelings about AI are contradictory: 85% fear, 15% excitement. The fear is that AI can produce Hollywood-quality content, that studios will discover cheaper ways to make things, and that a large number of jobs will disappear. The excitement is that within the next three years someone will be able to make a film of Hollywood caliber alone, with AI tools, going around Hollywood's gates entirely. That will bring forward the young creators who have been shut out, and among them will be a new Chris Nolan and a new Charlie Chaplin.

— Rhett Reese
18:23

Changing genres only works half a step at a time

Hollywood types you easily. We have deliberately moved only half a step at a time: from horror comedy to action comedy, then to straight action, then to romantic comedy. That broadens the credits gradually and proves we can handle multiple genres. At the same time, there are advantages to owning a brand that sells; but you have to push the boundaries consciously so you don't end up boxed in.

— Rhett Reese
21:30

Honesty is the bedrock of communication, and getting caught destroys everything

The first element of successful communication is honesty — it is the bedrock. If the audience discovers you are lying, your credibility is destroyed. The second is considering your audience: every act of communication is aimed at specific people, and it has to be tailored to them. The third is being brief and clear — no circling around, no repetition, no digressions. In an era of information overload, brevity is a form of respect for your listener's time.

— Rhett Reese
