Child Custody Through Divorce: Courts Now Default to 50/50, and a Winner's Mindset Is the Real Loser

A New York divorce lawyer lays out a ten-year trend: courts have shifted from "prove why one parent should have sole custody" to "why shouldn't it be 50/50." What actually determines a child's outcome isn't the schedule — it's whether the parents can put the child's needs ahead of their own pain.

Mediation or litigation depends on personality, not the facts of the case

Michael Fried's firm offers three paths: mediation, collaborative law, and litigation. He picks the path by reading the client's personality: if the client is passive and the spouse is dominant, he recommends litigation, because in mediation that side's voice will otherwise drown out the client's. The lawyer's role is to be the client's mouthpiece. Collaborative divorce requires both sides to be willing to cooperate; if talks break down, the collaborative lawyers must withdraw and cannot use the information they have already learned — which in turn pressures both sides to reach an agreement. Cases involving domestic violence are not suited to mediation or collaboration; they can only go through legal proceedings.

— Michael Fried
16:32

50/50 isn't automatic — it requires being able to enable the other parent

Over the past 10 to 15 years, the court's logic has shifted from "prove why one parent shouldn't have sole custody" to "why shouldn't it be 50/50." But 50/50 doesn't happen automatically: it requires a high degree of parental cooperation, so when the court looks at the best interest of the child, one of the most important factors is whether one parent has the ability to promote the child's relationship with the other parent. Without that ability, the logistics 50/50 requires — the diary, the medication, the pickups and drop-offs, notifying about school events — simply can't function. A sole-custody schedule is simple, while joint custody means the two of them have to negotiate over and over.

— Michael Fried
20:00

A court-ordered app ends the he-said-she-said

Michael introduces OurFamilyWizard (OFW), saying the courts recognize the app — just last week in court he saw a judge order both parties to sign up. OFW unifies all communication between the parents: messages are timestamped, so you can see who sent, read, and replied at what time, and there's a shared calendar; when the tone gets too sharp, it prompts you to soften it. Lawyers, the child's representative, forensic psychologists, and the parent coordinator can all see the record, so both sides automatically rein themselves in. He says this eliminates the he said/she said that is the most common thing in custody disputes, and creates accountability.

— Michael Fried
23:31

50/50 was bought by fathers getting up in the middle of the night

Sarah asks whether the court's shift toward joint custody is because the courts have taken in more child-development information. Michael's answer goes back to the actual change in family roles: over the past 25 to 30 years, mothers have worked, and fathers have also gotten up in the middle of the night, taken the kids on weekends, and been involved in baths and feedings. When his own children — now 26 and 23 — were small, he was the one getting up at night. What judges and mediators see is no longer "only one parent can take care of the child," but two parents both handling the daily routine. That change forms the social basis for the shift to 50/50.

— Michael Fried
30:15

The schedule follows the child's capacity, not the parents' work shifts

Common 50/50 schedules include 2-2-5-5: Monday and Tuesday with one parent, Wednesday and Thursday with the other, weekends alternating; there's also 2-2-3 or 3-4-4-3. Michael says which one you choose can't be based only on the parents' work hours — the core question is whether the child can handle the back-and-forth, how old the child is, and whether there are special needs. He gives the example of a neurodiverse child: 2-2-3 and 2-2-5-5 may both be unworkable, so you switch to larger blocks of four days/three days. When a child reaches 10 to 13, they may start not wanting to stay with one parent, and even then the parents still have to push that relationship forward.

— Michael Fried
42:24

Keep a diary, not a book — record only deviations from the baseline

Michael has clients keep a diary from the very start of the case, but stresses, "I don't want a book, I want short paragraphs." First record the baseline: the bedtime routine, the morning routine, how after-school and weekends go; after that, record only changes that deviate from the baseline, such as how an illness was handled. These records are evidence in litigation — he is the client's mouthpiece, and he has to be able to prove to the judge: my client has been the primary caregiver since day one, and under pressure did the right thing. He also warns: recording useless information is just noise; doctors, schooling, homework, and handling illness are the facts that actually move a ruling.

— Michael Fried
47:09

One or two late arrivals don't count — it's a pattern that counts

When clients complain the other parent was 12 minutes or 5 minutes late, Michael has them record it first and not act immediately. One or two isolated instances don't matter; only when it becomes a sustained, pronounced course of conduct does it constitute a "change of circumstances" that gives grounds to change joint custody to sole custody. He says that as a litigator he has to accumulate enough "ammunition." So the test for whether to come to a lawyer is: does it happen occasionally, or does it happen repeatedly? Only if it happens repeatedly is it worth starting a modification proceeding.

— Michael Fried
51:42

Putting the child first doesn't mean the parent suppresses all their own needs

At the end, Sarah offers a correction to "child first": what you need to suppress is your immediate pain reaction, not your core needs. The needs for safety, to be seen, and to be understood still have to be met — only if your legal team and psychological team take care of you will your child benefit from this process. Michael also always stresses to clients that they should go to therapy, because only if the client stays healthy and stable can they keep supplying the lawyer with the evidence needed for the defense. A child's protective factors ultimately include the parent's own mental health and physical state.

— Sarah Bren

Glossary

OurFamilyWizard
A court-recognized co-parenting communication app with timestamps and tone prompts; its records can serve as evidence.
collaborative law
Both sides each hire a collaborative lawyer and work with experts together; if talks break down, the collaborative lawyers must withdraw from the case.
parent coordinator
A third-party professional who helps parents carry out custody arrangements and reduce conflict after divorce.
change of circumstances
The legal threshold in New York for modifying a custody agreement: the arrangement must be based on a significant change in circumstances.
forensic psychologist
A forensic psychology expert who evaluates the family and issues a professional opinion in custody disputes.
best interest of the child
The legal standard for custody decisions in New York: which environment is most conducive to the child's future growth.
