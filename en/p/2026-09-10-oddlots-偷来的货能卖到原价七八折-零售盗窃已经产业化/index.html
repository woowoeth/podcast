Stolen Goods Sell for 70-80% of Retail, and Retail Theft Has Been Industrialized

Stolen merchandise passes through two layers of hands: boosters are recruited to steal from stores, and fences clean off the markings and resell, after which the goods go up on secondhand platforms. Networked goods fetch 70-80% of retail; T-shirts and deodorant only 30-something percent.

A third of shrink is self-inflicted

Home Depot splits shrink into two buckets: operational/administrative and malicious, which Scott describes as the things we do to ourselves versus the things others do to us. At Home Depot, about 30% of shrink falls into the former, with the rest on the malicious side. The industry line that has circulated for years: of malicious shrink, about 40% is caused by internal employees, 35% by people who walk into the store, and the rest is self-inflicted operational shrink. These percentages move only within a few points each year, but he thinks they are drifting upward, and that it has been underreported ever since COVID, with neither police reports nor the FBI's Uniform Crime Report fully capturing it.

22:52

Employees are explicitly told not to chase anyone

The bigger problem isn't money, it's people. Scott says professional criminals often use homeless people and addicts to go into stores and steal, and these people have one thing on their minds — get the goods out and turn them into cash — so they will get physical, and they have gotten physical. People have been injured in Home Depot stores, and even killed. That's why asset protection staff are trained in de-escalation, and the 400,000 store employees are explicitly told not to intervene, just to back off. His attitude: he'd trade shrink for people's safety every day, and management and the board would back that decision.

— Scott Glenn
23:53

RFID dies the moment it enters a steel store

Joe asks why RFID has been talked about for 25 years and yet in 2026 is still expensive and hard to scale. Scott says he doesn't understand it either, but explains what's peculiar about Home Depot: it's a hardware store, and the store is full of steel, essentially a Faraday cage, so passive RFID can't be read; you can only use solutions that actively transmit a signal. He also says tag costs are now almost zero, and the real bottleneck is the reading hardware — covering a 140,000-square-foot sales floor with eight separate entrances and exits is extremely costly. They've tried the idea of bricking merchandise before it leaves the store, but to do that for a single power drill you'd have to rework an entire production line, which is very hard to scale.

— Scott Glenn
25:58

Stolen goods have been pushed onto secondhand platforms

The division of labor works like this: boosters are recruited and given a list, cash, or the promise of drugs to go steal from stores; the goods go to a fence — maybe a garage, a warehouse or a flea market — who strips the price tags, store markings and electronic tags before reselling, and the money goes up to whoever runs the operation, which might be a small crew of a few people or a transnational gang. After the INFORM Act took effect in 2023, big platforms like Amazon and eBay were required to verify seller identities, so stolen goods shifted to Craigslist and Facebook Marketplace, because the law left a loophole that doesn't cover peer-to-peer sellers. Home Depot works backward from the platforms, using models to flag sellers pricing below cost while moving large volumes.

— Scott Glenn
35:19

One combo kit sold means restocking 4.2 times

Whether to lock up shelves is a financial question. Scott says they look at the data: there's a combo kit priced at $799, and for every one sold, they have to restock 4.2 times. Take that to finance and they immediately understand it's a gross margin problem — as long as it breaks even or even makes a little, locking it up is justified. But half of Home Depot's revenue comes from professional contractors, and if they break a bit on the job site, rush to the store and find it out of stock, that's a loss too. So they lock up a carefully selected set of categories, with a QR code to summon an associate, who opens the lock in an average of 90 seconds; and locking things up doesn't necessarily hurt sales — in some categories sales actually go up, because the goods are finally in stock.

— Scott Glenn
39:23

A fake bill of lading can just drive the goods away

On the supply chain he counts three plays: first, pure diversion, where whole containers get rerouted at the port or a transload warehouse and hauled somewhere else, even loaded back onto a container ship and shipped away; second, a driver drives eight hours across state lines, and while he sleeps someone pries open the trailer doors and moves boxes; third, forging bills of lading and pickup orders, using AI to make the documents look real, and the driver pulls in, says he's there to pick up a certain trailer, and just drives off. He says what's new may not be the methods but the volume, because these cases are very hard to pursue. Rail is the area that has clearly worsened over the past five or six years; cars sitting in a yard get broken into by a group, and the other side has good intelligence — they don't touch every car, only the ones carrying guns or electronics.

— Scott Glenn
41:28

AI in security is an efficiency tool

Scott says they have more cases than they can investigate, so for them AI is an efficiency question: a typical organized retail crime case takes an average of 70 days from discovery to closing and handing over to police, and compressing that cycle means they can work more cases. They just replaced the video management system across all 2,300 stores, letting existing cameras run edge analytics — identifying people who linger too long in an area, deliberately avoid associates, or show up where they shouldn't be. He also admits that to identify a suspicious combination of behaviors and then identify the specific person, he doesn't have enough cameras yet, but they will get there. Separately, they have an in-house company doing web scraping.

— Scott Glenn
44:30

Seven years ago nobody cared; now there's a federal bill

He says he's very optimistic now. INFORM is arguably the first federal bill driven by organized retail crime, and next is CORCA (Combating Organized Retail Crime Act): the retail industry broadly supports it, it has bipartisan cosponsors, it has passed the House, it's attached to the NDAA awaiting Senate passage, and the president has said he'll sign it. The rationale is that this crime is inherently interstate — it happens in Cobb County, Georgia, and the goods flow to South Carolina and North Carolina, which is interstate transportation of stolen goods, but there were no corresponding federal resources, and the bill would provide resources, prosecutors and a coordination center. Seven years ago he was thoroughly pessimistic about this: nobody took it seriously, and the prevailing mood then was decriminalization and defunding the police.

Glossary

shrink
Inventory is on the books but doesn't match up at count; includes both operational errors and theft.
ORC (organized retail crime)
Group theft across stores and even states, for the purpose of reselling for profit.
boosters
People recruited to go into stores and steal, given a list, cash, or the promise of drugs.
fence
The middleman who consolidates stolen goods and strips the markings and price tags before reselling.
INFORM Act
Took effect in 2023; requires online platforms to verify the identity of high-volume sellers, with peer-to-peer sellers exempt.
edge analytics
Letting the cameras themselves analyze footage in real time, identifying behaviors like lingering or avoiding associates.
