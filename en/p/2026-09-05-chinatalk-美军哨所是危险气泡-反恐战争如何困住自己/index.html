American Outposts Were Danger Magnets: How Counterinsurgency Trapped Itself

American outposts in Afghanistan were meant to protect villages but became magnets for violence. The more the military built, the heavier it got, losing mobility and eventually being trapped by its own fortifications.

Outposts Became Danger Bubbles

In the summer of 2010, Wesley Morgan accompanied US troops into the Pech Valley in Afghanistan and found that outposts meant to provide security for villages actually became "danger bubbles," drawing conflict toward the villagers. Both US forces and the enemy suffered few casualties; the real victims were the villages outside the outposts. This contrast made him ask: who built these outposts, why, and when? That questioning eventually led to his book The Hardest Place.

— Wesley Morgan
10:39

The US Military Got Heavier and Heavier

From 2006 to 2010, US forces in eastern Afghanistan gradually lost flexibility and mobility, becoming tied to heavy outposts and MRAPs. In 2006, the 10th Mountain Division could still use pickup trucks and ATVs to reach remote areas, but by 2010, due to the IED threat and top-down force-protection requirements, conventional units could hardly get to those places and had to rely on special operations forces for offensive missions. This overextension meant follow-on units could not sustain the earlier operational reach.

— Wesley Morgan
12:51

Two ODA Teams: Restraint vs. Recklessness

In 2004, two Green Beret ODA teams deployed to the Pech Valley in succession. The first, from the Utah National Guard's 19th Special Forces Group, used an "ink spot" tactic, trying to build a security zone around the district center, and refused to enter the Korengal Valley because they sensed something there was luring them in. The second, from the 3rd Special Forces Group at Fort Bragg, was eager to enter Korengal to find a fight. They got entangled in a local tribal timber dispute, were used as enforcers for one faction, and set off the Korengal "snowball" that escalated into a years-long intense battlefield.

— Wesley Morgan
19:27

Three Tribes Inside the US Military Were Incompatible

In eastern Afghanistan, there were three "tribes" within the US military: conventional forces, Green Berets (who worked with local partners, training Afghan National Army commandos and local police), and the Joint Special Operations Task Force (such as Task Force 373, made up largely of SEAL Team Six and Rangers, conducting night raids). These three had different goals; sometimes they supported each other, but more often they got in each other's way. For example, a task force might kill someone that conventional forces could easily have arrested, and only the task force could reach remote areas that conventional forces could not. This lack of coordination was a core problem of the Afghan war.

— Wesley Morgan
24:56

Commanders Measured Success with the Wrong Metrics

Many commanders lacked real understanding of counterinsurgency and fell back on quantifiable performance indicators: tons of ordnance dropped, miles of road built, number of meetings held. The book gives an example: the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry Regiment, fought in Kunar Province for 15 months, losing 24 soldiers. Its commander, Lieutenant Colonel Bill Ostland, defined success by the number of JDAMs dropped, ammunition expended, SERP budget spent, and 10,000 Beanie Babies distributed—metrics that did not reflect actual effectiveness and were even self-contradictory.

Naval Officers' Unlikely Ground Combat Resumes

Wesley Morgan points out that many current senior US Navy and Air Force officers have a chapter in their resumes that diverges sharply from the conventional career path: they led Provincial Reconstruction Teams (PRTs) in Kunar and Nuristan provinces in Afghanistan, including submarine officers, nuclear engineers, and naval aviators. For example, the current commander of Indo-Pacific Command, Sam Paparo, led a PRT in Nuristan for a year. These missions ran concurrently with the fighting in Wana and Narisan; they went out daily in MRAPs on counterinsurgency missions. This experience became a distinctive mark in their careers.

— Wesley Morgan
43:18

Military-Media Relations Deteriorated from Transparent to Opaque

Morgan recalls that in 2007, as a 19-year-old freelance journalist, he could almost freely choose which units to embed with; public affairs officers simply helped him get to company and platoon level. But now, since the embedding system ended in 2013-2014, the military's relationship with the media has sharply deteriorated. A new generation of battalion-level and below officers views the media as something to be feared. He believes the turning point was not the McChrystal incident but rather after Obama's second term, when the military began restricting embeds and keeping the war out of sight, because the complex information that "advisors" were really "combat advisors" was unwelcome.

— Wesley Morgan
57:52

How Multicam Became a Mark of the Elite

Morgan tells the story of how Multicam camouflage spread: it was invented around 2002 by a Brooklyn art school graduate, lost the Army's competition (the Army chose the ineffective ACU pattern), but Delta Force adopted it privately because ACU performed poorly at night. It then spread through special operations circles and became a mark of the elite. The key was that Crye Precision, by hinting to game developers that this was elite camouflage, got Multicam into games like Ghost Recon before real photos appeared, thus establishing its elite image in the minds of young soldiers.

Glossary

Mine-Resistant Ambush Protected vehicle, an armored vehicle designed to resist IEDs and ambushes.
