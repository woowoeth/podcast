US Outposts Were Dangerous Bubbles: How the War on Terror Trapped Itself

US outposts in Afghanistan were meant to protect villages, but became magnets for conflict. The harder they fought, the heavier they became, losing mobility, until they were trapped by the fortresses they built.

The outpost became a dangerous bubble

In the summer of 2010, Wesley Morgan embedded with troops in Afghanistan's Pech Valley and found that US outposts, meant to provide security for villages, actually became ‘dangerous bubbles’ that drew conflict toward the villagers. Both US forces and the enemy suffered few casualties; it was the villages outside the outposts that truly suffered. This contrast led him to ask: who built these outposts, why, and when—ultimately resulting in his book The Hardest Place.

— Wesley Morgan
10:39

The US military grew heavier and clumsier

From 2006 to 2010, US forces in eastern Afghanistan gradually lost flexibility and mobility, tethered to heavy outposts and MRAPs. In 2006, the 10th Mountain Division could still use pickup trucks and ATVs to reach remote areas, but by 2010, due to the IED threat and top-down force protection requirements, conventional forces could barely reach those places, relying on special forces for offensive operations. This overextension meant subsequent units could not sustain the earlier range of activity.

12:51

Two ODA teams: restraint versus recklessness

In 2004, two Green Beret ODA teams deployed to the Pech Valley in succession. The first, from the Utah National Guard's 19th Special Forces Group, adopted an ‘inkblot’ strategy, trying to establish security around the district center and refusing to enter the Korengal Valley because they sensed something was luring them in. The second, from the 3rd Special Forces Group at Fort Bragg, was eager to enter the Korengal to find a fight, only to get entangled in a local tribal timber dispute, used as muscle for one faction, starting the Korengal ‘snowball’ that eventually became a fierce battlefield lasting years.

— Wesley Morgan
19:27

Three incompatible tribes within the US military

In eastern Afghanistan, three ‘tribes’ existed within the US military: conventional forces, Green Berets (working with local partners such as training Afghan National Army commandos and local police), and the Joint Special Operations Task Force (such as Task Force 373, composed mainly of SEAL Team 6 and Rangers, conducting night raids). These three had different objectives, sometimes supporting each other but more often hindering each other. For example, the task force might kill someone conventional forces could have easily arrested, while only the task force could operate in remote areas beyond the reach of conventional forces. This lack of coordination was a core problem of the Afghanistan war.

Commanders measured success with the wrong metrics

Many commanders lacked true understanding in counterinsurgency and could only rely on quantifiable performance metrics such as ordnance dropped, miles of road built, and number of meetings held. The book gives an example: the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry Regiment, during 15 months of combat in Kunar Province, lost 24 soldiers. Its commander, Lt. Col. Bill Ostland, defined success by the number of JDAMs dropped, ammunition expended, SERP budget spent, and 10,000 Beanie Babies distributed—metrics that could not reflect actual effects and were even self-contradictory.

40:00

Navy admirals' ground combat résumés

Wesley Morgan points out that many current senior US Navy and Air Force officers have a chapter in their careers completely different from the conventional path: they led Provincial Reconstruction Teams (PRTs) in Afghanistan's Kunar and Nuristan provinces, including submarine officers, nuclear engineers, and naval aviators. For example, current INDOPACOM commander Sam Paparo led the Nuristan PRT for a year. These assignments coincided with the fighting in Wana and Narisan, and they went out daily in MRAPs on counterinsurgency missions. This experience became a unique mark on their careers.

— Wesley Morgan
43:18

Military-media relations went from transparent to hostile

Morgan recalls that in 2007, as a 19-year-old freelance journalist, he could almost freely choose which units to embed with, and public affairs just helped him reach company and platoon level. But today, since the embed system ended in 2013-2014, military-media relations have deteriorated sharply, with a new generation of officers at battalion level and below viewing the media as something terrifying. He believes the turning point was not the McChrystal affair, but after Obama's second term, when the military began restricting embeds, keeping the war out of sight, because the complex message that ‘advisors’ were actually ‘combat advisors’ was unwelcome.

— Wesley Morgan
57:52

How Multicam became a mark of elite status

Morgan tells the story of Multicam's spread: it was invented around 2002 by a Brooklyn art school graduate, lost the Army's camo competition (the Army chose the poorly performing ACU), but Delta Force adopted Multicam privately because ACU performed badly at night. It then spread through special forces, becoming a mark of elite status. Crucially, Crye hinted to game producers that this was elite camo, so Multicam appeared in games like Ghost Recon before real photos, building an elite image in the minds of young soldiers.

Glossary

ODA / Operational Detachment Alpha
The basic operational unit of the Green Berets, typically consisting of 12 men.
PRT / Provincial Reconstruction Team
A joint civil-military unit established by the US military in Afghanistan, responsible for local reconstruction and governance.
MRAP / Mine-Resistant Ambush Protected
An armored vehicle designed specifically to resist IEDs and ambushes.
