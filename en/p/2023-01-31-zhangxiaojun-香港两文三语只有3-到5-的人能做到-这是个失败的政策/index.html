Hong Kong's biliteracy and trilingualism policy is a failure: only 3% to 5% of people can meet it

Hong Kong clings to a language-policy target only 3% to 5% of people can reach, while Singapore spent 50 years and three generations shifting from equal weight on two languages to English dominance — at the cost of wiping out dialects and crushing the opposition.

1:00

Only 3% to 5% of people meet the biliterate-and-trilingual standard

After digging into the data, Wong Yu-cheung found that Hong Kong espouses biliteracy and trilingualism, but only about 3% to 5% of people actually reach first-language level across all four domains of listening, reading, speaking and writing. Singaporean research produced the same figure. His judgment: if a policy target is reachable by only 3% to 5% of people, it is basically a failed policy, because policy has to account for the majority. He also points out that when these language policies were drawn up, no one anywhere had yet reckoned with the later impact of STEM education on the economy, and students simply do not have enough time to allocate.

6:03

After the handover, English-medium secondary schools fell from 75% to 25%

Before the handover, English-medium secondary schools made up 75% and Chinese-medium schools 25%, and both parents and students believed English was the route to a good job. At the start of the handover period the government wanted to push mother-tongue teaching, but most schools were unhappy, and in 1999 it drew the line between English-medium and Chinese-medium schools, flipping the ratio entirely so that English-medium schools were left with only about 25%. In 2009 the government introduced fine-tuning of the medium of instruction, nominally breaking down the barrier, but in practice English-medium schools taught only in English while Chinese-medium schools kept opening English classes, and a school with good English results could convert fully to English-medium. The result was that every Chinese-medium school's goal became becoming an English-medium school.

— Huang Yuxiang
8:03

In English-medium schools, teachers use Chinese to teach English textbooks

Wong Yu-cheung describes the real state of Hong Kong's English-medium schools: the maths textbook is in English, but the teacher teaches in Chinese, because the teacher's own English is not good enough. He argues that learning a language well requires immersive education, but in this mixed state students learn neither good Putonghua nor good Cantonese, and their English is not good either. He gives the example that in Cantonese culture some English poetry and lyrics are quite good, and Cantonese opera has excellent Chinese, but students do not learn that either. He sums it up as a situation where everyone is dissatisfied: academics are unhappy that so much money was spent without achieving the result, parents and students are unhappy that they learned less English, and schools are unhappy too.

— Huang Yuxiang
13:04

If my Chinese and English were equal, I'd probably be rather dim

Wong Yu-cheung says that if his Chinese and English were at the same level, he would probably be a rather dim thinker. His explanation: for a student whose talent is not especially high, the language he can grasp well is Chinese; English is a second language, which he can write and read but in which his speaking and listening are still weaker. If he had spent all his secondary-school time pushing English very high, his secondary schooling might not have gone so well, and he would not have been able to make a career of writing in Chinese. He uses this personal experience to look back at Hong Kong: the government is insisting on a policy target it cannot achieve, and the adjustments are made so quietly that people who leave Hong Kong do not easily notice.

— Huang Yuxiang
15:06

Singapore took 50 years to go from equal weight on two languages to English dominance

On the first day of self-government in 1959, Singapore wanted to become a country with English as its main language, but in between it ran a bilingual policy giving equal weight to both languages for a full 20 years, until the 1979 Goh Keng Swee Report formally established English as the main medium of instruction with mother tongue as a supplement. From the decision in 1979 to its realisation in 1987 took eight years. Wong Yu-cheung does the arithmetic on time: a student entering primary school in 1987 would only finish secondary school 12 years later, in 1999, and would not graduate from university until the 2000s; from 1959 to the 2000s is 50 years and three generations. His conclusion is that the adaptation period for a language-policy change is extremely long — one adjustment takes a whole generation of young people passing through before you see the result.

— Huang Yuxiang
19:08

Singapore wiped out dialects, and the price was crushing the opposition

Singapore met strong resistance in pushing its English-first policy, because Chinese language and Mandarin were an expression of the identity of the Nanyang Chinese community, and at the time the main movements resisting colonialism and fighting for social welfare were conducted in Chinese schools. Singapore had a Chinese-language university, Nanyang University, which was eventually shut down. Where Singapore was more radical than Hong Kong was in essentially eliminating dialects: dialect broadcasts were not allowed on public radio, and old people had no Hong Kong TV dramas to watch. Wong Yu-cheung also mentions Operation Coldstore in Singapore in the 1960s, which rounded up all the opposition so that afterwards there was no opposition, and the labelling of defenders of Chinese as Chinese chauvinists, the shutting down of newspapers, and the tying of such people to socialists.

— Huang Yuxiang
22:11

Lee Kuan Yew learned six and a half languages, and only English at native level

In his memoirs Lee Kuan Yew wrote that he learned six and a half languages in his life: his mother tongue was English, he learned Chinese and Mandarin as a child, Japanese under the Japanese occupation, Hokkien and Hakka because of elections, and Malay in secondary school. Wong Yu-cheung points out that for someone as gifted as Lee Kuan Yew, learning so many languages was not easy, and for an ordinary person the question is how much productivity is left after learning so many. Of Lee's six languages, only English reached first-language standard; his Chinese was passable — he could write a little and give speeches, but his expression was not very fluent. Lee Hsien Loong's Chinese is better, because Lee Kuan Yew made a point of sending his children to Chinese-medium primary schools and only to English-medium schools for secondary, but Lee Hsien Loong's younger brother and sister, Lee Wei Ling and Lee Hsien Yang, are basically English-first, and only Lee Hsien Loong in the whole family can use Chinese and English proficiently.

— Huang Yuxiang
33:22

In Hong Kong, the better the student, the less logical the learning

Wong Yu-cheung points out that Singapore has special assistance schools — 14 former Chinese schools where good students can be taught more Chinese, because their first language is already English and good students have more of an obligation to learn more. Hong Kong is the opposite: good students go to English-medium schools and learn more English, which is not logical. Hong Kong's problem is that the better students' learning is more logical and the weaker students' learning is less logical, which makes it hard not to get angry, and they get worse and worse. He also says that 30% of students at Hong Kong's top universities have no problem speaking and writing English, while the weakest 20% cannot really speak Putonghua either — they are new immigrants who cannot learn English. Singapore's approach is to have the weakest 20% learn Chinese, because at least they can communicate and it has economic value.

— Huang Yuxiang
46:38

Hong Kong's top scorers all go into medicine; none study law or finance

Wong Yu-cheung observes the shift in what Hong Kong's top scorers choose: when he entered university, some studied law, some global business, some medicine — still relatively diverse, and in earlier years some studied IT. But what he sees now is that all of them go into medicine, not a single one studying law or finance. His explanation: finance is competitive and depends on connections, whereas medicine basically does not depend on connections, and the starting salary on graduation is over HK$60,000. Law requires passing a professional exam that only about half of people pass; being a solicitor is fine, but as a barrister the first few years pay very little, and with no big economic growth in Hong Kong the litigation pie has already been divided up by the famous barristers of the past, so getting good resources depends on connections.

— Huang Yuxiang
55:46

Hongkongers have an illusion of omnipotence

Wong Yu-cheung believes the most valuable thing Hong Kong can learn from Singapore is a mode of thinking: thinking very realistically. Lee Kuan Yew often said Singapore was just a city of five or six million people with no right to choose what to believe — it could only choose what to do to cater to the world's needs. Because Hong Kong's economy once accounted for 25% to 30% of China's in the 1980s, Hongkongers developed an illusion that they were extraordinary and omnipotent; now it is just back to normal. He says what Hong Kong's government had to do in the past was adjust to changing circumstances — when the Korean War broke out and an embargo was imposed, it shifted to industry; when China opened up, it shifted to services — it did not have to think about what it should do itself. But now the situation is that the gifts that came naturally have been mostly eaten up, and what is needed is planning with foresight.

— Huang Yuxiang

Glossary

biliteracy and trilingualism
Hong Kong's medium-of-instruction policy, referring to two written languages, Chinese and English, and three spoken languages, Cantonese, Putonghua and English.
fine-tuning of the medium of instruction
A 2009 Hong Kong government policy that nominally broke down the barrier between Chinese- and English-medium schools but in practice let English-medium schools teach only in English.
Goh Keng Swee Report
A 1979 Singaporean report that formally established English as the main medium of instruction and mother tongue as a supplementary medium.
special assistance schools
14 former Chinese-medium schools in Singapore that, because their first language is already English, can let good students learn more Chinese.
barrister
A type of legal profession in Hong Kong; the first few years pay very little, and one may not become a famous barrister.
liberal studies
A compulsory Hong Kong senior-secondary subject that Wong Yu-cheung calls an Introduction to sociology, covering today's China, Hong Kong, globalisation and more.
