After 11 Years Sober, She Secretly Drank for Another Year: Willpower Simply Can't Cut It

Weil argued that overcoming bad habits depends on attention rather than willpower—which explains why the author, after 11 years sober, secretly drank for another year instead of being saved by willpower.

Weil's "attention" isn't attention—it's watching without grasping

She cites Weil's line "we must heal our defects through attention rather than willpower," but Weil's attention bears no resemblance to what people mean by focus today—not tunnel vision, not shutting out everything else, but a state of willing acceptance, a posture of watching without seizing. Weil uses a painting as her metaphor: sometimes you need to step back and look from a different angle, sometimes you need to return day after day until one day it suddenly reveals itself. The process itself cannot be forced. Applied to addiction, this means the fantasy "if I just try harder, find the right way to drink, I can control it" is itself the delusion. What actually works is abandoning that fantasy, stopping the struggle, surrendering, and then listening to other people's stories—which is itself a form of attention.

8:15

After abandoning her faith, drinking; after seeking it again, secretly drinking for a year

She grew up evangelical, studied theology at Moody Bible Institute, encountered a faith crisis over "the problem of evil," dropped out and descended into alcoholism. At 28, she got sober through a 12-step program and stayed dry for 11 years—married, started writing. Around 2018, she was inexplicably drawn to Catholicism, attended Mass daily, nearly converted, but ultimately didn't. Then, right after years of telling her sobriety story in the church basement, she inexplicably started drinking again, hiding it from everyone for a full year. The book begins as an attempt to make sense of these two moments of apparent total irrationality.

18:44

The sudden insight is just the final touch after decades of incubation

William James wrote in The Varieties of Religious Experience that religious conversions seem like lightning strikes but are actually the final stage of long unconscious development—"the fruit is ripe, the slightest touch brings it down." People want to locate their sobriety, weight loss, or religious conversion at one dramatic moment, but the actual work that matters happens in the months or years before, beneath "the nag of reason." This matches her trajectory: all those years telling her story in the church basement seemed pointless at the time, but they were accumulating toward whatever transformation comes next.

27:03

Chesterton: Fairy tales' senseless prohibitions hold profound logic

Chesterton observed that all myths and fairy tales rest on some irrational prohibition—Cinderella must leave at midnight, Adam and Eve cannot eat from the tree of knowledge. The stories never explain why, only insist that "happiness depends on not doing one thing you could do anytime, and for no clear reason." Applied to addiction, she argues that the only thing an alcoholic cannot do is drink; if they don't drink, everything else functions. What destroys her and other addicts is the moment they begin to negotiate the prohibition rationally. Reason itself can drive toward ruinous conclusions, so certain prohibitions must be treated as non-negotiable hard limits.

— Meghan O'Gieblyn
31:14

Total abstinence is easier than moderation: hard rules spare you the struggle

Roberts points out the difference between abstaining and allowing one violation: eating zero chips at a party is much easier than eating just one—because once you've started, thirty seconds later eating another slice still looks fine, and before you know it you've consumed forty. She agrees and explains why the prohibition is a gift: one hard rule means you never spend mental energy negotiating whether one chip or two has crossed the line. It preserves enormous psychological resources. She also acknowledges the pattern is personal. When drinking, she had formidable discipline over work, diet, and exercise—willpower simply failed to function only with alcohol.

— Meghan O'Gieblyn
38:12

Everyone worships something: without God, we worship other things

Roberts quotes David Foster Wallace's axiom "everyone is worshipping"—if not God, then money, fame, beauty, or youth. O'Gieblyn describes the same truth from another angle: twentieth-century totalitarianism was essentially idolatry, satisfying the spiritual hunger to surrender will to a higher authority. When people lack a spiritual object for submission, they drift toward authoritarianism and ideology—things that demand equally total surrender. Both worry that as religion recedes in the West, what replaces it will not be Enlightenment reason but populism and strongman politics—humans want freedom and desperately crave constraint simultaneously, and without religion to provide limits, they'll find them elsewhere.

— Russ Roberts
52:18

What truly shapes our fate are the unremarkable moments between choices

Murdoch writes in The Sovereignty of Good that uninterrupted, sustained focused work quietly builds structures of value around us, so by the time a major decision arrives, most of it is already made. She offers an example: a mother-in-law thinks her daughter-in-law is vulgar, then one day thinks "maybe she's not vulgar, she's just direct"—the thought is purely internal, no corresponding action, yet Murdoch insists it counts as a moral act. Roberts adds his own example: every time you leave the lights on at the office because you're not paying the bill, you erode your own moral sense; every small right action reinforces it.

— Meghan O'Gieblyn
1:03:25

The mystery is not evil, but why there is so much good

She describes swimming with her husband and watching young people hurl themselves from a steep cliff, scraping rocks, falling but suffering no injury. She realizes the real question isn't the cliff jump itself—it's gravity. Why do these fragile, gravity-bound creatures not die at random? Why do healthy infants arrive, why do airplanes ascend and descend without falling? In that moment, the theodicy question that haunted her for years inverts: the mystery has never been the problem of evil but the problem of good. After that, evil ceased to obstruct her faith. Weil speaks of gravity and grace: the world is ruled by blind cause and chance, yet beauty, astonishment, and human goodness arrive unbidden and undeserved.

— Meghan O'Gieblyn

Glossary

akrasia
A Greek philosophical term for knowing what you should do but failing to do it, even doing the opposite instead.
ego depletion theory
A late-20th-century psychological framework positing that willpower functions like muscle—it can be depleted and also trained.
theodicy
A theological/philosophical problem: if God is perfectly good and all-powerful, why do evil and suffering exist in the world?

A staged recovery system used by mutual-aid addiction organizations like Alcoholics Anonymous.
