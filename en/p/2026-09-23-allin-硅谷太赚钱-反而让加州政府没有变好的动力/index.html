Silicon Valley Makes Too Much Money, and That's Why California's Government Has No Incentive to Get Better

California has been a one-party state for 16 years — not because voters want big government, but because Silicon Valley's tax payments are so generous that the government doesn't need to survive on results. Gubernatorial candidate Steve Hilton says the price is the highest poverty rate, unemployment rate and cost of living in the country.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:04

The rules aren't written, they're piled up every year

Hilton gives a countable form to the consequences of one-party rule: Sacramento passed 1,118 bills last year, and when he printed them out, the stack was twice his height. "No one is reading this" — do you think Gavin Newsom reads them all before signing? The result is that California has accumulated more than 420,000 regulations, and he promises to cut that below 200,000 within his first term. Who are the bills written for? He says it's for the narrow interest groups that control the process: unions, plaintiff's attorneys, and the people who donate to politicians. That's why California is the highest litigation-risk place in the country, and why starting a company in California carries a 90% chance of ending up in court. Structurally, over 16 years Democrats have taken every one of the eight statewide elected offices, a two-thirds majority in the state legislature, every big county and big city, and a 6-to-1 majority on the state Supreme Court.

— Steve Hilton
13:08

The key is that 26% of undecided voters

What he offers isn't a "Republicans flip it" story but an arithmetic problem: his internal poll at the start of the campaign was 33% firmly for him, 41% firmly for Becerra, 26% undecided. That 26% is the key — mostly people who have voted Democratic but aren't die-hards, overwhelmingly non-college-educated working people who are financially maxed out, and whose top concern is the cost of living. So the platform is all priceable line items: cancel high-speed rail, cut the government payroll by 10%, stop funding the nonprofits that make homelessness worse, and put together a "first $150,000 free of state income tax"; gas goes from $6 toward $3, because the low carbon fuel standard and cap and invest add roughly 150 dollars to the price of gas, and cap and invest money is flowing to high-speed rail; cap the vehicle registration fee at 73 dollars by abolishing the DMV. The polling gap has narrowed from 30 points on primary night to 8 points recently.

— Steve Hilton
19:14

Silicon Valley's money removes the pressure to perform

This is the most counterintuitive stretch of the whole piece. One host cites Peter Thiel's observation from years ago: Silicon Valley is like a resource curse for California — it generates so much wealth and tax revenue that the place doesn't need a good government, and that's also why one-party rule can persist. Hilton adds the evidence: before the primary, Becerra was asked on CNN what policies he would change given homelessness, the highest poverty rate, the highest unemployment rate and a terrible cost of living, and his answer was that this is the world's fourth-largest economy, people still love coming to Disneyland and the beaches, so things must be heading in the right direction. Hilton's rebuttal is that the fourth-largest economy figure includes the size of the government itself — which has doubled in a decade — while net private-sector job creation in California since the pandemic is essentially zero.

23:19

What worked was the content, not the AI

Spencer Pratt is running for mayor of Los Angeles as an independent and got a huge amount of free media off a batch of AI-generated videos. He breaks down the mechanism himself: what actually worked was what the videos said — he went on every platform and just told the truth as he knew it, and then creative people across the country threw ideas into this "machine." "I don't think it was the AI that worked; it was just one piece of a lot of puzzle pieces." He thinks there's evidence for this: most of the people who voted for him are Democrats. He also claims that if the 200,000 registered Republicans in Los Angeles had actually turned out to vote, he would have won.

— Spencer Pratt
27:21

Taxpayers are paying for needles handed out in the park

Hilton and Spencer together describe MacArthur Park: last Tuesday at 10:30 in the morning, with the sun shining in downtown Los Angeles, in every direction you look people are smoking crack, injecting drugs, hunched over from fentanyl. The reason is that four nonprofits show up every day handing out crack pipes, needles and drug supplies, and the money comes from taxpayers. Spencer adds the follow-up: one of the needle distributors was found to be selling drugs at the same time. Hilton stresses that this isn't a case of no one being in charge — Los Angeles has a new DA cracking down on drugs and crime, and there are federal prosecutors going after gangs and cartels — but the scene in the park is the same every Tuesday.

— Steve Hilton
29:22

Do the right thing and you're punished; do the wrong thing and you're rewarded

A Latino couple in South Los Angeles ran a shoe store for 14 years. The store was spotless, and they paid every tax, license, electricity bill and permit that got more expensive every year. They asked Hilton to step outside and look at the sidewalk two blocks away: it was entirely vendors selling shoes, clothing and T-shirts who pay no taxes, no electricity, no rent, no license. That's why the couple is closing. When did it start? In the last six or seven years — in 2018 Sacramento passed a bill legalizing street vending statewide, and cities and counties have no authority over it. His conclusion: here, doing the right thing is punished and doing the wrong thing is rewarded, and the whole situation is upside down. Spencer adds the numbers: 50,000 food vendors, a permit costs just 21 dollars, yet the city council allocated 500,000 dollars to buy them carts, while the unbuilt Palisades reservoir cover would cost only 200,000 dollars.

— Steve Hilton
42:32

Ballot collection is legal, so it can't be audited

Spencer's claim is that it isn't fake ballots, it's ballot harvesting, which California law allows. The mechanism has three steps: a witness signature can be a smiley face or an X; the same witness can witness 43,000 ballots, and there's no verification allowed; once ballots reach the counting center they're separated from their envelopes, the signatures stay on the envelopes, and the chain of custody is broken — so even if you know who these people are, you can't audit it, and a recount just recounts the same batch of ballots. The rebuttal from the other side: voter fraud is a felony and is prosecuted regularly; after Trump lost last time he filed 60 such lawsuits and lost all of them. Asked directly whether he believes he lost because of fraud, Spencer's answer is "without evidence I'd say no," and he lists the 200,000 registered Republicans who didn't vote as one of the reasons.

— Spencer Pratt
47:35

The empty reservoir and the lawsuit over rebuilding

Spencer's house, his parents' house and his neighbors' houses all burned down. Rebuilding is stuck on two things. First, that empty reservoir is still empty — even the one across from his house, the one he thought would never burn, is empty, and Palisades is now supplied by a hose run all the way from Tanga through regrown dry brush. Second, they're pursuing a mass tort suit with 10,000 victims, naming Gavin Newsom's state parks, the city and the city's water and power department as defendants. His chain of allegations: on New Year's Eve there was an 8-acre fire in Pacific Palisades, and state parks should have monitored it per the manual but didn't, letting embers smolder into the known high-wind weather of January 6 and 7; a firefighter whistleblower told him there were visibly smoldering coals on the hillside, and photos that state parks employees produced in testimony show the hillside smoking.

— Spencer Pratt

Glossary

ballot harvesting
Third parties collecting and submitting other people's ballots; legal in California, but paying per ballot is not.
resource curse
The phenomenon where too much resources or wealth actually makes governance and the economy worse.
cap and invest
California's program that puts carbon auction revenue into projects; Hilton says the money flows to high-speed rail.
low carbon fuel standard
California's regulation requiring the carbon intensity of fuels to fall every year; Hilton says it raises gas prices.
mass tort
A consolidated lawsuit in which many plaintiffs sue the same defendant over the same event.
