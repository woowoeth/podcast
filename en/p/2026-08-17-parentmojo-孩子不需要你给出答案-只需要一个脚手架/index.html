A Kid Who Asks Permission for Everything: The Problem Isn't the Kid, It's Perfectionism

A four-and-a-half-year-old boy comes home from preschool and starts asking permission for everything; his mother discovers that when she drops perfectionism and stops handing over answers, his problem-solving ability far exceeds what she expected.

6:02

"Be the best" teaches you "you're not good enough"

Sara grew up in a family whose motto was "be the best." Her parents of course meant well, but no one can be first in academics, sports, music and art all at once, so very early on, "be the best" got wired together with "you're not good enough" in her mind and body. Her anxiety started at 9 or 10 and got worse as she grew up. She knew she absolutely did not want Som to be like that, so she and her husband agreed early on: don't push him to perform, follow his interests. This starting point comes from her own lived experience, not an abstract idea.

7:58

The first thing preschool taught him was to ask permission first

After a year of preschool, besides separation anxiety, Som's behavior changed noticeably: if he wanted to draw, he'd run over and ask "can I get the markers?" Sara's home has a yes space, and materials on the shelf were always his to take freely, so this asking put her on alert. She guessed he'd brought it back from the preschool environment—where you have to get an adult's permission before doing anything. And the relationship she cared about most was this: at home, only unsafe situations get a "no"; everything else was supposed to be a "yes." This directly pushed her to look for tools to supplement his learning support at home.

— Sara
11:04

An immigrant's self-doubt most easily turns into class-signup anxiety

The child had just turned four, and all his same-age peers were signed up for swimming, soccer, skating, even musical theater. As an immigrant, Sara began doubting herself over and over: what if these extracurriculars are exactly what this society requires? What if not signing him up holds him back, and it's all my fault? In a coaching call in the membership course, Jen asked her "who is the child in front of you?" and had her think about her own core values. Those two things became her compass every time she wavered, and they stopped her from convicting herself with the identity of "I'm an outsider."

— Sara
14:56

Perfectionism sneaks back in through the place where you judge learning

Sara used to firmly believe that setting impossible standards for herself was what got her where she is today—and that's true—but the cost was wear and tear on body and mind. She would hold back from correcting Som's crooked stickers in her behavior, yet didn't notice that perfectionism had reappeared in another form: she kept judging their learning explorations as "not creative enough, not deep enough." After Jen pointed out that this was perfectionism, her anxiety and guilt both eased. Now when the inner critic pipes up, she tells herself: "That's perfectionism talking."

— Sara
22:00

The first step of change is just one thing: don't give the answer

When she first joined the Learning Membership, Sara was confused by the concept of "learning exploration" and didn't know where she was supposed to stand. The simplest entry point Jen gave her was: first, don't give the child the answer; the later steps will come naturally. So she practiced saying "I wonder why," "what do you think," "let's go to the library and find a book." A year later this has become second nature, and not only for knowledge questions: when real conflicts come up, she brainstorms with her child too, and sometimes the solution comes from Som himself. This minimal action lifts the adult down from the position of "the one who knows everything."

— Sara
29:25

Give only the smallest step, and the child extrapolates on his own

Sara says she no longer worries about giving a wrong answer or the child not being able to answer. A child's learning path is to observe patterns, form theories, then extrapolate using what he already knows—an iterative process that self-corrects through intrinsic curiosity. In the drone example, seagulls were startled by a drone, and Som asked "what is a drone." She knew this time she had to give a bit of key information, so she explained it's remote-controlled flight. Som immediately thought of his own remote-control helicopter and talked for 15 minutes about roughly how drones fly. The adult gives only the smallest step, and it lands exactly on what he already knows.

— Sara
35:19

A torn cheese lid is one round of problem-solving training

On the way home after grocery shopping, Som tore the seal off a slice of cheese while eating it, even though Sara had told him beforehand not to. She was tired that day and a bit annoyed, and mid-sentence she realized this was a problem-solving opportunity, so she asked: "What can we do so the cheese doesn't dry out?" Som first said "I don't know," she asked again, and the child immediately answered "put it in a Ziploc bag." Something that would have made them both uncomfortable turned into a real mental workout. Sara says it was almost effortless, because a year of practice had already made it a reflex.

— Sara
51:26

The most important thing isn't reading, writing or arithmetic—it's metacognition

Looking back a year, Sara says she'd tell her past self: you don't actually know yet what matters most. A year ago she thought the most important things were reading, writing and arithmetic; now she truly believes the most important skills Som can learn are creative problem solving and metacognition. Supporting these abilities doesn't require lesson plans—only quiet observation, giving him time and space, respecting his interests, and then scaffolding a little from the side. She says if she'd known this last year, she would have been much more relaxed. This is almost the final landing point of the whole episode's method.

Glossary

lantern consciousness
A phrase Sara borrows to describe how a child absorbs information slowly and evenly, like a lantern, and connects ideas.
yes space
A designated safe area at home where the child can freely take items and activities without asking.
learning exploration
An exploration process that starts from the child's interest or question, with the adult providing support.
scaffolding
The adult provides just enough support based on what the child already understands, without doing the thinking for him.
metacognition
Awareness and regulation of one's own thought processes, such as realizing how you think.
