Zuckerberg's Double Standard: Teen Safety Needs Regulation, AI Safety Needs the Market

Meta argues for uniform government regulation on teen safety, but insists the market will sort out AI safety — the same prisoner's dilemma, and he's on both sides.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:05

Glasses are not phones, don't conflate them

Nilay objects to the tech industry lumping Meta Glasses and phones into the same category. To film someone with a phone, you have to pull it out, open the camera, and hold it up to their face — that physical action is itself a declaration of intent, and others can react. With glasses on, you press once and it's recording; there's no such declaration. He thinks the industry is rushing to stuff passive surveillance devices into the mental frame of "smartphone" to dodge a messier question: whether these devices should have an entirely new set of social etiquette. And the social rules for phone recording weren't natural either — society argued over them for years and still hasn't finished.

— Nilay Patel
5:09

You demoted it yourself, so what's the good?

Nilay points to a fact Meta itself admits: Meta Glasses have been used to harass retail workers and women, and Meta has already demoted such videos on Instagram to reduce the incentive to make them. His follow-up is direct — if you know content made with these glasses is bad enough that you need to demote it on your own platform, then what exactly is good about these glasses, enough to keep fighting these fights over them? He also says Zuckerberg claims "we communicated clearly from the start," but the real question is: clear about what? The user's privacy, or the privacy of the person being filmed?

— Nilay Patel
6:10

Users define the product, not the company

Nilay offers a judgment he calls "the underlying truth of all mainstream consumer tech": users define the product. You sell a few million devices, people will use them in ways you never imagined, and those emergent behaviors often define the next generation of products. He cites Twitter — hashtags and @replies weren't invented by Twitter; users invented them first and Twitter adopted them later. So no matter how Meta explains what that light means, it can't stop how people use it. The real questions are how much control Meta has over bad actors who harass people with the glasses, and whether other platforms will follow suit in demoting the content.

— Nilay Patel
11:19

High usage doesn't mean no harm

Zuckerberg says "people use it so much, that shows they like the product," and Nilay cuts him off. He points out the core allegation in social media lawsuits is product addiction, and people compare social media to Big Tobacco precisely because smoking feels great, everyone enjoys it, and it kills you at the same time — usage rates and whether harm is caused are two entirely different things. So from the first sentence he wanted to press: can usage rate alone really measure whether something is good or bad for the world, or for an individual user?

— Nilay Patel
13:20

Punished for transparency, regulation should fix it

Nilay acknowledges Meta has genuinely done leading work on teen safety, and acknowledges a real unfairness: Meta publishes safety numbers, publishes research, does transparency reports, and ends up penalized for disclosing the bad things happening on its platform; while a company like Apple, which on encrypted iCloud doesn't even know what's happening and doesn't publish numbers, gets off scot-free. But he points out this negative feedback loop is exactly what government regulation should solve — if all social platforms were required to disclose teen safety metrics, Meta would be compared against peers rather than penalized for the mere existence of the information. That idea didn't appear in the settlement agreement.

— Nilay Patel
14:24

Teen safety needs regulation, AI safety doesn't

Zuckerberg describes teen safety as a prisoner's dilemma: if Meta unilaterally limits teens to one hour a day, users go to TikTok, no one is safer, and Meta only hurts itself. Nilay says this is telling — Zuckerberg doesn't believe a "safety market" exists; he thinks government must force everyone to disarm together. But on AI safety, he stands on the other side of the prisoner's dilemma: he posted on Threads and X that the market will solve it itself, that trust and alignment will become the most important capabilities distinguishing agents from models, that labs which don't prioritize alignment will fall behind, and that model-caused harm will carry major liability. Nilay's question is simply: why does teen safety require government action, while AI safety is suited to going it alone?

— Nilay Patel
16:27

A trillion in liability still isn't enough

Nilay cites Casey Newton's observation: in the recent teen safety case, Meta's own estimate of potential damages liability was one trillion dollars, and that sum apparently still isn't enough to make Meta unilaterally act to prevent teens from being harmed. His follow-up: if a trillion dollars in potential liability isn't enough, how much would be enough to make Meta take AI safety seriously? Is there any liability number that would make everyone stop at the same time?

— Nilay Patel
20:41

Muse wants to be the next Apple tax

Zuckerberg says Muse's business model is "taking a very small cut of transactions," maybe not even from the side that pays. Nilay says this is aggregation theory: Meta holds all consumer demand, big enough that merchants are willing to pay a small fee to reach those customers. But the problem is that merchants downstream of an aggregator hate the aggregator — Zuckerberg knows this better than anyone, because Meta itself is downstream of Apple's App Store: to reach iPhone users you have to go through the App Store, accept a pile of privacy rules, and pay 30% on most transactions. Apple's App Tracking Transparency nearly killed Meta's ad business, and boosting an Instagram post on mobile also means giving Apple 30%. So Nilay's question is: how do you make downstream merchants feel good about this arrangement, when you yourself felt very bad about it back then?

Glossary

aggregation theory
Whoever controls consumer demand can charge merchants who want to reach those consumers.
App Tracking Transparency
Apple's privacy rule change requiring apps to get user permission to track across apps, which hit Meta's ad business hard.
prisoner's dilemma
A situation where acting alone doesn't pay off for anyone, and only being forced to act together achieves a better collective outcome.
