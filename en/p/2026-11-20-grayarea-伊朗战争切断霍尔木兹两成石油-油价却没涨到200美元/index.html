Iran war cuts one-fifth of Hormuz oil, yet prices never reached $200 per barrel

The largest oil supply disruption in history should have pushed prices to $200. Instead, Chinese hoarding, Saudi bypass pipelines, and strategic reserves held the line. The real hit to the global economy came from diesel, not gasoline.

History's largest oil disruption produced history's mildest price response

The Strait of Hormuz handles roughly one-fifth of global oil and one-fifth of global liquefied natural gas transport. After the war broke out, this corridor was substantially cut off, with supply losses more than double the 1973 Arab Oil Embargo. Yet prices peaked at only $110-120 per barrel and have since fallen below $100. Bordoff attributes this to multiple buffers: a pre-war supply surplus of two to four million barrels per day above demand, commercial and government strategic reserves, Saudi and UAE investment in bypass pipelines completed ahead of time, and an unexpected factor—China's import behavior.

— Jason Bordoff
7:12

China's oil import cuts reflected reserve strategy, not geopolitical coordination

During the crisis peak, China's imports fell by roughly five million barrels per day, freeing up supply for the global market. Bordoff rejects conspiracy-theory framing of a US-China deal. Instead, he favors a simpler explanation: for two decades China has built strategic reserves exceeding one billion barrels (while America has shrunk from 700 million to under 300 million), electric vehicle sales now exceed half of new car sales—reducing oil import dependence—and during the war they paused high-price purchases, drew down stocks, and limited refined product exports.

— Jason Bordoff
11:17

Diesel, not crude, is the hidden price bomb

Crude oil flow through the Strait of Hormuz has largely returned to normal, but refined products—diesel and jet fuel—still move at only 40-50 percent of pre-war volumes because tanker transit is more complicated. Compounded by Russia's status as a major diesel exporter and Ukraine's sustained drone strikes on Russian energy infrastructure, diesel prices are tracking what crude would cost at $180 per barrel. Agriculture and transportation bear the brunt.

— Jason Bordoff
13:22

Energy-independent America still sought help from hostile nations during crisis

The US is already a net oil exporter. Yet this year, to manage oil-price shock, Trump relaxed sanctions on Russia three times to allow more Russian oil through, initially loosened sanctions on Iran—the country the US had just bombed—and during his meeting with Xi Jinping pressed China to increase refined product exports. The reason: global markets set unified pricing. One additional barrel of American oil might be sold to Asia; US domestic oil prices are ultimately a wealth transfer at the national scale, not genuine decoupling from the world.

— Jason Bordoff
22:40

Energy security fears drove faster decarbonization than the Paris accord did

In the decade after the 1973 Arab Oil Embargo, the global economy reduced fossil fuel intensity faster than it did in the decade following Paris. This crisis prompted the International Energy Agency to raise its forecasts for renewable energy and electric vehicle deployment. But Bordoff stresses that motivation is often not environmental: countries like the Philippines and Pakistan that depend on energy imports want to escape the leverage that other nations can wield through energy policy. Solar is cheap and secure; coal is cheap and creates local jobs—the path forward isn't always green.

— Jason Bordoff
27:48

Trump repealed climate policy yet solar and batteries still grew faster than gas

Although the Trump administration rescinded nearly all Biden-era climate policies—the Inflation Reduction Act, EPA power plant emissions rules—the US solar and battery capacity added over the next two years still exceeds new natural gas generation, because solar costs have fallen 90 percent, battery costs 90 percent, and wind 70 percent. The driver is explosive AI and data center electricity demand. For the first time in two decades, power prices have risen steadily, fueling a building wave that happens regardless of policy subsidies.

— Jason Bordoff
29:56

Depending on Chinese green tech is fundamentally different from depending on Middle Eastern oil

China controls 70-90 percent of global supply in solar panels, batteries, electric vehicles, and their upstream critical minerals—far exceeding Saudi Arabia or Russia's 10-15 percent share of oil. This seems riskier. But Bordoff notes the distinction: oil is a flow—you must buy it every day, so a supply cutoff hits the economy immediately. Solar panels are a stock product, bought once and operating for roughly 20 years. If China cut supplies, the impact would fall on building future energy systems, not running today's economy. Rare earths used in magnets and defense technology carry genuinely higher risk and deserve scrutiny.

— Jason Bordoff
43:38

US shale growth is ending and Democrats may get blamed for geology

The American shale revolution lifted oil production from five million to 14 million barrels per day—the largest increase any nation has achieved. But this growth is nearing its end; production may soon decline. Bordoff warns that if Democrats continue taking political stances against oil and gas development, even if they take no action, the production drop that geology will bring anyway may be attributed to them. The realistic path is cross-partisan support for permitting reform—making it easier to build all types of energy infrastructure—rather than trying to force transition by suppressing supply and raising prices.

— Jason Bordoff

Glossary

Strait of Hormuz
The critical sea corridor through which Middle East oil reaches global markets; disruption of this channel cascades through global energy supply.
Strategic Petroleum Reserve
Government-held oil stockpiles mobilized to offset sudden supply disruptions and stabilize markets.
petrostates vs electrostates
The divergence between oil-and-gas-dependent economies and countries accelerating electrification to escape energy import vulnerability.
permitting reform
Legislative proposals to streamline environmental review processes and speed construction of energy infrastructure projects.
