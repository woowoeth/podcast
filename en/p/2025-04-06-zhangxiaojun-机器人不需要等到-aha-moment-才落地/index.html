Robots don't need to wait for an aha moment to ship

Robotics hasn't had the kind of aha moment large models had, but the slope of capability improvement is already rising — every year, every month of capability growth unlocks new commercial opportunities along the way. You don't need to wait for general intelligence to mature before looking for applications.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:04

The biggest variable in this robotics wave is large models

Chen Jianyu separates this wave from the robotics booms of the past decade: the real variable is the emergence of large models. Large models caught fire in the first half of 2023, and after half a year they began radiating into robotics. Before that, the AI for Robotics lineage ran through Deep Learning entering Computer Vision for perception first, and AlphaGo representing deep reinforcement learning, which let neural networks make behavioral decisions in continuous spaces — but neither was general enough. Only when ChatGPT appeared did people see that ‘some kind of AI method can be general enough’, so there was no longer any need to build 100 specialized models for 100 scenarios — that approach simply cannot scale.

— Chen Jianyu
13:08

The hardest problem is that a scalable architecture hasn't been found

Asked what the hardest problem in general-purpose robotics is, the one whose solution could set off an explosion, Chen Jianyu's answer is ‘an architecture that can scale’. He thinks the whole industry is advancing rapidly in that direction, but hasn't found it yet. The problem is both scientific and engineering: the architecture itself is largely a scientific question, because doing embodied AI means thinking about how humans think and how humans learn — ‘a human is a standard, general-purpose VLA model boss; a human is an AGI’. He also judges that the final form of AGI will be embodied, and that large models for language, autonomous driving and robotics will all eventually unify.

— Chen Jianyu
24:14

Say Can matches what ‘can be done’ with what ‘is doable’

Google's Say Can from 2022 solves the mismatch between language-model planning and a robot's actual capabilities. The method: on one side, have the language model produce and score the things needed to accomplish the goal; on the other, use a separately trained Value Function to judge which of those things the robot can actually do in its current state; match the two sides, and you get a plan that both advances the goal and is achievable by the robot. Chen Jianyu points out its limitation: it plans all 12345 steps up front, and if something fails midway, there is no mechanism to replan and correct.

— Chen Jianyu
29:51

After Inner Monologue, feedback needs to be more timely

Inner Monologue's improvement is to let the robot get environmental feedback after executing an action and then reason and correct — for example, if the key won't go in, try another one. But Chen Jianyu's team's 2023 work points out its problem: if some intermediate subtask takes a long time to execute, high-level feedback only comes after the task is done, and all that intervening time is wasted. Their approach uses a VLM as a real-time detector, observing at roughly 10 Hz whether the current task has gone wrong — for example, if a box is dropped while being carried, it can be spotted immediately and replanned, whereas Inner Monologue might only discover the box is gone upon reaching the destination.

— Chen Jianyu
56:23

Data diversity matters far more than data volume

RT-1 was trained on 130k episodes, 700 tasks, 13 robots and 17 months of collected data, reaching near-100% success on tasks it had seen and about 50% on tasks it hadn't. Its important insight is diversity is all: with data volume on the x-axis and success rate on the y-axis, simply adding volume gives a smooth improvement, but remove diversity and performance drops sharply. Chen Jianyu draws an analogy to autonomous driving: human drivers mostly drive in the middle of the road, so the data converges, corner-case data is extremely rare, and without enough diversity it's hard to generalize.

— Chen Jianyu
1:33:21

VLM outputting actions directly is too slow and lacks action processing

After reproducing RT-2, Chen Jianyu's team found the limitations of this kind of VLA: it is essentially taking a VLM and directly decoding tokens into actions, with far too little dedicated handling at the Action level, and the VLM runs slowly — RT-2 is basically only 1 to 3 Hz, a very low frequency for a robot, hurting both effectiveness and precision on dynamic tasks. Their HiRT adds a dedicated Action Policy module for action decoding and splits the frequency: the VLM runs at low frequency, while the Action Policy, with only a few tens of M parameters, runs at high frequency and simultaneously receives visual feedback to form a closed loop. The result is performance comparable to or even slightly better than RT-2, with markedly higher inference speed.

— Chen Jianyu
1:48:33

A unified action space is worse than separating out a cerebellum

RDT scales Diffusion Policy to the B level, with roughly 1M in pretraining volume, making heavy use of RT-X data. One of its innovations is a Unified Action Space: all embodiments share one unified vector, with different segments of the vector assigned to single-arm, dual-arm, wheeled and so on. Chen Jianyu says explicitly that he prefers the CrossFormer approach with different Action Heads, because it's hard to predict how many embodiments will exist in the future — the unified vector might grow too long to prepare, or you might finish training and find the vector isn't big enough. His judgment is that ‘adding a cerebellum is better’: share most of the brain, and the cerebellum part only needs a small amount of data to finetune.

— Chen Jianyu
2:06:49

Training the whole VLA directly with reinforcement learning makes it worse

Chen Jianyu's team tried using reinforcement learning to enhance a VLA and found that standard PPO training the entire network directly doesn't work — it can even get worse the more you train. The indirect method they found is two steps: first freeze the VLM and train only the action head, and reinforcement learning can make progress; then save the successful trajectories, unfreeze the VLM, and train it with supervised learning. In terms of results, pure imitation learning scores under 50, while their method approaches 100, with an even clearer advantage on unseen tasks. He says that in the future he still hopes reinforcement learning can directly train the entire model end to end.

— Chen Jianyu

Glossary

VLA / Vision-Language-Action model
A robot model that handles the three modalities of Vision, Language and Action end to end.
Action Chunking Transformer
The architecture proposed by Aloha; it outputs a future sequence of actions at once and takes a weighted average over past plans to make trajectories smoother.
Model Predictive Control
At each moment, plan a future action trajectory, execute only the first step, then replan at the next step.
Co-Fine Tuning
RT-2 mixes in the original VLM data while finetuning on robot data, to avoid overfitting to actions and losing vision-language understanding.
Diffusion Policy
A training method that generates robot action trajectories using the noising-and-denoising process of diffusion models.
Unified Action Space
RDT maps the actions of different embodiments into one long vector, with segments assigned to single-arm, dual-arm, wheeled and so on.
