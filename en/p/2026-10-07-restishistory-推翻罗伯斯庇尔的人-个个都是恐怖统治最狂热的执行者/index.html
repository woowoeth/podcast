Robespierre's Destroyers Were All the Terror's Most Dedicated Executors

7:18

The Law of Prairial multiplied executions tenfold

Passed 10 June, the Law of Prairial created a new crime: ‘enemy of the people.’ Spreading false rumors, corrupting public morals, speaking against the Republic all counted. The accused had no defense counsel, no witnesses, needed no evidence; the jury convicted on ‘conscience and love of country’ alone, with only one sentence: death. In seven weeks after passage, Paris executed roughly 1,400 people — more than the previous 14 months combined. Conviction rates jumped from 50% to 80%; executions happened ten times faster.

15:32

Robespierre himself drafted very few decrees

Of 608 decrees issued by the Committee of Public Safety in May and June, 90% came from three ‘technocrats’: Carnot, Lindet, and Prieur — each working until 2 or 3 a.m., many sleeping on cots in the committee room itself. Robespierre drafted only 14; his ally Couthon drafted 8. He supplied the slogans and soundtrack; someone else did the heavy lifting.

23:46

After a battlefield victory, he quit for six weeks out of spite

The Battle of Fleurus triumph (the French army's first use of a hot-air balloon for reconnaissance) meant France could no longer be invaded — the war's urgency evaporated. Days later, Robespierre fought bitterly with colleagues over the Catherine Théo case and accusations of ‘dictatorship,’ then stormed out. For six weeks he avoided both the committee and the National Assembly, sulking at the Duplay house, convinced his colleagues had been corrupted. This gap gave his enemies time to organize.

32:51

The wage ceiling gutted the common people's support for government

After Fleurus, Parisians threw spontaneous street celebrations, which authorities shut down as ‘counter-revolutionary.’ Days later, on 23 July, a wage cap law took effect: carpenters' daily pay cut in half; ironworkers in military factories lost two-thirds of earnings. The sans-culottes had driven the revolution in the streets; now they hated the Public Safety Committee and the Commune — explaining why, on coup night, almost nobody would fight for Robespierre.

48:26

Unnamed accusations made every delegate think ‘Could it be me?’

In his two-hour speech on 26 July, Robespierre kept saying ‘conspiracies’ and ‘monsters’ lurked among them, but never named one. The intent was intimidation; it backfired. Every representative wondered ‘Am I next?’, and fear became a reason to strike back. The first to strike was Cambon, the General Finance Committee's head, usually neutral — he stood up and declared that Robespierre himself was paralyzing the National Assembly's will.

1:11:24

The artillery commander had the perfect chance but retreated instead

At 8:45 p.m., National Guard commander Hanriot — released by the Commune, commanding 3,000 troops and 30 cannon aimed at a defenseless National Assembly — held the battle's outcome in his hands. One order and the whole story ends differently. But three hours of captivity had left him confused; he hesitated, saying only ‘Follow me back to the Commune and we'll see what's happening’, and turned his troops east. That moment of doubt turned the coup.

1:14:28

At the critical moment, he couldn't get a word out

Carried into Paris City Hall amid cheers — his chance to rally the uprising — Robespierre's voice was gone. Hoarse, he could only mumble ‘Virtue... this isn't my fault.’ He saw himself as a lawful good man, not an ambitious tyrant; he wouldn't massacre enemies to seize power, so he never gave an order. One by one his troops left the square. By 1 a.m., it was empty.

1:26:38

‘Terror’was a term the winners invented to assign blame

After Robespierre's death, men who'd bowed and scraped before him claimed they'd always opposed him. Only after Thermidor did the word ‘terror regime’ come into use — a way to pin everything on him alone. Yet Collot, Billaud, Barère, Fouché, Tallien — the men who brought him down — were the Terror's most energetic enforcers, not its opponents. English officer Watkin Tench, captured at the time, saw through this hypocrisy.

Glossary

Thermidor
The month in the Revolutionary calendar when Robespierre was arrested (9 Thermidor = 27 July)
sans-culottes
Paris's working poor, the driving force of street revolutionary movements
Committee of Public Safety
The revolution's highest executive body, controlling war and administration
Committee of General Security
Responsible for policing and the Revolutionary Tribunal; rival of the Public Safety Committee
Girondins
The moderate faction purged earlier; some survivors were freed after Thermidor
Montagnard
The radical faction controlling the National Assembly; Robespierre's faction
