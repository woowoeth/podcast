New Housing Fund Rules: Take What You Can, But Don't Treat It as Savings

The housing provident fund is not savings you can withdraw at will — fraudulent withdrawal gets you pursued for repayment and barred from any withdrawal for three years. But the new rules put renting first in the withdrawal order and scrap the rent-to-income ratio cap, so money you should take is still money you should take.

Six months into automatic withdrawal, I paused it myself

After Zhou Yi set up Beijing's no-threshold rental withdrawal of 2,000 yuan a month, she found the money landed and slid straight into another card, completely decoupled from the mental account for paying rent; when her spending card ran dry at month's end, the money was auto-deducted, which only added friction to daily life. Once she confirmed the fund balance earns 1.5% interest — above the roughly 0.95% on a one-year bank time deposit — she paused the withdrawal the next day. Yu Bai points out this contrasts with the advice three years ago to ‘take everything you're entitled to and use everything you can’, and two young colleagues in the department have made the same choice. Lao Qian cautions that this is indeed a low-efficiency use of funds, but most people can't achieve the efficiency they imagine for themselves — people just keep fake books for themselves.

— Zhou Yi
21:12

The provident fund is not savings; not being able to take it out is the norm

Yu Bai walks through an extreme case: a young person loses their job and goes home with no liquid funds, a few tens of thousands in the provident fund, but the home is owner-occupied, so it qualifies neither as a home purchase nor as renting; even if the new rules allow withdrawal for renovation or property fees, if the family has no such needs there is nothing to be done. If you risk it and invent a pretext for fraudulent withdrawal, you will be pursued to return the entire amount and barred from withdrawing any provident fund for three years. So both approaches hold — ‘take everything you're entitled to’ and ‘treat it as forced savings at a rate above demand deposits’ — and the key is the individual's situation.

— Yu Bai
23:43

The new rules put renting first in the withdrawal order

This revision adjusts 20 clauses in total. First, renovating an owner-occupied home and paying property fees on an owner-occupied home are included in the withdrawal scope for the first time; the fund no longer serves only loan-financed home purchases. Second, rental withdrawal used to require that rent exceed a set proportion of household wage income; this version removes that restriction, and renting is placed first in the withdrawal order. Third, flexible-employment workers are formally brought into the system for the first time — self-employed individuals and part-time workers may contribute voluntarily and apply for provident fund loans. In addition, the management center should reply to withdrawal applications within three days and to loan applications within ten days, and nationwide mutual recognition of contribution records is being pushed forward.

— Zhou Yi
30:18

The share of contributions lent out has fallen from 70% to 30%

The figure Lao Qian saw: this year, the share of provident fund contributions lent out is as low as just over 30%, versus over 70% at the peak — a steady decline in just a few years. In other words, the theoretically most stable, lowest-rate, highest-quality lending channel is actually used by only a bit over 30% of people. That directly reflects how low the willingness to buy is. Lao Qian also notes the new rules point toward housing consumption rather than leverage: renting moved to first place, property fees and renovation are withdrawable, thresholds dropped sharply — supporting measures as the property market shifts from an incremental to a stock phase, and also echoing the aging trend: the monthly uptick in withdrawal share began in 2023, exactly matching the retirement of the 1960s cohort.

— Lao Qian
32:20

If you want to buy a home, be careful about withdrawing

Yu Bai warns: the provident fund loan limit is tied to the account balance — in some cities you can borrow 16 times the balance, in others 20 times, varying by city. Guangzhou has several caps, and at application the smallest of the applicable standards governs: for a first or second owner-occupied home the minimum down payment is 20%, so on a 1 million yuan home the loan cannot exceed 800,000; and a monthly repayment exceeding 50% of monthly income also fails. So before taking the provident fund out as living expenses, check your city's loan rules. Yu Bai is also cautious about the buy-versus-rent logic of ‘the rental yield is close to the provident fund rate, only the down payment is missing’: you still have to weigh depreciation, price swings, and how to withstand risk once a 600,000 down payment is locked up.

— Yu Bai
44:05

Flexible-employment contributors carry the full amount alone

For flexible-employment workers the contribution base and rate are calculated the same as for employees, but no employer pays half, so one person bears the full 10% to 24%. The base must be self-declared: below 10,000 no proof is needed; above 10,000 you must log into the individual income tax app and pull your tax details as documentation, to prevent inflating the base to extract a higher loan limit. Zhou Yi ran the numbers: 10,000 at 10% to 24% is 1,000 to 2,400 yuan, and this money is not counted toward individual income tax, but it's a loss if you never use it. In addition, most pilot cities require 6 to 12 consecutive months of contributions before you can apply for a provident fund loan, and most do not allow retroactive lump-sum payment.

— Zhou Yi
47:33

In the public sector, the provident fund is an invisible benefit

Zhou Yi asked a friend with a public-institution post back home, who openly showed a screenshot of the account: the monthly deposit is more than double hers, and the balance already exceeds her savings; the friend lives at home with no rental need, so the fund just accumulates as forced savings or future loan capacity, plus a职业 annuity on top, with bonuses and subsidies all folded into the contribution base. Lao Qian adds: the provident fund is tax-free, and with personal pensions capped at 12,000 yuan a year and taxed at 3% on withdrawal, the public sector already gets an extra layer of tax advantage. On the data: in 2025 the national average annual wage for urban non-private unit employees was 129,441 yuan, versus 71,590 yuan for private units — while working hours run the other way.

— Zhou Yi
52:44

Full provident fund contributions are a lens on a company

Lao Qian's judgment on ‘is a company that pays the provident fund a better choice’ has not changed at all: a company that contributes in full respects its employees and respects the rules, which means it is probably well-run in every other respect too, rather than finding workarounds for every policy. He goes further: this points to not making profit maximization the first goal, to treating people as people, and the invisible returns show up elsewhere. Zhou Yi did the math: on a wage base of 100, you take home just over 70, but the boss pays out over 130, and everyone along the chain feels aggrieved. Lao Qian cites Ford's Model T wage increase — letting workers afford the car and have time to use it wasn't capitalist conscience, it was simply a better utility function.

— Lao Qian

Glossary

Transfer payment
Government spending with no goods or services exchanged, used to redistribute income and wealth across society.
Occupational annuity
A supplementary pension system established by government agencies and public institutions on top of basic old-age insurance.
Personal pension
A supplementary retirement system that individuals join voluntarily and fund entirely themselves, capped at 12,000 yuan a year.
