AI can't play a real person, because it never makes a mistake — the human part lives in the flaws

AI performance doesn't lose on image quality, it loses on never making a mistake: the power of a real performance comes from rhythm, flaws and lived experience, and none of that can be dialed in yet.

Wu Hankun is the actor who understands AI best and the AI creator who understands performance best. This episode breaks ‘will AI take actors' jobs’ down to micro-mechanisms like rhythm, mistakes and empathy — not empty anxiety talk.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

15:06

The director's job is to say the idea clearly

Wu Hankun quotes Quentin Tarantino: when every craft around you is more professional than the director, the director's only job is to tell everyone, in simple clear language, the image in his head. He thinks this perfectly defines the AI creator: the precondition for giving a model instructions is having an image in your own head first — the person stands on the left, does this action, interacts with another person this way. The prompt tricks in circulation and the prompt marketplaces that sell for money, he judges, will be flattened out fast — just as learning every button on a camera doesn't mean you can shoot a good image. The precondition for writing a prompt is having something worth expressing.

— Wu Hankun
19:12

Rhythm is millisecond-scale, and AI can't learn it

Comedy lives and dies on rhythm: a nod, a glance, holding two seconds longer or 0.5 seconds longer, and the story means something different. Text instructions can't tune performance rhythm — tell AI which muscle should move at second 3.9 and it doesn't understand. Wu Hankun's solution is to perform it himself first, letting AI capture and learn his sense of timing. A one-minute monologue he made these two days was all done by himself in single takes: the glances, the muscles, the beat of the tears were all designed, and AI was only responsible for swapping the background. As an actor, he knows better than most creators what should be left to AI and what should be left to people.

— Wu Hankun
23:15

Your own face isn't a moat, your consciousness is

On AI face-swap acting, Wu Hankun raises a key question: if I license my face to a third-party AI platform, and someone uses prompts and motion control to make my face perform something, blown up on a big screen — can that still count as my performance? His answer is no, ‘the consciousness isn't in it’. But he isn't a pure artiste: a streamer licensing out her face, going from one stream a day to twenty, reaching more viewers, is a business that adds value for both sides. The real line to think through: when artistic expression is involved, are you willing to hand the right of expression to someone else. As for whether technical capability will actually replace actors, he offers a different layer of judgment: whether the technology can is one thing, whether we let it do so is another.

— Wu Hankun
28:18

The moment you know it's AI, the empathy breaks

He showed a friend an AI-generated animal documentary; the friend was moved, then learned it was AI and immediately felt deceived. Wu Hankun says AI doesn't itch, doesn't hurt, isn't truly sad, and human empathy has a precondition: believing a person stands behind the work. Liu Fei adds an example: in Project Hail Mary, Ryan Gosling acts opposite a rock, and the audience all knows it isn't human, yet still empathizes — because the voice acting, the plot and the music put ‘the human thing’ in there. Once you confirm the other side of the screen isn't a person, the original emotional connection breaks, and all that's left is the feeling of being deceived.

— Wu Hankun
37:25

Cut it down and only mother and child remain

The script for Population Anomaly was originally a boy from the security-army faction falling in love with a girl from the Earth-defense organization; he found it too much like a family drama and changed it to a stress-resistant couple; found it not dramatic enough and added a gravely ill child; finally he deleted the husband entirely, leaving only mother and child — this child should never have been born and must die, but as a mother she will absolutely not allow the child to die, and she has to carry this child that shouldn't exist out with her. Wu Hankun says a short film can't hold complex drama; at most it can ‘pose the audience one interesting question to linger on’. This chain of cuts shows: the real bottleneck in AI creation isn't the tool, it's whether you can push people into an extreme situation.

— Wu Hankun
40:26

People chasing new models lose from the start

Two months ago C-Dance 2.0 launched and the whole internet thought the era of one person, one film had arrived; two months later a new model appeared and everyone looked back and found it wasn't that impressive. Wu Hankun says something new pops up every month, and chasing new models is a guaranteed loss: if you chase A, that means giving up B, which is certain to appear, so you've lost from the very start. His way of dissolving technical anxiety is to use the tools — keep making short films, and only after using them do you know where the ceiling is. Those online know-it-alls saying ‘learn this now or be eliminated’ are mostly manufacturing anxiety to sell courses.

— Wu Hankun
44:27

The era of waiting for assignments is over

Liu Fei pulls the topic wider: media used to be a closed system — get into a TV station as a script supervisor, as a camera operator, and climb up layer by layer; now the system has dissolved, the ecosystem has become self-media, no one hands you assignments anymore, and you're forced to decide for yourself what to do. Wu Hankun answers, ‘exactly right, it's like that in every industry’. When process-based roles are deconstructed and survival is no longer the problem, the people who can't earn a sense of value are the most anxious. What AI brings is ‘temperament equality’: you used to need to get into a big production to make a film, and now as long as you have something you want to express, one person might get the work made — the question is no longer which tool to learn, but whether you have a purpose of your own.

— Liu Fei
