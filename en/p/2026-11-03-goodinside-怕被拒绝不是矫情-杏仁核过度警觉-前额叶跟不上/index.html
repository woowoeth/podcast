Fear of rejection is neurological: hyperactive amygdala, sluggish prefrontal cortex

Psychologist Sasha Hamdani explains that fear of rejection is not a character flaw but a genuine neurological response—the result of an overactive amygdala and sluggish prefrontal cortex. Logic alone cannot override it.

A concerned comment spiraled into a story of total ruin

During a TEDx talk, Sasha fell on stage. Her husband embraced her afterward, shirt soaked in sweat. When she asked why, he said, "I was so nervous for you." Within seconds, her brain had rewritten this: he thought her talk was terrible, the fall would destroy her career. She admits that in that moment, no words could have talked her out of it—her nervous system had already reacted before reason could intervene.

Rejection is chemical, not just a feeling or flaw

Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) is not metaphorical but a neurological cascade triggered by real or perceived rejection and criticism. The key: even if the threat exists only in perception, not in reality, the body's physiological response is absolutely real. This is why "you're overreacting" fails completely—the person's amygdala actually is firing, their stress hormones actually are flooding their system, and the alarm is genuinely real to their nervous system.

— Dr. Sasha Hamdani
9:19

The amygdala shouts while the prefrontal cortex lags behind

Neurologically, highly sensitive people show two brain abnormalities: an overactive amygdala (the threat detector) and an underresponsive prefrontal cortex (the reasoning center). The result is reactions that jump from zero to 60, wildly disproportionate to what triggered them. It's not a character flaw—it's that the emotional brain is at full volume while the thinking brain is too quiet to correct it.

— Dr. Sasha Hamdani
16:46

Praise for another child becomes evidence she's less loved

When Sasha's daughter hears praise directed at her brother or another child, she instantly interprets it as "they'd rather have that child instead." She stops wanting to play basketball, walks away. Sasha doesn't force an explanation—she lets her daughter's amygdala cool down first. Then she offers unrelated praise: "You had a great idea moving the basketball hoop by the trash can." Only after reconnecting this way does she ask what her daughter actually fears: that her brother is better and there's no point in trying.

— Dr. Sasha Hamdani
27:16

A period at the end of a text signals rejection

Sasha admits to extreme sensitivity to punctuation in messages. A period at the end? She reads it as "you hate me, we're done." Only exclamation points feel safe. She attributes this to how deeply feeling people assign meaning to ambiguity: reply speed, tone, punctuation all get converted into evidence of what the other person actually thinks of her. This interpretation system rarely matches reality, but it runs constantly.

— Dr. Sasha Hamdani
28:21

Three words of agreement get rewritten as pure resentment

A mother texts another mom to ask about carpooling. The response: "that's fine." Instantly she spirals: "Does she not like my kid? Is she complaining about me to other moms?" Sasha's point: separate the signal from the story. "My body is reacting" and "what my body thinks that reaction means" are different things. The other mom was probably just busy—three words cannot carry an entire narrative of rejection. When they talked later, every assumption was wrong.

— Dr. Sasha Hamdani
36:36

The thing that makes her suffer could be her gift

When her daughter worried about a smell in the garage, Sasha didn't say "there's no smell, stop being dramatic." Instead she said, "You're a super sensor. You notice things other people miss." She compares this to great comedians—they catch life's details because they're deeply feeling people. This doesn't deny that sensitivity causes pain. It acknowledges the pain is real, then connects that same wiring to a strength.

— Dr. Sasha Hamdani
45:55

Rehearsing the worst words before they arrive softens the blow

After Dr. Becky returns from a book tour, her children are often distant or resentful. This hits her two deepest fears: that she's been replaced, and that they feel abandoned (a fear rooted in their father's 2024 death from lung cancer, when she was frequently away). They call this practice "emotional vaccination." Imagine the words you most dread hearing when you walk through the door. When those words actually come, the pain drops from a 10 to an 8—still real, but no longer devastating.

Glossary

RSD (Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria)
A neurological reaction to real or perceived rejection or criticism, in which the body experiences an intense emotional and physiological response; common in people with ADHD.
amygdala
The brain region responsible for detecting threats and triggering emotional alarms; when overactive, it produces disproportionate emotional reactions to perceived danger.
prefrontal cortex
The brain region responsible for logic, reasoning, and decision-making; when underresponsive, it cannot override or correct the amygdala's alarm signals.
DFK (deeply feeling kids)
Children with heightened emotional sensitivity who perceive subtle environmental cues others miss and react more intensely to them.
emotional vaccination
Mentally rehearsing the worst-case scenario or the words you most fear before they happen, so the actual impact is less overwhelming when it occurs.
