Global Liquidity Peaks: The More Aggressively You Spent on AI in H1, the Sooner You Get Discounted

A stock market rally needs new money to keep buying. Global liquidity is already showing signs of peaking, and the companies that most aggressively invested in AI in the first half are shifting from being rewarded to being discounted.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:33

Low IPO break rate is not a good sign

Don't take the low IPO break rate in Hong Kong as a sign of market strength. In the first half of this year, the break rate was only in the teens, far below the roughly 50/50 normal level under a market-driven mechanism. That means the participants were mainly short-term speculative arbitrage funds, not long-term investors. Recently, the supply of new listings has decreased, and some IPO-related funds have had problems, but the two newly listed companies still broke their issue price, showing that temporary supply-side controls haven't solved the fundamental problem: Hong Kong also faces peaking liquidity.

12:07

The more aggressive the AI spending, the sooner the punishment

Market sentiment reversed within a quarter. Before Q1, the capital market rewarded aggression: the louder the AI capex talk, the higher the valuation. By the end of July, when half-year reports were released, any large internet company that had made aggressive capital expenditures in H1—especially those that burned through free cash flow—was discounted, and even Google was not exempt. Q2 was the most FOMO quarter across the supply chain; everyone rushed to spend before prices rose, and inventories still supported gross margins. By Q3, inventories are depleted, the impact of price increases returns, and the negative factors will appear together.

15:54

Nvidia is closer to Cisco in 2000

Nvidia's earnings look good, but its cash flow is changing shape. Before the dot-com bubble burst in 2000, Cisco launched a 'rent-to-own' program to help customers finance purchases, recognizing future revenue as sales upfront. Nvidia is now also pushing rent-to-own. In its latest report, free cash flow fell by half quarter-over-quarter, and accounts receivable are far above historical levels—which some speculate may be partly related to the credit enhancement and payment structure from rent-to-own. So Nvidia may have reached a point where it needs more aggressive financial measures to sustain market expectations, similar to Cisco's situation back then.

33:21

Primary market money comes from bullets three years ago

The answer lies in the timing of fund raises. The money concentrated in AI and robotics from H2 2025 to H1 2026 mainly comes from funds raised at the market peak in 2020-2022. From 2023 to 2025, they barely invested, until they saw state capital placing bets when no one dared and making the most money—the stories of CXMT and YMTC, the two big model companies, and the two big chip companies stimulated market-based funds. The mentality is: copy the homework of state capital. So this is not a rational long-term increment, but more like a delayed emotional release.

47:31

New property policy removes households from the financing chain

The key to recent property policy is not to rescue developers, but to detach households from the old financing loop. In the past, buying off-plan housing meant households played the role of financing counterparty in the investment-driven chain: paying first, receiving the home later. The policy pushes the chain toward marketization, so buyers now get completed homes and only play the consumer role, with risk reset to developers and financing institutions. The policy was introduced when home prices were near the bottom, to first stabilize the denominator on the asset side and avoid panic from breaking the chain during a decline. Japan after the 1990s and the consolidation of China's coal and mining industries followed similar paths.

1:01:34

Slow bull is not a slogan; it's about chip structure

A slow bull doesn't rely on slogans but on layering market chips by duration: at the bottom are low-valuation, high-dividend blue chips held by insurers as long-term money that rarely trades; above that are public funds and index funds, serving a quasi-stabilizing function; CSFC and Huijin adjust the index at a higher level; and at the top are retail investors, private funds, and active management money. New insurance rules require liabilities and assets to match in duration, yield, and liquidity, institutionally ensuring that long money invests long, medium money invests medium, and short money invests short—not allowing short-term household money to be invested in ten-year illiquid assets.

1:22:49

Crises grow bigger with each rescue; at least one and a half bubbles

Since the dollar left gold in 1971, global central banks have become increasingly accustomed to directly intervening in crises by printing money, and each action is faster than the last. The quantitative easing after 2008 was supposed to be cleared during the rate hike cycle, but it was interrupted by political intervention, and then the pandemic hit in 2020, so another larger wave of easing was layered on before the previous bubble had dissipated—today we have at least one and a half bubbles accumulated. Central bank governors have learned to print even more money immediately when a crisis appears, and the endogenous result is that inflation, financial asset appreciation, and wealth inequality all intensify simultaneously. As long as bubbles are not allowed to clear, the world may face some form of long-term stagflation.
