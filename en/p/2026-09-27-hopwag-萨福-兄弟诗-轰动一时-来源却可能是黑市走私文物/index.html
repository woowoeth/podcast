Sappho's ‘Brother Poem’ caused a sensation, but may have come from looted antiquities

The Sappho ‘Brother Poem’ papyrus published in 2014 had holders provide three or four contradictory accounts of its discovery; Egypt was in political upheaval at the time, and scholars widely suspect it emerged via black-market looting, with authenticity unconfirmed to this day.

2:01

Sappho's biography is almost entirely reconstructed from her own poems

Sappho was a poet of ancient Greece's Archaic period (c. 620–570 BCE), from the island of Lesbos, only 36 kilometers from Turkey. Biographical sources about her are sparse; the most reliable details come from her own poems: she had a daughter, Cleis, mentioned brothers, but never a husband. She came from a wealthy family and received elite education in Homer and choral performance, later going into exile in Sicily with her family due to factional political conflict. The term ‘Lesbian’ does derive from her reputation, but the word itself wasn't coined to describe her until the mid-18th century—applying it to Sappho herself is anachronistic.

— Marguerite Johnson
8:32

Only 650 lines survive from Sappho's approximately 10,000 poems

Sappho is said to have composed about 10,000 lines of poetry, which the ancient Library of Alexandria organized into nine books, but most of it is lost. Only about 650 lines survive: two complete poems—the Hymn to Aphrodite (Poem 1) and Poem 58 on old age (discovered only in the early 21st century)—plus nearly complete Poems 16 and 31, and roughly 200 fragments, some just single words. Her work didn't disappear due to systematic destruction (like the often-cited myth of ‘Christian book-burning’, which is explicitly refuted), but rather due to the simple precarity of how ancient texts survived.

— Marguerite Johnson
10:39

Most surviving Sappho fragments come from an ancient Egyptian garbage dump

The primary source of surviving Sappho poetry is the Oxyrhynchus papyri, which came from an ancient garbage dump in Egypt where massive quantities of casually discarded papyrus fragments were excavated in the 19th and 20th centuries—invoices, tax returns, private letters—interspersed with references to ancient authors, including Sappho. Other fragments are preserved in ancient texts like Longinus's On the Sublime; the famous Poem 31 fragment was rediscovered in an 1554 manuscript copy of Longinus's work, a discovery that directly sparked a Sappho craze during the Renaissance.

— Marguerite Johnson
16:55

The sensational Brother Poem's claimed provenance does not withstand scrutiny

In 2004, the Cologne papyrus—actually mummy-wrapping cartonnage—yielded a complete text of Poem 58, the ‘old-age poem’, with clear provenance and no scholarly dispute. But the ‘Brother Poem’ announced in 2014 is entirely different: its holder gave three or four contradictory accounts of its discovery and never confirmed the source. Egypt was in violent political upheaval at the time, making looting and black-market circulation highly likely. Some Sappho scholars have included the Brother Poem in new translations, but Marguerite Johnson's own position is clear: she confidently cites the old-age poem but deliberately avoids using the Brother Poem for now.

— Marguerite Johnson
19:58

Sappho invented the concept of ‘bittersweet’ love

When we speak of love as ‘bittersweet’, the concept comes directly from Sappho's Fragment 130: a Greek compound word joining 'sweet' (glukus) and 'bitter' (pikros). In her work, Eros—closer to desire than love—is not a god to be appeased but an unconquerable beast that loosens one's limbs and destroys reason in an instant. This echoes the Homeric logic where heroes' emotional loss of control brings disaster: like her male contemporaries, Sappho views this uncontrolled force with deep wariness.

— Marguerite Johnson
25:10

Poem 1 depicts power imbalance, not a female utopia

Some readers interpret Sappho as depicting a utopia of equal love between women, but Marguerite Johnson disagrees. In Poem 1, the Hymn to Aphrodite, the goddess explicitly says she will ‘violate that woman's will’, thereby making her love Sappho—the same power structure as later Greek pederasty, just between women. The poem may even bear traces of curse-tablet magic: such spells often featured crude language about dragging someone by the hair, a brutal coercion formula.

— Marguerite Johnson
31:40

Sappho expressed relativism about beauty a generation before Protagoras

In Fragment 16, Sappho says that while others consider cavalry, infantry, or fleets the most beautiful things on earth, she believes beauty is whatever one desires—a relativism almost identical to Protagoras's later claim that ‘man is the measure of all things’, yet Sappho may have expressed it a generation earlier. She supports this through Helen's story: Helen abandoned her husband and children for Paris because she followed her own desire, not the male value system of war and honor celebrated in Homer's epics.

— Marguerite Johnson
47:35

Each era reshapes Sappho in its own image

From 19th-century suffragists using her as a symbol of a ‘forgotten female genius’ to second-wave feminists in the 1970s reading her as an openly queer pioneer, Sappho has been repeatedly appropriated and projected by different eras. Marguerite Johnson's stance is clear: readers can reimagine Sappho freely, but scholars must keep that imaginative projection separate from textual analysis—the two must never mix.

— Marguerite Johnson

Glossary

Oxyrhynchus papyri
Papyrus documents from an ancient Egyptian garbage dump excavated in the 19th–20th centuries, a major source of surviving texts from antiquity including Sappho fragments
Curse tablets
Inscribed lead plates used in ancient Greece to magically compel another's desire or romantic affection
Pederasty
Institutionalized relationship in ancient Greek cities between an older man and a youth, involving mentorship and eroticism
Aeolic dialect
Greek language variant from Lesbos and surrounding regions, archaic in character and used in Sappho's poetry
Brother Poem
A Sappho fragment published in 2014 referencing her brothers, with disputed authenticity and provenance
