El Niño is not a universal price button—supply concentration decides inflation

When drought hits palm oil and soybeans (concentrated in two or three countries), prices spike sharply. When it hits rice and wheat (produced everywhere), prices barely budge. Whether El Niño empties your wallet depends on supply concentration, not the weather itself.

El Niño is not a universal price button—it's an amplifier

Zhang Siqi breaks agricultural pricing into five factors: supply, demand, inventory, policy-and-logistics, and financial conditions—weather is just one. When inventories are ample, local production losses can be offset by other regions, so prices needn't spike; when inventories are tight and exports concentrate in a few countries, even a 1–2% global production drop can make markets price in heavy risk premiums. The same El Niño hitting commodities with different inventory levels and supply concentration produces completely different results—there is no fixed percentage for weather's share of price.

— Zhang Si Qi
9:05

Super El Niño has occurred only once per 15–20 years

El Niño is defined as three consecutive months of above-0.5°C sea temps in a specific equatorial Pacific zone; above 2°C counts as strong. September's monitoring index already exceeded 2.5—this qualifies as super El Niño. By NOAA's ONI index, from 1950 to now, super El Niño has appeared only in 1972–73, 1982–83, 1997, and 2016. It creates an east-wet, west-dry pattern across the Pacific; China typically sees south-wet, north-dry the following year.

— Zhang Si Qi
16:10

Palm oil shortages stem from fewer female blooms, not tree collapse

Oil palms are hermaphroditic; environmental stress shifts flower sex ratios toward more male blooms and fewer female ones, directly cutting fruit bunch yield—this is the biological mechanism behind the historical 10–20% palm oil price spikes during El Niño. Natural rubber suffers differently: drought slows photosynthesis and depletes tree water content, so latex that once flowed 3–4 hours daily now flows for 1 hour, compounded by Thailand's 40% of rubber trees exceeding 35 years old and declining productivity. Output comes under further pressure.

— Zhang Si Qi
22:16

Peru's anchovy ban raises pet food prices before sushi prices

Peru is among the world's single most important fish meal sources, with extremely concentrated supply. Its anchovy ban pushes international fish meal spot prices, not the price of salmon at sushi restaurants tomorrow. This cost first transmits to farmed fish feeds—salmon, sea bass, shrimp—where 70–80% of fish meal goes to aquaculture, some to hog feed, some to premium pet foods. The transmission channel runs through invisible feed input costs, not the fish on shelves, and companies can hedge by reformulating recipes and substituting soy or rapeseed meal for some protein.

— Zhang Si Qi
29:22

Supply concentration decides price explosions, not how much it rains

Soybeans are basically concentrated in Brazil, the US, and Argentina (81% of global output); palm oil in Indonesia and Malaysia (over 80%). High-concentration crops see wild price swings from even 3–5% marginal production cuts. By contrast, rice and wheat are geographically dispersed, so equivalent production drops have limited global impact. JPMorgan economists expect global food inflation to reach 5% in H1 2027, notably higher than this period's 2.8%; the UN food commodity index also hit its highest level since late 2022.

— Zhang Si Qi
45:36

Government hoarding to reduce risk paradoxically amplifies price swings

Export restrictions like India capping sugar shipments through September and Peru's anchovy ban, paired with stockpiling, look risk-reducing but backfire: global trade integration has been splintering under trade wars and protectionism. When deficit regions convert global inventory into national stockpiles, the liquid buffer shrinks, and any additional supply shock compounds volatility instead of absorbing it.

— Zhang Si Qi
47:36

The canal's water crisis forces a complete freight-cost rebalancing

The Panama Canal regulates lock levels by releasing Gatun Lake water; May–August rainfall ran 34% below the historical average, and each ship transiting loses 50 million gallons, shrinking daily traffic from 36 ships on September 3rd to 32 by September 15th. The canal handles ~10% of global grain and 17% of soybean trade. Near-term, freight costs face pressure, but long-term rebalancing will find new equilibrium—those truly hurt are specific trade routes that must use this canal or the Asia–US East Coast corridor.

— Zhang Si Qi
50:39

El Niño takes the blame for copper mines' structural decline

Zhang directly refutes the popular claim that ‘El Niño floods Chilean mine tunnels and halts output’: the biggest factor in global copper production is long-term ore grade decline—down 40% cumulatively since 1991 by authoritative measures. Add 17+ years from discovery to first production, and lengthening approval and environmental processes extend development timelines further. Weather's impact on mining is localized, event-driven risk: wet years hinder extraction but aid hydro-powered smelting; dry years reverse both. Effects aren't unidirectional or systematic.

— Zhang Si Qi

In their own words · checked verbatim

Simply put, El Niño is not a button that makes everything expensive—it changes rainfall, temperature, ocean conditions, and water availability across regions. Prices only actually respond when those changes hit a crop's critical growing season, coinciding with low inventories, concentrated exports, blocked logistics, or tightened policy.

Glossary

ONI (Oceanic Niño Index)
Sea-surface temperature index measuring El Niño intensity; three consecutive months above 2°C qualifies as strong El Niño.
IOD (Indian Ocean Dipole)
Climate phenomenon arising from east-west sea-surface temperature divergence in the Indian Ocean; can amplify or offset El Niño effects.
ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation)
Overarching climate system describing coupled changes in equatorial Pacific sea temperatures and atmospheric circulation.
