To Make Criticism Land, Lead With High Standards and Trust

Getting criticism believed isn't about opening with a few compliments — it's about letting the other person see that the standard is high and that you believe they can reach it. In one experiment, a single line — "the standard is high, but I know you can meet it" — pushed Black students' rate of actually revising their essays to the top.

1:04

Identity bias makes people underperform on the spot

When a situation carries a negative label about one of your identities, and the thing at stake matters to you, you worry about being judged through that label. That worry comes from the situation itself: it depresses performance on tests, sports or intellectual tasks in the moment, and it makes people avoid such situations over the long run, which in turn shapes who they befriend, where they live, and where they send their kids to school. The anxiety doesn't come from ability; it comes from the expectation of being defined by the label.

— Claude Steele
3:10

Social media lets labels chase you down

Social media's power lies in rapidly spreading sketches of what a group is like. People have no patience to see the elaboration and the exceptions, so it naturally multiplies the number of situations in which you'll be seen through your identity. Occasionally it also spreads images that reduce threat, but the overall tone skews negative, and the result is that in a diverse society, everyone is more on guard about their own identity and about what others might be thinking.

— Claude Steele
6:16

15 minutes of self-affirmation lifts grades

In racially integrated classrooms, Black students live with the constant possibility that their ability is being underestimated. Teachers asked seventh graders to spend just 15 minutes writing a short essay about the people and things they valued most. White students were barely affected, but Black students used the occasion to let the teacher know "I'm not just that label," and their grades began to rise — feeding a positive loop: better grades → less vigilance about the label → still better grades. The intervention cost almost nothing, yet it changed long-term trajectories.

— Claude Steele
9:20

Vigilance in diverse settings: remember or forget

The author calls this state churn. In settings where people of different backgrounds are together and the outcome matters, people are pulled back and forth between two options: remember that "others might read me through my group's stereotype" in order to protect themselves, or simply forget it, relax, and trust the person in front of them. This isn't fragility; it's real pressure coming from the situation, and when the setting matters, it continuously drains attention that should be going to the task.

— Claude Steele
12:24

High standards plus trust make feedback work

In the experiment, students wrote an essay about their favorite teacher and received critical feedback. Under the first two approaches — giving it straight, or prefacing with a "positive cushion" — white students trusted it, while Black students were highly distrustful. The third approach added only one line: "We have high standards, because these essays will be published; I read your essay and I believe you can reach them." Under that condition, Black students took the essay home to revise at three times the rate of the other conditions.

— Claude Steele
14:26

You can't assume others already trust you

After years as a dean and provost, Claude considers the most useful skill to be realizing that everyone who works with you needs that signal of respect. Society's common mistake is to assume trust already exists, when in reality people worry about how you view the group they belong to. Truly effective leaders are good at giving that signal first: even if an employee disagrees with a decision, as long as they feel the other person respects who they are and their potential, the team atmosphere is completely different.

— Claude Steele
17:35

Let people see you're human before you talk trust

Claude breaks good communication into three things: first, let the other person know you understand what this situation means to them — when power is unequal, the person with more power has to send the safety signal first; second, near-obsessive clarity, because beating around the bush destroys valuable information; third, warmth and goodwill, because people need to confirm that the person across from them is a good person before they'll hand over trust. Matt sums it up as connect, be clear, care.

— Claude Steele

Glossary

stereotype threat
Situational pressure from worrying that you'll be judged by your group's negative label, which distracts and impairs performance in high-stakes settings.
churn
A term coined by the author: the vigilance and anxiety that arise in diverse, high-stakes settings from worrying about being devalued because of your identity.
disidentify
To stop seeing school or career as part of your self-worth, as a way of escaping constant label pressure.
