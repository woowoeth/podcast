Nazi Spy Mission Foiled by a Bunch of Fools: The Only Smart One Escapes with a Weird Trick

In WWII, the Nazis sent eight spies into the US, but the mission collapsed due to stupidity, infighting, and betrayal. The only smart one escaped with a bizarre trick involving injecting baking soda, revealing the fatal importance of recruitment and judgment.

1:18

The sabotage team's fatal flaw was selection, not training

The German military intelligence agency Abwehr recruited nine Germans who had lived in North America to form a sabotage team codenamed Operation Pastorius, tasked with infiltrating the US to blow up railways, canal locks, and aluminum plants. Training took place on a farm near Brandenburg, covering explosives, timing devices, invisible ink, and more. But the quality of the members was poor; most were fools, setting the stage for later failure.

— Tim Harford
4:24

Faking a venereal disease by self-mutilation was the only rational decision in the group

Joseph Schmidt realized his teammates were all idiots who could get him killed, so he devised an extreme plan: injecting a baking soda solution into his penis to fake a venereal disease, thereby avoiding boarding the submarine. The naval officer examined him and indeed refused to let him aboard, so Schmidt escaped unscathed, becoming the only member to walk away. This episode highlights an individual's self-preservation wisdom in a dangerous environment.

— Tim Harford
7:36

Their first lucky escape only emboldened them

George Dasch's four-man team landed on Amagansett Beach on Long Island, but got lost in the thick fog and were spotted by a young coast guardsman. Dasch tried to bribe him with $260, but the guardsman refused and reported it to his superiors. Although the Coast Guard was initially suspicious, they failed to pursue promptly, allowing Dasch and his men to escape by train to New York. This lucky escape only made Dasch and the others bolder.

— Tim Harford
12:48

A spy carrying explosives first bought a gold watch and a car

Herbie Haupt was a German-American raised in Chicago. He left home to dodge the draft and because his girlfriend was pregnant, wandering through Mexico, Japan, and France before being recruited by the Abwehr. He landed in Florida by submarine with $14,000 and explosives, but put the mission aside, buying a gold watch, a car, and proposing to his girlfriend, completely unlike someone on a sabotage mission. His naivety and impulsiveness eventually led to his arrest.

— Tim Harford
22:13

The decision to betray was made on the spot, but the surrender took days

In a 13th-floor hotel room in New York, Dasch threatened Berger to cooperate or jump out the window, and Berger readily agreed to betray. The two decided to surrender to the FBI, but Dasch delayed for days, playing cards and spending money until Friday before contacting the FBI. Berger, meanwhile, provided crucial intelligence, including the handkerchief with invisible ink and Schmidt's existence. The betrayal stemmed from fear of the mission and disillusionment with the Nazis.

— Tim Harford
31:42

The two who surrendered voluntarily were the only ones not executed

Based on the confessions of Dasch and Berger, the FBI quickly arrested all eight spies. The trial was held by a secret military tribunal. Dasch's testimony ran to 254 pages but was rambling; Berger's was concise and detailed. Although defense lawyers argued they had not actually sabotaged anything, the prosecutors demanded the death penalty, and all eight were sentenced to death, with Dasch and Berger having their sentences commuted to life imprisonment for cooperation.

— Tim Harford
33:47

What crushed these eight men was not the Nazis but conformity

The host cites a classic psychology experiment: when a room fills with smoke, a person alone will leave quickly; but if actors in the room ignore the smoke, only one in ten will leave. The experiment shows that when those around you are unresponsive, people doubt their own judgment. In Operation Pastorius, only Schmidt had the courage to break away; the other eight were driven to destruction by conformity.

— Tim Harford
