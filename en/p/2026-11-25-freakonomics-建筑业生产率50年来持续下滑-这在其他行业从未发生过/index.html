Construction is the only major industry where productivity fell for half a century

American construction is the only major industry with long-term productivity decline—not from insufficient capital, not from wages rising too fast, not from excess profits, but from accumulated friction stacking up across the whole industry.

4:16

Construction is the only major industry where productivity fell for half a century

Syverson and Goolsbee found U.S. construction productivity grew from the 1950s through the late 1960s, even faster than the overall economy. But it's been falling for 50 years since. This is unprecedented—Syverson stresses no other industry shows this pattern. Technology normally drives productivity gains that multiply. The fact that construction once did grow means it wasn't always immune; something specific broke at a turning point.

— Chad Syverson
7:50

Rising capital per worker proves it's not money—the problem is structural

Syverson checked all the popular theories one by one. Capital per construction worker kept pace with the broader economy—so not under-capitalization. Wages and material costs didn't rise faster than in other industries. Profit margins didn't spike abnormally. The only real anomaly: construction prices rose faster than overall inflation, yet worker output per person—adjusted for house size and quality—has been flat since the mid-1970s. No actual efficiency gains.

— Chad Syverson
11:18

The incentive structure rewards chasing the next job over finishing this one well

Syverson points out construction's incentive structure is fundamentally broken. Once a company lands a contract, its attention shifts immediately to the next bid rather than perfecting the current job. The industry's money comes from landing new deals, not from smooth execution—poor work still gets paid. This creates no industry-wide drive to improve processes. The profit motive points the wrong way.

— Chad Syverson
14:43

Zoning killed the chance to build at scale by fracturing land into tiny pieces

Glaeser sees regulation as the key variable. Minimum lot sizes range from one or two acres up to 60 acres in parts of San Francisco. Wetland definitions are drawn so broad—one town defined it as ‘a giant muddy area’—that they block almost any project. Together these rules make large-scale production impossible. Glaeser admits the evidence isn't airtight, but this is his preferred hypothesis.

— Ed Glaeser
17:18

Construction's 600,000 tiny firms lock it out of the scale economies

The U.S. construction industry has nearly 600,000 firms with fewer than 5 employees, but only 101 establishments with over 1,000 workers. Manufacturing, by contrast, has 171,000 small firms and 990 large ones—a completely different shape. Land is increasingly fragmented into small parcels. This ‘small sites, small firms’pattern locks the industry structurally out of scale economies. California's push for backyard accessory units is worth trying, but it's not mass production.

— Ed Glaeser
34:50

Katerra spent over $800 million and couldn't rewrite 150 years in two years

Katerra, founded by Tesla's former interim CEO with more than $800 million from SoftBank, attempted to overturn every aspect of construction at once and went bankrupt in 2021. Buckley says they failed by trying to rewrite 150 years of industry history in just two years—money can't solve a structural time problem. Rupnik disagrees: Katerra's real legacy was positive. It proved that modular methods running successfully overseas for 30–40 years actually work technically. Some factories and staff were acquired by other companies and continue operating.

43:34

Singapore mandated 70% offsite construction on public projects to force industry capacity

Hough notes that Singapore mandates at least 70% offsite construction on government projects, and this regulatory demand catalyzed a strong prefab industry. But he highlights the chicken-and-egg problem: the U.S. is only at around 6% modular construction. Mandating 70% instantly would fail because factories can't scale production without upfront policy signals. Companies need government commitment before they'll invest in building the capacity.

— Michael Hough
47:57

Pay double or triple for existing homes, buy them out, abolish density restrictions

Glaeser proposes a thought experiment for high-cost metros like Silicon Valley, Boston, and New York: pick certain zones where government or developers buy out existing owners at two to three times current prices at a generous rate, then eliminate all density restrictions. Allow ‘by right’construction up to 50 stories as long as it meets safety codes. This would draw developers exploring how to build cheaply and at scale, shifting supply toward average buyers instead of wealthy ones.

— Ed Glaeser
