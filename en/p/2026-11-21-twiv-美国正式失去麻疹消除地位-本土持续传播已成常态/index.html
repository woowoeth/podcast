Congenital syphilis has sextupled in a decade due to cuts in maternal care

Between 2012 and 2022, the rate of congenital syphilis among U.S. newborns increased nearly sixfold. The cause is not increased sexual behavior, but declining access to prenatal care in rural areas, restrictions on reproductive healthcare in some states, federal funding cuts to Title X, unsafe sexual practices driven by drug abuse, and shortages of penicillin injections. Broken down by ethnicity, the largest increase was among Native Americans—a classic problem of unequal healthcare resource distribution. If untreated, infection causes permanent neurodevelopmental damage.

9:10

Measles is the canary, not the crisis itself

Daniel points out that measles is not the only problem but a warning signal of systemic public health collapse. The U.S., Bolivia, and Mexico have been declared by PAHO to have lost measles elimination status—meaning endemic, locally sustained transmission rather than sporadic imported cases. Weekly new cases now reach hundreds. In the past, such flare-ups were quickly contained, but over the past two years these ‘embers’ have not been extinguished; instead they've been ignored or even encouraged. There is now discussion of moving up the first dose of measles vaccine from age 1, because an increasing number of infants under 1 year depend on herd immunity for protection but are being infected as community vaccination rates decline.

— Daniel Griffin
11:17

CDC withheld measles death data until Congress demanded transparency

Historically, when state health departments reported measles deaths, the CDC would promptly publish the statistics. Recently, however, the CDC claimed it needed to ‘carefully verify’ the data before releasing it, causing delays and lack of transparency. The chairman of the House Energy and Commerce Committee, Brett Guthrie, wrote to CDC director Erica Schwartz expressing strong dissatisfaction. Daniel believes this signals that the incoming Congress may increase oversight hearings demanding restoration of CDC transparency.

— Daniel Griffin
16:32

Pregnant women exposed to measles need immunoglobulin—but most doctors don't know this

An analysis co-published by NEJM Evidence and CIDRAP shows that unvaccinated pregnant women or those without evidence of immunity who are exposed to measles should immediately receive intravenous immunoglobulin as post-exposure prophylaxis per CDC guidance. However, since measles has long been taught as a ‘historically solved disease,’ many medical students never learn this in school, so when clinicians encounter exposed pregnant women, they may not think to administer it. The analysis shows 98% of pregnant women infected with measles were unvaccinated. Newborns also need immunoglobulin injection at birth; without it, they face acute neurological damage or delayed complications like subacute sclerosing panencephalitis.

— Daniel Griffin
19:43

Screwworm, once eradicated, is surging back exponentially

Screwworm disease was once completely eliminated. Now U.S. animal cases jumped from 4 in August to 3 in September to 34 in October, reaching 55 by November, with the outbreak now spanning half of New Mexico. This parasite historically cost the U.S. cattle industry over $1 billion annually (in nominal dollars). Daniel emphasizes this is not a ‘waste’ but the real cost of a weakened public health system—not just economic loss, but actual suffering for animals and people.

— Daniel Griffin
24:58

French researchers proposed universal Ebola vaccination; Daniel cited hydroxychloroquine as a warning

The current Ebola outbreak has seen development of cross-reactive vaccines and Moderna's mRNA vaccine targeting Bundibugyo ebolavirus. Daniel mentioned that colleagues at a French three-letter agency advocated for ‘universal rollout since the vaccine will likely be effective.’ He countered using hydroxychloroquine as a lesson from the pandemic: when it was deployed at scale without verification, mortality rose by 20%, causing thousands of unnecessary deaths. He stressed that efficacy must be verified with data before resources are committed to mass rollout.

— Daniel Griffin
29:12

Natural RSV infection loses antibodies within a year; vaccine protection persists

A study published in JID tracking hospitalized adults over 50 with RSV for three years found that RSV-binding and neutralizing antibodies from natural infection peaked during recovery then gradually waned. By the next winter season they were statistically indistinguishable from those never infected, and no sustained T-cell response was observed. By contrast, current RSV vaccines still show protective effect in the second season, with no booster dose currently recommended. Daniel uses this as a canonical example countering the claim that ‘natural immunity is inherently superior.’

— Daniel Griffin
