Why used cars are priced like new cars

New-car price increases drag used cars up with them through the "price waterfall"; pandemic production cuts left the car market digesting for years like a snake swallowing a rat; and US automakers simply stopped building cheap sedans — the car you can get for $10,000 to $15,000 went from 5 years old to 9 years old.

3:08

New-car price increases have actually stabilized

Many people assume the car market is still in runaway inflation, but the data doesn't say that. From 2019 to now, new-car prices rose 4.3% annualized, while overall CPI rose slightly under 4% annualized over the same period — the gap isn't outrageous. In other words, the new-car line has basically returned to the broader market's level. The real problem is the other half of the market: over the same period, used-car prices rose 5.5% annualized, beating both inflation and new cars. Today a used car just three years old averages over $32,000. So "cars are expensive" is mainly not a new-car problem — it's a used-car problem.

— Ricky Mulvey / Angel Carreras
4:13

Used-car prices follow new cars, and tariffs pass through too

The first mechanism is the "price waterfall": used-car prices follow new-car prices, and for the same brand and model, when the new-car price goes up or down, the used one moves with it. This isn't new. The key point is that even if a used car was built before tariffs took effect, its price is still affected by tariffs — because tariffs raise the cost of new cars and of the materials that go into them, the whole water level of the waterfall rises, lifting the used cars downstream with it. So hoping that "I'm buying an old car, tariffs have nothing to do with me" doesn't hold up.

— Jeremy Robb
5:17

Pandemic production cuts are a snake swallowing a rat, not yet digested

The second mechanism is supply. The drop in car production in 2020 means that years later — say, in 2026 — used-car buyers have fewer cars to choose from. Jeremy Robb's metaphor is "watching a snake swallow a rat": the low-production bottleneck transmits down the timeline, and when supply is thin, prices are generally high. The car market is that snake still slowly digesting the used-car rat, and its stomach is still uncomfortable. This mechanism explains why, long after the pandemic, the selection of used cars is still narrow.

— Jeremy Robb
5:17

Automakers deliberately killed off cheap sedans

The third mechanism is a business decision, not an act of God. Ivan Drury says the automakers' judgment was "nobody wants sedans, so let's build more big SUVs" — big SUVs selling for $60,000, $70,000, $80,000, even $100,000, while the $20,000-to-$30,000 entry-level sedan was removed from the market entirely. US automakers have all but emptied out the whole category; Ford stopped building ordinary sedans like the Fiesta years ago; the Japanese and Koreans still have a few, the Germans fewer. The result is that an entire vehicle category has been compressed into a handful of choices, and anyone who wants "just a tool to get from A to B" has to wait in line.

— Ivan Drury
6:18

The car you get for $10,000 to $15,000 is four years older

Stack the three mechanisms together and the cheapest segment of the market has essentially been "scrapped." Ivan Drury looked into that range: in 2019, $10,000 to $15,000 bought a car about 5 years old; today, the same money buys a car close to 9 years old with about 100,000 miles on it. This isn't just a change in numbers — it creates a personal-finance trap: trying to stay on budget invites more expenses instead. A 100,000-mile car usually needs more repairs, and the expensive, annoying parts — water pumps, timing belts — will fail, and anyone who can't do the work has to pay someone to fix it. The money saved on the purchase price can flow right back out through the repair bill.

— Ivan Drury
7:22

Auto loan rates have doubled, but inflation data doesn't count them

There's another big cost that the statistical measure misses: interest. In 2019, a borrower with "good but not great" credit could get a 4.5% auto loan rate; today the same person with the same loan pays close to 9%, roughly double. The key point is that the agencies that record inflation data count only the price of the car, not the monthly interest expense. So the official numbers don't look that bad, but the actual monthly payment burden on car buyers is much heavier. Ivan's advice is that you have to run the numbers yourself now: if you can get a very low APR on a new car, that low rate can completely erase the premise that "a used car is the better deal."

— Ivan Drury
8:27

Used EVs are the one corner still on sale

The car market isn't one single thing. Ivan Drury says there's one segment where you can still find a bargain: EVs. He describes EV resale value as "atrocious," driven by misconceptions about EVs — how long the battery lasts, how reliable they are. Those misconceptions push down used EV prices, which in turn creates an opportunity for buyers, and he is bullish on that market. He admits the technology really is iterating fast, which makes people feel that "buy it today and it's obsolete tomorrow," but for many cars, even once the tech is obsolete, it still works functionally.

— Ivan Drury

Glossary

The annualized cost of a loan; here, the interest rate on an auto loan.
MSRP
The official suggested retail price set by the automaker.
