Singapore Is Not a Web3 Hub, Just a Refuge for Global Gold Rushers

The Singapore government has been giving officials Bitcoin to experiment with since 2010, but its small size and shallow technical talent pool mean it is destined to be a hub, not a center. What is truly valuable is its mechanism of running government like a business and reimbursing startups 70% of exhibition costs.

3:06

This gold rush is not the first, and it won't be the last

Han Yidong has been in Singapore for 16 years and has lived through several gold rushes. The earliest was the Nanyang generation, then industrial companies going overseas, and later the spillover of Chinese internet companies from 2016 to 2018—shared bikes, P2P cash loans, and tool and gaming companies pouring out. He specifically distinguishes two driving forces: one is spillover, where a saturated domestic market forces companies to find a way out; the other is being forced onto the mountain, like cash loans that can no longer operate in China, but the team is still there, so they can only move from Singapore to Indonesia, India, or even Latin America. This Web3 wave started at the end of 2021, and the biggest difference from before is that it's not just startups coming, but also a large number of financially free internet people.

— Han Yidong
10:11

During Token2049 week, Singapore had over 100 events

Han Yidong pinpoints the peak of Web3 in Singapore at last September's Token2049. It wasn't just a two-day event; from early September there was a series of Web3 events, and by the week of Token2049, according to incomplete statistics, there were over 100 events, including after-parties hosted by companies and technical talks for developers. He says this scene is unprecedented in Singapore, at least he hasn't seen it in his 16 years here. Three factors combined to fuel this boom: loose policies, network effects, and an influx of capital. But he also gives a time marker—before April-May last year, fundraising was still easy, but afterwards, with the global economic downturn, rising interest rates, and a string of crypto scandals, fundraising is now extremely, extremely difficult.

— Han Yidong
13:13

FTX and Terra proved that centralization is the problem

Han Yidong says the eerie thing about the crypto world last year was that ‘things you only heard rumors about, you actually experienced firsthand’: the Terra wave brought USDT close to zero, then FTX, the world's second-largest exchange, also nearly went to zero. His investor had put $200 million into FTX, and the returns were edited down to zero. But he wasn't surprised; instead, he thinks it objectively proves the core logic of Web3—these problematic exchanges and platforms manage assets and transactions in a relatively centralized way, users don't know what's happening internally, and the data isn't transparent. He says if you want to do Web3, you have to do the core of Web3, not use Web3 as a cover and operate in the traditional Web2 way.

— Han Yidong
24:20

Ethereum does 15 transactions per second, Visa does 24,000

Han Yidong divides Web3's development into three stages: Bitcoin, Ethereum, and the massive user influx brought by Ethereum scaling. He gives a very concrete comparison—Ethereum can only handle an average of 15 requests per second. If you use it for payments, you can only process 15 transactions per second, which cannot support billions of users globally. But now there are related technologies that can handle 10,000 requests per second, while the world's largest credit card, Visa, has a capacity of only 24,000 per second. He believes this milestone means more users can come in and use this infrastructure, leading to larger-scale user adoption and entry into Web3.

— Han Yidong
33:29

The Singapore government has never said it wants to be a Web3 hub

Han Yidong says he has never heard the Singapore government come out and say it wants to be a Web3 hub, and a society like Singapore can hardly become a hub. There are three reasons: the economy is too small to incubate things like an independent economy—it's just an experimental ground; Web3 needs more users to scale, and Singapore doesn't have that base; continuous applications and innovations require a large number of technical people, which Singapore also lacks. He thinks Singapore will become a very important hub, but calling it a center is a stretch, and the Singapore government wouldn't be so blindly arrogant. He also doesn't feel the government's attitude has changed; it's just that as its understanding of Web3 deepens, regulatory policies are becoming clearer.

— Han Yidong
38:33

The government reimburses startups 70% of exhibition costs

Han Yidong says the Singapore government strongly encourages locals to start businesses because the country has no resources, only people. When he first started a music industry business, he received 100,000 Singapore dollars, about 2.5 million RMB, given free by the government. For his second startup, the government invested 300,000 Singapore dollars for 15% equity, but the exit conditions were very simple—any investor could buy that 15% at the valuation at which they invested, so it wasn't really an investment. Now his company is going to Vietnam for an Ethereum developer conference, and 70% of the entire conference cost is covered by the government, 30% by themselves, and they haven't hit the cap yet. The government also covers the cost of hiring locally when you enter a new market. He jokes that in Singapore, if you don't want to start a business, you can ‘do a crash project’ targeting the government to get money from them.

— Han Yidong
43:37

They did short video four or five years before Toutiao, then shut it down

Han Yidong's second project was called SNIPIT, the first short video in Asia-Pacific, four or five years ahead of Toutiao, with exponential growth in Thailand, possibly faster than Toutiao's early days. But the company was eventually shut down. He attributes the main reason to the lack of a good investment environment in early Southeast Asian internet—without actual revenue, investors would question when there would be revenue and when they would break even, which was unimaginable in China at the time. At that stage, as long as you had user growth and could be in the top three domestically, investors were willing to put in money. A more specific detail: LINE's angel investor wanted to invest, but the Singapore government's investment arm said it didn't want to put in more money because it couldn't see the possibility of breaking even. The investor said, fine, you don't have to invest, but you can dilute your shares, right? The government investor said, we won't dilute, we insist on 30%. The investor found it unbelievable and didn't continue investing.

— Han Yidong
1:10:59

Singapore officials really have KPIs, and they contact you on WeChat 24/7

Han Yidong says the Singapore government runs the country and government like a business, and the most direct example is that officials really have KPIs. When he was at PingCAP and wanted to put the Asia-Pacific headquarters in Singapore, the Economic Development Board came to them with preferential policies, followed up very closely, and also had people in China. Their communication was on WeChat, available 24/7 for contact and replies. He says he has been to so many countries, and no country is willing to give him their WeChat or WhatsApp contact. On high official salaries, he remembers the Prime Minister's salary is 2 million Singapore dollars, about 10 million RMB, at the level of a multinational corporate executive. Middle and senior officials are also close to corporate levels, so corporate executives who want to go into government at least don't have to worry too much about salary. High pay also relatively ensures cleanliness, and given the small society, once you make a mistake, you basically have no chance to rise.

— Han Yidong

Glossary

Token2049
A global Web3 industry conference held in Singapore, which reached its peak of popularity last September.
Terra
An algorithmic stablecoin project that collapsed in 2022, causing many investors to lose everything.
FTX
The world's second-largest cryptocurrency exchange, which went bankrupt in 2022, causing heavy losses for investors.
Temasek
Singapore's sovereign fund, operating like a VC with extremely large investment scale, check sizes of tens of millions or more.
Sandbox
A regulatory mechanism that allows emerging industries to experiment within limited scope; Singapore uses it to observe Web3's impact.
