The Princes in the Tower murder: Richard III and Henry VII as suspects

The timeline of the two young princes' disappearance into the Tower of London is clear, but who killed them remains unsettled—Richard III had most to gain, Henry VII had equal motive, and some scholars argue one survived far longer than believed.

3:05

The princes were teenagers when they vanished, not toddlers

These brothers are routinely depicted as infants, but Edward V was 12 years old and Richard was about to turn 10. Their father Edward IV's secret marriage to Elizabeth Woodville was contested from the start; when the marriage became public, rumors immediately spread that it was illegitimate and the children had no inheritance rights—suspicions that would later serve as the legal basis for Richard III's claim to the throne.

— Holly Frey
9:13

Richard's feud with the Woodvilles long preceded the Tower

Richard's animosity toward the Woodville family was not recent opportunism but rooted in history. His brother George, Duke of Clarence, had been drawn into a plot born of resentment at Woodville power and was executed in 1478—an execution Richard blamed on Elizabeth. This deep grievance explains why Richard moved to consolidate control so rapidly after Edward IV died, and was not simply the natural response to a power vacuum.

— Tracy V. Wilson
18:31

Putting both brothers in the Tower shifted its purpose from custody to execution

The Duke of Buckingham persuaded the Queen Mother to send Richard's younger brother to the Tower as well, justifying it with reasons that sound reasonable: company for his older brother, the Tower being the safest location, the awkwardness of the boy's absence from his own coronation. But the episode points out the darker logic: perhaps the point was simply to get both boys in one place. It exposes how the Tower's purpose slipped from 'royal safekeeping' to 'site of imprisonment and execution.'

— Holly Frey
18:50

A phantom pre-contract stripped the young king of legitimacy

Before Edward V's coronation was to take place, someone—according to some accounts, Bishop Stillington of Bath and Wells—came forward to claim that Edward IV had been bound by a prior marriage contract to Eleanor Talbot before marrying Elizabeth, which would void Edward V's legitimacy as heir. Many observers at the time dismissed this as a convenient fiction Richard invented on the spot, yet Parliament accepted the claim and used it to justify transferring the crown to Richard.

25:43

The princes did not vanish at once but gradually disappeared from view

Contemporary records place the last sighting of the two boys playing with bows and arrows in the garden on June 16. After that date, the near-contemporary chronicler Mancini reports that they "day by day began to be seen more rarely behind the bars and windows until at length they ceased to appear altogether"—the closest thing to eyewitness testimony from the period, suggesting they had already been killed.

— Tracy V. Wilson
32:56

Everything about the princes' deaths comes from one man's account

The specific particulars of how the princes died—smothered, then buried under a stone heap beneath the stairs—come entirely from the pen of Thomas More, a prominent Tudor official. The Tudor dynasty had an obvious incentive to cast Richard III as a regicide and killer of nephews, to justify Henry VII's violent takeover of his throne. Shakespeare's Richard III later solidified this version in popular memory.

— Tracy V. Wilson
33:59

Henry VII's claim to the throne required the princes to die

For Henry VII to marry Elizabeth of York and legitimize her claim, he would need a single act of Parliament—but that act would also automatically restore legitimacy to her brothers. As long as they lived, Henry's claim to the throne could be challenged. Some historians argue the princes remained imprisoned in the Tower for years after 1483, until Henry VII came to power, when he finally had them killed—not in 1483, but after his own ascension.

— Tracy V. Wilson
38:05

The 1933 examination of bones was designed to confirm, not to investigate

Two child skeletons discovered in 1674 beneath the White Tower stairs were immediately identified as the two princes, but the 1933 re-examination has drawn criticism for seeming ‘designed to confirm these were the princes rather than genuinely investigating the truth.’ The examination failed to employ methods already available at the time and cannot be called rigorous by any standard—not until 2018 did researchers begin attempting genuine verification using mitochondrial DNA analysis.

— Tracy V. Wilson

Glossary

pre-contract
A formal, public pledge of marriage recognized by late medieval English law as equivalent to matrimony; could be invoked to declare a subsequent marriage invalid and any children from it illegitimate.
Titulus Regius
A 1484 act of Parliament that legitimized Richard III's claim to the throne and declared Edward IV's children by Elizabeth Woodville to be bastards.
