Minié rifles and French cliffs won the Alma, not generals' orders

The Franco-British alliance won the Alma through the Highland regiment's Minié rifles and a French flank assault up the cliffs, not through Raglan or Saint-Arnaud's battlefield decisions—Raglan was trapped on an isolated hilltop throughout and could give no orders.

11:27

The allied army did almost no reconnaissance or logistics preparation before attacking Crimea

The allies' only goal was destroying the Russian Black Sea Fleet at Sevastopol and leaving, but before departure they had no intelligence on Russian troop numbers, Crimean geography, fortress artillery, or even weather. They read a few travel memoirs, decided Crimean winters were mild, brought only summer tents with no winter kit, and gambled they could take it in a week.

Raglan's hat-box joke about locking the Light Brigade became the whole conflict's setup

Raglan feared horses and riders would suffer severe disease losses, so he forbade the Light Brigade from reconnaissance patrols. He joked about putting them "in a hat box." This throwaway line became catastrophic: Cardigan wanted battle, the cavalry had nothing to do, resentment accumulated daily, and it planted the emotional powder keg that would explode in the Light Brigade charge days later.

— Tom
27:08

The cavalry suffered a shameful retreat but blamed the wrong man

At the Bulganak River, the Light Brigade was formed up to charge Cossack cavalry when Raglan ordered retreat. It was actually the right call—from his high position, Raglan saw Russian infantry and artillery in ambush behind them. But the cavalry couldn't see this. They only felt humiliated in front of Russian and British troops, so they vented all their rage at Lucan, and the mocking nickname "Lord Look-on" was born and spread through the army.

— Tom
35:30

The Alma was a competition in which general could make the more fatal error

Historian Cecil Woodham-Smith writes that the battle was won by the ferocity of British soldiers, not their generals—both commanders' incompetence reached the "Crimean War-specific madness" that characterized the conflict. Raglan had no real battle plan, just vague orders to advance. Menshikov miscalculated that the seaside cliffs were unclimbable, and these two fatal miscalculations together determined the outcome.

— Tom
38:35

French soldiers hand-climbed a cliff and broke the Russian left wing

Russian commander Menshikov assumed the coastal cliff was impassable, so left it weakly defended. Algerian troops led by Pierre Bosquet—expert climbers—scaled the cliff anyway, fixed ropes to haul up large French numbers, and drove directly into the Russian left. This is what sent Russian officers' wives fleeing in panic from their riverside picnic and broke the entire battle.

— Tom
40:40

The Minié rifle's first battle—firepower equivalent to half a dozen machine guns

The Highland regiment, commanded by Colin Campbell (a man who rose by merit rather than buying his commission), used the Minié rifle in combat for the first time. Its range, accuracy, and penetration far exceeded the Russian smoothbore musket. Historian Orlando Figes describes the volley fire as "equivalent to half a dozen machine guns"—this is what let British forces outnumber the enemy, uphill, into the teeth of superior Russian numbers.

— Tom
51:05

A week of hesitation after victory let Sevastopol escape

After the Alma, Sevastopol was nearly undefended—only 5,000 Russian troops, and the landward walls were crumbling. Menshikov himself thought it indefensible and ordered withdrawal. But the allies didn't press their advantage. They lingered on the battlefield tending to casualties, and by the time they moved, Russian crews had repaired the defenses day and night and sunk seven ships to block the harbor. What should have been a few days of assault became one of history's longest sieges.

— Tom
1:03:37

25,000 Russian troops massed—the Balaclava battle is about to break

By October 24, Russians had assembled 25,000 troops, infantry and cavalry plus 78 guns, just outside the Causeway Heights. The goal was simple: seize Balaclava's port and cut the allies' supply line, collapsing the Sevastopol siege without direct assault. When Russian artillery opened up on the redoubts before dawn, Cardigan was still asleep on his yacht—the Battle of Balaclava had begun.

— Tom

Glossary

Minié rifle
A newly issued rifled musket (from 1853) with far greater range and accuracy than smoothbore muskets; first used in combat at the Alma.
redoubt
A temporary small fortified position, typically built to mount artillery and hold a defensive line.
Lord Look-on
A scornful nickname for Lucan that plays on his name; 'Look-on' suggests passivity when he should have fought.
