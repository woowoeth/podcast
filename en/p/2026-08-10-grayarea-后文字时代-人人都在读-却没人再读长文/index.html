The Postliterate Age: Everyone Is Reading, No One Reads Long Texts Anymore

The share of Americans who read a book or an article on a given day has fallen 40% in twenty years; more alarming than a literacy crisis is the postliterate age — abstract reasoning, education and political communication are exiting along with long-form reading.

4:12

We read more than ever, but can't get through long texts

Rose distinguishes the literacy crisis from postliteracy: a literacy crisis is a breakdown in fundamentals like decoding words and understanding short sentences, whereas postliteracy is text no longer serving as the primary channel for information, news, entertainment and cultural connection. The problem, she says, is not that people "can't read," but that they read so many short fragments every day that the time once reserved for long, complex texts is squeezed out; retirees, college graduates and women — historically the groups that read the most — are collapsing at the same rate.

10:00

Linear argument is not innate; literacy gave it to us

In Orality and Literacy, Walter Ong records Soviet researchers' tests on Uzbek peasants: literate peasants could answer the syllogism about what color polar bears are, while illiterate peasants refused to reason on the grounds that "I have never been to the North." Rose notes these experiments have not always replicated since, but Ong's larger judgment holds: literate cultures value linear argument with evidence and rebuttal, and that way of thinking does not exist naturally in purely oral societies.

— Rose Horowitch
14:32

Reading rates fell for twenty years, and reasoning fell with them

The mechanism cognitive science offers is that the brain gets good at whatever you practice over time. The less you read, the less background knowledge you have, the lower your tolerance for long texts, and the more difficult and unpleasant reading feels. Rose cites research showing that American adults' ability to solve logic puzzles, analyze patterns and reason has declined over the past twenty years alongside reading rates — the brain is simply adapting to what we no longer ask it to do.

— Rose Horowitch
19:59

Schools are personally accelerating the exit of long reading

On the education side, surveys of middle and high school teachers show only 0 to 4 books assigned per year, with classrooms increasingly using short informational excerpts to prepare for standardized tests; 80% of elementary school teachers say students receive a school-issued tablet or Chromebook before they even start kindergarten. The effect carries into university: professors have to teach students how to read, how to recognize irony and metaphor, before they can teach content. Schools are both responding to the trend and personally accelerating the exit of long-form reading.

— Rose Horowitch
30:00

Trump will not be the last postliterate president

Rose says Trump is the first postliterate president: he speaks and contradicts himself as if there were no historical record, leans heavily on repetition and emotional language, and introduces people with fixed epithets like "Sleepy Joe" and "Crooked Hillary" that are nearly Homeric. Media theorists long predicted such a figure would appear — short video and social media algorithms reward simple, inflammatory, memorable expression — so Trump will not be the last.

— Rose Horowitch
42:24

The brain isn't broken, and attention can be trained back

Neuroscientists told Rose there is no permanent change or damage to the brain; if you want to, you can still retrain sustained focus. She offers one concrete sample: after the Dallas, Texas school district banned phones on campus, library checkouts were 200,000 books higher than the year before the ban. She also notes that paper books make it easier to reread and remember than ordinary digital devices, because readers have a spatial sense of "which page the content is on."

— Rose Horowitch
45:00

Socrates feared writing would destroy memory, and he was right

Facing the objection that every new technology provokes panic, Rose responds with Plato's Phaedrus: Socrates worried that writing would destroy memory, making people "think they know" without truly internalizing anything. Her rereading is this: later generations often use this example to prove the worry was overblown, but looking back, Socrates and Plato were right — no one today can recite the Iliad or the Odyssey from memory. Still, literacy and writing remain worthwhile, and new media have real benefits; the key is being clear about the trade-offs.

Glossary

postliterate
A media-theory term: a society still surrounded by text, but in which long-form reading is no longer the primary vehicle for information and ideas.
secondary orality
Walter Ong's term: oral-culture traits reproduced through radio, television and the internet, while literacy remains the underlying substrate.
syllogism
Deductive reasoning in which two premises necessarily yield a conclusion, as in "all polar bears are white."
epithet
An epic-style fixed label, such as "Sleepy Joe," built for memorability and repetition.
