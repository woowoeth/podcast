Same development cost, but scale crushes: Costco can't catch up to Sam's Club in China

Sam's invested in supply chain and product development early, turning it into a scale advantage; Costco pays equal development costs but suppliers get only a fraction of Sam's order volumes—money can't bridge that gap.

9:03

First-mover wins scale: same development cost, ten times the order volume

Sam's and Costco pay identical product development costs—testing, labor, recipe sampling—but Sam's suppliers might receive 60–100 million yuan orders per SKU while Costco suppliers get only 5–10 million yuan. Fixed costs amortize differently across volume; Sam's spreads them over larger orders while Costco can't. Sam's also cherry-picked the best suppliers first; Costco works with whoever's left. That gap can't be closed with spending.

— Wang
19:45

Supermarkets once thrived on rental fees, not sales margins

Traditional Chinese supermarkets inherited a Taiwan retail model: checkout codes, entry fees, promotion fees, poster fees—sometimes 500–1,000 yuan per SKU per store, plus holiday surcharges. Traditional hypermarkets' gross margins hit 20–30%, nearly double Sam's, but most came from supplier fees, not actual retail spread. Warehouse clubs like Sam's collect almost none of these fees and make profit entirely on sales margins, forcing them to genuinely care who the customer is and what they need—not who pays to get shelved.

— Wang
29:12

Retail has no secrets; excellence gets copied immediately

As Buffett and Munger note, retail is hard and has few secrets—when you do it well, competitors copy. Today's new Chinese supermarkets increasingly look alike: richer assortments, better visuals, smoother flow. Jinglilimen's fresh presentation, Sam's warehouse style—everyone borrows them. As SKU inventories converge and supplier rosters become transparent, 200–300 SKUs are easily replicated. Competition ultimately hinges on operational efficiency and cost discipline, not innovation.

— Wang
33:15

Jinglilimen undersells Sam's Club on the exact same mochi snack

Jinglilimen and Jiduo Qi (几多鸡), both based in Changsha, drained foot traffic from Sam's at Hangzhou's Xixi Impression City this year. Price checks show Jinglilimen's mochi at 1.1 yuan per piece—cheaper than Sam's. Shelf visuals and T-shaped transparent box design are also superior. The gap suggests Sam's supply chain isn't optimized: the company relies on a fixed roster of suppliers that rarely admit newcomers, leaving room for flexible competitors to hit lower prices and better aesthetics. These short-shelf-life models without membership fees are called ‘Sam's without the card.’

— Wang
49:17

China's Aldi is not America's low-income discount store

Aldi operates in rural-urban fringe zones in the US and Germany; in New York, you see wilted cabbage, serving lower-income shoppers. China's Aldi is entirely different: it sits inside malls facing white-collar workers and young professionals. Bowl combos, salads, cold desserts—better executed than traditional hypermarkets. It's best framed as a middle-class budget store, closer to Trader Joe's than American Aldi. Over 90% private label, transitioning from raw ingredients to authentic taste—partnering with heritage brands to restore traditional flavors.

— Neng Yi
1:06:22

Developing a private label product costs 150,000 yuan and four months

Creating a new PB product for retailers like Hema (盒马) takes three to four months from concept to launch. Direct project labor alone runs 150,000 yuan, not counting testing, prototyping, packaging, and later promotion. This barrier means factories without R&D teams and supply chain depth can't take the order. Retailers now demand you define products, not just pay for shelf space. Industry convention suggests a 70:30 or 80:20 ratio of classics to novelties; but novelties cost equally to develop and scale slower, creating hard ROI tradeoffs.

— Wang
1:11:25

Walmart's private labels look polished but give no reason to repeat

Chengdu's new Woji Select (沃集选) shows sophisticated curation, naming, value propositions, and visual design—excellent seeding on Little Red Book and Douyin. But it's missing one thing: a reason to come back. Name one Walmart private label that's exclusive to Walmart, one you must buy there. His actual shopping: olive oil and beef at Sam's Club, or vegetables and Hunan smoked salt-cured pork at Ding Ding (丁盯) because the latter's range is complete and flavors are unbeatable and irreplaceable. Walmart's classic PLs match competitors closely—first purchase driven by trend content, but repurchase doesn't stick.

— Wang
1:29:33

Japanese supermarkets stratify by income; same curry, three price tiers

In Osaka, the same 180–200 gram curry beef package sells at 100 yen in discount chains, 300 yen midmarket, and 680 yen in premium stores—all healthy sellers, because they serve different income groups' competing versions of the same need. Premium is short-shelf cold storage, midmarket gets brand partnerships and upgrades, budget solves the basic problem. China's retail endgame will mirror this layered ecosystem: hard discounters, soft discounters, budget stores, mid-market, and luxury all coexisting, not one format claiming everyone.

— Wang

Glossary

SKU
Each distinct product configuration counts as one SKU; a standard measure of store category width.
PB
Private label—products developed by the retailer and sold exclusively through their own channels.
PDQ
Pre-designed display case: factory-packed cartons designed to go straight to shelf without repacking, saving labor.
CBS
Convenience store chain; a format distinct from hypermarkets and warehouse clubs.
