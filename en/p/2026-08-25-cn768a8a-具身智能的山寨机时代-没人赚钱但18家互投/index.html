48 Observations from the Robot Conference: Nobody's Profitable, but 18 Companies Made 36 Mutual Bets

4:04

Robot dogs can be assembled at home; humanoids are still getting face swaps

This year two companies launched assemblable robot dogs and humanoid robots: the robot dog arrives as a suitcase and users assemble it like Lego; the humanoid is first assembled as a thin skeleton, then gets a new face and body. By comparison, the genuinely mature product is the robot dog — humanoids are still at the stage of being assembled in a factory and then having their heads and faces swapped on site. A centaur form also appeared, combining humanoid upper limbs with the stability of quadruped lower limbs, for scenarios like firefighting and disaster relief. The gap shows product forms are at uneven maturity: robot dogs can already be assembled at home, humanoids are still ‘changing skins’.

10:07

Folding clothes, which audiences are tired of, is the real hard problem

Folding clothes was the most common demo this year, with staff showing it over and over, but audiences are already numb to it. Consumers think young people use integrated wardrobes and simply don't fold clothes; B2B buyers and technical people, however, consider it ‘the best thing we can give you’ — because clothing is a deformable object and hard to manipulate. On site, Galaxea used two grippers to fold a T-shirt; if one gripper misses, it retries repeatedly until it grips. It's only a T-shirt — it still can't fold down jackets or quilts — but this is exactly the problem the industry needs to crack within one to two years. It shows robots are starting to have a bit of decision-making ability.

14:08

The humanoid's advantage isn't that it can work, it's that it isn't specialized

Figure ran a 7x24 livestream, having the robot flip packages on a conveyor belt so the shipping label faces up, and even hired humans to compete against it and won. Some industry experts called it out, arguing large logistics centers have long solved this with suction-cup equipment and don't need humanoids. But another view holds that the humanoid's advantage is precisely being ‘non-specialized’: the equipment costs a hundred-something thousand, lower than the millions for specialized equipment; it can also be iterated over the air, and do other jobs later. For small and mid-sized logistics centers, buying one to test the waters is a reasonable decision logic. The dispute reflects the industry's disagreement over what counts as the ‘real problem’.

24:14

The stuttering is what proves these demos aren't fake

There were no fake demos in this year's displays; several companies were willing to let audiences interfere, and the robots showed real inference stuttering — for example, stopping between point A and point B to wait for inference. Why did real demos become possible? Because the two technical routes, VLA and world models, are starting to mature. Previously you could spot a fake robot by things like whether the left and right feet lifted at the same time; now such mechanical-feeling motions are rare. This shows the honesty of the displays has improved, and the industry is shifting from selling concepts to presenting real states.

29:48

Anyone who needs an adjective to be number one isn't in the first tier

We took photos at the booths and counted roughly 10 companies shouting ‘number one’: fastest growth, number one in shipments, number one in quadruped dog shipments, number one in service robots, number one in quadruped service robots… as long as you keep adding adjectives, you can always find your own ‘number one’. Behind this mindset is anxiety, because genuinely confident companies don't emphasize this — Unitree, for instance, sold 18,000 units by the end of July, three times its full-year 2025 shipments, and it doesn't need adjectives. Most of those adding adjectives are not in the first tier.

33:18

Most aren't profitable yet, but the money is already spilling out into upstream and downstream bets

From January 2026 to August 19, 18 robot companies had already made about 36 investments, internal and external. Most of these companies aren't profitable yet, and the targets are mainly upstream and downstream: foundation models, chips, core components, data fusion, and so on — essentially opening up the supply chain. There are also competitors investing in the same company. This is history repeating itself — in consumer electronics, many ODM factories long ago stopped earning from contract manufacturing and earned from investing instead. Compared with the previous three humanoid robot waves, money spilling over to the point of making external investments is the special feature of this fourth wave: Chinese robotics is starting to form an ecosystem concept.

43:23

Costs have fallen enough to rent, but standards still aren't unified

EngineAI founder Zhao Tongyang said the general-purpose humanoid robot body fell from 1 million to 100,000 in nearly three years, with room to fall further. In the supply chain, precision ball screws fell from ten or twenty thousand yuan to one or two thousand, and reducer prices will halve again. Falling costs are spawning new business models: a company called ‘Weilai Buyuan’ offers robot rentals, 3,000 yuan a month to take one home. At the same time the industry still has no unified standards; each company's models and hardware aren't interoperable, and the road to standardization is still long.

Glossary

VLA / vision-language-action model
A model that lets a robot generate physical actions directly from visual input and natural-language instructions.
ODM / original design manufacturer
A firm that designs and produces products for brands; ODM factories that can put a brand's label on robots have already appeared in the robotics industry.
UWB / ultra-wideband
A high-precision indoor wireless positioning technology, used in the episode as a hard beacon on a quilt.
Zero-shot
A model directly completing a task it has never seen; Generalist 1.5 was called a GPT-3 moment by some self-media outlets.
