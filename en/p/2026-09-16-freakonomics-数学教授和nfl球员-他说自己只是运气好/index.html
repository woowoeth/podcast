A Math Professor and NFL Player Who Says He Was Just Lucky

John Urschel played in the NFL while finishing a math PhD at MIT, yet says talent is only a small part of it — his success came from ‘an enormous amount of hard work’ and luck. He even hopes his football fame did not get him his MIT job.

A coach's joke made him realise he could reach the NFL

After Urschel's junior season, his position coach called him in, meaning ‘don't go thinking about anything crazy, stay another year’ — the crazy thing being declaring early for the draft. He hadn't been thinking about the NFL at all, but that remark made him realise for the first time: this would happen if he wanted it. Later the coach would say in practice, ‘they (the league) will like this move.’ He wasn't running on self-confidence; he was running on other people assuming he belonged at that level.

— John Urschel
13:25

He asked his coach about a PhD; the coach thought he already had one

Urschel did a math PhD at MIT while playing in the NFL, and says he ‘didn't cheat’, because he asked coach John Harbaugh in advance whether he could go back to school for a degree. The coach replied ‘absolutely, we support players finishing their education’, then a minute later added by text: ‘I thought you already had a degree and a master's.’ Urschel replied: ‘But I don't have a PhD.’ The coach replied ‘oh, OK’, and that counted as approval. He deliberately picked courses with no exams that he could pass by reading the textbook — three PhD courses in one semester.

— John Urschel
15:35

On planes he pretended his math book was a chess book

During the NFL season almost all his time each day went to football, with film study at night. Math could only be done in the hours other people relaxed — say on the team flight, when everyone was watching movies. He says reading a chess book would have been completely fine, but reading a math book was ‘a little suspicious’, and teammates might think he was too distracted, so he would say ‘it's not a math book, it's a chess book’, flip it open for them, and they'd say ‘OK’. The real solution was that he finished the textbooks before the semester even started; otherwise it would have been impossible.

— John Urschel
17:42

The CTE controversy conveniently gave him cover to retire

The host asks whether the years when CTE (brain damage from repeated head impacts) was making NFL headlines happened to coincide with his thinking about retiring. Urschel says ‘it gave me very good cover.’ But he also says he had been worried before that. He was weighing whether to play one more season — the last year of his rookie contract, with the next contract worth at least a million dollars a year, while his first-year base salary was 420,000. He had at one point convinced himself to stay with ‘there's one year left on the contract, I'll see it out’ — the same mindset as when he honoured his commitment to Penn State.

— John Urschel
19:49

Looking back, he doesn't know what he was agonising over

Urschel says that in hindsight, agonising over retirement made little sense: the team could cut him at any time, the contract wasn't guaranteed, and an injury would end it. What he actually cared about was math and his newborn child, and both were growing in importance, while football ranked third yet was a real burden on the first two — not just his head, but his whole body, whether he'd still be able to walk at 50, to pick up grandchildren and his daughter. He says ‘I needed to retire, focus on math, and go be with my kid.’

— John Urschel
20:52

He estimates only 4% of Americans like math

The host asks him to guess how many out of 100 Americans like math. Urschel says that if you define ‘math’ the way the general public understands it, 4% might even be high. The host says that number is a little sad. Urschel's own definition is very broad: math is the quantitative language we all share for reasoning about problems, and engineering, theoretical physics, statistics and theoretical computer science all count as math — not far from doing puzzles, not far from the goal of ‘understanding something’.

— John Urschel
27:07

His daughter won't play football; his son must truly need it

Asked whether his two children should play football, Urschel says his daughter shouldn't — that level of risk he won't accept. For his son, if it looks like something he ‘really needs to do’, he would allow it. He explains what needing means: football suits young people who crave it and can benefit from ‘consensual violence, controlled, mutually agreed-upon aggression’ — with rules, not trying to hurt people, essentially a proxy for war. He says it was very good for the combative high-schooler he was. If it's just ‘because Dad played’ or ‘because all my friends play’, he'd have the kid try wrestling or judo first.

— John Urschel
29:09

He says he actually had no talent as an NFL player

The host praises him for being good at several hard things at once. Urschel says talent does play a role, but compared with NFL players he ‘had no talent’ — he concedes he has some athletic gifts, like broad shoulders, but in the league that doesn't count. So how did he succeed? ‘Hard work, an enormous amount of hard work.’ The source is ‘wanting to be better’. Asked whether his MIT job came from football fame, he says ‘I don't know, I hope not.’

— John Urschel

Glossary

CTE / chronic traumatic encephalopathy
A degenerative brain disease caused by repeated head impacts, hard to diagnose in life and mostly studied through post-mortem brain donation.
NIL / name, image and likeness
The rules letting US college athletes profit from their name, image and likeness; not allowed when Urschel was playing.
Cantor's diagonal argument
The classic mathematical argument proving the real numbers are uncountable and that infinities come in different sizes.
uncountable
An infinite set that cannot be put in one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers, such as all real numbers between 0 and 1.
