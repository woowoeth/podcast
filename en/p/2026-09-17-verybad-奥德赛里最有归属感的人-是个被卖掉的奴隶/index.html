The Most At Home Person in the Odyssey Is a Slave Who Was Sold

Odysseus wanders twenty years without getting home, Telemachus grows up among the suitors; the only person who treats Ithaca as solid ground is Eumaeus, the swineherd sold into slavery as a child — because it is the only place he has ever known.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:30

The person who seems most at home is the slave sold as a child

This episode's theme is "what is home." Eumaeus — the swineherd sold into slavery — is the one who feels most at home of anyone: he was sold as a child, this is all he has ever known, so he is like a foundation stone; everyone else is adrift. The speakers break homelessness into two layers: the literal having-nowhere-to-go, and a deeper layer — you are missing some kind of connection to roots. This is exactly what Odysseus has been struggling with since Calypso's island: he is bit by bit rebuilding his connection to home, but still cannot stop lying and disguising himself, perhaps more than he should.

6:31

The swineherd taught him how to be a host

Odysseus has been absent twenty years, Telemachus grew up in a house full of suitors — how would he know how to be a host? The speakers realise here: Eumaeus modelled it for him. Eumaeus has very low status, but his domain is caring for people — the scrap of land with his few pigs; as a low-power figure he plays the most generous host. And Telemachus is already considerate toward Athena disguised as a stranger at the start of this book, so it is not something he learned on this trip: more likely the swineherd gave him the heart to want to do it.

25:40

Only with company can a person begin to enjoy his grief

Eumaeus invites Odysseus to sit and drink, says such nights are wondrous, then delivers the episode's heaviest line: after long years of pain and exile, a man can begin to enjoy his own sorrow. The speakers press on it: why has Laertes been eaten away by grief while Eumaeus can grow old with his? The answer lies in sharing — unloading the pain and having someone witness it is not just relief but a real, positive pleasure; the same logic behind mourning rituals where everyone tells stories together. It is fighting grief itself that makes people suffer.

29:42

The Phoenician nurse's end is like a seagull

Eumaeus tells how he got here: Phoenician traders spent a whole year earning trust, and the nurse agreed to steal her master's son and sell him for money in exchange for being sent home. Seven days of fair wind, and on the eighth Artemis shot her dead with an arrow — she pitched headfirst into the cargo hold like a seagull, then was thrown into the sea to feed the fish. The speakers say the ending is so cursory it reads like a declaration that fate is both cruel and arbitrary; we know little about the Phoenicians, also because their records were written on papyrus and did not survive, and most of what remains comes from Greeks and Romans who looked down on them. After hearing it all, Odysseus still says, you were lucky, you met good people — wrecking all the tenderness that came before.

33:47

The first to embrace him is the swineherd

The moment Telemachus enters the yard, Eumaeus jumps up, the cup in his hand falls and the wine spills; he runs to kiss his master's face and shining eyes, weeping like a father seeing his only son return from a foreign land after ten years. The speakers stress: this simile gives the position of "father" to the man who raised the child — in the years Odysseus was away, Eumaeus was more of a father than Odysseus. And in that moment the only one who can jump up is the swineherd; Odysseus is still trapped in a beggar's body and getup. This is also the first time in the whole poem anyone is called "godlike Telemachus" — until now that epithet has belonged to Odysseus.

38:50

He never got any joy from me — the line reads two ways

Telemachus explains why the suitors can behave this way, says only one shoot is left in the house, and ends with: he never got any joy from me. The speakers offer two opposite readings: one, he is grieving for his father, who never enjoyed the pleasure of raising a son — which resonates with a speaker who has just had a nearly two-year-old son; the other, the father simply had no interest in raising a baby and went off to fight a war no one forced on him. Support for the latter is Odysseus's own account that after returning from Crete he felt family life did not suit him, back when he was still in a warrior's mindset. Neither of them can tell from the English translation, so they agree to check other translations next episode.

49:53

Testing everyone is not caution, it is adaptation gone into overdrive

Odysseus asks his son to keep the secret from everyone, even Penelope must first be tested for her attitude, and then all the male slaves. The two disagree here: one holds that this is extreme suspicion, an "overfiring adaptation" — what kept you alive in war is now out of control; the same speaker adds that it could also be procrastination, a way of preparing himself before "can I really kill them all," and admits he is not sure. The skill he knows best is testing people, lying, and impersonating others. Telemachus pushes straight back: testing everyone one by one takes too much time, and the suitors will keep eating your estate.

57:00

However hard she curses him, she never says I won't marry you

Penelope publicly tears into Antinous: you are a beast, a thief, a criminal; people say you are the cleverest of your peers, that is not true, you are mad. She also reminds him that when his father was hunted by the men of Ithaca, Odysseus saved him — and now he eats his benefactor's estate, courts his wife, and wants to kill his son. But she never says the most direct thing: "I will never marry you, get out." The speakers' explanation is that she too is stalling: if Odysseus really is dead, she still needs someone to support her, and the web woven and unravelled, unravelled and woven is "I do not want to decide now." In the same scene Eurymachus is worse — he says he loves Telemachus most, and that no one can harm him as long as he himself lives.
