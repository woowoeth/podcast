Entropy Doesn't Mean Getting Messier: Oil and Water Layering, Frost Flowers, and Other Spontaneous Order

The second law of thermodynamics is usually told as "things get messier," but oil separating from water, beach sorting, and frost on a window are all spontaneous order. Carlo Rovelli says low entropy may not be in the universe but in how we look at it.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:03

Oil separating from water isn't anti-entropy — it's entropy producing order

Rovelli uses a set of everyday images to dismantle the popular claim that "entropy = disorder": water and vinegar mix into a uniform blend, but water and oil separate into layers; on a beach, fine sand and pebbles sort themselves apart; frost on a window grows regular structures on its own. He says that under a properly defined notion of "order," a great many things in nature become spontaneously ordered if you just leave them alone. This doesn't overturn the second law; it says popular science draws the correspondence between "order" and "entropy" too crudely. He points out in particular that the complexity of biology and the structure of the Earth's surface are often misread as "anti-entropy," when in fact entropy growth itself is constantly producing order.

7:12

Low entropy may not be in the universe but in your coarse graining

Rovelli proposes a hypothesis he has put into a paper himself but isn't sure is right or wrong: low entropy means "special," but what is special is not the state of the universe — it's coarse graining itself. Coarse graining is a perspective: looking at only some variables and not all the details. If the hypothesis holds, the universe can be in a completely arbitrary state, yet from our perspective, which sees only some variables, it appears low-entropy in the direction we call "the past." This turns the "initial low-entropy boundary condition" from a fact about the universe into a question about how an observer slices up information.

— Carlo Rovelli
12:16

You can be in two places at once, just not to yourself

Rovelli pushes quantum superposition onto people: if quantum mechanics is universal, Brian can be in a superposition of Los Angeles and San Francisco, and Carlo can measure that interference. But to Brian himself, quantum mechanics never tells you that you're in a superposition — you look around and see either San Diego or Los Angeles. So "where you are" is two different things for you and for me. He compares this to Galileo's discovery that velocity is relative: saying "velocity is zero" actually omits "relative to the Earth." What quantum mechanics says is that all properties must be understood relative to something.

— Carlo Rovelli
19:34

Galileo got the tides wrong using his own relativity principle

Rovelli uses Galileo's tidal model to show that "having an idea" and "pushing the idea to the end" are two different things. Galileo thought the combination of the Earth's revolution around the Sun and its rotation stirred the seas and produced the tides. But Rovelli points out that revolution is, to first order, uniform straight-line motion, and rotation alone can't produce an effect either, so the tides can't come from this — what actually explains the tides is Newton. Galileo himself violated precisely the relativity principle he had discovered. Rovelli says Einstein was the same: after writing general relativity in 1915, he later wrote a paper denying gravitational waves (and corrected it after a referee sent it back), and insisted the Schwarzschild singularity was the end of the world — an error he never corrected until he died.

— Carlo Rovelli
31:06

Without dissipation you can't record anything

Rovelli says that already in classical mechanics he found a fact that made him "see a light": if you want to model a measuring device with classical mechanics — a barometer, say, or a device that records whether a ball is on the left or the right — you can't, unless you introduce dissipation. A ball dropped into a box will bounce back up; it has to dissipate to stop and leave a record. So measurement is essentially a statistical process that requires dissipation, and this was already true before quantum mechanics — it isn't peculiar to quantum mechanics. This directly answers the puzzle of "unitary evolution is reversible, so where does irreversibility come from": recording itself requires that something be thrown away.

— Carlo Rovelli
33:45

A chess program deliberates, and so do you

Rovelli uses his chess computer to take apart free will: the computer is deterministic, and given its internal state and the opponent's move, its output is necessarily predetermined. But why does it still spend time enumerating all possible moves and evaluating them one by one? Because the only way to reach that result is to go through this evaluation process, and that process is called deliberation. He says it's foolish to say it isn't deliberating, because deliberation is exactly this. Brian is the same: without going through this complex process, he wouldn't make the decision to start a project or choose a career. So-called freedom is not violating the laws of nature; it is precisely the fact of "having gone through deliberation."

— Carlo Rovelli
40:56

In principle yes, not now, and within two years he doubts it

Asked whether a machine could have Einstein's "happiest thought" (the equivalence principle in free fall), Rovelli answers in three layers: in principle yes, he doesn't think humans are so special as to be unreproducible; not now; and within two years he's skeptical, though he has no hard evidence that it's impossible. He mentions that last week he had Claude do something his PhD student hadn't finished in a month, and Claude finished it in half an hour, so the capability is beyond doubt. But he says Claude didn't invent the equivalence principle, and doing that within two years he finds hard. At the same time he states plainly that there is a lot of hype in AI.

— Carlo Rovelli
56:45

Ten years and unlimited funding, he'd build a roomful of Josephson junctions

Asked what he would do with "ten years and unlimited funding," Rovelli's answer is not a telescope or a collider but a large room full of Josephson junctions, plus an engineering team. The reason: loop quantum gravity predicts particles of Planck mass, possibly dark matter candidates, with a mass roughly equal to a small piece of hair or a flea egg. He says we already know the direction, mass, and interaction strength of dark matter clouds — only extremely feeble Newtonian gravity, but Josephson junctions could catch it with simple physics. He thinks it's technically feasible within ten years; what's missing is only the money.

— Carlo Rovelli

Glossary

coarse graining
A way of observing that looks at only some variables and ignores all the details; the definition of entropy depends on it.
loop quantum gravity
A quantum gravity theory Rovelli co-founded, which holds that space itself is discrete.
relational quantum mechanics
Rovelli's preferred interpretation: quantum states are relative to an observer, not absolute.
Josephson junction
A superconducting device that Rovelli thinks could be used to detect the feeble gravity of Planck-mass particles.
equivalence principle
In free fall you feel no gravity; Einstein called it his happiest thought.
