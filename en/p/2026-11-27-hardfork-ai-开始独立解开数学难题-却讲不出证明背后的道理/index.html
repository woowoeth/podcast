AI independently solves unsolved math problems, yet cannot explain the reasoning behind the proofs

OpenAI and Anthropic's models can now independently solve unresolved math problems, but Terence Tao says these proofs often have incomplete citations, lack transparency, and humans cannot even understand how they were solved.

Tao is one of the rare elite mathematicians who has deeply participated throughout the entire process of AI solving difficult math problems. His account of how major tech companies are actually using AI for research is far more concrete than any external commentary.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

15:27

Math problems are just training fuel for big tech to optimize AI models

Tao explains that math problems make an ideal objective benchmark for training large models: they're scorable and continuously refinable. Once a model improves at math, it simultaneously improves at programming and similar tasks like website intrusion; that's the real reason big tech cares about math, not for mathematics itself. He notes this dynamic has both ‘positive and negative’ implications.

— Terence Tao
16:30

AI solves the problem but cannot explain the reasoning behind the proof

Tao uses the ‘800-pound gorilla’ metaphor for the current situation: the gorilla is well-funded and can crack an unsolved hard problem, but after handing you the proof, it cannot do what human mathematicians do—lecture, write textbooks, teach students the underlying logic. Mathematicians now must reverse-engineer these hundreds-of-page AI proofs trying to discover what's actually new, and this yields far less insight than human involvement from the beginning.

— Terence Tao
18:32

AI's direct route to answers skips the discoveries that advance mathematics

Tao likens traditional mathematics to hiking: false starts, detours, unexpected discoveries of wildlife and grander waterfalls—these ‘wayside finds’ are themselves part of mathematical progress. Today's AI is more like a windowless helicopter that flies straight to the waterfall, snaps a photo, and returns home—mission accomplished, but missing the new insights and new questions that should have emerged.

— Terence Tao
20:40

Proofs are just byproducts of big tech's model training

Tao is frank: many major tech companies have closed, cut, or reassigned their research divisions because competition is fierce and shipping the model fast is paramount. Proofs are just occasional ‘byproducts’ after feeding the system vast quantities of math problems. Companies don't treat them as a primary goal, so they ‘sit atop a pile of material to be dealt with,’ do minimal cleanup, and ship.

— Terence Tao
21:41

Citations are incomplete and the process remains too opaque to verify doubts

Early AI-generated papers cite poorly; many build on prior work without attribution. More troubling: mathematicians have uploaded problems they were researching to tools like ChatGPT for help, only to have the company release a press release solving the same problem weeks later. Tao emphasizes this could be coincidence, but companies refuse to disclose compute or data used, making these suspicions neither verifiable nor disprovable.

— Terence Tao
25:50

Deploying mathematics nobody understands into the real world is dangerous

Tao's worry extends beyond lost intellectual pleasure to safety: if future systems like air traffic control depend on extremely sophisticated mathematical models that no human truly understands, we'll eventually pay the price. He also fears young scholars encountering only AI outputs will enter a barren ‘whose AI is stronger’ competition, losing person-to-person academic connection.

— Terence Tao
32:00

AI-generated papers solve problems efficiently but cannot teach or inspire researchers

AI excels at generating PDFs that resemble papers—they do solve unsolved problems, but rarely cite, rarely explain, rarely point to new research directions. Tao argues that academic evaluation must shift from ‘papers published’ to rewarding work that genuinely excites people and draws them in. Otherwise, hiring and award criteria will keep being gamed by ‘paper products.’

— Terence Tao
37:08

When you optimize a metric, it stops being a valid metric

Tao concludes with Goodhart's Law from economics: ‘problems solved’ once measured mathematical understanding well, but after AI overoptimized it, machines now solve problems without advancing field understanding. He reminds every industry to ask what metric it truly pursues and whether a machine could ‘game’ that metric while missing the real target—the same question writers should ask themselves.

— Terence Tao
