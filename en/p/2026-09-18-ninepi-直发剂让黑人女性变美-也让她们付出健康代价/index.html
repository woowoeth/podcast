Hair relaxer made Black women beautiful, and made them pay with their health

Chemical hair relaxer built the Black community's earliest millionaire industry and has also been linked by research to elevated uterine cancer risk — the same product is both an economic ladder and a bodily cost.

Hair relaxer is "creamy crack"

Chemical hair relaxer has a nickname in the salon: creamy crack, because it is said to be as addictive as cocaine: once you start, it is very hard to stop. When you stop, the new curly growth breaks off where it meets the already straightened dead hair. The week before use you cannot wash your hair or scratch your scalp, or you will get burned; when applying it you have to avoid the scalp and coat only the hair shaft, but leaving it too long near the ends still stings. In the episode the host recalls being coated with relaxer for the first time at age 9, having already seen women in the salon cry out in pain.

— Priscilla Alabi
5:18

From African hair tradition to a survival strategy

Hair historian Lorie Tharps says starting the story from slavery is starting from chapter two and missing chapter one: Africa already had rich hair traditions, and hair connected tribe, family, and identity. After being enslaved in America, straightening went from self-expression to survival strategy — because they were told their hair was inferior, ugly, more like an animal's, straightening became a visual way to prove "we are equal to you." They used butter instead of palm oil, made shampoo from cornmeal and kerosene, used butter knives as crude curling irons, and wrapped hair in yarn to straighten it.

— Lorie Tharps
8:21

Barbers' money built Black colleges

The methods these enslaved people invented were good enough to make them the first choice as barbers and wig makers for white people. White people paid for their craft, and this channel let many of them buy their own freedom. Tharps says some early Black colleges and institutions were funded or founded by Black barbers, such as Wilberforce University — an HBCU entirely owned and operated by African Americans, built to a large extent on barbering income. But the irony is that these Black barbers did not cut Black people's hair at the time; they served white clients.

— Lorie Tharps
10:22

The hot comb gave way to an accidental discovery

Madame CJ Walker and Annie Turnbo Malone used the hot comb plus hair oil to produce sleek straight hair, turning scattered straightening methods into a system, and Walker thereby became the first self-made millionaire woman in America, of any color. But the hot comb's effect was temporary, reverting with sweat, moisture, or rain. In the early 1900s, Cleveland tailor Garrett Augustus Morgan wanted to invent a substance that would keep high-speed machines and needles from damaging wool and other animal fur, and after trying one formula found the fur had straightened. He first tested it on a neighbor's dog, then on his own hair, and both straightened — he had accidentally discovered that lye (sodium hydroxide) plus other things could straighten hair.

— Lorie Tharps
14:24

The man who put relaxer on drugstore shelves

George Johnson is called by Tharps the Madam CJ Walker of his era. A barber doing homemade conks came to this chemist and asked whether he could make something fast and easy, and that produced Ultra Wave for men, followed by Ultra Sheen for women. Unlike the temporary hot comb and dangerous homemade lye formulas, Ultra Sheen was long-lasting, safer, and available at any drugstore, with no need to go to a salon. That privacy mattered: straightening hair with chemicals carried shame, and doing it at home meant no one saw you going to the salon.

— Lorie Tharps
16:26

Profits flowed back to the Black community, then regulators took notice

Johnson put profits back into the Black community: Soul Train, Essence Magazine, Chicago's Independence Bank — then the largest Black-owned bank in the country, lending to homeowners and entrepreneurs redlined by white banks; funding Martin Luther King Jr. and Jesse Jackson; employing hundreds of Black and brown workers who could not find jobs because of racial discrimination, giving them profit sharing, full health insurance, on-site nurses, full college tuition, paid maternity and sick leave, and four-day weekends every holiday. In 1964 Johnson Products became the first Black-owned company listed on the American Stock Exchange. But in 1975 the FTC required Johnson to put a warning label on the outside of relaxer packaging, saying the product contained lye and could be harmful — while Revlon's relaxer, which also contained lye, was not immediately required to add a label, and the delay ran nearly two years.

— Lorie Tharps
20:28

The civil rights movement flipped the politics of straight hair

During the Black Power era, Black people decided straight hair had solved nothing — from Madam CJ Walker's straightening comb to relaxer, every method had been tried, and it still had not worked. So came a simpler and more radical idea: I will no longer straighten my hair, I will use my hair as protest. Black hair grows up and out, and does not make you smaller, it makes you taller. The political flip was so complete that some Black people with naturally straight hair got perms, because not everyone's hair can grow into an Angela Davis-style afro. But the beauty industry then commodified the afro, selling wigs and marketing it as beauty and empowerment while also pulling its teeth, turning it into just another fashion trend. By the 1980s, straight hair was back.

— Lorie Tharps
28:38

A 34,000-woman study: risk up two and a half times

A large 2022 study followed 34,000 women and found that frequent users of chemical hair straightening products — most of them Black women — were two and a half times as likely to develop uterine cancer as never-users. New York Times Magazine contributor Linda Varosa says this was the first time a high-quality study linked straightening products, Black women, and two cancers (endometrial cancer and uterine cancer). The study also linked these products to fibroids, and Black women have fibroids at far higher rates than other women. The study shows correlation, not causation, but Tharps thinks agonizing over correlation versus causation misses the point: she thinks the red flag should simply be waved, with warnings on packaging, labels, and in salons.

— Linda Varosa

Glossary

creamy crack
A nickname for chemical hair relaxer, because using it is considered as addictive as cocaine.
lye
Sodium hydroxide, a corrosive chemical that can straighten hair but is dangerous to use directly.
conk
A sleek straight hairstyle made with chemical hair relaxer, popularized by jazz musicians in the 1920s.
HBCU
Historically Black Colleges and Universities, institutions of higher education established for Black Americans in U.S. history.
uterine fibroids
Noncancerous tumors in the uterus, with Black women affected at far higher rates than other women.
