Stood up one afternoon, a film that made Jia Zhangke decide to become a director

After being stood up for a meeting, Jia Zhangke wandered into a cinema and caught Yellow Earth. He discovered that his hometown life—so familiar yet overlooked—could be filmed this way. He decided that very moment to become a filmmaker.

Mother switched jobs to access rationed goods as household privileges

Born in Fenyang county, Shanxi in 1970, Jia's father was a middle school Chinese teacher. His mother originally worked at the local militia office, but in the era of material scarcity when everything required ration tickets, she deliberately switched to a sugar research company. This gave the family an advantage in accessing tobacco, alcohol, tea, and candy—scarce items at the time. Every Mid-Autumn Festival they could buy rare brown sugar by ticket, and had more sweets and pastries than other families. This small material advantage formed the foundational layer of his childhood family memories.

— Jia Zhangke
31:25

Street fights between neighborhoods followed an almost ceremonial process

Jia recalls that neighborhoods in old Fenyang frequently had turf wars. Before each fight, older kids would hold a war council, crouching by telephone poles to plan strategy. Younger kids supplied the logistics—carrying bricks, tiles, and rocks. The actual fighting rarely lasted more than 15 to 20 seconds. Afterward came a war summary, with designated people even sent to the hospital to count the other side's stitches—the more stitches, the bigger the victory. This almost ritualized process was his earliest memory of violence.

— Jia Zhangke
34:26

A classmate's mother pursued him onto school grounds with a hunting rifle

After a minor scuffle with a classmate, the boy's mother arrived at the school playground with a hunting rifle, looking for Jia to settle the score. He was so frightened he squeezed between his classmate's legs and ran all the way back to a neighbor's storage shed to hide. He notes that the street fight itself lasted maybe ten seconds and was quickly forgotten, but what left a lasting psychological scar was this uncontrollable retaliation—in that era, rural families still had hunting rifles, and nobody confiscated them.

— Jia Zhangke
52:36

A weatherman's mention of Ulaanbaatar became the edge of his world

When Jia was young, Shanxi radio weather forecasts often mentioned cold fronts coming from Ulaanbaatar through Erenhot toward North China. That place name sounded to him like what the North Pole and South Pole sound like to people today—the edge of the world, the most desolate and distant place imaginable. This geography shaped by radio later entered his films directly: in The World in 2004, he used the song "Ulaanbaatar's Night," which he first heard at a Mongolian-style bar in Beijing when he was in university.

— Jia Zhangke
1:31:00

Being stood up led him to stumble into film history

The year he failed the art school entrance exam, Jia had arranged to meet a friend, but the friend stood him up. At loose ends, he walked into a small cinema that showed films in a loop. He arrived just as Yellow Earth was playing. The clothes, labor, and silent nights in the northern Shaanxi countryside in the film were almost identical to his aunt's village on the Loess Plateau—especially the detail of a fish dish with gravy served at a wedding, which moved him to tears on the spot. That feeling—familiar life seen for the first time through a strange perspective—struck him as he left the theater: I'm going to make films.

— Jia Zhangke
2:33:33

A Kodak salesman quietly added ten rolls of film at his own expense

When shooting his feature Xiao Wu, Jia and his collaborators had a total budget for 16mm film at a 2-to-1 shooting ratio—meaning each shot could be filmed only twice. When they went to Kodak to order, the salesman initially concluded that amount would only yield a 45-minute short. But after they'd walked out with the film, he caught up with them and said he was willing to add ten more rolls at his own expense, raising their shooting ratio to 3-to-1. Jia says that over the years, whenever he faced difficulty, he remembered this benefactor whose name he never even recorded.

— Jia Zhangke
2:48:37

A French sales company abandoned him when Platform came home empty-handed

When Platform premiered at Venice, the response was explosive. International media assumed it would win the Golden Lion. A French company bought the international sales rights in advance. But when the awards were announced and it won nothing, that company's attitude flipped. They dragged their feet on a distribution plan, then proposed either cutting the price in half or breaking the contract entirely. Jia confronted them at a breakfast meeting at the Rotterdam Film Festival, asking why they were violating their agreement. Their reply: we're in business, not charity. The deal fell apart.

— Jia Zhangke
2:59:41

A director friend's advice to hedge and save—advice he never took

A Hong Kong director friend once well-meaningly warned Jia to keep several projects in development and choose which to shoot based on market conditions. The friend also urged him to save money while riding high, since many directors end up destitute. Jia says he didn't take either piece of advice. Instead, he only writes scripts based on what he most wants to shoot right now, starts shooting before funding is complete—his company fronts money, he borrows equipment from friends, figures it out as he goes. Through this unplanned persistence, except for the documentary Tales from the Island, his fiction films have nearly all broken even. He's never had to use commercial films to subsidize art films.

— Jia Zhangke
