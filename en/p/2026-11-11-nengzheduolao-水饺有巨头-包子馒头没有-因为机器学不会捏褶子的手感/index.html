Dumplings have giants, buns don't: machines can't replicate the hand-pinch feel

Dumplings and glutinous rice balls were scaled by giants like Sifu and Sanquan, but buns require fermented dough and hand-pinched pleats—machines can't achieve that fluffiness—so for a decade, the category has left room for smaller brands.

Regional dominance becomes a liability when scaling to national competition

The brand built strong word-of-mouth in Shanghai's refrigerated-food market, but when it tried to scale nationally with frozen products, it crashed. Consumers had never heard of the brand and only compared prices; the products felt too sweet, not fluffy enough, not firm enough for national tastes. The supply-chain advantage formed regionally evaporated instantly when competing in the uniform, price-driven national frozen-food category.

Visible filling became the breakthrough product that drove growth

After the national setback, the team stopped trying to recreate classic flavors and made their first genuinely differentiated product: cheese-filled buns with the filling placed on the outside, visible at a glance. This product launched at the moment when Xiaohongshu users wanted ‘see what you get’, and visibility and novelty created organic word-of-mouth. It was the team's first real success after stepping out of their comfort zone.

14:08

A hit product spreads in weeks but the original survives when imitations fade

The cheese-filled bun took nine months to develop and optimize, but competitors could replicate it in roughly a month. After the product went viral, over a dozen cheaper knockoffs flooded the market in two years. The team panicked enough to consider switching to cheaper mechanization and lower-cost ingredients, but nothing reproduced the same texture. Eventually, time proved the principle: the imitations disappeared, and consumers kept buying the original.

— Leng Jia Yi
20:11

Dead dough scales to giants; live dough stays artisanal because machines can't pinch

Dumplings and glutinous rice balls use unleavened dough that needs no fermentation and was mechanized from the start, which is why Sifu and Sanquan became giants and essentially closed off the space for newcomers. Buns use leavened dough that must ferment and be hand-pinched into a dozen pleats—when a machine does it, the fluffiness that skilled hands create is gone. This is why the bun category has stayed regional and handmade, paradoxically leaving room for new brands.

— Leng Jia Yi
34:14

Even in tier-three cities, consumers will pay extra for quality

While attending Pang Donglai's central kitchen during the height of 9.99-yuan price wars, the sourcing and production standards were remarkably high and the product wasn't cheap, yet consumers still bought in. People in that tier-three city seemed more relaxed and happy than in tier-one cities. This convinced him that the premium for health-conscious choices was sustainable. He returned and told the team not to panic: quality products will be recognized.

44:24

Bootstrapping stays steady when venture money makes boom-and-bust cycles

The company has never raised capital. Manufacturing heritage made the team extremely cautious about spending; development has been steady but slow. The e-commerce business is still in investment mode, not profitable. Peers who raised and spent aggressively hit ten-billion-yuan scale, then crashed back down to one or two billion. The founder believes in compounding slowly through repeat purchases rather than chasing growth that evaporates when marketing stops.

— Leng Jia Yi
54:29

Categories sealed by unit economics and scale should be avoided entirely

The company deliberately skips dumplings and wontons because those are crushed by scale effects and procurement costs—even with the best supply chain, you'd likely get flattened anyway. It also abandoned baked goods because specialized factories already own the baking equipment and supply chains needed to compete. The team decided it can only build a moat in fermented and leavened pastries, where handwork is hard to mechanize.

— Leng Jia Yi
1:02:34

Better doesn't win when the product photographs worse

The team developed white-filling pork buns, believing them more authentic and closer to the original pork taste than the red-filled versions dominating the market. Pricing was not higher, yet consumers showed complete indifference. The root cause, on review, partly traces to white filling looking poor on video for e-commerce—yet small soup dumplings with white filling remain perennial bestsellers. That contradiction, they say, remains unsolved.
