Quantum-resistant infrastructure must be deployed today, not after quantum computers arrive

Once quantum computers can break today's encryption, nearly every encryption key in enterprise IT systems becomes worthless. Worse: if an encrypted hard drive is stolen today, an attacker can store it now and crack it later once quantum computers mature in five years. Post-quantum cryptography algorithms are already written and publicly available; enterprises simply haven't deployed them. Arvind estimates that roughly half of enterprise IT budgets this year go to infrastructure without quantum resistance.

— Arvind Krishna
7:26

AI cannot break quantum cryptography—it is not an engineering problem but physics

Feynman's principle: you cannot simulate one architecture on another without losing efficiency. Quantum computers don't follow arithmetic rules; GPUs and classical computers do. AI might discover new quantum algorithms, but running them on classical hardware would be thousands or even millions of times slower—proving an algorithm exists is not the same as capturing quantum speedup. That said, if classical mathematics advances (say, a better model for prime distribution), theoretical classical methods could eventually break encryption, though no one knows the timeline.

— Arvind Krishna
12:42

Seventy-five to ninety percent of enterprise tokens will flow to open-weight models

Today's frontier models cost roughly $20 per million tokens, up to $50 at peak. An 800-billion-parameter open-source model, optimized on a GPU over a week, drops that to $1–2 per million tokens: ten to forty times cheaper. Enterprises also fear that offloading inference to a third party exposes internal IP, and frontier vendors won't hand you the weights. Add geopolitics—today's best open-weight models come from China—and Arvind predicts 75–90% of future token consumption will flow to open-weight models.

— Arvind Krishna
16:47

Mythos hallucinates often, but attackers need only one real gap to win

When Mythos launched, 30% of its code-vulnerability assessments were fabrications. But cybersecurity has always been asymmetric: attackers need one winning exploit, defenders must find and patch every flaw. In practice, the model's reported vulnerabilities are about 60–80% genuine; the rest are hallucinations. That means even with the model, you must manually verify; you cannot deploy its patches directly.

— Nikesh Arora
23:17

Build and release an AI system, and your company owns the consequences

Against a backdrop of global AI arms-race reality—no one will voluntarily slow down—the realistic lever is not a research pause but product liability. You build it, you release it; you own it. Nikesh rejects the notion that ‘the model coded itself and spiraled out of control.’ It didn't. Your company did, and as a commercial entity collecting fees for the service, you must accept responsibility. Existing regulatory frameworks already have the tools to hold companies accountable for what they release.

— Nikesh Arora
27:20

Distance from compute centers—not wealth—determines who can access AI capability

The real divide is not who can afford to pay, but proximity to the compute center. Technology gets cheaper; most enterprises that operate normally can likely afford AI safety and deployment. Today, compute is acutely scarce, concentrated in one or two superpowers. Countries and companies far from that center lack access not from poverty, but because supply simply cannot reach them.

— Nikesh Arora
30:21

Sixty-five years of IBM mainframe leadership came from owning the entire supply chain

In the 1960s, IBM integrated the entire supply chain from sand upward: sourcing materials, building wafers, constructing fabs, manufacturing chips, designing every peripheral component. Vertical integration blocked IP leakage and kept profit across the value chain. Today IBM no longer builds its own fabs, but chip design, engineering, system resilience, and most software remain in-house. Arvind brackets NVIDIA the same way—Jensen Huang owns the stack from hardware to applications and funds ecosystem partners to keep them within the vertical-integration orbit.

— Arvind Krishna
35:36

When enterprises use 500–5,000 software vendors, consolidation of platforms becomes inevitable

Security buyers now need 40 different products and must stitch them together. The security industry itself barely understands its own products, much less how to integrate a dozen vendors' wares. Nikesh draws a parallel to Fidelity: it once cobbled 23 systems into a CRM, then a unified platform replaced them all. A typical enterprise uses 500–5,000 software vendors—a massive attack surface. If AI can replace 490 of them and leave 5–10 core platforms, consolidation becomes inevitable.

— Nikesh Arora

Glossary

QBI (Quantum Benchmarking Initiative)
A DARPA-led competition among companies to demonstrate usable quantum computers
logical qubit
A quantum bit that remains reliable after error correction on physical qubits; the effective clean unit of quantum computation
post-quantum cryptography
Next-generation encryption algorithms designed to resist attacks by quantum computers
ZDR (zero data retention)
A contractual commitment that a service provider will neither retain nor use customer data to train models
open-weight model
A model whose parameters are publicly available for download and deployment, though training data and code may remain proprietary
