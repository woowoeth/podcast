Egg freezing isn't extracting your youth, it's taking out the eggs you were wasting

A woman releases twenty-odd eggs a month and uses only one; egg freezing retrieves the batch that would otherwise be wasted, not the four hundred of the future drawn down in advance — so it doesn't diminish your youth, it moves your life's deadline.

Before freezing eggs, women live inside a deadline

Tan Jing (谈婧) says that before freezing her eggs, no matter how professional she looked on the outside, internally there was always a deadline — around age 38 egg quality drops sharply, an irreversible loss of capability. People work backward from that deadline: by what age you must marry, by what age you must have a child, or there's no way back. She describes that state as ‘you sit down and it feels like going to an interview, like other people are picking you’, positioned as the one being chosen in both the workplace and intimate relationships. This subconscious clock shapes how she judges and chooses her life's value.

— Tan Jing
14:35

After freezing eggs, the switch of agency flips on

Freezing her eggs was equivalent to moving the deadline away. She was 33 at the time, and suddenly felt there were still 60-plus years ahead to do things, so instead she had to choose — she couldn't just do one thing. With the constraint removed, psychologically and in terms of confidence ‘anything goes’, and she began to think about what she actually wanted and how she wanted to walk through life, feeling that everything else could be chosen. She calls this shift the rise of what feminism often stresses as ‘agency’ — when interacting with people around her, who chooses whom, whether she is active or passive, all of it gradually changed.

— Tan Jing
18:29

The most painful part isn't the injections, it's the moment of deciding

The injections gave her morning sickness that lasted a week; every day even drinking water made her want to throw up, and she could only feel less awful by playing white noise. Every two days the dosage was adjusted, needles into her belly, until there wasn't a patch of good skin left on it. But she says the most painful part was the deciding — deciding in July or August, actually doing it only in January or February; at the moment of decision she was in a Shanghai KTV crying with a friend, feeling ‘I was always the top good student, how did I end up like this on the marriage track’. She explains this as inertia: from childhood she was expected to score high, find a good job, be a good wife and mother, and within those expectations she felt she was simply failing the exam.

— Tan Jing
22:32

Egg freezing is an employee benefit at Silicon Valley tech companies

Tan Jing says egg freezing is an especially mature technology, and assisted reproduction as a whole is now very mature; the side effects may just be slightly irregular periods or a bit of weight gain, which comes off easily. In Silicon Valley, egg freezing is an employee benefit at tech companies — work at Apple or Google and you can freeze for free, the company buys the insurance for you, so this cohort does it a lot; some Wall Street financial firms have the benefit too. She recommends doing the whole cycle in the US, because the ecosystem is mature, and if you do half in China and the last few injections abroad the dosage is hard to manage. The whole cost is about 100,000 RMB, including flights and hotel.

— Tan Jing
26:45

An anti-marriage act instead opened up her view of marriage

Before freezing her eggs, including before filming the documentary, she felt she didn't need marriage, that work alone was enough. But after doing this thing that looks against marriage, she became especially open to intimate relationships and began to feel their beauty, and recently started dating. In retrospect she says her past intimate relationships, like her appearance, were in a state of being sized up and gazed at, not natural, not arising from within, all external conditions, so they could hardly bring true enjoyment; once all those external conditions were stripped away, she could instead enjoy the beauty that intimacy itself brings.

— Tan Jing
31:17

Two years of pandemic cut the world into three closed loops

The most interesting thing she found from her travels: after two years of pandemic, everyone was sealed inside their own country and culture, and the world has clearly become polarized. After the US, Europe and Asia each slowed down for two years, each formed a complete closed-loop narrative and logic — the US has its own set, Europe is telling the anti-capitalist, anti-development set, Asia has its own set, and every place is very much its own closed loop. Specifically for Web3: the US gathers the world's most imaginative, most cross-disciplinary founders, with capital capability at the top of the food chain; Europe throws up social innovation ideas like carbon neutrality and mind-body-spirit; Asia gathers a large number of coders enjoying a low cost of living, with gaming and social ecosystems well developed.

— Tan Jing
48:48

Vietnam looks like China twenty or thirty years ago

Asked which country excites her most, she answers Vietnam. The reason is that Vietnam is especially like China twenty or thirty years ago, also a communist country, and the look of its cities takes her back to her childhood. She met quite a few people from Web3 and Web2 startups and tech companies, and found that young people there are all especially hungry, especially eager to rise, with light in their eyes; the level of basic education is relatively high compared with other Southeast Asian countries, talent density is high, there has long been experience doing outsourcing for foreign companies, and the internationalization of the people is also decent by Southeast Asian standards. She met TikTok's team and teams from Tencent-like publishing companies, and the look of the offices and the style of the people were quite similar to China and Silicon Valley. Overall she is bullish on Vietnam, but not limited to the Web3 track.

— Tan Jing
55:28

FTX blowing up is a good thing in the long run

She judges that in the short term there will certainly be more chain reactions — funds deeply tied to FTX like MultiCoin and Genesis have all shown more or less funding problems, and there may be more blowups. But in the long run it's a good thing: first, in the previous cycle all behavior revolved around trade, and trade whose underlying asset value was not very clear — the underlying asset was small but the bubble blown on top of it was huge, which is not healthy; once trade is challenged, other financial applications like payments and asset management have more room. Second, this is an intermediate step in the industry's iteration — experts go in to handle assets and look at corporate governance, and find management failures at a very childish level; these aren't appearing for the first time, the last cycle also had projects using the naive idea that ‘if the quantity of money stays fixed it can resist inflation’, violating the most basic economic principles.

— Tan Jing
