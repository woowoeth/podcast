Opening a store credit card might quietly open a second account on you

Some major department store cards automatically bind a Visa or Mastercard without your knowledge, with unpaid monthly balances quietly transferred to that card—by the time you discover it, the bill has gone to collections.

That card machine left 80s-90s cashiers with lasting trauma

Holly joked on social media that retail workers who heard the "knuckle buster" card reader in the 80s and 90s still react with panic. Many confirmed the fear in comments. She shared her own experience working at a department store hair salon: when cards failed, she had to hand-write the card number, couldn't reprint the receipt, and customers waited 12 minutes on Saturday afternoons.

— Holly
15:28

A winter storm proved the credit card worth keeping

Tracy got her first credit card only for emergencies. The real emergency came during her time at UNC Asheville, when a winter blizzard forced her to return to campus immediately or risk being trapped at her parents' home for over a week due to poor road clearing. Her dorm wouldn't let her check in early, so she used the card to book a motel.

— Tracy
17:31

Credit card companies target students before judgment develops

The hosts discussed how card companies recruited on campus when students' judgment centers were still developing. Combined with cultural messaging about student loans as "good debt," this led many graduates to carry both student debt and credit card debt they shouldn't have had.

— Tracy
19:32

Your department store card secretly opened a second account

Holly shared a 90s cautionary tale: many store cards automatically created linked Visa or Mastercard accounts without telling you. Unpaid monthly balances were quietly transferred to that hidden account. She only discovered hers existed when collections called—by then the bill had already escalated. She admits some fault but says the structure itself was deeply problematic.

— Holly

Glossary

knuckle buster
A manual credit card imprinting device common before the 1990s, where cashiers slid a handle to press the card number onto a carbon paper receipt.
