Smashing Threshing Machines Won 252 Death Sentences, and the Workers Still Lost

Nineteenth-century English farm laborers smashed threshing machines to protest unemployment, and got 252 death sentences, 481 or 505 people transported to Australia, and the limited parliamentary reform of 1832 — the workers' fate unchanged, the political map redrawn.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:09

The threshing machine was only the last straw; enclosure was the root

Enclosure in England began in the twelfth century and peaked after 1750; between 1770 and 1830 roughly six million acres of common land were enclosed. People who lost grazing rights and the right to cut fodder either went to the cities to find work or lost their livelihood entirely with no compensation, left dependent on the farmers who owned the land. Unlike the usual "machines cause unemployment" narrative, the episode lays out the institutional dispossession first: enclosure, a labor surplus after the wars ended, the Corn Laws, and reform of the Poor Law. The threshing machine was just the straw that broke the camel's back.

7:18

One machine took half a winter's income

Threshing was the autumn and winter farm work of separating grain from the ear, done entirely by hand with a flail before machines appeared. It was not just physical labor; it could account for fifty percent of a farm worker's winter income, and in winter there was almost no other way to earn money. Though people tried to build threshing machines as early as the seventeenth century, it was not until the 1710s that water- and horse-powered models became reliable and cost-effective. Once a landowner bought one, the hand threshers were out of work. The more efficient the machine, the more directly it hit seasonal unemployment — that was the economic root of the threats and the riots.

9:19

Machine breaking was not a Luddite invention

Machine destruction as a form of protest in Britain goes back at least to the seventeenth century. In 1675 London Spitalfields weavers smashed new looms that "could do the work of twenty men"; in 1779 three hundred of Samuel Nead's knitting frames were destroyed. The Luddites pushed this pattern to its peak. Supporters argued that in the long run machines would lower prices, expand demand and increase employment, but that did not help the people unemployed right then; another often overlooked reason was that machine-made goods were of lower quality. For artisans who depended on the guild system, none of this was abstract economics — it was whether they ate.

13:26

Captain Swing on the threatening letters was a signature, not a person

The Swing Riots take their name from the signature on threatening letters, Captain Swing. Unlike Ned Lud, who was built into a legendary leader during the Luddite movement, Captain Swing had no organizer persona; he was simply the author of the threatening letters. The letters threatened that "if you don't put up your threshing machines, you will have a swing" — swing meaning both the sway of the gallows and the swing of the flail at threshing. The actions were not purely smash-and-grab either: demonstrators would first negotiate with landowners and parsons, and some landowners smashed their own machines or agreed to raise wages and refund tithes to avoid greater losses.

16:33

The workers' spokesman was a royalist who had opposed reform

William Cobbett was initially a royalist who denounced the French Revolution, opposed democratic reform and supported enclosure. After buying a farm in 1805 and traveling around in person, he saw the destitution of agricultural laborers, admitted his earlier views had been wrong, and turned to vigorously advocating parliamentary reform. But he was not simply a spokesman for laborers: his thinking included racism and antisemitism, and he yearned for a romanticized England that had never actually existed. This contradiction meant that later he condemned arson as an "abominable crime" while insisting the root cause was extreme poverty — the government did not arrest him for nothing.

27:55

The real violence happened in the courts, not the fields

The uprising swept southern and eastern England, with at least three thousand incidents. The government did not wait for the regular assizes but set up special commissions to try people quickly. Between December 1830 and January 1831, 252 people were sentenced to death, though only 19 were actually hanged; 481 or 505 people were transported to Australia (both figures appear in the research materials); nearly 650 were imprisoned. The defendants were not only farm laborers but also blacksmiths, shoemakers, carpenters and wheelwrights. This repression made the Swing Riots the largest popular revolt in England since the Peasants' Revolt of 1381.

29:57

The revolt did not improve workers' lives, but it drove parliamentary reform

After the Swing Riots, the daily life of farm laborers barely changed: many of the concessions landowners promised were never honored, and the new Poor Law of 1834 instead expanded the workhouses, producing the Victorian workhouses of Dickens. But the riots were clearly connected to the passage of the Great Reform Act of 1832: the constituencies where demonstrations occurred overlapped heavily with those that supported reform. The act abolished a large number of "rotten boroughs," created new constituencies and expanded the franchise, but only men qualified, and the poorest workers still did not. Cobbett was elected to Parliament from a new constituency; after the Poor Law of 1834 he publicly called for uprising, and in 1835 he died in bankruptcy.

Glossary

threshing machine
Mechanical threshing equipment that appeared in the eighteenth century, far more efficient than human labor, and the trigger of the Swing Riots.
flail
A hand threshing tool: a long handle swinging a short stick to beat the sheaves and separate the grain.
Corn Laws
British laws restricting grain imports and protecting landowners' grain prices, which workers saw as driving up the price of food.
workhouse
A place where the poor were forced to work and live, the symbol of Victorian poor relief.
select vestries
Parish committees that administered poor relief, with the power to distinguish the "deserving" poor from the "idle."
rotten boroughs
Constituencies with tiny populations that still held parliamentary seats, abolished en masse by the Reform Act of 1832.
