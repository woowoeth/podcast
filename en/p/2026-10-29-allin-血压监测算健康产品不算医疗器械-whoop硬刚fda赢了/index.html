Blood pressure monitoring as health feature, not medical device—Whoop won the FDA fight

Whoop classified blood pressure as a health function, withstood FDA's warning letter, got the agency to revise its guidance, and kept the feature live.

High retention, not hardware sales, saved the company

When Whoop launched at $500 per device in 2017, a year of data showed two things: users who bought wore the device long-term—rare for wearables—but few consumers would pay $500 upfront. The team dropped entry prices and shifted to subscription, letting more people try before committing. Between 2018 and 2019, this strategy drove growth and transformed the business from hardware sales to recurring revenue.

— Will Ahmed
4:02

Subscriptions inverted the upgrade cycle incentive

Hardware-only companies face an incentive to release new models every 12 months regardless of innovation, because each sale funds the next, and older devices can't gain features without killing new-device sales. Subscriptions flip this: Whoop releases a new generation only when hardware fundamentally improves, while continuously pushing new capabilities to devices already in the field—ECG, atrial fibrillation detection, blood testing, HealthSpan, and blood pressure insights all rolled out as software updates to existing users.

— Will Ahmed

Two extra breaths per minute sent him to the ER

Jason shared a personal story: one day his respiratory rate jumped from a normal 16 to 17–18 breaths per minute, skin temperature rose 2 degrees, and the pattern repeated for two straight days. Sunday night, chest pain arrived. At the cardiac center, CT and EKG revealed inflammation around his lungs and chest cavity, compressing the heart—not a heart attack. Three years earlier, the same pattern—elevated skin temperature and HRV anomalies—had warned him of COVID infection before symptoms emerged.

— Jason Calacanis
13:28

Abnormal heart rate revealed stage-four lymphoma and testicular cancer

Will shared two user cases. A healthy baseball player with a baseline resting heart rate of 50 once saw his heart rate spike to 120–130 during sleep; he was diagnosed with stage IV Hodgkin lymphoma and recovered after treatment. Another user who had worn Whoop for nearly a decade with a stable HRV baseline of 100 saw it suddenly drop to 50 and stay there; testing revealed testicular cancer. Will acknowledged that this kind of disease signal reverse-engineered from physiology wasn't familiar to medicine at the time.

— Will Ahmed
19:43

Whoop trains AI on its own data rather than selling it

When asked whether AI companies wanted to buy the 50-billion-hour dataset, Will said Whoop chose to train its own foundation model on the data rather than sell it. As users uploaded blood tests, physical exams, and medical records, the team began observing that certain biomarker abnormalities—such as cortisol levels—could be predicted from physiology alone without direct measurement, giving users a concrete recommendation: go get this tested.

— Will Ahmed
35:11

Blood pressure as health feature won the FDA fight

Four years ago, Whoop wanted to offer wristband blood pressure monitoring without a cuff. The company collected data from 70,000 participants in a stress-sleep-blood pressure study to train the algorithm. Historically, FDA classified all blood pressure monitoring as a medical device requiring approval—unlike heart rate monitoring for fitness, which is a health function. Whoop argued that blood pressure is tightly linked to stress and sleep, treated it as a health feature, and launched. FDA sent a warning letter; the two sides clashed publicly. Ultimately, FDA updated its guidance, and Blood Pressure Insights remains a health feature in the product today; ECG took a separate 510(k) clinical pathway and earned formal medical device approval.

— Will Ahmed
42:29

The member, not the data, is the product

When suggested that Whoop should make its own blood pressure cuffs and scales, Will redirected to business model. The company maintains strict data policies and does not sell user data. "You are not the product. Membership is the product." He viewed this as the key differentiator from ad-driven Big Tech companies and the foundation of user trust when sharing blood tests, medical records, and GLP-1 medication information.

— Will Ahmed
47:34

LeBron's endorsement proved organic use beats paid marketing

Whoop announced LeBron James as global ambassador, but he had already worn Whoop for 11 years as one of the first 100 users—with no formal relationship beforehand. Will explained the early-stage policy: never pay athletes to wear the device, because it must be worn 24/7 and no payment can change that if they don't genuinely like it. Only when a product truly works and athletes use it organically does it lead to formal endorsement deals. This echoes disputes over whether wearables are allowed in competition; the Australian Open once required players to remove Whoop.

— Will Ahmed

Glossary

HRV
Heart rate variability—fluctuation between consecutive heartbeats; higher values typically signal healthier autonomic nervous system regulation.
GLP-1
GLP-1 receptor agonist—a class of weight-loss and blood sugar control medications, including semaglutide.
AFib
Atrial fibrillation—an irregular heart rhythm requiring accurate detection by medical devices.
510(k)
510(k) pathway—a streamlined FDA approval route for medical devices.
