The biographer of power's corruption: America can't build things because it overcorrected

He says today's environmental reviews, community committees, and lawsuits are no fewer obstacles than in Moses's era; what's truly scarce is someone willing to find workarounds and get things done despite all resistance.

America's building paralysis stems from overcorrecting Moses-era obstacles

Moses forced his way around landowner opposition to build Jones Beach, yet today's Second Avenue Subway took nearly 50 years to open 1.5 miles at $4 billion, and California's high-speed rail burned through $15 billion without completing a single mile. Caro argues these are the costs of safety mechanisms added to prevent another Moses; they block everyone, not just the ruthless. America now needs some ‘Moses’ energy—but without his disregard for public opinion.

— Robert Caro
16:57

Idealists turn ruthless when repeated obstacles force adaptation

Moses started as a true idealist: he studied civil service reform to make promotion merit-based, not political, and wanted to open Long Island beaches to the poor. But building the Cross Bronx Expressway, he refused compromise even when offered to shift two blocks south and reduce displacements from tens of thousands to hundreds. He bulldozed through anyway; when completed, it became the world's longest traffic jam, displacing thousands without relocation. Caro notes this wasn't sudden moral collapse but gradual learning: after enough failures, ruthlessness becomes pragmatic.

— Robert Caro
20:30

When power vanishes, the phone stops ringing

At Moses's peak influence, secretaries interrupted Caro's interviews bearing stacks of callers requesting favors. Years later, when Moses had lost power, Caro noticed no calls arrived after they finished talking—nobody needed him anymore. More dramatically, in 1962 Moses tried his usual tactic: threatening to resign unless Rockefeller obeyed. Rockefeller's answer: ‘I regret to accept it’—the threat that had worked on every previous mayor and governor simply failed.

— Robert Caro
26:05

Destroying opponents became Moses's tool as resistance hardened

Bronx politician Henry Epstein opposed the Cross Bronx route and proposed shifting two blocks south to cut displacements from tens of thousands to a few hundred. Instead of reconsidering, Moses destroyed Epstein by spreading rumors about an affair. Caro shows this became Moses's pattern: find someone blocking you, uncover their weakness—infidelity, corruption, any leverage—and deploy it without hesitation. This ruthlessness was Moses's self-taught survival law, born from years of failed attempts at compromise.

— Robert Caro
29:50

Depression-era cheap labor built faster and more beautiful

Moses accomplished so much partly because the Great Depression flooded the market with desperate architects he could hire cheaply and fire at will. Caro notes a paradox: early 20th-century buildings like Grand Central Terminal were more beautiful than later ones, also because labor was cheaper—developers could afford to hire craftsmen to carve decorative stonework on rooftops nobody would see. Scarcity of money paradoxically enabled abundance of beauty.

— Robert Caro
37:00

Kennedy lacked Johnson's obsession with remaking America

When Dwarkesh asked whether civil rights and anti-poverty legislation would have passed sooner if Kennedy had won without needing Johnson as vice president, Caro said no. Kennedy was charming and skilled diplomatically, but lacked Johnson's drive to fundamentally transform America. Johnson's obsession stemmed from youth teaching poor children in Cotulla—it fueled his later education and anti-poverty laws. Caro admits he still doesn't understand why Johnson kept escalating Vietnam; that mystery will be his next book.

— Robert Caro
45:57

Cutting 350,000 words meant losing entire lives from the record

Caro submitted The Power Broker at 1.05 million words; fitting one volume required cutting to roughly 700 pages—350,000 words gone. His editor Bob Gottlieb said ‘I deleted some of the best things I've ever read.’ Among the cut material were detailed accounts of figures like Sam Rayburn, who actually controlled the House and passed countless Roosevelt bills, yet has no proper biography. Caro donated his files to the New-York Historical Society at reduced cost so future researchers could access this unpublished material.

— Robert Caro
52:02

The frontier's true cost was grinding isolation, not violence

Western films never captured the real loneliness of hill country: Johnson's ancestors moved to the Texas frontier where Indian raids were possible, but the grinding agony was daily life without neighbors. Caro's chapter on Johnson's mother describes her walking uphill and seeing only more hills. Elderly women he interviewed would say ‘22 miles from Austin you'd see a cattle pen; another 22 miles before you got to our place’—no other households between. Caro admits this frontier isolation shaped himself: he stopped answering his phone decades ago, a habit that echoed through his entire social life.

— Robert Caro
