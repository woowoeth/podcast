ExtraCredits 2026-09-26

Two diplomats drew the Middle East's borders using place-name letters

Britain secretly divided the Middle East with France while simultaneously promising Arab rebels independence—two conflicting commitments that became the foundation of today's border disputes.

To understand how today's Middle Eastern borders came about and what the Sykes-Picot secret agreement actually specified, this episode tells the story in accessible terms.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:04

The sick man of Europe was a political invention, not historical inevitability

Historians now recognize the ‘sick man of Europe’ label for the late Ottoman Empire as outdated—decline was not inevitable. The empire's economy remained stable in the early nineteenth century; it pursued economic, legal, and social reforms, and even decriminalized homosexuality in 1858. The real turning point came with the global economic crisis of 1873. Two years later, the empire went bankrupt and was forced to let Britain and France take over debt management and parts of its economy. This humiliation drove Sultan Abdulhamid II in the 1890s to redirect his resentment toward internal scapegoats, unleashing a massacre of Armenian Christians.

14:20

The promises to Hussein were deliberately vague to preserve flexibility

In its promise to Sharif Hussein of an independent Arab state, Britain left disputed areas like Syria vague—‘to be decided after the war’—while Palestine was omitted entirely. This wasn't oversight; it was deliberate. Both sides could read the same text as meaning either ‘this counts toward me’ or ‘this doesn't count for me,’ preserving room to reverse course later. Meanwhile, another British representative was simultaneously promising the same territory to someone else—the first hidden bomb in this entire history.

16:21

Two diplomats drew the Middle East's borders with a ruler and letters

When Britain's Sykes and France's Picot negotiated how to divide the Ottoman Middle East, they literally drew on a map: ‘from the E in Acre to the K in Kirkuk,’ slicing Arab territory into direct-rule zones and spheres of influence. France received Lebanon, most of Syria, and northern Iraq; Britain received southern Iraq, Jordan, and parts of Palestine; Palestinian holy sites would be internationally administered. The secret pact was formally signed on May 16, 1916, and nowhere in it was there room for Hussein's envisioned independent Arab state.

19:24

The Balfour Declaration promised Palestine to three conflicting parties

The 1917 Balfour Declaration publicly backed establishing a Jewish homeland in Palestine to sway American Jews to push Washington into the war, and to encourage Russia's Bolsheviks to rejoin the fighting. Britain miscalculated on both counts: most American Jews wanted to integrate into American society rather than emigrate; the Bolsheviks viewed Zionism as capitalist intrigue. Furious, the Bolsheviks publicly released Russia's archived secret pacts with the Allies, including Sykes-Picot—Britain's conflicting promises to multiple parties instantly exposed.

26:49

Faisal's Arab Syrian Kingdom lasted only 139 days before collapse

In March 1920, the Syrian National Congress declared the Arab Syrian Kingdom with Faisal as monarch, vowing to resist French invasion. France refused recognition, America refused to accept it as a mandated territory, and Britain's allied forces had already withdrawn the previous year. On July 24, French forces defeated Faisal's army, occupied Damascus, and exiled him to Britain. The kingdom, established with high hopes, survived from declaration to collapse in only 139 days—though Faisal would later become king elsewhere.

31:08

Iraqis unified only when facing British occupation

British colonial officials worried that Iraq, hastily assembled from disparate regions, lacked a shared sense of identity. Events provided a simple answer: the moment Britain announced its rule, everyone unified—unified in resistance to British mandate authority. Sunni and Shia Muslims, different ethnicities, urban and rural tribes joined the rebellion. Britain had to bring in troops from India to retake cities and deployed the air force with indiscriminate bombing to barely restore control. The uprising killed 10,000 Iraqis.

35:12

Only two Arabs attended Churchill's conference that divided the Middle East

At the 1921 Cairo Conference, Churchill's assembled team was humorously dubbed by Churchill himself the ‘Forty Thieves’—and only two Arabs were seated at the table. The conference ratified the ‘Sharifian Solution’: install three of Hussein's sons as kings of Jordan and Iraq respectively, while keeping them economically and diplomatically dependent on Britain. Lawrence later wrote that Churchill's arrangement had ‘untangled all the knots’ and fully kept Britain's promises—yet the violence it subsequently unleashed exceeded anything Ottoman rule had inflicted in a century.

41:32

RAF bombing made Iraq into a colonial occupation success story

When the Royal Air Force took over Iraq's internal security, tactics shifted to relentless indiscriminate bombing around the clock. One operation nearly razed a rebellious Kurdish village and killed a third of its inhabitants. The bombing campaign dropped 97 tons of ordnance, killed 9,000 Iraqis, and cost the British 9 soldiers and 11 aircraft. Parliament declared it a triumph. Meanwhile, French counterinsurgency in Syria killed 6,000 and left 100,000 homeless. Saudi forces captured Mecca in 1925 and established Saudi Arabia in 1932—the essential outline of today's Middle Eastern map took shape in this violence.

Glossary

Sykes-Picot Agreement
1916 secret accord between Britain and France dividing Ottoman Middle Eastern territory into zones of control and influence.
Balfour Declaration
1917 British statement publicly endorsing the establishment of a Jewish homeland in Palestine.
Mandate system
International trusteeship system under which Britain and France administered Middle Eastern territories while formally acting as trustees rather than colonial rulers.
Sharifian solution
Winston Churchill's 1921 plan to install three of Sharif Hussein's sons as rulers of Jordan and Iraq while maintaining British economic and diplomatic control.
Wahhabism
Eighteenth-century conservative Islamic reform movement that became the ideological foundation of the Saudi dynasty.
