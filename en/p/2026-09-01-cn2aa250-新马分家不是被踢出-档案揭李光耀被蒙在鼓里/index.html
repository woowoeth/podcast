The Separation Was Not an Expulsion: Archives Reveal Lee Kuan Yew Was Kept in the Dark

The 1965 separation of Singapore and Malaysia was not Singapore being expelled by Malaysia, but rather Lee Kuan Yew's negotiating representative Goh Keng Swee pushing it through behind his back. The archives uncover this reconstructed founding narrative.

4:03

The Albatross archives do not overturn history, but reinforce Goh Keng Swee's role as the independent actor

It was only in 1994, when writing his memoirs, that Lee Kuan Yew was permitted to read the oral history recordings Goh Keng Swee made between 1980 and 1981, and learned for the first time that Goh had not followed his instruction to press Tun Razak for a looser arrangement between the two territories. Lee wrote: "I knew they were single-mindedly determined to get Singapore out of the federal parliament, so I had no choice but to comply with their wishes and separate us." The archives strengthen Goh Keng Swee's role as more resolutely pro-independence than Lee Kuan Yew.

13:58

The "Albatross" metaphor: the Federation of Malaya was a burden on Singapore

Goh Keng Swee cited Coleridge's 1798 poem "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner": the old sailor senselessly shoots the albatross, disaster strikes the ship, and the crew hangs the dead bird around his neck as an inescapable burden. Goh used this to refer to the Federation of Malaysia—Singapore had joined hoping for a common market and complete decolonization, but found itself continuously colonized by the new Kuala Lumpur government: fewer parliamentary seats, higher taxes, with the tax burden on attap houses reaching 25%.

29:49

"Operation Coldstore" was the price of admission for merger

On February 2, 1963, before the Federation of Malaysia was formally established, a three-party council comprising Britain, the Federation of Malaya government, and the Singapore self-government arrested 113 leftists in Singapore. This was seen as Singapore's gesture of sincerity to Kuala Lumpur—clearing out leftist forces in exchange for eligibility to join the Federation. Lee Kuan Yew's non-communist faction thereby eliminated pro-communist rivals such as Lim Chin Siong, but the leftist Barisan Sosialis still took with it more than half of the trade unions and local cadre networks.

— Xu Zhenhua
35:49

Merger was built on institutional inequality

Kuala Lumpur feared the Chinese population would upset central politics, so out of 159 parliamentary seats it gave Singapore only 12, suppressing its representation while demanding higher taxes from Singapore. This "no representation, high taxation" arrangement ran counter to Singapore's original intent of joining the Federation to gain an economic hinterland. On citizenship policy, Singapore had always been liberal, while Malaya set multiple thresholds for Chinese and Indian naturalization; the two sets of standards created a fundamental rift.

— Xu Zhenhua
47:23

Lee Kuan Yew's ambition: replace the MCA and the MIC

A note in the Albatross archives titled "Post-Malaysia Situation" records Lee Kuan Yew's political roadmap: he sought to guard against both the communist threat and Malay-supremacist racism. He judged that the People's Action Party would eventually replace the Malaysian Chinese Association and the Malaysian Indian Congress, becoming a partner UMNO would have to work with. He was confident that through the "Malaysian Malaysia" campaign he could force UMNO to concede under the pressures of Konfrontasi and Anglo-American pressure.

— Xu Zhenhua
1:00:31

PAP leaders once planned a government-in-exile in Cambodia

The Albatross archives exhibition revealed that PAP leaders feared UMNO would wipe them out just as it had dealt with the leftists. Toh Chin Chye and others had already conceived of a worst-case scenario: using personal ties with Sihanouk to establish a government-in-exile in Cambodia and preserve the flame. This history highlights the extreme risk assessment of Singapore's leaders at the time: they knew well the experience of arresting people could backfire on them at any moment.

— Xu Zhenhua
1:07:53

Goh Keng Swee tossed out the "separation" plan in casual conversation

A footnote in Lee Kuan Yew's notes records the day and time—"5:30 on which day"—when he realized Goh Keng Swee had deceived him. Goh never directly told Lee Kuan Yew that UMNO might not insist on separation; the break was something he and Tun Razak proactively proposed in negotiations. Once the idea was spoken, the wheels could not be stopped. Goh is described as more resolutely pro-independence than Lee Kuan Yew—he pushed the separation plan through almost without prior authorization.

— Xu Zhenhua
1:22:24

The archives exhibition serves Singapore's national narrative of "premeditated independence"

The Albatross archives exhibition, with exquisite lighting and 3D-to-4D interactive elements, reshapes Singapore from "forced independence" to "a destiny foreseen"—actively taking charge of its own fate. Goh Keng Swee directly called the separation the best gift to Singapore, and the twelve months of negotiations defined what it means to be Singaporean. This narrative reinforcement appears increasingly important today as it responds to global upheavals, new immigration, and tariff wars.

Glossary

Albatross archives
The oral history archives left by Goh Keng Swee, named "Albatross," referring to the Federation of Malaysia as a burden on Singapore.
Operation Coldstore
The mass arrest of leftists in Singapore by Britain, Malaya, and Singapore before the 1963 merger.
Malaysian Malaysia
The PAP's political line advocating equality for all Malaysians beyond racial boundaries.
Malay supremacy
Article 153 of the Malaysian Constitution grants statutory quotas to Malays, the core ideology of Malay nationalism.
