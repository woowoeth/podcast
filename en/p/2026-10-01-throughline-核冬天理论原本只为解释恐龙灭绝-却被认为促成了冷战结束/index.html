Nuclear winter theory originated in dinosaur extinction research, but is credited with ending the Cold War

Gorbachev and Reagan both credited nuclear winter theory with convincing them that nuclear war could not be won—the decisive factor in their decision to reduce nuclear arsenals.

The K-T boundary's thin clay layer challenges gradual extinction theory

The layers above and below the K-T boundary are starkly different: the Cretaceous layer below is full of dinosaur bone fragments and footprints, while the Tertiary layer above contains no dinosaur fossils at all. Paleontologists long knew extinction happened between the two layers, but not how—they only assumed gradual Darwinian selection. Walter Alvarez initially studied this clay simply wanting to measure how long the deposits took to form, not to explain dinosaur extinction.

12:33

The physicist who built nuclear weapons solved the dinosaur mystery

Luis Alvarez helped the American team solve critical technical challenges for the atomic bomb during World War II, and voluntarily rode the observation plane the day Hiroshima was bombed. After seeing the mushroom cloud, he wrote to his four-year-old son Walter expressing regret for the deaths caused, but hoping the terrible weapon would unite the world and end all war. Decades later, this father and son together unraveled the dinosaur extinction mystery.

— Alec Nevala-Lee
16:37

An anomalous iridium spike uniquely points to asteroid impact

Luis used his physics methods for measuring rare elements to search for iridium in the K-T boundary clay—an element extremely rare on Earth but common in meteorites and asteroids. The iridium content measured 30 times higher than expected, and when the team initially suspected sample contamination, they retested samples from multiple locations. After two years, they confirmed in 1980: the only source that could explain these numbers was a massive asteroid.

23:01

Dinosaur impact models became the template for nuclear war calculations

NASA Ames's Brian Toon saw Alvarez's asteroid hypothesis and immediately thought of the 70,000 nuclear weapons on Earth at the time. He found Richard Turco, who had run nuclear bomb simulations, and first asked how much dust one explosion would kick up, then: "What if all the bombs went off at once?" The result: enough soot would drop global temperatures below freezing, and smoke from burning cities and forests added to the model. Nuclear winter theory was born.

— Brian Toon
28:08

Carl Sagan joined the research; his boss threatened to shut it down

Brian's doctoral advisor Carl Sagan was thrilled by the discovery—the great question he'd always cared about: could nuclear war end human civilization? But the NASA Ames director was furious, threatening to fire everyone in the building if they continued. Sagan personally convinced the director, and the research continued.

— Brian Toon
30:10

One television debate brought nuclear winter to millions of American households

On November 20, 1983, the anti-nuclear film The Day After drew 100 million American viewers. The Nightline roundtable that followed invited policymakers like Kissinger and McNamara to discuss nuclear war. Kissinger criticized the film for oversimplifying nuclear issues, but Sagan said on air: reality is far worse than the movie shows. That's nuclear winter. He used an analogy—‘a room soaked with gasoline, two enemies each holding 9,000 and 7,000 matches’—and after that debate, nuclear winter entered millions of homes.

— Carl Sagan
45:48

Scientists still debated whether nuclear winter was warning or manufactured fear

Since the theory's birth, doubters have said it was peddling fear—including climate skeptic Fred Singer, later exposed as having long received funding from oil and tobacco companies, who proposed a counter-theory of ‘nuclear summer.’ Historian David Sapkovskii (戴维·萨普科夫斯基) admits Sagan and others used the most pessimistic models to shake the public awake, but Sagan's response was: if there's any possibility of destroying everything you care about, no matter how small the odds, all you can do is sound the alarm. Even Luis Alvarez, who first proposed the asteroid hypothesis, privately harbored doubts about nuclear winter but never said so publicly.

— David Sapkowski
51:05

The Soviet cosmonaut said the person to thank most was Carl Sagan

After the Cold War ended, both Gorbachev and Reagan separately stated that nuclear winter theory convinced them nuclear war could not be won—driving their decision to reduce nuclear arsenals. In a speech, Alexei Leonov, the first Soviet to conduct a spacewalk, said everyone in the room owed one person more than anyone else—Carl Sagan. After Gorbachev heard Sagan's presentation on nuclear winter, he told those present: ‘It's over, isn't it?’
