Steve Jobs Archive 1996-11-22

Jobs: Pixar's power structure is inverted, CEO at the bottom

Top creative talent and top graphics talent are both extremely scarce and can leave anytime, so Jobs says Pixar's management inverts itself to serve them—the CEO becomes the bottom.

Talent managementCompany cultureEquity incentivesAnimation industryPixarBusiness model
A 1996 interview, unhurried pace and not particularly dense, but two segments on inverted-pyramid talent management and stock-based retention without contracts deserve close attention.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

What he bought was a dream, not a company

In 1985, Jobs met Ed Catmull, then leading Lucasfilm's computer division, who described his dream of making the first fully computer-animated feature film. Jobs said he bought that dream ‘both financially and spiritually’—not just financially, but as an act of faith. The bet took ten years to come true, becoming Toy Story, cinema's first fully computer-synthesized feature, and one of the most commercially successful animated films ever made.

— Steve Jobs
2:03

Elite talent only goes to a handful of companies

Jobs says Pixar leads the industry because it attracts two extremely scarce categories of people simultaneously—the world's best animation talent (who normally go only to Disney, Pixar, perhaps DreamWorks) and the world's best computer graphics talent. Most studios lack the technical culture to attract the latter. Pixar spent ten years synthesizing Hollywood culture and Silicon Valley culture—these two ‘really different’ cultures—into a system where both groups work as peers with equal standing, nearly unmatched in the industry.

— Steve Jobs
6:10

Contract work margins eroded, so Pixar stopped doing it

Jobs reviews the past business model of computer animation—most people in the field operated as labor of love, making money by taking on ads and title sequences, work-for-hire jobs. The problem: these businesses kept getting squeezed. Even if a Listerine ad sold more Listerine, Pixar captured none of that upside, only per-job fees. As more competitors entered, margins collapsed. This year Pixar quit advertising entirely, pulling 25 top talents from contract work to focus on projects where the company retains ownership.

— Steve Jobs
9:18

The CEO serves at the bottom of the hierarchy

Jobs says that in both Silicon Valley and Pixar, elite talent is extremely scarce; if not treated well, they can find another job in ten minutes. So ‘the hierarchy of power inverts’—power structure flips, with the CEO actually at the bottom. He says he feels he's working for these people, because they're the ones doing brilliant work; management's job is to clear obstacles for them on the front lines.

— Steve Jobs
11:19

Hire 125 elite employees in one year without lowering quality

Pixar expanded this year from about 175 people to about 300, with the greatest challenge being ‘not to lower the quality bar one iota’. Jobs says the company built a strong recruiting team, using some ‘competitive advantages’ he wouldn't disclose, ultimately hiring 125 employees he called A-plus—he holds this up as exemplary of what management should actually do.

— Steve Jobs
12:20

Disney taught Pixar: edit the film before you animate it

Jobs explains the fundamental difference between animation and live-action: live-action directors can shoot 10 to 25 times as much material as the finished film and rebuild the film in the editing room; animation is too expensive—even shooting 10% more is unaffordable—so the film must be edited before actual production. This ‘edit-first, animate-second’ methodology is experience Disney developed over decades and cannot be bought; collaborating with Disney gave Pixar direct access to that accumulated knowledge, which they internalized while making Toy Story and later improved upon.

— Steve Jobs
13:21

Hollywood locks talent down with contracts, Pixar uses stock options

Hollywood and Silicon Valley use two completely different methods to retain talent: Hollywood relies on the contract ‘stick’, Silicon Valley on stock options as ‘carrot’. Jobs says signing contracts easily deceives managers—if someone has five years left, they don't worry, only starting to court them again six months before expiration, then filing them away again after renewal. Pixar chose the Silicon Valley model: give everyone stock, align everyone's interests, so the company thinks every day about making Pixar better—so good no one wants to leave—rather than binding people with a contract.

— Steve Jobs
18:28

Technology makes richer images, but cannot fix a bad story

Jobs mentions Disney's 1937 Snow White, which a few years ago was rereleased on video and sold 28 million copies, netting roughly $250 million—sixty years later, the story still works for new generations. He believes people will still watch Toy Story sixty years hence, not because of computer graphics but because of the story about friendship. He also points to another technology curve: ten years ago, the short film Luxo Jr. took about three hours per frame to render; today computers are a hundred times faster, yet Toy Story still takes about three hours per frame, because visual complexity grew a hundredfold too—but he says ‘no amount of technology can turn a bad story into a good story’, something John Lasseter taught him and Pixar's true creed.

— Steve Jobs
