Theodore Roosevelt didn't create the NCAA; a campus death did

After 1905's ‘death harvest’, the NCAA's rules committee came from the NYU president bypassing the Big Three, not from Roosevelt's conference.

8:17

The flying wedge transplanted military tactics directly to the field

Without forward-pass rules, 1870s football became pure collision. Offenses formed a V-shaped flying wedge—one player at the point, teammates behind using collective force to drive him forward. The tactic borrowed its name directly from military wedge-charge formations. Head protection was minimal—just long hair and a wool cap. Defense meant launching another group of bodies into collision.

— Dennis Crawford
9:22

Football became combat training to preserve young men's military fitness

After the Civil War, America's economy shifted from agriculture to commerce, raising fears that young men would lose combat readiness. Football filled the void: it demanded obedience, teamwork, strength, and courage—military virtues. The sport's violence wasn't accidental; society actively encouraged it.

— Dennis Crawford
10:25

Nineteen players died in 1905, defining college football's deadliest year

In 1905, roughly 19 people died in college football games—including high schoolers and amateurs, not just college players—while 137 suffered serious injuries. The San Francisco Call documented the causes: 4 deaths from internal collision injuries, 6 from concussions, 3 from spinal damage, plus 19 skull fractures. This toll drove every subsequent reform.

12:31

Theodore Roosevelt's White House summit produced no rule changes

Roosevelt, a football enthusiast whose son was injured in 1905, brought Harvard, Princeton, and Yale presidents to the White House to demand safer play. Historian Eric Moyen called it a ‘nothing burger’: the three dominant programs had no incentive to change rules that would help rivals compete. A month passed with no action.

14:32

The real reform catalyst was a player's death at Thanksgiving

A player's death at Thanksgiving 1905 in an NYU-Union College game pushed NYU's president to act. He sent a telegram to Harvard's president demanding a new meeting; when refused, he bypassed the Big Three, convened every college president with a football team, and they established the rules committee that became the NCAA.

15:33

Forward-pass legalization was designed to break up mass collisions

The committee swiftly changed the rules: first-down advancement moved from 5 to 10 yards, and the forward pass became legal in 1906. The goal was clear—disperse players across the field to catch passes instead of forming solid lines for head-on collision. This was the crisis's most direct technical legacy.

16:36

The NCAA fought over money and power from its founding

Despite the safety crisis driving its creation, the committee was ‘never just about safety’, according to historian Eric Moyen. It debated payment for players, their name-and-image rights (the precursor to NIL), academic standards for athletes, and—most crucially—who enforces the rules: the NCAA, individual universities, or coaches. These exact issues resurfaced virtually unchanged a century later.

— Eric Moyen
18:40

Today's NIL chaos echoes the 1905 power vacuum exactly

The confusion around NIL, transfer portals, and coaching salaries directly mirrors 1905, according to Moyen: then, university presidents negotiated safety rules among themselves; now, leagues and coaches must negotiate money and authority instead. Same power vacuum, different stakes. The NCAA declined to comment. No clear answer exists yet.

— Eric Moyen

Glossary

flying wedge
A V-shaped offensive formation with one player at the apex and teammates using collective force to drive him forward; borrowed from military wedge-charge tactics.
mass momentum play
An offensive strategy relying on multiple players using collective force to advance the ball, with the flying wedge as its most extreme form.
NIL
Name, image, and likeness rights—an athlete's ability to profit from their personal fame, name, and public image.
death harvest
Contemporary newspaper term for the 1905 college football season, marked by roughly 19 deaths and 137 serious injuries.
