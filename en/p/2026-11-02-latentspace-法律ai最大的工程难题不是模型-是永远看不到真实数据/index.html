The biggest engineering challenge in legal AI is not models—it's data you can never see

Most client data isn't even visible to the law firm itself. Legora's evals team has to guess what lawyers actually do, working only with classified and rewritten synthetic data.

The hardest hire: lawyers from top firms who can code

Legora's evals team blends engineers and lawyers, plus a distinct group of legal engineers—lawyers with CS backgrounds who spent three to four years at top firms. They're the hardest intersection to fill. Jacob calls them the company's hardest hire, but they're invaluable: they run evals and directly shape product design because they use the product every day and see clients every day. They become force multipliers who can summon a hundred-person expert pool to reshape the product.

— Jacob Lauritzen
14:30

AI may not be able to fix the law firm partnership model

Partnership exists because law firms' only capital is human capital—lose people, lose the firm, so just make people shareholders. Jacob argues AI should let firms codify experience and methods into reusable systems—say, a self-iterating knowledge graph—building firm-owned assets independent of any person. Instead, partners are paid by eating what they hunt, so encoding experience costs them clients. And those assets don't follow you when you leave partnership. The incentive misalignment has no answer yet.

— Jacob Lauritzen
16:45

Junior lawyers aren't disappearing—they're splitting into two tiers

The host suggests paralegals are obsolete, junior lawyers are obsolete. Jacob pushes back: that's not what they're seeing. He uses engineers as analogy: a great engineer is worth far more than two years ago because AI multiplies output; same for lawyers. Good junior lawyers become more valuable because AI lets them produce more, higher-quality work. This split always existed—now it's amplified, not the whole group gone.

— Jacob Lauritzen
26:40

Build evals when you cannot see a single user's real data

Legora's biggest engineering problem isn't model ability—it's that you can't see what customers do with the product. Data stays under lock. Often it doesn't even belong to the law firm; it belongs to their clients. Looking is forbidden. The company's only move is its evals team: guess, approximate real scenarios, build those guesses into test sets. Rewritten, anonymized synthetic traces are the only workaround. The stricter the anonymization, the weaker the signal. Jacob calls this the most maddening knot in the business.

— Jacob Lauritzen
28:50

Three years ago law firms wouldn't touch AWS

Legora runs entirely on Azure. Early in the company, law firm customers flatly didn't trust AWS or Google—no one had audited them. But Microsoft was already Office 365's vendor, so it had passed their audit test. Azure became the only option. In three years, that cloud skepticism nearly evaporated. Talk of private deployment vanished too. Legal's cloud migration was compressed into three years by AI pressure.

— Jacob Lauritzen
43:00

Legal engineers don't write code; frontline engineers do

Legora splits on-site support in two: legal engineers are big-firm alumni who know the product and customer workflows—they configure like power users, no code. Forward deployed engineers actually write code and hit customer APIs, usually serial entrepreneurs or large-company veterans. Big customers' custom needs go to legal engineers first; if they can't handle it and it needs product change or deep integration, escalate to forward engineers. This two-tier split lets the company staff big customers without assigning a full engineering team per customer.

— Jacob Lauritzen
50:00

Twenty engineers to beat everyone—he gave up that idea

Jacob once planned to keep the company to twenty engineers forever and beat much larger competitors. The team is now a hundred; he still thinks it's short. Hard competition and customer demand killed the minimalism. Now his thinking is different: collapse the product to a few core primitives—Lists, Projects, Agent. Customers think the company builds many things. Actually they're combinations of those three. He's even imagining a future where agents are themselves APIs, one agent call instead of a full interface to maintain.

— Jacob Lauritzen
1:03:50

Will large model companies squeeze out companies like Legora?

Anthropic and OpenAI have already squeezed out startups dependent on their models—once in code, once in search. Will legal be next? Jacob's read: no. Model companies must cover all enterprise knowledge work at once—law, data analysis, sales, finance. They can't go deep enough in one vertical. Small customers can use Claude for light legal work. But a $400 million acquisition? Enterprises pick the specialist.

— Jacob Lauritzen

Glossary

Batch-evaluate thousands of documents with an agent asking questions on each, then aggregate results into a table.
Forward Deployed Engineer
Engineer based at a large customer site who writes code directly for deep-custom integration.
Legal Engineer
Lawyer with a CS background who spent years at a top firm; handles product configuration and evals, writes no code.
Pareto frontier
Strategy of always choosing the current best model mix for the tradeoff between ability, cost, and latency.

Data processing agreement; contract specifying which suppliers may receive data, determining which AI models or tools can be used.
