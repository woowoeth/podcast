The Opium War Wasn't Fought for Opium, It Was Fought for Tea

Britain bought Chinese tea but had nothing to sell in return; China took only silver, and the world's silver was drained dry. What Britain finally picked up was Indian opium. The driver of that 1839 war was not opium — it was tea.

What's in a British tea bag is dust and fannings

Tea is graded by wholeness into four levels: leaf, broken leaf, fannings, dust — the last two being the siftings left over. Yangwen says the British market drinks mainly these last two grades, packed into tea bags; China drinks whole leaves, with attention to leaf shape, body, and a complete ritual. Her words were that China doesn't drink dust, only leaves. Hugh jokes that dust sounds like a country singer, and Greg admits the British really are drinking fannings. Yangwen came to Britain in 1996 and still doesn't understand why milk and sugar go into tea — once added, by her standard it is ‘no longer tea’.

17:26

The Mongols wanted tea, the Central Plains wanted horses

Yangwen says tea defined China's premodern relations with Mongolia and Central Asia. Nomadic peoples were not settled and needed tea, iron pots, and silk, which could only be obtained from China; China was a rice-and-wheat farming civilisation and lacked horses. Hence the historical tea-horse exchange, trading goods at fixed ratios. Once the relationship broke down, the Mongols, lacking tea, raided southward — the 1449 Tumu crisis is an example: the Ming army was encircled by the Mongols, and roughly fifty thousand soldiers were captured along with the emperor. Afterward the Ming closed the Great Wall for a time, but blockade was never a long-term solution, and in the end the gates had to be reopened for the tea-horse trade.

— Yang Wen
21:34

The name of tea exposes the routes it travelled

Tea's spread left linguistic traces. Yangwen says that in Mongolian, Russian, Turkish, Arabic, and Persian, tea is called cha, while in English it is tea — the overland camel caravan routes left cha, the sea routes left tea. Greg made up a rhyme for it: ‘If it travels by car, it is cha. If it goes by the sea, it is tea.’ The value of this clue is that the same product entered different civilisations through two completely different trade networks, and even its name forked. Tea reached Europe in the early 17th century, with the Portuguese and the Dutch East India Company as the earliest suppliers.

— Yang Wen
29:53

China took only silver, and drained the world dry

By the late 18th century, China had already sucked the world's silver into its treasury. Yangwen says that from Vasco da Gama's voyage in 1497 to 1793, Europeans and Americans came to buy tea, porcelain, and silk, while China took only gold and silver, and over the centuries Europeans found they had nothing the Chinese wanted. She quotes the missionary Robert Hart: the Chinese have the best food (rice), the healthiest drink (tea), the best clothing material (silk), and need nothing from Britain. In 1793 the Macartney mission demanded free trade, and the emperor's attitude was: I permit my subjects to sell you a little tea, and that is a favour. The only thing Britain had left that could break into China was Indian opium.

— Yang Wen
39:04

Stolen green tea grew into black tea in India

In 1843, to break its dependence on Chinese tea, Britain sent the botanist Robert Fortune to infiltrate China's interior and steal tea plants. He bought a fake queue, wore a hat to cover his unshaved forehead, and travelled deep into the mountains, digging up whole tea plants and shipping them back to Assam in India. Yangwen points out an irony: what he stole was mainly green tea from the southeastern coast, but grown in Assam's stronger sunlight and richer soil it turned into black tea — the raw material now regarded as the archetypal British black tea was in fact a Chinese green tea variety. Fortune himself did not get rich from it; Yangwen says he was not wealthy when he died.

— Yang Wen
42:12

The Industrial Revolution was pushed along by tea and sugar

Some economic historians have argued that the Industrial Revolution was driven by tea. Yangwen says caffeine plus the sugar in tea let the working class refuel at half past ten in the morning and half past three in the afternoon, working longer and producing more — Sydney Mintz's Sweetness and Power is the representative statement of this argument. The same logic explains why tea replaced small beer in the mid-18th century as the middle class's breakfast drink of choice. By 1923, 53% of the world's tea supply was being drunk in Britain.

— Yang Wen
45:13

Britain's tea today comes mainly from Kenya

Tea cultivation followed the colonial map. The seedlings stolen from China first took root in Assam, India, and in 1885 India exported 35,000 tons of tea a year, which Greg converts into 7,000 African elephants. In 1903 Britain opened plantations in Kenya, and Yangwen says Kenya is today Britain's main source of tea. She also notes that Chinese tea bought today at places like Fortnum Mason has mostly been systematically machine-cut, so the leaf shape can't be seen, and what you drink is not the tea she knows.

— Yang Wen
48:26

Tea bags cost the British tea's ritual

In the nuance window, Yangwen offers a prediction: tea will revive. The reason is the environmental movement — people should eat natural things and drink natural things, and tea is a plant to begin with, like a vegetable, good for health and the environment; on the other hand, the modernisation of the tea bag makes her feel tea has lost its ritual, and perhaps its nutrition too. Greg steers the topic to the China–Britain difference: in Chinese tea drinking, the waiting itself is the ritual; the British place more emphasis on the time spent chatting with people after the tea is brewed. Hugh admits he has never experienced so-called ‘slow tea’.

— Yang Wen

Glossary

Camellia sinensis
The scientific name of the tea plant, an evergreen shrub and the sole source plant for tea.
fannings
A grade finer than broken leaf in tea classification, mostly used as tea bag material.
dust
The finest grade in tea classification, the main ingredient of British tea bags.
Commutation Act
The 1784 British act that cut the tea tax from 119% to 12.5%.
tea brick
Tea pressed into bricks to make it easier to transport to Mongolia and Russia.
Tumu crisis
The 1449 battle in which the Ming army was encircled by the Mongols and the emperor was captured.
