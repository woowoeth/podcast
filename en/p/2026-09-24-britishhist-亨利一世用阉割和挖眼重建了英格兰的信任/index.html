Henry I Rebuilt England's Trust With Castration and Blinding

When forged currency and private aristocratic warfare froze the market and collapsed the social contract, Henry I used torture and a unified court to bring violence under royal control — inadvertently laying the foundation for the Anglo-American legal system.

3:08

Two negotiators are summoned by the pope, and the ceremony is forced to wait

The agreement between Henry and Anselm needed formal ratification, but the pope suddenly required the two chief negotiators — Henry's representative William of Warlwest and Anselm's confidant Baldwin of Tornay — to attend the Easter papal council. These two had been at odds since the start of the dispute, and had even travelled to Rome together to argue face to face. With them absent, the ceremony could only be postponed to the next major feast, Pentecost. This shows that although the agreement had been reached, its execution was still constrained by external church politics.

4:13

Anselm's "diplomatic illness" leaves Henry on the back foot

As Pentecost approached, Anselm suddenly pleaded illness and withdrew. Jamie argues this was very likely a diplomatic illness: Anselm was old and had a history of fasting, but more crucially he knew Henry needed papal support to rule England and Normandy at the same time. Henry had twice bypassed the normal line of succession, his neighbours were unhappy, the threat of war was rising, and Anselm therefore had the leverage to stall and force Henry into further concessions. In the end Henry himself proposed 1 August, the feast of Lammas, and Anselm agreed.

— Jamie
11:22

The bishops oppose Anselm, but the nobles support him

At the ratification ceremony an unusual alignment appeared: many bishops had been appointed by Rufus or Henry, and if Henry gave up the right of investiture unconditionally, their appointments might be declared invalid by Anselm, so they urged Henry to hold firm. Nobles such as Richard of Redbirds and Robert of Mulan, however, held lands on both sides of the English Channel, and if the pope opposed Henry, war would damage their interests, so they urged Henry to concede. The two sides argued for three days, until Henry summoned Anselm into his presence and announced that he renounced secular investiture, while Anselm guaranteed that bishops who performed homage would not lose their positions.

— Jamie
16:34

Anselm takes the monasteries, Henry takes the bishoprics

After the agreement was ratified, Henry appointed a great many church positions. The abbots Anselm consecrated were mostly connected to Bec, Caen and Canterbury — Anselm's political bases — and through them he built a network of support for reform. But the bishops were entirely different: Roger of Salisbury and Richard of London had mistresses, William Warlwest was Henry's administrator, and William Giffard came from a predatory family. Jamie speculates that the two men reached an understanding: Anselm got the monasteries, Henry got the bishoprics, so long as no one like Ranulf Flambard was appointed.

— Jamie
22:52

Henry plants his own men first, then starts reforming

From 1100 onward Henry kept replacing Rufus's men with his own loyalists, and after the victory at Tinchebray he acquired a large number of confiscated secular estates. He distributed these lands to his supporters, including Robert de Bruce, who received land in Yorkshire. Jamie points out that before reforming, Henry made sure his allies held power — otherwise he might have ordered the arrest of an opponent and been arrested himself. By Pentecost 1108, Henry began to reveal the purpose of these personnel arrangements: first reform the church, then reform the judiciary and the currency.

— Jamie
33:30

The real predators are the fully armed Norman nobles

Henry's target was not the lower clergy but the Norman nobility. Since the Conquest, the king's travelling court had become vast and unpredictable, and wherever it went people fled into the forests to escape theft, rape and murder. Jamie stresses that Rufus was not the cause but a symptom; the rot began in 1066 or even earlier. England had once had a unified currency, nationwide taxation and hundred courts; now corruption, theft, murder and currency debasement were rampant, the social contract had collapsed, and the market had frozen for lack of trust.

— Jamie
49:22

Restoring the old courts is only the surface; centralisation is the substance

Henry issued a writ declaring that the shire courts and hundred courts were restored to the traditional times and places of the age of Edward the Confessor. The writ speaks of "my shires and my hundreds", directly asserting royal control over justice. He also ruled that land disputes were to be heard in the king's court or the shire court, and that private warfare was forbidden. Jamie points out that this looks like a return to the old order but in fact unified the administration of justice and placed it under royal authority, laying the foundation for English common law, which later influenced the United States and roughly 80 countries.

— Jamie
55:27

Coin forgers are blinded and castrated, and halfpennies must be round

Around 1108 Henry issued a law against currency debasement: anyone who forged or debased currency would be blinded and castrated. He required that halfpennies be struck round rather than cut from pennies, so that the edges could not be clipped. He also ordered moneyers to cut a small notch in pennies, halfpennies and farthings to prove the coins were not plated. To restore trust in goods, he standardised the unit of length, the ell, taking the length of Henry's arm as the official standard. Jamie comments that this was both tyrannical and more efficient.

— Jamie
58:33

Theft and robbery become capital crimes, and the king's own men are not exempt

After consulting with Anselm and the nobles, Henry decided on harsh law: theft and robbery became capital crimes, and murder, already a capital crime, now more so. However rich a man was, a capital offence meant hanging, unless the king pardoned him. This further concentrated the monopoly on violence in the king. More crucially, Henry also issued an ordinance forbidding his own household staff from plunder, extortion, theft and rape, on pain of blinding and castration. Edmer praised this greatly. Jamie points out that Henry was not merciful but a clever dictator: an orderly flock is easier to shear.

Glossary

investiture controversy
The medieval conflict between monarchs and popes over the power to appoint bishops and abbots.
Gresham's Law
Bad money drives out good: when two currencies circulate, people hoard the good and spend the bad.
Shire and Hundred courts
The basic units of local justice and taxation in England, where public assemblies handled cases.
common law
A case-law-based legal system applied uniformly across the country, originating in England.
halfpenny
Literally half a penny; at the time pennies were often cut in half for use.
