Americans Suddenly Fell in Love With Japan — Not Because It's Cheap, Because It's Normal

The real driver of Japan's tourism explosion isn't the exchange rate, it's the functionality of public space — clean subways, on time, no trash. What American visitors experience in Tokyo is a society where entropy has been paused.

She counted 119 people, 96 of them her own guests, averaging one person every five days, 20% of all her days. She says she spends all her free time helping visiting friends plan itineraries. This data visualization is itself evidence that the problem has spun out of control, and it's the starting point for the question this episode sets out to answer.

8:34

Japan is poor in resources, rich in story

Roland Kelts argues the first reason for American interest in Japan isn't economic, it's story. He says Japan has almost no natural resources, no oil, very little land, only small rice paddies, yet its story culture is astonishing. The world's first novel is Japanese, The Tale of Genji, a thousand years old. Through manga, anime and the internet, the whole world discovered this narrative capacity. This didn't start in the 1990s; Roland says there were two earlier waves: Japanism in late 19th-century Europe, and the Beats' and hippies' fascination with Zen and Buddhist concepts in the 50s and 60s.

— Roland Nozomu-Kelts
15:05

America used to fear Japan

David Marks points out that for seventy years after World War II, mainstream American feeling toward Japan was fear, not affection. Before 1945 Japan was an enemy nation; afterward it was seen as a developing country rebuilding itself. In the 70s and 80s it became anxiety about Japanese economic power, and Michael Crichton's Rising Sun cast Japan as the villain. After the Cold War ended there was even a CIA position paper discussing whether Japan should be the next enemy. When Japan's economic bubble burst in the 90s, America actually felt a kind of relief. When David moved to Japan in 2003, a friend visiting Japan was the event of the year, because nobody came at all.

— W. David Marks
24:29

The government made Japan cheap by hand

The third wave was a government plan. In 2003 Junichiro Koizumi launched the Visit Japan campaign, with a target raised from 5 million tourists a year to 10 million, and ads full of Western stereotypes: geisha, cherry blossoms, temples. But what actually worked was Shinzo Abe deliberately weakening the yen in the 2010s. The intent was to help exports; the result was making hotels and restaurants cheap for foreigners. Roland says in Tokyo you can eat a very good soba lunch for under $10, while in New York $10 won't even buy you an apple and a banana. In 2013 Abe relaxed visas for Thailand and Malaysia, then China and Indonesia; Asian tourists surged first, and European and American tourists followed.

— Roland Nozomu-Kelts
29:37

Japan's functionality is the biggest draw

Roland says the most important reason is functionality. Public space in many countries, especially the United States, has become disabled: parks are dirty and dangerous, rail systems are terrible, airports are a mess. In Japan, things simply work. PJ adds that American ears hear complaints about poor public order and dirty subways as elitism, as rich people's complaints. But in Japan, wanting a clean subway isn't elitism — it's the reverse: why shouldn't public things be good? On Tokyo streets you see no trash, not even cigarette butts, and you don't smell urine or feces. PJ says this isn't the reason people go the first time, but it may be the reason some people go again.

— Roland Nozomu-Kelts
36:52

Japan reveres the people who make things, not the people who sell them

The sixth reason is craft. The Japanese word shokunin means artisan, someone who can make things extremely well, and such people are still respected in Japan, a status traceable to the 17th-century Edo period, when makers outranked merchants. Roland tells of an animation industry person explaining the Walmart model to him: in America, the company selling the goods comes first, nobody cares who made it, and a $5 pillow was made by someone who knows nothing about pillows. But in Japan, the person who makes a pillow extremely well is the most valuable. He took an American friend to a ramen shop, they queued to buy a ticket, the ticket said come back at 3 p.m., and after eating the friend said it was the best soup of his life, then asked why they don't franchise. Roland said because franchising means you can't control the quality of the noodles, the scallions, the pork bone broth and the bamboo shoots — their scallions come from a man named Sato in Chiba who has done this for many years.

— Roland Nozomu-Kelts
41:10

The listener survey and the experts almost perfectly overlap

Search Engine sent a questionnaire to hundreds of thousands of listeners and got a huge number of replies. Nearly half mentioned culture; some said they binge-watched anime growing up, played Nintendo and Sony, and are fans of Pokemon and One Piece. About a quarter mentioned the weak yen; one said the exchange rate is the best in 30 years. Others mentioned craft. What made PJ happiest were the reasons only a big survey can capture: someone decided to go because of FOMO after seeing so many people go, someone went to see the Godzilla museum, and Godzilla brought in five listeners. There were also one-to-one stories: someone went to Japan to see the work of the legendary bonsai master Mazuhiko Kimura; someone fell in love with a person at 18, lost touch more than a decade later, and the only way to see them again was to fly to Japan, and ended up spending four magical days in Osaka.

— PJ Vogt
44:14

Japan is a place where entropy has been paused

Listener enthusiasm for functionality exceeded PJ's expectations. One said they took their family to Japan so they could see what a high-functioning society actually looks like, everything on time, perfectly clean. One said public toilets are everywhere and have bidets, because everyone has to poop, and a shitty butt is disgusting. One said if every country respected each other the way Japanese people do with their punctuality and queuing, the world would be far better. PJ himself says he still loves America, loves that anarchic mixture, but feels something broke about ten years ago — not just politics; drivers are angrier, people are angrier, elbows are sharper, and more people watch TikTok on the subway without headphones. He says America is like an eccentric friend who turned from quirky to not-so-good-quirky, and that makes Japan's appeal understandable.

— PJ Vogt

Glossary

soft power
A concept coined by Harvard professor Joseph Nye, referring to influencing other countries through cultural attraction rather than coercion.
shokunin
A Japanese word for an artisan or chef who makes things extremely well, holding higher status in Japan than merchants.
Japanism
The late 19th-century European fascination with Japanese art and culture, which especially influenced the French Impressionists.
