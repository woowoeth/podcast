Li Xiang: What top founders share is not picking right, but inhumanely-high iteration speed

He says that picking the right category and persisting long-term are just entry-level skills for top founders—what actually separates them is the inhumanely fast iteration speed practiced by Nvidia, Bytedance, and Meituan.

27:15

Reframe college as a choice problem, not a right-or-wrong problem

Li Xiang's monthly income at 18 was ten times his parents'. Rather than argue over whether he should attend university, he reframed the question as a choice between college and entrepreneurship, forcing his parents to weigh trade-offs instead of issuing commands. He showed them bank records proving he could support himself. The reframe worked—they let him go.

— Li Xiang
1:06:36

Only two organizational models win globally; he is building a third

The world's winning organizations fall into two categories: those built on strict process (Huawei, IBM) for complex products where any broken link fails the whole system, and those built on high talent density plus simple tools (Bytedance, Google) for platforms and models. Li Auto tries something different—treating talent density as the body and validated best practices as tools, not structure, to handle both supply-chain complexity and AI software simultaneously.

— Li Xiang
1:31:49

Range anxiety forced the counterintuitive choice of extended-range hybrid

Industry consensus held that extended-range was backward. Li Xiang's decision didn't come from market research—it came from real experience. Driving a Model S to a lakeside resort, he encountered a broken charging station; he barely limped back on downhill momentum. His team resisted, wanting a pure plug-in hybrid instead. He showed them Nissan's e-power, which uses less than one kilowatt-hour and drives extended-range the entire way, yet feels as smooth as a pure electric. That proof convinced them.

— Li Xiang
1:36:51

The investor he'd rejected a year earlier rescued him from fundraising ruin

During Ideal One's production phase, Li Xiang met 150 investors. None committed. Tesla's valuation had crashed to $30 billion, NIO's stock had fallen from 6-7 yuan to around 1 yuan, and the sector was in free fall. Li Xiang was so ill he needed an hour on an airport lounge chair before each meeting. Wang Xing (Meituan founder) had once asked to invest; Li Xiang refused, wanting to avoid Didi's control. This time, Li Xiang simply chatted with him—never asking for money, only discussing value. Wang Xing spontaneously offered 300 million yuan.

— Li Xiang
2:03:02

His five-million-yuan luxury-car claim came from test-driving, not positioning

Li Xiang's claim that the Ideal One was "the best family SUV under 5 million yuan" drew fire from reviewers. The genesis wasn't marketing strategy—it was empiricism. He test-drove cars worth 3 to 10 million yuan owned by industrialists he knew, and found them disappointing. He concluded that at that price point, a car solves social identity problems, not transportation. Wealth simply signals status (e.g., stranger approach at nightclubs). This insight later became L9 marketing copy.

— Li Xiang
2:17:06

Competitors orchestrated the Mega 'coffin car' scandal, not grassroots criticism

After three years in development, the Ideal Mega launched into coordinated "coffin car" attacks. The design team was devastated; some urged an immediate redesign. Li Xiang assessed it as orchestrated black PR—he knows people arrested for similar campaigns. Modern black PR no longer relies on extortion; it monetizes traffic itself, and many operators aren't even from the car industry. The Mega did have genuine issues: the interior copied the L series with no new experience and higher pricing. He fixed it later with a rotating seat and price cuts, but never directly rebutted the coffin car narrative.

— Li Xiang
2:44:23

Top founders don't succeed by picking right; they win through inhumanely frequent iteration

Li Xiang identifies three traits among top founders he's observed. First two are obvious: picking the right category, and persisting long-term. The third is completely inhuman—facing real market feedback at extreme frequency. Huang Renxun releases one GPU generation per year while competitors do one every three. Bytedance runs dozens of parallel versions. Meituan adjusts operations so frequently outsiders never see it. This is reinforcement learning: pretraining matters, but growth comes from constant real-world iteration.

— Li Xiang
3:11:37

A shareholder coup taught him that asking for help matters more than dying alone

In 2008, three minor shareholders—one had just borrowed from Li to buy a house—cornered him in a teahouse, demanding he and co-founder Fanzhen resign, keeping only 15% equity. They blamed cash-flow decline after hiring Qin Zhi and blamed failed fundraising. An angel investor backed Li and stopped them. Weeks later, the ringleader revealed the real reason: Li had been grinding alone, never consulting the team, making everyone feel disposable. This taught Li two lessons: tell core management immediately when trouble hits, and stop punishing himself to isolation.

— Li Xiang
