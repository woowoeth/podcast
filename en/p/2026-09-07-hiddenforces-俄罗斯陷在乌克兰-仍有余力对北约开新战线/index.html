Russia Is Stuck in Ukraine, but Still Has Room to Open a New Front Against NATO

Russia has not won in Ukraine, but its war of attrition has forged the world's most advanced drone-infantry combat experience; the guest argues it has ample capacity to mount a limited probe against NATO, and the window is in the final phase of Trump's term.

The guest is the Wall Street Journal's chief diplomatic correspondent, and was in Kyiv on the day of the 2022 invasion; his judgments on Russian military capability and Europe's fractures are far more concrete than those of TV commentators.

19:16

Trump's anti-Europe talk may be taken literally by Putin

Everyone is speculating about whether John Ratcliffe's secret trip to Russia was there to negotiate terms. The guest's judgment is that the visit looks more like carrying a message to Moscow on Trump's behalf: don't take those on-air remarks about ‘America not caring about Europe’ seriously — if Russia invades Lithuania, Estonia or Finland, the US will send troops. The reasoning is that Moscow is bogged down in a war of attrition in Ukraine and badly needs to cut off European support for Ukraine, so escalating with a probe against NATO is one option, while Trump's earlier statements that ‘Europe is a bunch of freeloaders’ gave Putin room to misread, hence the need for a dedicated trip to make the red line clear. One caveat: the guest does not have intelligence from inside that room; he is explaining why the account ‘makes complete sense’, not confirming it.

21:27

Russia's army isn't exhausted — it can still open a limited front

Russia has been unable to take Ukraine for a long time — does that mean it cannot open another front? The guest's answer is blunt: it can absolutely open a ‘limited front’ — not tanks rolling across Poland to Paris, but something in the Baltic direction, such as blockading the narrow land corridor of the Suwalki Gap. He says the Russian and Ukrainian armies are the only two in the world today that fight modern drone-infantry warfare as a daily routine. The story of a 20-man Ukrainian drone team destroying an entire traditional battalion is widely told, but the Russian military has the same technology in hand: 20 Russians can likewise wipe out a NATO battalion that has had no anti-drone training. Outsiders assume Russia grows weaker the longer it fights, but overlook that it is also one of the parties being tempered by this technological revolution.

22:31

NATO might not even rescue Lithuania from Poland

A war game designed to simulate a Russian invasion of Lithuania led the guest to a deeply unsettling conclusion: even Poland did not send troops to save Lithuania, let alone other allies. The reason is not insufficient military power, but that Russian forces advance too fast and nuclear deterrence hangs over everything, so allies fall into hesitation over ‘whether to enter’, producing a chain of waiting and watching. The guest says Moscow has factored exactly this into its options. He also cautions that this is not quite the same as before 2022 — back then, a hundred thousand-plus Russian troops massing could be clearly seen on the German and Belarusian borders, and there is no such material preparation now. So the most immediate escalation may not be a conventional invasion, but small, ambiguous attacks that can be denied (hitting a warehouse or a drone first).

23:40

Russia is already probing NATO's limits with drones

At this stage, with no sign of a massed army on the border, Russia still holds a set of low-cost, low-attribution options: a limited strike on a NATO warehouse near Poland or Romania, or a provocation using a captured Ukrainian drone that has been modified, then watching whether NATO splits and whether the US steps in. The guest notes this kind of probing is already underway — an armed drone attacked a Ukrainian aircraft parked at Leipzig airport in Germany, and only a drone malfunction prevented casualties; Russian risk-taking is rising steadily, and the West has so far given no serious response. The senior European officers he has spoken with all believe the most dangerous window is the next one to two years, roughly falling in the final phase of Trump's term — the Kremlin will want to act before the US might let go.

— Yaroslav Trofimov
27:53

Putin wants Ukraine disarmed, not a ceasefire

Would Putin accept a decent ceasefire deal? The guest's view is that you only have to look at what is put on the table: Russia has consistently demanded not just territory, but a cap on the size of Ukraine's army, limits on the types of weapons it may hold, and a cutoff of Western military aid to Ukraine. In his words, the goal is to make Ukraine ‘lose the ability to defend itself’; not neutral, but ‘castrated’, so that it cannot rise again in the next phase of the war — a year, five years or ten years later. Putin signed Minsk-1 and Minsk-2, and violated both. So the guest quotes the summary of Ukraine's former foreign minister: this war can only end one of two ways — the end of Ukraine, or the end of the Russian imperial project. Waiting for the other side to change its mind is not on the menu.

— Yaroslav Trofimov
31:08

Street protests can decide who stays in the wartime cabinet

Kyiv's recent personnel earthquake looks on the surface like an anti-corruption storm, but the key mechanism lies elsewhere: elections are frozen in wartime, and the president's legitimacy rests on just one ballast stone — ‘street support’. Zelensky initially fired Defense Minister Fedorov, who had pushed drone innovation, because he needed the backing of Commander-in-Chief Syrsky; after Fedorov was dismissed, Kyiv's elites took to the streets, and the protests, small as they were, were enough to make the president reverse course — he removed Syrsky, brought in a younger general more popular with the front line, and appointed a defense minister with a veteran intelligence background acceptable to all sides. Ukraine's independent anti-corruption bodies can still pursue people in the president's innermost circle, and when Zelensky tried to amend the law to absorb that body, he backed down within a week under street pressure. When elections are absent, the square equals the ballot box.

— Yaroslav Trofimov
38:37

Resistance comes from memory, not heroism

Summing up Ukrainian perseverance as patriotic heroism misses what actually drives them. The guest says that after Russian troops take a Ukrainian town, one of the first things they often do is send bulldozers to tear down monuments to the victims of the 1930s famine — what they want to delete is a memory. Russian state television talks about how to ‘re-educate’ Ukrainians, making them forget their language and culture and become Russians. Around World War II, the Soviet Union carried out a systematic purge of Ukraine's cultural elite, and in the guest's childhood he had no idea those writers existed, because collective memory had been erased. Ukrainians today are almost all descendants of survivors of that meat grinder of history, carrying the trauma in their bodies; it is precisely the fear of ‘never again’ that makes them prefer to die on the battlefield rather than return to that life.

— Yaroslav Trofimov
45:59

Half a million veterans blocked the first wave of collapse

On the night of February 23, 2022, most people in Kyiv still did not believe there would be a war; the guest remembers knowing from his experience in Kabul and Baghdad that ‘impossible’ can flip overnight, but ordinary Ukrainians had no such reference point. What really kept the country from collapsing in the first days was eight years of war in the Donbas: that fighting was far from Kyiv, but it produced roughly 500,000 Ukrainians who had traded blows with the Russian army, and volunteer organizations that evolved into a flexible, aggressive force. The psychological threshold had already been crossed in the Donbas — ‘we know what they look like, we know how to fight them’. Because of that experience, the swift collapse Putin expected did not happen, and the West's widespread expectations were wrong too.

Glossary

Article 5
The core collective-defense clause of the NATO charter: an attack on any member is treated as an attack on all members.
Suwalki Gap
The narrow land strip between Poland and Lithuania, the only land choke point for NATO to reinforce the Baltic states.
Minsk-1 / Minsk-2
Ceasefire agreements reached in 2014 and 2015 to end the fighting in the Donbas, both of which Russia failed to honor.
Operation Spiderweb
A Ukrainian intelligence operation against strategic targets inside Russia, which destroyed Russian strategic bombers as far away as Siberia.
