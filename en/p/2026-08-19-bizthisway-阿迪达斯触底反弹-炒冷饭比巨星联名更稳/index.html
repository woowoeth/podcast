Adidas Bounces Off the Bottom: Reheating Old Shoes Beats a Superstar Collab

After nearly halving from its 2021 peak, Adidas staged a recovery by thawing out retro sneakers, repairing relations with retailers and tightly controlling inventory — but the stock bounced and then fell back, revealing that the sports-brand cycle is really a game of inventory versus expectations.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:53

The stock bottomed and rebounded earlier than the reputation did

Adidas's reputational turnaround this year came from a string of good news: New Chinese Style clothing went global during Spring Festival, the top two men's elite finishers at the London Marathon broke 2 hours and all three podium finishers wore Adidas running shoes, and the team that won the World Cup final — Spain versus Argentina — ended up wearing Adidas kits. What came back earlier than the buzz was the stock price: it peaked above 300 euros in July 2021, slid all the way to near 100 euros, recovered to above 255 euros, and has now fallen back to 160 euros — nearly halved from the high, but up 60% from the low. This history provides the coordinates for the five-year review that follows.

4:10

The fall began with goods stuck in transit, not with nobody buying

Adidas outsources production, and it takes 8 to 9 months from design to shelf. In 2021 global shipping had not recovered and the Vietnam base was frequently shut down temporarily, pushing the share of in-transit inventory from close to 29% in 2019 and 28% in 2020 to 38.8% in 2022. Months of saleable inventory fell to just 2.21 months in the second half of 2021, showing goods were stuck in transit and stores were severely anxious about stockouts. At the same time Greater China stalled for its own reasons: it had still grown 37% in the first half of 2021 and accounted for 23% of global revenue, then declined for two consecutive years, with its share dropping to 12% to 15%. A logistics choke point stacked on top of a slide in a core market — that was the starting point of the fall.

9:19

The Yeezy inventory wasn't destroyed — it was sold off in three waves

Adidas and Kanye West began working together in 2006 and launched Yeezy in 2015; in 2020 and 2021 it contributed $1.7 billion in sales a year, about 8% of company revenue, and possibly around 40% of profit. In October 2022, after Kanye's offensive remarks, the partnership was terminated, and upstream production could not react quickly, so Adidas took a write-down provision against 1.2 billion euros of finished and in-transit inventory. It did not destroy the stock all at once; instead it re-released part of the inventory in three waves in 2023 and early 2024, donated the remainder, and even recorded a reversal of the write-down loss in its financials. The handling was later judged to have worked well.

12:19

Exiting Russia hurt stock sentiment, not the business

In 2022 Adidas also dealt with the Russian market: it suspended operations in March and exited completely in October. Russia had historical ties to Adidas — it had done business in the Soviet Union during the Cold War and sponsored the Soviet team at the Moscow Olympics; in 2012 the then-CEO called Russia one of its fastest-growing markets. But by 2022 the business had shrunk, and the eventual shutdown brought a loss of about $300 million. The impact on the business was limited, but it was another layer of bad news for stock sentiment. In 2022 Adidas faced pressure on three fronts at once — Greater China, Yeezy and Russia — making it arguably its hardest year.

14:29

The retro line decided on day five of the job beat Yeezy on economics

In 2023 new CEO Bjørn Gulden came over from Puma and handled the thorny problems diplomatically: rather than destroying the Yeezy inventory he sold it off in batches, halted the big direct-to-consumer push and instead helped retailers improve the quality of their inventory. At the same time he made three retro lines including the Samba a company strategy, deciding it on his fifth day on the job. Through celebrity street-style photos and TikTok, the retro styles were bought up by a new generation of young people. Compared with Yeezy, reheating retro styles has a higher return on investment and lower risk, and it also offset part of the lost Yeezy revenue. In 2023 company revenue still declined, but gross margin and free cash flow recovered, and Greater China's gross margin approached 70% in the second half — the signs of a bottom bounce were already clear.

18:41

The one thing to do in an Olympic year is not stockpile

2024 was a Paris Olympics year, and historically Olympic years tend to become big inventory years, but Adidas did not take the opportunity to build inventory, keeping months of saleable inventory at about two and a half months. Footwear and apparel sales were fine-tuned at the margin, with footwear's share slipping slightly in the second half. Gross margin returned to 51% in the first half of 2024 and free cash flow improved. R&D spending reached 170 million euros, the highest in the past five years, supporting technology investments such as racing shoes. The sub-2-hour shoe weighs no more than 100 grams per shoe, with a thick but flexible midsole, sacrificing durability for performance — more like a technology advertisement. Adidas is more conservative than Nike in R&D, but it knows technology is the real selling point.

24:02

What held the stock back wasn't Adidas itself but the second and third tiers

In early 2025 the stock rebounded to 255 euros, but then fell back to 160 euros without forming a reversal. The reasons: months of saleable inventory returned to 2.6 to 2.8 months, and revenue growth and gross margin slipped slightly; retro shoes, as fashion items, need something new after one buying cycle; and more importantly, the competitive landscape of sporting goods changed, with second- and third-tier brands rising and eating into Nike's and Adidas's market share — possibly the main reason the stock did not return to its high. North America and Greater China both returned to double-digit revenue growth in the first half of 2026, and Greater China's profit recovery was striking — the business was actually doing well, but the market was waiting for something new.

26:13

Sports brands have a pig cycle too, but marketing can rewrite it

This review brings to mind the pig cycle: the fundamentals of sports brands are jointly determined by major events such as the World Cup and the Olympics, and the core is inventory levels and the ability to adjust them. But sneakers and apparel differ from a staple like pork — brand and marketing strength can soften the cycle effect. Looking at the stocks, both Nike's and Adidas's share-price peaks came in 2021, not because business was astonishingly good but because excess global liquidity pushed valuations up. Adidas's stock recovery ran more than half a year ahead of its fundamental repair, showing the market corrects mistakes in advance. It is also a reminder not to chase gains and dump on declines: judging by stock moves alone means missing the chance to watch how a company actually recovers.

Glossary

Samba
A classic Adidas retro shoe with a T-shaped leather toe, which became popular again with the retro sportswear trend.
Months of saleable inventory
Inventory divided by average monthly sales over the past six months, measuring how many months of sales the stock can support.
Write-down provision
A loss provision set aside in advance for inventory that may become unsellable or lose value, charged to expenses in the current period.
Breaking 2
Running a marathon in under 2 hours, once an extreme challenge, achieved by multiple runners for the first time at the 2025 London Marathon.
