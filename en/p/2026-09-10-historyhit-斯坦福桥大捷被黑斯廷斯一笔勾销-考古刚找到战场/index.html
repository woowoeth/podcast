Dan Snow's History Hit 2026-09-10

Stamford Bridge Was Wiped Out by Hastings, and Archaeology Just Found the Battlefield

In September 1066, King Harold of England force-marched roughly 200 miles in four days and annihilated Harald Hardrada's Viking army; but a victory that could have cemented any monarch's reputation was erased days later by the catastrophe at Hastings.

3:04

More than one king of England died in 1066

Edward the Confessor died in the first days of January with no heir, and the throne instantly became the most tempting vacancy in all of Europe. The show stresses that from the late 900s to the 1300s, almost every English king who took power faced a claimant: brother fought brother, cousins fought each other, and outsiders always circled the empty throne. Edward had a distant English blood relative, but he was only a boy of probably no more than 13, not yet old enough to stand in a shield wall and cut down enemies — and that was precisely a king's first duty at the time. A child on the throne, in an England that had been through a century of war and invasion, meant inviting more war.

— Dan Snow
6:04

Hardrada was no pirate, he was a Byzantine veteran

Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, was called "the Thunderbolt of the North" and was said to stand seven feet tall. Exiled from his homeland as a youth, he made his name and fortune along the rivers of Russia and Ukraine, fighting for the Byzantine Empire: he campaigned in the Crimea, is said to have flattened 80 Arab fortresses in the Middle East, fought on the Tigris and Euphrates, helped Christians conquer Sicily, served in southern Italy, and rose to command Byzantium's Varangian Guard. He crushed a Bulgarian revolt and earned the nickname "Bulgar-burner." When he returned to Scandinavia, his ships were so full of gold in the hold that they lost their balance. By the mid-to-late 1040s he was king of Norway, and he never stopped wanting to rebuild Canute the Great's North Sea empire spanning Norway, Denmark and England.

— Dan Snow
9:07

His own brother became the invader's guide

Harold's brother Tostig was still earl of Northumbria in 1065 when a popular uprising drove him out for being too unpopular. He went south expecting his brother the king to restore him, but Harold judged that he had done a bad job, backed another candidate, Morcar, and expected Morcar to support his own bid for the throne later. Tostig could not believe the deal — his own brother had sided with the enemy. In exile he raided down the east coast, was soundly beaten on the Humber by Edwin and Morcar, fled to Scotland, found little support there, and so took his fleet to Norway. Hardrada already wanted to invade England, and now he had a "useful idiot": a man who had been close to the king and had ruled northern England, able to supply all kinds of intelligence.

— Dan Snow
13:10

A city that refused to pay was erased from the map

Hardrada's fleet reached the mouth of the Tyne in the first week of September, possibly three or four hundred ships. They sailed south along the coast, past Holy Island — where the Viking Age itself had begun with that raid on Lindisfarne nearly 300 years earlier. At Scarborough he landed and demanded all its wealth; the inhabitants said no. Hardrada needed to make an example: he had great piles of firewood bound and set alight and rolled down the slope into the town, the fleeing inhabitants were cut down, and he ordered the entire population killed. Scarborough ceased to exist — it does not appear in the Domesday Book compiled about a generation later, and that book records every last pig. One historian's verdict on Hardrada is called by the show "the most understated line I've ever read": his personal morality seems not to have matched Christian ideals.

— Dan Snow
17:13

Fulford: one exposed flank lost the north

At Fulford outside York, the first great battle of 1066. The English position was good: the Ouse on their right, marshland on their left, and they held the only firm ground leading to York. But Hardrada was not on a battlefield for the first time: he led his troops along the riverbank out of the English line of sight, possibly wading, then had Tostig show himself openly in the centre of the line. He bet that Morcar would want to crush Tostig's band of "English traitors" first — and he bet right. Morcar charged out, the right flank was exposed, and Hardrada came at them from behind the riverbank. The result was one-sided: the English dead were said to be so numerous that you could walk across the marsh on them without getting your feet wet. York surrendered, and the city's leaders swore to recognize him as king and Tostig as the new earl.

— Dan Snow
20:25

Four days, about 200 miles, Harold marches the other way

Harold had been waiting on the south coast for an invasion from Normandy, raising a large force to stand by, but no masts ever appeared on the horizon. August passed, the weather turned in September, the invasion window on the English Channel closed, supplies ran out, and he was forced to disband the army. A day or two later, back in London, he got the thunderbolt: the invasion had come in the north, not the south, and not from William. Harold took his professional troops — the full-time house carls — and raced north along the Roman road (today's A1), while sending messengers to tell the Fulford survivors to rendezvous south of York. The show estimates he covered about 185 to 200 miles in four days, calling it one of the most astonishing and audacious marches in English military history, and true to his style: fast, a reader of battlefields, and judging that the best way to deal with a Viking invasion was to strangle it early.

— Dan Snow
21:29

The Vikings left their armour and shields in camp

Hardrada had just won an overwhelming victory and was overconfident. He and part of his force walked from the ship camp toward Stamford Bridge, many without armour, with shields and heavy kit left behind — on a hot day's march, these things are a burden. He took only about a third of his army, leaving the rest at Riccall to guard the ships. They strolled to Stamford Bridge and lounged in the fields on both sides of the river in the sun. The weather was clear, and Hardrada was the first to see a cloud of dust rise on the western horizon — in dry weather an army on a metalled road cannot hide. Then the sagas say he saw a glint, "like sunlight on broken ice": that was not ice, it was iron, the morning light on the advancing English royal army, with the dragon banner of the English king and Harold's own warrior banner flying above it.

— Dan Snow
23:30

The story of one man holding the bridge may be true

The English rolled over the Vikings on the west bank, then had to cross the river. The most famous story comes from this moment: a giant berserk Viking held the narrow bridge alone, fending off the entire English advance with an axe, said to have beaten back dozens, possibly 40 Englishmen, until a single soldier drifted under the bridge in a barrel or container and thrust up through the planks into his unprotected groin, running him through from crotch to chest. The show says the story was written down much later, but "it's a good story, and it may well be true" — because in reality there were other crossing points, fords as well as a bridge, and it is impossible that everyone used that one. The show's host waded one of those fords himself last summer; the water was deeper than he expected, but he could still stand.

— Dan Snow
24:30

A parley between brothers: six feet of English soil

Some sources say the fighting paused and Harold rode forward alone to speak with his brother Tostig, offering to restore his earldom if he changed sides. Tostig called back asking what Hardrada would get, and Harold answered: six feet of English soil for a grave — or more, since he was taller. Tostig replied: then prepare for battle, we would rather die with honour, or win England by victory. He turned his back on his brother and stood with Hardrada one last time, the two sharing death or victory.

— Dan Snow
26:38

Hardrada threw away his shield and charged in two-handed

Hardrada knew how to fight, how to win, and how to die to preserve his name. Instead of hiding behind the shield wall all day waiting for rescue, he made a decision: to die in a way that people would still talk about a thousand years later. He dropped his shield, took his sword in both hands, and launched a mad personal charge at the enemy, ploughing a furrow through the English ranks. The show uses this to correct the Hollywood image of war: real fighting was more like a rugby scrum — two rows of men pressed so close they could barely raise a hand, shoving, spitting, cursing, occasionally stabbing, until one side broke. But this time, possibly the greatest warrior in Europe really did decide to go for broke. He hacked at the English until the inevitable happened: an English archer, possibly at very close range, loosed an arrow that flew straight into the throat of the "hard ruler," the "Thunderbolt of the North," the "Bulgar-burner."

— Dan Snow
28:43

Hundreds of ships, only 24 sailed home

Reinforcements trickled in through the afternoon, but as a trickle, out of formation and exhausted, some said to have died of overexertion. They were led by Eystein Orre, a nobleman and Hardrada's close man — he was in fact betrothed to the king's daughter, but never made it to the altar. He held the English at bay for a time and rallied the Vikings, known to history as "Orre's Storm," but it was not enough. The English eventually pushed them south toward the ships, the army shattered into a rout, and the dead piled up; years later, heaps of bones were said to still be visible, whitening in the sun. Hardrada and Tostig were among them, Tostig the first of Harold's family to die that autumn, but not the last. Harold called off the fighting and allowed the few survivors to sail home after swearing never to attack England again. Hardrada's 16-year-old son Olaf had stayed with the ships and took the survivors home: only 24 ships left Riccall, out of the hundreds that had sailed in a week or two earlier.

— Dan Snow
30:46

The real battlefield is at Low Catton, not in the town

The battlefield's location had never been confirmed by archaeology. Today there is a modern town called Stamford Bridge, with a bridge over the Derwent, street names all tied to the battle, images of the conflict all over town, and anyone you stop in the street can tell you about the fight — but archaeologists have never found any artifacts or remains there. Recently, though, finds have started coming out of the ground at Low Catton, about a mile south of Stamford Bridge, and in quantity. The show's host watched metal detectorist Simon Richardson on site dig up a Viking belt buckle, its loop shaped into a mythical beast's head with recognizable eyes, ears and snout, 11th century and definitely Scandinavian. Minutes later, in the same field, came a Viking sword pommel — the fitting at the end of the hilt that protects the hand, stops it slipping and acts as a counterweight. Then fragments of chainmail.

— Dan Snow
31:46

A scabbard fitting points to Vikings from the Balkans

The host's favourite find is a very fine chape — the decorative metal fitting at the tip of a scabbard that stops it scraping the ground or a horse's flank. They contacted Scandinavian experts, who said it closely resembles chapes found at Viking sites in eastern Europe, the Balkans, and possibly Bulgaria. So the evidence in these fields along the Derwent in Yorkshire is not just Vikings, not just a battle, but Vikings from the east, or with eastern objects — exactly matching the crowd around Harald Hardrada.

Glossary

berserk
A warrior in Norse legend who worked himself into a battle frenzy before fighting; the show uses it for the giant Viking who held the bridge alone.
chape
The decorative metal fitting at the tip of a scabbard that stops the sheath scraping the ground or a horse's flank.
house carls
The professional standing warriors around an English king; the men Harold took on his forced march.
Varangian Guard
The Byzantine emperor's bodyguard, largely made up of Norsemen, which Hardrada rose to command.
Domesday Book
The English royal land survey compiled about a generation later, recording everything down to every pig.
