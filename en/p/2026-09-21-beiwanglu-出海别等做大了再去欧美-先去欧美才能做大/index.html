Going overseas: don't wait until you're big to go to Europe and America — go to Europe and America first to get big

Wei Zhe says many companies think they'll go to Europe and America once they're big, but it's exactly the opposite — you go to Europe and America to get big. Choose the wrong region and the later product 3.0 and channel pyramid won't stand up.

1:30

The old cross-border big-seller path is a dead end

Wei Zhe says the ‘cross-border big seller’ popular in Shenzhen is a distribution-type mindset, with dozens or hundreds of accounts; he has seen companies with over a thousand accounts and hundreds of thousands of SKUs. Ten years ago these companies made money faster than other paths from a profitability standpoint, and many investors liked them. But distribution-type companies are essentially traffic-type companies: the more stores, the more likely to get traffic. When platform goods aren't abundant enough, distribution isn't opposed; once goods are abundant enough, using distribution to grab traffic is bad money driving out good. More critically, three red lines: goods not matching descriptions harms consumer interests, fake orders harm other merchants' interests, and inserting small cards to build private domains harms platform interests. Amazon's flywheel places great importance on consumer experience, and these behaviors hit exactly that protective wall.

— Wei Zhe
4:38

Four choices, region comes first

Wei Zhe says it's not just cross-border e-commerce; all of life is about choices being more important than effort. He gives four choices: region choice, channel choice, product category choice, brand positioning choice. On region choice, he has always advocated seizing the European and American markets first, because of strong paying power, high internet penetration, and more importantly, brands can build momentum. This isn't their invention; when Japanese and Korean brands went global back then, they all seized Europe and America. On category choice, they change their recommendation every three years: the earliest three years supported products common to China and abroad, where domestic supply chains could sell overseas without modification, like Anker's power banks back then, almost identical except for voltage; the second three years recommended overseas-exclusive products, because Chinese supply chains all went overseas, and common products had no differentiation; the most recent three years is product 3.0 — electrified, with chips, with algorithms.

— Wei Zhe
12:29

It's not that you go to Europe and America after getting big; you get big by going to Europe and America

Many companies tell Wei Zhe ‘I'll go to Europe and America when I'm big’, and he says it's exactly the opposite — you go to Europe and America to get big. He gives the example of Cupshe, a swimwear company: a husband and wife, stocked $50,000 worth of goods, and their first stop was the US. $50,000 is sales goods, actually very little, so there's no minimum threshold. He recently looked at AI smart hardware, starting from Kickstarter crowdfunding platforms with a thousand or ten thousand products, even collecting pre-payments before placing orders, with no capital — this is also a good way to test the waters. He even advises big companies entering the European and American markets to learn from small startup companies. They always say the needle must be fine enough to pierce in; both big and small companies should first do well in four singles: single market, single category, single channel, single brand.

— Wei Zhe
20:36

After AI, good companies don't just find demand, they create it

Wei Zhe says with AI, excellent companies don't look for demand, they should lead demand and create demand. He gives the example of a Chinese company making astronomical telescopes: originally not electrified, they electrified it, with chips and algorithms; the algorithm is actually very simple, with GPS positioning, and the map of the universe's movement is regular. He wasn't an astronomy enthusiast but would buy one, click at home in Silicon Valley, it automatically aligns Orion for you, tells you 3 a.m. is the best effect, you press it and go to sleep, and the next day it takes the photo for you. This lets everyone become a good astronomy enthusiast. Another example is a cross-border class student making curtains; after electrification they didn't sell well in the US because American electricians are expensive, so he put solar panels on the windows, turning electric curtains that need electrician installation into DIY ones; originally always one or two hundred million, now four or five hundred million, with profit of one or two hundred million.

— Wei Zhe
24:48

In the AI era, still using subscription fees means you're behind

Wei Zhe says in the AI era, if you still have the subscription fee mindset, you're behind again; they all advise companies to do back-end charging, software charging, called tokenized. A monthly subscription fee is the dumbest way, a bit like advertising charging by time instead of by usage. This time AI has brought out the most ideal charging model in all human education: paying according to your usage of AI software, services, or content. He gives an example: Bessie, if you take two weeks off this month, isn't subscribing for a month wasting two weeks? They suggest AI-transformed hardware should be charged like AI, not going back to the old SaaS monthly $9.9 subscription model. An AI reading platform he looked at charges by how many articles you read, and doesn't charge if you don't read; the same with smart bike trainers, after recharging, one ride is one dollar, two hours is two dollars.

— Wei Zhe
27:28

Adding AI is more worth favoring than AI plus

Wei Zhe has a viewpoint called AI plus or plus AI; he reverses the phrase and favors plus AI rather than today favoring AI plus. The difference is: plus AI is hardware humans have already accepted, and you AI-ify it; rather than creating a previously unseen hardware because of AI. It's very hard for humans to accept a brand new hardware. For example, Oura makes rings because you first accepted rings; some make you hang a camera under your neck, which I find harder, I haven't accepted that hardware. So they are very optimistic about adding AI to already-accepted hardware. They invested in a capacitive stylus that is number one globally, because everyone already writes on a Pad, it doesn't add an extra pen for you, it just AI-ifies your pen. He also particularly wants to transform a top-two overseas fitness equipment company, replacing the fitness coach on the screen with AI-generated ones, you want a male, it's male, you want a black person, it's black, installing two cameras enables AI-guided fitness.

— Wei Zhe
29:43

Channels aren't flat; they're a pyramid

Wei Zhe says channels aren't multiple channels multiple paths, each channel parallel or even competing; excellent brands all have a channel pyramid. Like luxury brands and traditional brands, there must be different channel strategies, pricing strategies, and which channel new products launch in first all have讲究. What is a light goods channel, what is the main waist volume channel, the pyramid design differs for different categories and brands. He gives Anker: making power bank mecha seems a bit low, to enter the consumer electronics high ground, Soundcore earphones must enter Apple flagship stores; whether it really sells a lot isn't important, but it can transform the brand, Apple is the pyramid's top. Pop Mart must open on London Oxford Street, even if it doesn't make money, how much can it lose, but it must hold up there. He says some companies treat Temu and AliExpress as parallel channels for selling goods, which definitely won't work; AliExpress is a light goods channel, when Amazon delists you, dump it in that channel, you can't sell on Amazon and here too, consumers get confused.

— Wei Zhe
36:14

R&D is 1, marketing is 0

Wei Zhe says he really dislikes spending heavily on marketing. Many Chinese brands going overseas or cross-border e-commerce are very willing to add 2% of revenue to marketing expenses, but consumers don't get it. He says, do you dare to add 2% of revenue to R&D expenses? He has met many bosses with 1 billion or 2 billion scale; at 1 billion, saying 20 million for R&D, I don't know what for. He says see, there's the problem, you don't know what for. Your 2% thrown into marketing expenses makes the platform happy, consumers don't get it; but 2% thrown into R&D, consumers get it. He has always firmly believed good products speak for themselves, marketing is just, your R&D is 1, marketing is 0, if you have good stuff you can amplify it, if you don't have that 1, never put many 0s. He gives three judgment signals: first, dare to find a benchmark company, definitely a mid-to-upper brand, and you double and crush it in performance; second, you sell at the same high price as it; third, you sell online, and hit offline channels to come to you.

— Wei Zhe
41:02

AI doesn't just change products, it transforms every position

Wei Zhe says AI has two important things for enterprises, big, medium, and small all must do well: first, the combination of products with AI; second, how the organization changes through AI. He sees cross-border e-commerce companies as almost the industry among all he has seen that most should use AI to transform every position. Every position asks itself what AI can do for you: product selection, content generation, ad placement choices, essentially all algorithms; previously relying on human experience, experience is definitely not as powerful as algorithm iteration. Customer service AI-ified, no more customer service, customer service is just you training your customer service agent; ad placement you train an agent; product selection, product display, product image and video generation all AI-ified. He says this is the first and only industry he has seen so far where the entire company organizational chain can be transformed by AI. He also says they most oppose placing many people overseas, oppose it with or without AI, called no unnecessary overseas staffing.

— Wei Zhe
46:16

Not just let AI work, but let it participate in decisions

Wei Zhe explains AI and organization very simply; many companies have P series and M series, P is professional, M is Management. Many people are used to letting AI do P, doing the work, generating images, which was originally a P series job. But truly powerful companies don't just let AI do P series work, they gradually let it do Manager, Director, VP work. He gives a real case of AI pricing: the company originally wanted to sell at 35 euros, AI said you can sell at 55, the company really went to 55, and it really sold, volume similar to before at 35, but earning 20 euros more. He says AI has no courage, it's bolder than you, it has compared everything across the web for you. He also says even if AI has hallucinations, with so many large models in the world, China and the US, they won't dream the same dream at the same time, so you must cross-validate.

— Wei Zhe
