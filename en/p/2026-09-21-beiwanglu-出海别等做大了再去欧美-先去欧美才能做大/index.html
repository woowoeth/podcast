Going global: don't wait until you're big to go to Europe and America — go there first so you can get big

Wei Zhe says many companies think they'll go to Europe and America once they're big, but it's exactly backwards — you go there in order to get big. Pick the wrong region and nothing that follows — product 3.0, the channel pyramid — can be built.

1:30

The old cross-border mega-seller playbook no longer works

Wei Zhe says the ‘cross-border mega-seller’ model popular in Shenzhen is a distribution-driven mindset, running dozens or hundreds of accounts; he has seen companies with over a thousand accounts and hundreds of thousands of SKUs. Ten years ago these companies made money faster than other paths, and plenty of investors loved them. But a distribution-driven company is essentially a traffic-driven company: the more stores, the more likely you are to get traffic. When a platform's goods aren't rich enough, he doesn't oppose distribution; once goods are rich enough, using distribution to grab traffic is bad money driving out good. More critically, three red lines: goods not matching the listing harms consumers, brushing orders harms other merchants, and slipping in little cards to build private traffic harms the platform. Amazon's flywheel cares enormously about consumer experience, and these behaviours run straight into that protective wall.

— Wei Zhe
4:38

Of the four choices, region comes first

Wei Zhe says this applies not just to cross-border e-commerce — all of life is choices mattering more than effort. He gives four choices: region, channel, product category, brand positioning. On region he has always argued for seizing the European and American markets first, because paying power is strong, internet penetration is high, and more importantly a brand can build momentum there. This isn't something they invented — when Japanese and Korean brands went global back then, they all seized Europe and America. On category they change their advice every three years: the earliest three years they supported products common to China and abroad, where the domestic supply chain could sell overseas without modification — Anker's power banks back then were almost identical apart from voltage; the second three years they advised overseas-exclusive products, because China's supply chain had all gone overseas and China-abroad commonality offered no differentiation; the most recent three years it's product 3.0 — powered, chipped, algorithmic.

— Wei Zhe
12:29

It's not that you go to Europe and America once you're big — you go there to get big

Many companies tell Wei Zhe ‘I'll go to Europe and America when I'm big’; he says it's exactly backwards — you go to Europe and America to get big. He cites the swimwear company Cupshe: a husband and wife, with $50,000 of inventory, and their first stop was the US. $50,000 is goods for sale, actually very little, so there's no minimum threshold. He recently looked at AI smart hardware, starting from a thousand or ten thousand products on the Kickstarter crowdfunding platform, even taking prepayment before placing orders, with no capital — that too is a great way to test the water. He even advises big companies entering the European and American markets to learn from small startups. They always say the needle must be fine enough to pierce through; big and small companies alike should first nail the four singles: single market, single category, single channel, single brand.

— Wei Zhe
20:36

After AI, good companies don't just find demand — they create it

Wei Zhe says that with AI, excellent companies shouldn't go looking for demand; they should lead demand and create demand. He cites a Chinese company making astronomical telescopes: originally unpowered, they electrified it, with a chip and an algorithm — the algorithm is actually very simple, with GPS positioning, since the movements of the sky follow rules. He wasn't an astronomy enthusiast before but would buy one: at home in Silicon Valley you tap once, it automatically aligns Orion for you, tells you three in the morning is the best time, you set it and go to sleep, and the next day it has taken the photos for you. This lets anyone become a good astronomy enthusiast. Another example: a cross-border class student making curtains; electrified, they didn't sell well in the US because American electricians are expensive, so he put solar panels on the window, turning electric curtains that need an electrician into something you can DIY. Revenue went from one or two hundred million to four or five hundred million, and profit is now one or two hundred million.

— Wei Zhe
24:48

In the AI era, still charging subscriptions means you're behind

Wei Zhe says that in the AI era, still thinking in terms of subscription fees means you're behind again; they're all urging companies toward back-end charging, software charging — what's called tokenized. A monthly subscription fee is the dumbest approach, a bit like advertising that used to charge by time rather than by usage. This time AI has brought out the most ideal charging model in all of human education: paying according to your usage of AI software, services or content. He gives an example: Bessie, if you take two weeks of vacation this month, aren't you wasting two weeks of a monthly subscription? They advise that AI-transformed hardware should be charged like AI, not go back to the old SaaS model of $9.9 a month. An AI reading platform he looked at charges by how many articles you read, and charges nothing if you don't read; the same with smart bike trainers — after topping up, one dollar per ride, two dollars for two hours.

— Wei Zhe
27:28

Adding AI is more worth backing than AI adding

Wei Zhe has a view he calls AI-plus versus plus-AI; he flips the phrase around and says he's more bullish on plus-AI than on today's AI-plus. The difference: plus-AI is hardware humans have already accepted, which you make AI-powered; rather than creating a hardware nobody has seen before because of AI. It's very hard for humans to accept an entirely new piece of hardware. Take Oura making a ring, because you'd already accepted rings; something that hangs a camera under your neck is harder for me — I've never accepted that hardware. So they're very bullish on adding AI to hardware that's already accepted. One capacitive stylus they invested in is number one globally, because everyone already writes on a Pad; it doesn't add an extra pen for you, it just makes that pen AI-powered. He also particularly wants to transform one of the top one or two companies in overseas fitness equipment, replacing the fitness coach on the screen with AI generation — you want a man, it's a man; you want a Black person, it's a Black person; install two cameras and it can guide your workout with AI.

— Wei Zhe
29:43

Channels aren't laid out flat — they're a pyramid

Wei Zhe says channels aren't multiple channels and multiple roads, each parallel or even competing with the others; excellent brands all have a channel pyramid. Like luxury brands and traditional brands, there must be different channel strategies, pricing strategies, and rules about which channel a new product launches in first. What counts as a light-goods channel, what counts as the main waist channel for volume — the pyramid design differs by category and by brand. He cites Anker: making power bank mecha seems a bit low, so to enter the consumer-electronics high ground of Soundcore headphones you have to get into Apple flagship stores; whether it really sells a lot doesn't matter, but it can transform the brand — Apple is the pinnacle of the pyramid. Pop Mart must open on London's Oxford Street; even if it doesn't make money, how much can it lose — but it must hold that position. He says some companies treat Temu and AliExpress as parallel channels for selling goods, which definitely won't work; AliExpress is a light-goods channel, somewhere to dump stock when Amazon delists you — you can't sell on Amazon and here too, and consumers get confused.

— Wei Zhe
36:14

R&D is the 1, marketing is the 0

Wei Zhe says he really dislikes splashing out on marketing expenses. Many Chinese brands going overseas or cross-border e-commerce companies are very willing to add 2% of revenue as marketing expense, but consumers don't get it. He says: do you dare add 2% of revenue as R&D expense? He's met many bosses at the one or two billion scale; at one billion they say 20 million for R&D, and I don't know what for. He says, see, there's the problem — you don't know what for. Your 2% thrown at marketing expense makes the platform happy, and consumers don't get it; but 2% thrown into R&D, consumers get it. He has always firmly believed a good product speaks for itself, and marketing is just — your R&D is the 1, marketing is the 0; if your thing is good you can amplify it, but if you don't have that 1, absolutely don't put on a lot of 0s. He gives three signals for judging: first, dare to find a benchmark company, definitely a mid-to-upper brand, and crush it by doubling its performance; second, you sell at the same price as it; third, you sell online, and it pushes offline channels to come find you.

— Wei Zhe
41:02

AI doesn't just change products — it remakes every role

Wei Zhe says two things about AI matter for companies, and big, medium and small companies all need to do them well: first, combining products with AI; second, how the organisation changes through AI. He sees cross-border e-commerce companies as, of all the industries he's seen, the one that most should use AI to remake every role. Every role should ask what AI can do for you: product selection, content generation, ad placement choices — all essentially algorithms; before, it relied on human experience, and experience is definitely not as strong as algorithmic iteration. Customer service AI-powered — there's no customer service anymore, customer service is just you training your customer-service agent; for ad placement you train an agent; product selection, product display, product image and video generation all AI-powered. He says this is the first and only industry he's seen so far where the entire company's organisational chain can all be remade by AI. He also says what they oppose most is putting lots of people overseas — with or without AI they oppose it; it's called: don't station people overseas unless necessary.

— Wei Zhe
46:16

Don't just let AI do the work — let it into the decisions

Wei Zhe puts AI and organisation very simply: many companies have a P series and an M series — P is professional, M is management. Many people are used to letting AI do P work, doing the job; generating images was originally P-series work. But truly formidable companies don't just let AI do P-series work; you gradually let it do the work a Manager, Director or VP should do. He gives a real AI pricing case: the company originally wanted to sell at 35 euros, AI said you can sell at 55, the company really went to 55 and it really sold, volume roughly the same as at 35, but 20 euros more profit. He says AI has no courage — it's bolder than you, it has compared everything across the whole web for you. He also says even if AI hallucinates, with so many large models in China and the US, they won't all dream the same dream at the same time, so you must cross-validate.

— Wei Zhe

Price-performance ratio versus price-power ratio: price-performance means my performance and quality are the same, you've matched me, so I'll compete you down on price.
