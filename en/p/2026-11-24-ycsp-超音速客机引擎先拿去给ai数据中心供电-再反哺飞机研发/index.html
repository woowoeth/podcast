Engines for supersonic jets power AI data centers first; profits fund aircraft development

Boom Supersonic originally built engines for supersonic aircraft, but discovered that removing the fan and adding a generator transforms the design into the high-power turbine data centers urgently need—and power sales fund aircraft development.

1:35

Rolls-Royce's last-minute split forced Boom to build engines themselves—the right move

Boom initially planned to find Rolls-Royce and modify an existing engine, saving development costs. But in September 2022, Rolls-Royce announced the split to media without notice. That single day, Blake's funding conversations, customer meetings, and supplier calls all stopped. He called it "a startup's stage-four cancer diagnosis." Forced to respond, Boom committed to building the engine from zero—a decision born of desperation that proved to be the company's salvation.

— Blake Scholl
3:31

One message to Sam Altman revealed the entire market Boom should pursue

After XB1 broke the sound barrier, Blake saw on Twitter that power grids required five to ten years for connection and gas turbines were already sold out. He texted early investor Sam Altman asking if a 40-megawatt natural gas turbine would be useful for AI. Altman replied: "Yes, we need as much as you can make." This validation showed Boom that its Overture engine could simply drop the front fan and add a generator—transforming it from producing thrust into producing electricity.

— Blake Scholl
4:52

Electricity sales cover all aircraft-development costs without additional venture funding

Boom's original plan required tens of billions in venture capital to build Overture. But the power-generation turbine business is highly profitable on its own. With production of just two turbines per month, positive cash flow arrives the day the first unit ships. Turbine revenue covers all the funding needed for aircraft development.

— Blake Scholl
5:10

Twenty-four-hour turbine operation generated the reliability data Boom needed

Standard jet engine testing runs 5,000 hours to catch one-in-10,000 defects, but cannot reveal one-in-100,000 or one-in-a-million failures. Turbines running around the clock in data centers for years accumulate millions of hours of real-world operation. Boom can now tell the FAA and customers: this is "the most thoroughly tested new jet engine in history."

— Blake Scholl
6:02

The US faces a $300 billion power gap in the next decade

To support AI data center expansion, the US must add roughly 100 gigawatts of power generation capacity in the next decade—a market worth around $300 billion in advanced turbines. Boom's original plan was one gigawatt per year. But a single potential customer wants 1.6 gigawatts deployed every 30 days. Demand far exceeds available supply.

— Blake Scholl
6:34

Legacy power firms chose price hikes over innovation because margins rose faster

Trillion-dollar legacy industrial firms stopped innovating decades ago because customers are desperate for power. They raise prices three-fold, profits explode, stock prices climb, stock options vest. Nobody wants to risk capital spending that Wall Street might punish. Blake notes that per-capita electricity growth stalled in 1973 and went negative after 2000—the same year the US banned supersonic flight.

— Blake Scholl
7:43

Buying the printer proved cheaper than outsourcing blade manufacturing

A shop quoted $1.3 million and 18 months to 3D-print turbine blades. Blake asked how much the printer itself cost. The answer: $1.1 million, delivered in two weeks. Boom bought the printer. Now it prints its own blades—faster and cheaper than outsourcing.

— Blake Scholl
11:49

Software engineers embedded in hardware teams cut iteration to three months

Boom discovered a decade ago that most hardware engineering is fundamentally software engineering. It assigns one software engineer to each hardware team for automation. Now even front-desk staff count as programmers in the AI sense. The in-house tool Blade Runner lets one engineer drag sliders to watch blade aerodynamics and structure changes in real time—work that once took 106 person-months now happens instantly. Full-system iteration now takes as little as three months, typically six.

— Blake Scholl
