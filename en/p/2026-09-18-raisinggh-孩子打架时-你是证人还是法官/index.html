When Your Kids Fight, Are You a Witness or a Judge

The mistake parents most often make when stepping into sibling conflict is refereeing with their own childhood fairness wounds. What kids need isn't a verdict on who was right, but practice repairing a relationship inside low-stakes conflict.

Deal with your own sibling baggage before you step in

The episode opens by pointing out that almost everyone carries sibling baggage — even people without siblings carry either a longing for that ‘mythic connection’ or relief at having escaped it. The fairness trap is the thing most likely to trigger a parent's emotions: a cake sliced unevenly, a rule that was looser for one kid, a new dress given to only one of them. Those memories get activated the moment you see your own kids fighting. If you step in before dealing with that baggage, you are effectively imposing your sibling history on your children. So the first step is always clearing your own head, not rushing in to hand down a verdict.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
4:07

You are a witness, not a judge

This is the single most important line in the whole piece, and Dr. Pressman repeats it a dozen-plus times. The moment you start deciding who was right, who hit first, who was worse, that judgment comes from your own history, not from what actually happened between these two children in front of you. As a witness, your job is to observe and describe, not to adjudicate. That distinction determines your tone, your words and your posture when you step in. Once you become the judge, you will unconsciously favor, convict and punish — and what the kids feel isn't justice, it's your unfinished childhood emotions making decisions on their behalf.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
5:09

When you must step in

Dr. Pressman gives clear signals for intervening: a child is in danger, there's a power imbalance, or they're stuck in a loop they can't get out of. If the two are evenly matched and physically safe, you can stay nearby and occasionally ask ‘Is everyone okay?’ But watch especially for a power dynamic that turns into toxic bullying — rare, but when it happens an adult has to cut it off. Kids who experience peer or sibling bullying have far worse adult outcomes, and the damage is much bigger than we realize. Bullying, though, has a strict definition: a power differential plus repeated, malicious, intentional abuse. Merely ‘being a jerk’ isn't bullying, and kids being jerks to each other is completely normal.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
6:17

Sportscaster mode: describe, don't adjudicate

If you do need to step in, Dr. Pressman recommends ‘sportscaster mode’ — you act like an observant, reflective commentator and simply describe what happened. For example: ‘I noticed this happened over here, and that happened over there — did I get that right?’ This lets you validate both kids at once without taking sides, and there's no winner. The key is to avoid questions like ‘Who started it?’ ‘You always do this’ ‘There's something wrong with you.’ When you tell a child there's something wrong with who they are rather than with a behavior, you are feeding the exact habit you don't want them using on each other — turning anger and outbursts into a verdict on a person's worth. ‘You're stupid’ means ‘You are a stupid person,’ and no one needs to hear that.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
8:21

Stop dehumanizing language on the spot

Dr. Pressman says kids can scream, yell, be vicious to each other, even get physical — but if she hears dehumanizing language, she pauses the fight right there to change the wording. Because what you want to constantly ‘wire’ and ‘bake’ into your kids is: talk about the behavior, not about who the person is. This isn't a lesson delivered afterward, it's immediate intervention. She'll say ‘Let's rephrase that’ in the moment. The logic behind it: language habits need repeated reinforcement to internalize, and every dehumanizing expression you let slide is reinforcement in the opposite direction.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
14:33

Fair is not equal — it's needs-based

Dr. Pressman uses the peanut butter cupcake example to explain the difference between fair and equal: giving two kids peanut butter cupcakes of identical size and identical recipe looks equal, but if one of them is allergic to peanuts, it's completely unfair. Fair means giving each child what they need, not giving everyone the same thing. This framework protects you when a child demands ‘Why does my sister get a phone at 12 and I don't?’ — you can answer ‘You're different people, I've learned more since then, and I'm making a different decision that's better for you.’ If you try to be perfectly equal, you're assuming every child is the same age, the same temperament, the same personality, the same interests, the same needs — which obviously doesn't hold.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
16:37

Don't put labels on your kids

Dr. Pressman points out that giving all your kids the same label (say, ‘you're all helpers’) does promote positive behavior, but giving the label to only one child in the family creates tension and problematic dynamics. Labels plant seeds of resentment, insecurity and lack of motivation, and they push siblings apart. Teachers, friends, other parents and relatives will all apply labels; a parent's job is to counteract that damage, not join in. Labels ‘haunt’ a child — Dr. Pressman uses the word haunt, and says it sounds dramatic but it's true.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
18:41

Repair is not a forced apology

Dr. Pressman distinguishes real repair from fake repair. Repair isn't necessarily a public apology — if the kids finish fighting and suddenly giggle together, share an inside joke, make faces at each other, that is repair, that is reconnection, and you are watching it happen. Every reconnection strengthens the relationship and tells your kids ‘this relationship is forever, it gets messy, but we always come back.’ But a forced apology is not repair. Saying ‘sorry’ and saying ‘apologize to your brother’ don't teach what the research literature calls repair. An insincere apology only makes people angrier. You can ask ‘How do you think that made your sibling feel? Is there anything you could do to help them feel better?’ If the child says no, you don't just drop it — you come back later and ask ‘Have you had time to think about that?’

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
23:55

Be curious, not punitive, with the kid who starts it

When one child repeatedly instigates, Dr. Pressman suggests first telling the other child: ‘I've noticed your sister started it the last few times, and I also see how hard you're trying to avoid a fight. I know this is hard, and we'll keep practicing these skills.’ With the instigator, start with genuine curiosity: why are they starting things so often? Do they need more one-on-one time? Is there something you haven't noticed that they're longing for, jealous of, or needing? Then find a way to meet that need without depriving the other child. If you just yell ‘It's always you’ and punish, you haven't taught the underlying skill or self-reflection — ‘Why do I keep starting this? What do I actually want?’ Teaching the instigator to figure out what they want and then find a productive way to get it is one of the most important skills there is.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
29:17

Forced family time is an underrated ritual

Dr. Pressman says one of her favorite currently unpopular things is ‘forced family time.’ She often hears parents say ‘We used to have dinner together on Friday nights, but then the kids didn't want to come, they had other things to do or friends to see.’ She thinks it's important to find one sacred, non-negotiable family time in the week. Maybe the kids can invite friends, but you can't give up the time. We tend to fear our kids will be annoyed at being forced to spend time together, but forced family time isn't something you do every time — it's certain things that are sacred to the family, and they cultivate and build connection. Even if both kids are annoyed by the tradition, it's something they can complain about together, a time of connection, and once it becomes ‘that's just how it is’ it stops being a fight.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
31:23

Kids ganging up on you is healthy

Dr. Pressman shares a story from the car: her two daughters were fighting in the back seat, she got very annoyed, said something in a tone that may have gotten away from her, and they started laughing at how unhinged she was, stopped fighting, and even started helping her regulate. Annoying as that moment may have been for her, she was delighted to see them discover ‘when we team up against her, we're a team.’ She specifically notes that if one child is difficult and the other child teams up with you to complain about that difficult sibling, she's less interested in that dynamic — she's far more interested in them teaming up against you. That's much healthier, and it's the picture you want to see long term.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
33:30

Mixed-age play and shared interests

Dr. Pressman encourages mixed-age play. There's a trend now toward five-year-olds playing with five-year-olds and seven-year-olds with seven-year-olds, but really you should mix playmates of different ages, like an old-fashioned neighborhood where whoever's around plays in the cul-de-sac. Kids of different ages can be both teacher and learner at once, both big contributor and little kid still figuring things out. Beyond that, every child should have their own dedicated play time, but they should also find at least one shared interest — the whole family playing wiffle ball, going to a show, picking a comedy to watch together. That shared interest should be separate from the hobbies each child develops on their own.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
34:34

Make your kids cooperate to persuade you

Dr. Pressman suggests creating things your kids can only accomplish by cooperating — say they want to go out to eat, so they have to figure out together how to convince you to take them to their favorite restaurant. That way they have no time to argue about which restaurant, because they're busy figuring out how to pitch the idea to Mom or Dad. You can also create fun little rituals that look ‘anti-parent,’ like one day a week when dessert comes before dinner, Wacky Wednesday, Silly Sunday, and have the kids come up with ideas together to persuade you. Anything that gets them cooperating works. Also, when you're at the grocery store with one child, ask what they think their sibling would like — get them inside each other's heads. That's the beginning of practicing larger human relationships, peer relationships, romantic relationships.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
36:44

Call siblings friends

Dr. Pressman says calling siblings friends helps them treat each other like friends. The expectation is that from early on they truly treat each other like a deep friendship. There has to be one non-negotiable rule in your family: your friends are not allowed to be mean to your sibling. That's when you teach siblings to have each other's backs. In this world, it's hard to feel like no one has your back, and knowing that at school, on the playground, out there, someone has your back — and that person is your sibling — is enormously empowering and lets you feel belonging inside being loved, a belonging that's very rare right now. So snuff it out early: no ganging up on a sibling, no friends being mean to a sibling.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
39:52

The goal isn't no fighting, it's learning repair

Dr. Pressman says at the end that the sibling relationship is the longest relationship of your life. The goal isn't no fighting — let that dream die. The goal is learning to repair in a healthy, sincere way. Ask yourself: how do you want them to describe each other as adults? How do you want them to see each other as adults? Do you picture them laughing together? Build all of it now with the deep realization that along the way there will be some ugly fights, and that's part of the process.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman

Glossary

sportscaster mode
A parent acts like a commentator, describing only the observed facts without adjudicating right and wrong, so both sides feel acknowledged.
glass child
A child who gets ‘seen through’ and overlooked by parents because another child's needs are higher.
emotion coaching
Helping kids identify, express and process emotions rather than resolving the conflict for them.
repair
The process of reconnecting after conflict; not necessarily an apology — it can be laughing together or sharing a joke.
