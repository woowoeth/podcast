When Your Kids Fight, Are You a Witness or a Judge?

The biggest mistake parents make when stepping into sibling conflict is acting as a judge, carrying their own childhood fairness wounds. What kids need isn't a verdict on who's right or wrong, but practice repairing relationships in low-stakes conflict.

Deal with your own sibling baggage before stepping in

The episode opens by pointing out that almost everyone carries baggage from sibling relationships—even if you don't have siblings, you may have a longing for that ‘mythical connection’ or relief at not having it. The fairness trap is what most easily triggers parents' emotions: memories of a cake divided unevenly, a rule that was looser for one child, a new dress given to only one of you—these get activated when you see your own kids fight. If you step in without dealing with that baggage first, you're essentially imposing your own sibling history on your children. So the first step is always to sort out your own head, not rush in to pass judgment.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
4:07

You are a witness, not a judge

This is the most core line of the whole piece, and Dr. Pressman repeats it a dozen times. When you start judging who's right or wrong, who started it, who was more out of line, that judgment comes from your own history, not from what's actually happening between these two children right now. As a witness, your job is to observe and describe, not to adjudicate. This distinction determines your tone, your words, and your stance when you intervene. Once you become a judge, you'll unconsciously favor, convict, and punish—and what the children feel isn't justice, but your unprocessed childhood emotions making decisions for them.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
5:09

When you must step in

Dr. Pressman gives clear signals for intervention: the child is in danger, there's a power imbalance, or they're stuck in a loop they can't get out of. If both sides are evenly matched and physically safe, you can stay nearby and occasionally ask ‘Is everyone okay?’ But be especially wary of power dynamics turning into toxic bullying—this is rare, but when it happens, an adult must cut it off. Children who experience peer or sibling bullying have much worse outcomes as adults, and the damage is much greater than we realize. However, bullying has a strict definition: a power difference plus repeated, malicious, intentional abuse. Just ‘being a jerk’ doesn't count as bullying, and kids being jerks to each other is completely normal.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
6:17

Sportscaster mode: describe, don't adjudicate

If you do need to step in, Dr. Pressman recommends ‘sportscaster mode’—you act like an observant, reflective commentator, just describing what happened. For example, ‘I noticed this happened over here, and that happened over there—did I get that right?’ This lets you validate both children at once, without taking sides, without a winner. The key is to avoid questions like ‘Who started it?’ ‘You always do this,’ ‘There's something wrong with you.’ When you tell a child there's something wrong with who they are, rather than with a behavior, you're feeding the very habit you don't want them to use on each other—turning anger and outbursts into a questioning of a person's worth. ‘You're stupid’ says ‘You are a stupid person,’ and no one needs to hear that.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
8:21

Stop dehumanizing language on the spot

Dr. Pressman says kids can scream, yell, be vicious to each other, even get physical, but if she hears dehumanizing language, she'll pause the fight right there to change the wording. Because what you want to constantly ‘wire’ and ‘bake’ into your kids is: talk about the behavior, not about who the person is. This isn't a lesson afterward; it's immediate intervention. She'll say in that moment, ‘Let's rephrase that.’ The logic behind this is: language habits need repeated reinforcement to internalize, and every time a dehumanizing expression is let slide, it's reinforced in the opposite direction.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
14:33

Fairness is not equality—it's needs-based

Dr. Pressman uses the peanut butter cupcake example to explain the difference between fairness and equality: giving two kids identical peanut butter cupcakes of the same size and recipe looks equal, but if one is allergic to peanuts, it's completely unfair. Fairness is giving each child what they need, not giving everyone the same thing. This framework protects you when a child demands ‘Why does my 12-year-old sister have a phone and I don't?’—you can answer, ‘You are different people, I've learned more since then, and I'm making a different decision that's better for you.’ If you try to be perfectly equal, you're assuming every child is the same age, same personality, same temperament, same interests, same needs—which obviously doesn't hold.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
16:37

Don't label your children

Dr. Pressman points out that giving all children the same label (like ‘You're all helpers’) can indeed promote positive behavior, but if you give only one child in the family the ‘helper’ label, it creates tension and problematic dynamics. Labels plant seeds of resentment, insecurity, and lack of motivation, making relationships between kids more distant. Teachers, friends, other parents, relatives will all label them; parents' job is to counteract that damage, not join in. Labels ‘haunt’ children—Dr. Pressman uses the word haunt, saying it sounds dramatic but it's true.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
18:41

Repair is not forced apology

Dr. Pressman distinguishes true repair from fake repair. Repair isn't necessarily a public apology—if after a fight the kids suddenly giggle together, share an inside joke, make faces at each other, that's repair, reconnection, and you're witnessing it. Every reconnection strengthens the relationship, letting kids know ‘This relationship is forever, it gets messy, but we always come back.’ But forced apology is not repair. Saying ‘I'm sorry’ and saying ‘Apologize to your brother’ doesn't teach the repair the scientific literature talks about. Insincere apologies just make people angrier. You can ask, ‘How do you think that made your sibling feel? Is there anything you can do to help them feel better?’ If the child says no, don't just drop it—come back later and ask, ‘Have you had time to think about that?’

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
23:55

Be curious, not punitive, with the instigator

When one child repeatedly instigates, Dr. Pressman suggests first saying to the other child, ‘I've noticed your sister has started it the last few times, and I also see how hard you're trying to avoid a fight. I know this is hard, and we'll keep practicing these skills.’ To the instigator, first be genuinely curious: why are they instigating so often? Do they need more one-on-one time? Is there something you haven't noticed that they're longing for, jealous of, or needing? Then find a way to meet that need without depriving the other child. If you just yell ‘It's always you starting things’ and punish, you haven't taught them the underlying skill or self-reflection—‘Why do I always start this? What do I really want?’ Teaching the instigator to figure out what they want and find productive ways to get it is one of the most important skills.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
29:17

Forced family time is an underrated ritual

Dr. Pressman says one of her favorite, currently unpopular things is ‘forced family time.’ She often hears parents say, ‘We used to have dinner together on Friday nights, but then the kids didn't want to come, they had other things to do or friends to see.’ She believes it's important to find a sacred, inviolable family time each week. Maybe kids can invite friends, but you can't give up that time. We tend to fear kids will be annoyed at being forced to spend time together, but forced family time isn't about doing it every time—it's about certain things being sacred to the family that cultivate and build connection. Even if both kids are annoyed by the tradition, it's something they can complain about together, a time of connection, and when it becomes ‘just the way it is,’ it's no longer a struggle.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
31:23

Kids uniting against you is healthy

Dr. Pressman shares a story from the car: her two daughters were fighting in the back seat, she was very annoyed, said something in a tone that maybe got a bit out of control, and they started laughing, laughing at how out of control she was, then stopped fighting and even started helping her regulate her emotions. Though it might have been annoying in the moment, she was very happy to see them discover ‘when they unite against me, they're a team.’ She specifically notes that if one child is difficult and the other child allies with you to complain about that difficult sibling, she's less interested in that dynamic—she's more interested in them uniting against you. That's much healthier, and the picture you want to see long-term.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
33:30

Mixed-age play and shared interests

Dr. Pressman encourages mixed-age play. There's a trend now for five-year-olds to play with five-year-olds, seven-year-olds with seven-year-olds, but really you should mix different ages together, like in old-fashioned neighborhoods where whoever was around played in the cul-de-sac. Kids of different ages can simultaneously be teachers and learners, both big contributors and little ones still learning. Also, each child should have their own dedicated playtime, but they should also find at least one shared interest—like the whole family playing wiffle ball, going to a play, picking out a comedy together. This shared interest should be separate from the kids' individually developed hobbies.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
34:34

Have your kids collaborate to persuade you

Dr. Pressman suggests creating things that require your kids to cooperate, like if they want to go out to eat, they have to work together to persuade you to take them to their favorite restaurant. That way they don't have time to argue about which restaurant, because they're busy figuring out how to pitch the idea to Mom or Dad. You can also create fun little rituals that seem ‘anti-parent,’ like one day a week dessert before dinner, Wacky Wednesday, Silly Sunday, and have the kids come up with ideas together to persuade you. Anything that gets them cooperating works. Also, when you're at the supermarket with one child, ask them what they think their sibling would like, getting them into each other's heads—this is the beginning of practicing larger interpersonal relationships, peer relationships, romantic relationships.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
36:44

Call siblings friends

Dr. Pressman says calling siblings friends helps them treat each other like friends. The expectation is to truly treat siblings like a deep friendship from a young age. There must be one non-negotiable rule in your family: your friends are not allowed to be mean to your siblings. This is when you teach siblings to support each other. In this world, it's hard to feel like no one has your back, and knowing that at school, on the playground, out there, someone has your back, and that person is your sibling, is very empowering, lets you feel belonging in being loved—and that belonging is very rare these days. So nip it in the bud early: no ganging up on a sibling, no friends being mean to a sibling.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
39:52

The goal isn't no fighting, it's learning to repair

Dr. Pressman says at the end that sibling relationships are the longest relationships of your life. The goal isn't no fighting—let that dream die. The goal is learning to repair in a healthy, sincere way. Ask yourself: How do you want them to describe each other as adults? How do you want them to see each other as adults? Do you imagine them laughing together? Build it all now with deep understanding: along the way there will be some ugly fights, and that's part of the process.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman

Glossary

sportscaster mode
Parents act like a commentator, only describing observed facts without judging right or wrong, so both sides feel acknowledged.
glass child
A child who is ‘seen through’ and overlooked by parents because another child has higher needs.
emotion coaching
Helping children identify, express, and process emotions, rather than solving conflicts for them.
repair
The process of reconnecting after conflict, not necessarily an apology—it can be laughing together or sharing a joke.
