Cacti aren't as drought-resistant as you think: under extreme drought, a quarter of species will lose eighty percent of their habitat

Cacti are treated as a drought-resistance benchmark, but when researchers added extreme drought to climate models, they discovered that nearly a quarter of species will lose over eighty percent of suitable habitat—far beyond what conventional climate models predicted.

1:33

Cacti are only adapted to their evolutionary conditions, not drought itself

Researcher Mikhail Pillet said the finding "broke his heart": cacti are only adapted to the specific conditions where they evolved. When climate changes rapidly, they're as vulnerable as other plants and animals. This challenges the stereotype of "cacti equal drought-resistant benchmark" and sets up the paper's core argument: if even cacti can't handle extreme drought, climate models may have systematically underestimated drought's impact on biodiversity.

— Mikhail Pillet
3:06

Cacti's social media popularity enabled a 650,000-observation research dataset

The research team used observations from the community science platform iNaturalist, plus contributions from the public and researchers, to build a database of nearly 650,000 cactus records. They created individual models for each species, describing their current suitable climate conditions and how those conditions will shift geographically under future climate change. Cacti have such a large dataset precisely because they're internet-famous—people enjoy observing and sharing their locations.

— Mikhail Pillet
5:11

Adding extreme drought to models yields results so dramatic they seemed wrong

When Pillet showed early results to collaborators, they thought the model had a bug. The gap between versions with and without extreme drought was unexpectedly large, forcing the team to recheck their calculations. After verification, they confirmed the results were correct: most species decline under climate change regardless, but including drought reveals a critical difference in magnitude. Some species show slight overall increases with and without drought—the real difference lies in how steeply they decline.

— Mikhail Pillet
6:15

A quarter of cactus species will lose eighty percent of suitable habitat

Here's the key number: in the model incorporating extreme drought, the share of species losing more than eighty percent of suitable habitat jumps from "a few percentage points" to "nearly a quarter." Northeastern Brazil, the Chile-Argentina border region, and other areas face the worst impacts, with some regions expecting species losses exceeding twenty-five percent and a few approaching fifty percent. This shows that conventional climate models—which use only temperature and rainfall averages—systematically underestimate how extreme events shift species distributions.

— Mikhail Pillet
7:19

Field observers had already documented these losses; models just hadn't quantified them

When the team shared results with field researchers, they were unsurprised: they'd already observed reduced flowering, slower growth, sunburned plants, and in some regions mass die-offs—like the widely reported saguaro cactus deaths in America's Southwest, the iconic species from Western films. The model numbers quantified these scattered field observations into systematic risk for the first time.

— Mikhail Pillet
8:20

The study acknowledges it missed species interactions and evolutionary adaptation

Pillet openly acknowledged the model's limitations: it didn't include other types of extreme events, didn't account for potential evolutionary adaptation (despite a mismatch between evolution's speed and current climate change), and couldn't model pollination and herbivory interactions because modeling these for so many species is nearly impossible. This means the results probably underestimate true extinction risk.

— Mikhail Pillet
9:24

The IUCN Red List hasn't assessed cacti's status in over a decade

The IUCN Red List—the main tool conservation organizations use to allocate resources—hasn't updated its cactus assessment in more than a decade. The team hopes to use these insights, incorporating climate change factors, to reassess all cactus species' conservation status. They've already found that for some species, conditions have noticeably worsened in less than ten years.

— Mikhail Pillet
13:46

New obesity definition combines BMI with waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio

A panel of more than fifty experts proposed a new "combined anthropometric definition of obesity" (CAD obesity) that adds waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio to BMI. Testing both definitions on 800,000 patients across four countries, researchers found the new definition better predicted mortality risk. It identified high-risk individuals missed by BMI alone, and revealed people labeled "obese" by BMI who were actually healthy.
