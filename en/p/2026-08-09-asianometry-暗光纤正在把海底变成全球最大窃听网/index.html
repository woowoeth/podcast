The Undersea Cable Is Becoming the World's Largest Listening Network: Not Spies, Physics

Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) turns ordinary fiber into a continuous vibration listener: oil and gas well surveys, earthquake monitoring, whale tracking and anti-submarine ship detection all run on the same technology chain, and the ocean is rapidly losing its privacy.

3:10

The scattering that makes the sky blue is also the source of the eavesdropping

Light attenuation in fiber comes mainly from scattering. Brillouin scattering is produced by low-frequency phonons (triggered by temperature or strain), Raman scattering comes from molecular vibrations and the latter is often used for temperature measurement; but the most prominent is Rayleigh scattering — light reflected back by density fluctuations smaller than its own wavelength. These fluctuations act like tiny mirrors scattered throughout the entire fiber, reflecting a small fraction of the light back the way it came. Rayleigh scattering transfers no energy and does not change frequency; it is the reason the sky is blue and the source of every DAS signal: both the later OTDR and DAS are picking up this reflected light.

5:22

Polarization sensing failed because there were too many causes to attribute

In the 1980s OTDR was commonly used to find fiber breaks: send out a pulse of light, measure the arrival time of the Rayleigh backscatter echo, and thereby locate the break or high-loss point. In 1981 AJ Rogers of the UK's Central Electricity Research Laboratories proposed polarization optical time domain reflectometry, hoping to infer temperature, strain, acoustic waves and other external factors from polarization changes; but in the 1980s this path failed — there were too many causes of polarization change to attribute a specific change to a single factor. The Rayleigh signal is the sum of all fluctuations along the entire fiber, the signal is over-averaged, and temperature sensing could not be done either. The industry then turned to Raman and Brillouin scattering: weak signals, but their Stokes/anti-Stokes frequency shifts correspond definitively to temperature and acoustic strain.

7:25

What commercialized DAS was not the navy, but oil wells

Three breakthroughs turned Rayleigh backscatter from a diagnostic tool into a sensing method: narrower-linewidth lasers (purer light), compute capable of sampling tens of thousands of times per second, and a clear commercial need. The US Navy did some early work, still half-classified today; what truly broke DAS into commercialization was the oil and gas industry — in the mid-1990s oil companies began using fiber to monitor wellbores. Downhole temperatures are around 200°C and pressures up to 2,000 bar, a harsh environment where any electronic device wears out; the fiber approach puts all the sensitive equipment on the surface, with only cable downhole.

11:38

What fiber VSP won was not accuracy, but speed

The oil and gas industry first used Raman scattering and the anti-Stokes signal for distributed temperature sensing (DTS), then in the late 2000s applied Rayleigh-scattering DAS to vertical seismic profiling (VSP). Traditional VSP relies on a downhole geophone array, stringing about seven geophones at a time and re-deploying after each survey, slow and expensive; the fiber approach has a signal-to-noise ratio slightly below wired geophones but already very close, and the killer application is speed: minutes in some cases, versus 10 hours for the geophone approach. The early challenge was data processing — DAS can generate up to 10,000 times the data per second of an ordinary temperature sensor.

12:38

Seismic stations are too expensive to deploy; dark fiber is already laid

Two teams, at Berkeley and Stanford, applied DAS to earthquake monitoring in the early 2010s. Traditional seismic stations are costly and cannot be deployed at scale; meanwhile telecom and tech companies have laid vast amounts of unused dark fiber, which Ajo-Franklin of Berkeley Lab estimates at about 1 million kilometers globally, with another 100,000-200,000 kilometers added each year. These cables float freely underground rather than being fixed, and earthquakes are extremely low-frequency signals that are hard to distinguish from the noise of cars and trains; but they successfully tracked over a hundred small earthquakes both on campus and in the field, some with epicenters as far away as Mexico.

15:46

One existing undersea cable is worth ten thousand seismometers

The ocean-floor seismic monitoring network is even scarcer than on land. Giuseppe Marra's team at the UK's National Physical Laboratory used laser interferometry (related to but not identical with DAS) to detect a major earthquake in central Italy from the UK — using cable that was not laid for earthquake monitoring. The Berkeley team went further: Nate Lindsey used a four-day maintenance window on an undersea cable in Monterey Bay to turn 20 kilometers of cable into 10,000 sensors, recording a small earthquake and revealing multiple fault zones on the seafloor. A four-day window, existing cable, ten thousand listening points — that is the paradigm of dark fiber's reversal of fortune.

16:50

It can hear elephants on the tracks, and whale song too

Uses quickly went beyond earthquakes. As early as 1993, Taylor and Lee at Texas A&M filed a patent for intrusion detection based on Rayleigh backscatter, covering large-area protection scenarios such as airports and high-speed rail perimeters; Indian Railways even uses it to detect elephants wandering onto the tracks. The most eye-catching is marine life: by 2022, DAS could use existing undersea cables to track baleen whale song on the open ocean. The undersea acoustic environment is noisier than on land — internal waves, sediment transport, storms — but combined with grid search and Bayesian filtering, tracking is approaching automation.

18:55

A ship can turn off its transponder, but not its acoustic signature

In 2021, Diane Rivet's team in France found that DAS could track an oil tanker at a depth of 2,000 meters, working best in 85-meter shallow water, and could hear the engine and equipment noise of a tanker 2 kilometers away. Traditional passive sonar arrays — civilian or military — are reported to cost hundreds of millions to billions of dollars; DAS can cover a wider area with existing cable, and with compute and AI recognition it will only get stronger. A ship can turn off its transponder to hide from satellites, but it cannot hide its acoustic signature; threats like a dragging anchor on the seafloor can also be warned against in advance. The host's conclusion: perhaps before long, the ocean will be less private than ever before.

Glossary

DAS (Distributed Acoustic Sensing)
Uses the Rayleigh backscatter signal in fiber as it changes with external vibration/strain to turn an entire cable into a continuously distributed vibration sensor.
Dark Fiber
Fiber that has been laid but carries no traffic, often pre-installed by telecom companies as spare capacity, and can be used temporarily for DAS.
OTDR (Optical Time Domain Reflectometry)
A testing tool that sends pulses into fiber and analyzes the arrival time of backscatter echoes to locate breaks or high-loss points.
VSP (Vertical Seismic Profiling)
A technique that sends acoustic waves into the ground and receives reflected signals inside the well, used to image the rock around a reservoir.
Geophone
A traditional electronic vibration sensor lowered into a well for seismic surveys; expensive, fragile, and only deployable at discrete points.
Rayleigh Scattering
Light scattered by particles or density fluctuations smaller than its wavelength; the reason the sky is blue and the source of the DAS signal.
