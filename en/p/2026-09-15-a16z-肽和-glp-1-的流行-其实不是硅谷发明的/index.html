The peptide and GLP-1 boom wasn't invented in Silicon Valley

Peptide culture went mainstream in Texas and bodybuilding circles before Silicon Valley professionalized and accelerated it; what's really worth watching out for is that there's more content about content than content itself, which makes people misjudge what's actually happening.

There's more content about content than content itself

Ruby introduces the concept of paracontent, meaning "content about content." She observes that for things like game trailers and movie trailers, the volume of discussion around them often exceeds the content itself. The GTA 6 trailer is one example: people can keep discussing a thing, inflating it out of proportion, making it feel real even when it has no material reality underneath it. This bears directly on how to judge whether a trend is actually happening or is just being discussed a lot.

Peptide culture went mainstream in Texas and bodybuilding circles first

Many people assume peptides are a cultural trend exported by Silicon Valley; Ruby says that's wrong. The first time she heard about peptides was in Austin, Texas, when a friend took her to a med spa to get peptides from China injected — a gray-market, semi-legal use. The culture actually originated in bodybuilding communities in the American heartland or Midwest, and only later got professionalized, accelerated and managed in San Francisco. Elena adds that creatine is now starting to show up among her non-tech friends too, and the distribution chain is very rich: a TikTok influencer doing classic women's wellness might see a bodybuilding product on some other blog and just pick it up and use it, which makes it hard to say who invented it first.

Ten times as many people know about GLP-1 as use it

A Gallup poll a few months ago showed 11% of American adults are using GLP-1, and 90% of Americans have heard of GLP-1. Ruby had Astra find similar polls for comparison: 95% of Americans have heard of cryptocurrency, 14% hold it; VR and meal kits show similar ratios. These are almost all tech categories, and the ratio between users and people who've heard of them is roughly one to ten. Elena says this is the effect of the marketing machine — every subway in New York has ads from GLP-1 companies, and ordinary people know the name Zempic but may not know which company makes it.

Promotional content and personal-experience content are merging

Ruby analyzed roughly 960 peptide-related videos posted on TikTok between 2024 and 2026. Most of the content is positive, about weight loss and feeling better, and much of it is still tied to bodybuilding, showing the connection to the originating subculture persists. In 2025 and to some extent 2026, more skeptical content appeared, like "is this a scam" and "does it even work," and safety concerns became one of the most common themes in the later period. She also found a trend: promotional content and personal-experience content are merging. At first promotional content was more prevalent; as more people used it, content like "a day in the life of someone on Ozempic" appeared, and then the two types of content started to flip and fuse.

Men's weight-loss history starts with seventeenth-century sugar

Ruby lays out two parallel weight-loss histories: men's and women's. Men's weight loss appeared in the seventeenth century, after England increased sugar production in its Barbados colony. At the time sugar was exclusive to the upper class, and in medieval imagery fat was a symbol of wealth. But as sugar entered English society and its price fell, sugar showed up in coffeehouses, people started drinking a sweet wine called sack, the English got fatter and fatter, and even the middle class got gout. In 1620 Dr. Vener coined the word obesus from Latin to describe the unhealthy fat man as he saw him, and designed one of the earliest weight-loss diets. From then on, unhealthiness, mental dullness and obesity were linked together.

Thinness got encoded as a symbol of virtue and intelligence

Ruby says that as elites became more ascetic, they believed that restraining desires and passions through an ordered diet was a sign of control, of intelligence. From the pope to Descartes, people talked about controlling appetite as a way to display intelligence. So thinness got bound to the idea "I am good, I am smart." This idea was also encoded into fashion — the Victorian corset, for instance, was a garment that pushed you thinner, an outward expression of that believed-in virtue. Ruby points out that the elites were the first to adopt this idea while the masses were still eating sugar and staying poor, and this has the same shape as the trickle-down effect today where celebrities and elites use GLP-1 first after the price falls.

Body futurism: after silicon, the turn to the body

Ruby cites Toby Shorn's concept of body futurism: once we hit the limits of innovation on silicon, we turn the microscope on ourselves and try to optimize and innovate the body. She argues that the dashboards on these apps, going to longevity clinics, even things like Don't Die, are all body futurism. Elena adds that the quantified self is actually an example of Silicon Valley genuinely exporting to the whole world; Kevin Kelly proposed the concept back in 2007, when it was still just a matter for biohackers and a small group in the Bay Area, and now anyone can sign up for Function Health or Superpower, anyone can get a DEXA scan or use a CGM, and many people buy CGMs meant for diabetics to monitor their own blood sugar.

29:21

Microplastic panic will spawn consumer-grade testing

Elena mentions a group funded by Nat Friedman and others that sampled different foods — Whole Foods packaged meals, boba, protein bars — and worked with Austin's Light Labs to run microplastic tests, open-sourcing all the results. They found microplastics in baby food too, so one mother started testing more baby food and formula. The medical establishment has no consensus on whether microplastics are harmful, but enough smart people in the Bay Area believe they are. Elena judges this will bring more consumer-grade testing and advances in lab technology, and more microplastic-free products. Ruby thinks similar logic applies to demonized foods like seed oils: certification aggregators will appear, with members paying to confirm that what they eat is good.

37:53

Quantifying the body and mysticism will grow at the same time

Ruby cites a study: in a blind test on people wearing Oura rings, they were told incorrect sleep scores, and when the numbers were low or negative they reported having a worse day, even though the real numbers were correct. This shows these devices can have a harmful effect on how you feel about your health if the numbers are inaccurate. She sees many people starting to push back against quantification and turn to more holistic, more mystical ways of understanding the body, like reiki and acupuncture — and among the same people who previously did a lot of quantification. Ruby thinks we're in a pluralistic culture where quantifying the body and mysticism will grow at the same time, whereas before people swung in the same direction together.

Glossary

paracontent
The discussion, trailers and commentary generated around a piece of content, whose volume may exceed the content itself.
body futurism
Proposed by Toby Shorn: once innovation on silicon hits its limits, the turn to optimizing and innovating the body.
quantified self
The idea of continuously tracking your own body metrics with devices and data, proposed by Kevin Kelly in 2007.
GLP-1
A class of drugs used for weight loss and diabetes, such as Ozempic and Zempic.
peptides
Chains of amino acids, used on the gray market for muscle repair, anti-aging, cognition and more.
