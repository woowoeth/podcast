Churchill and Gandhi in the Boer War: who faced the cameras, who made the myths

The Boer War was a media war. Baden-Powell and Churchill both became famous by exaggerating their roles in siege warfare. Gandhi carried stretchers to treat British soldiers because he believed loyalty to Britain would bring Indian freedom—a conviction he later publicly admitted was mistaken.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

12:25

Mafeking's true saviors were the Batlaping, not Baden-Powell

The siege of Mafeking was portrayed in the British media as a Herculean feat, and Baden-Powell, with good PR instincts, exaggerated his role as ‘Mafeking's savior’. But much of the actual defense work was done by the Batlaping, who assisted the British. They sided with Britain over the Boers because they believed loyal service would earn them better treatment after the war—the same logic Gandhi would later follow in assisting the British.

— Bill Nassau
17:36

Churchill's armored train heroics were probably exaggerated

The story goes that young war correspondent Churchill kept his composure during an ambush on his armored train, directing removal of wreckage to rescue casualties while taking fire. But Bill Nassau plainly stated, ‘I don't think that's what happened,’ arguing that Churchill was just a young reporter at the time, and only became this heroic figure through years of embellishment—even his later story of barefoot escape with thorns drawing blood is questionable. Fourteen-year-old diarist Frieda Schlossberg's records show that after Churchill's capture, he actually stayed in comfortable conditions at a prestigious model school, as a ‘privileged prisoner’.

— Bill Nassau
22:54

Boer soldiers elected their officers and could vote to refuse battle

In the Boer command structure, officers were elected by soldiers, who could also vote on whether to participate in any given battle—if they thought it would mean senseless death, they could collectively vote to refuse. This highly democratic military setup explains why Louis Botha, at thirty-seven, was elected to replace a commanding officer who fell ill, and also explains why many Boer commandos would abandon their posts during siege to go home for weddings or cattle auctions, forcing commanders to resort to the whip for discipline—an early sign of the problems that would plague the Boer military later.

— Bill Nassau
29:19

Lord Roberts at five-foot-three was the actual adult who turned the tide

Faced with Buller losing while eating corned beef sandwiches, the British replaced him with Lord Roberts, just five-foot-three but famous from the Indian Rebellion and the Afghan campaign. He had led troops on a three-week forced march of 313 miles from Kabul to Kandahar, and soldiers genuinely loved him—a stark contrast to Buller, who left them merely ‘not too displeased’. Kitchener joined at the same time, bringing the rigorous methodology of his engineering background, and their partnership was described as ‘finally, the adults have arrived’, marking when the British seriously began to redress the shame of Black Week.

34:29

Churchill immediately wired the newspaper a war assessment after escaping

Once aboard a ship bound for Durban, Churchill immediately wired the Morning Post a military assessment: a trained Boer fighter was worth four or five regular soldiers, modern rifles made frontal assault hopeless, and you'd need equally skilled riflemen or enormous numbers to suppress them—he estimated 250,000 troops, and noted that sending sufficient forces early would actually be cheaper. He also mocked English gentlemen for clinging to their fox-hunting saddles instead of coming to fight themselves.

38:35

Gandhi and Churchill probably passed each other at Spionkop without recognizing each other

After moving to Natal in 1893, Gandhi immediately faced racial discrimination. Following the famous Pietermaritzburg train incident, he founded the Natal Indian Congress in 1894. When the Boer War broke out, he organized the Natal Indian Ambulance Corps, composed of 300 free Indians and 800 indentured sugar workers, serving as stretcher-bearers without compensation. At Spionkop, Gandhi and his team carried the wounded across several miles of rough terrain to field hospitals, while in the same battle Churchill was serving as a dispatch rider carrying messages up hillsides—the two may well have passed each other that day without either knowing who the other was.

40:47

Behind Gandhi's belief in British loyalty lay open racial contempt for Black Africans

Gandhi's assistance to the British aligned with his nonviolence principle, which explains why he chose to serve as a stretcher-bearer rather than take up arms. But the deeper motive was his then-belief that ‘proving loyalty would make the British grant you freedom’—a conviction he later publicly admitted was wrong. The show directly notes that Gandhi held open racial contempt for Black Africans during his South Africa years, believing Indians were superior to local Africans, and his opposition to the pass system was only to benefit Indian merchants and professionals like himself, not all people.
