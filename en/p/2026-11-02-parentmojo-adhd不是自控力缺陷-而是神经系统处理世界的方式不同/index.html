ADHD is not a self-control deficit, but a different way the nervous system processes the world

Mainstream frameworks treat ADHD as an executive function deficit correctable through self-control; this counselor argues the root is inborn differences in attention regulation and sensory processing, traits that once were survival advantages for humans.

Attention regulation, not executive function, is the root problem

The mainstream account traces ADHD to executive-function deficits, but B. Lourenco asks: why would executive function fail? She locates it instead in attention-regulation differences—not deficits but different regulation. The mechanism is sensory variation: the world is noisier, more intense, harder to screen out for ADHD brains. One theory she cites: ADHD may stem from early hunter-gatherer neurology, hence an interest-based nervous system—not a switch to do what you should, but constant alertness to what is that? because it could mean food or survival.

10:23

The lifespan gap is real, but the explanation is oversimplified

B. Lourenco acknowledges the data—ADHD people do have worse health outcomes and shorter lifespans—but rejects the pure impulse-control story. By that logic, living longer just means controlling yourself better, which doesn't hold. She points out that the medical model omits the comorbidities common in ADHD: mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, fibromyalgia. Those diseases themselves shorten life. If doctors fixate on impulse control, a patient with these comorbidities gets told to manage themselves, not screened for other conditions.

— B. Lourenco
13:37

ADHD requires structure and design, not control as the answer

Against objections like "rules help everyone" (traffic laws), B. Lourenco draws a line between structure and control. ADHD does need structure and constraint—free-form chaos won't work for this nervous system. But treating control as the only answer is broken: it puts all burden on the ADHD person or parent, not the environment. It reinforces "if you wanted to, you could," which isn't true. There is an impulse-control piece, she allows, but the answer isn't more control. It's finding structures people actually adopt and stick with.

— B. Lourenco
16:27

One household, two divergent medication histories

B. Lourenco has a child who was diagnosed and medicated for ADHD from childhood; she herself wasn't identified until adulthood. This split explains why she and the other parent clash on drugs. She tells listeners with similar childhood-medication histories: the problem wasn't just "too many kids on meds"—it was how much each kid took and that no one heard their voice. Those two wrongs—overdosing and silencing—can be separated; her child's path doesn't have to repeat them.

— B. Lourenco
17:24

Whether children should have a right to refuse medication: they clash

The episode mentions ADHD authority Barkley's stance: prefrontal development is incomplete, so kids lack the capacity to decide; medication is 100 percent a parent call, kids' input irrelevant. B. Lourenco calls this infantilizing, a denial of the child's autonomy as a whole person. She allows that medication works fast and gets picked first for that reason—environmental change takes upkeep—but speed alone shouldn't make it eternal or primary. Drugs can be a starting point, not the only or forever top option.

— B. Lourenco
19:41

One needs quiet, one craves noise: neither is wrong

When a parent desires calm but the child is wired for novelty and stimulation, B. Lourenco's first step: name it. Sensory need isn't chosen; it just is. This doesn't make anyone bad. But within the parent-child bond, the power is asymmetric. Parents bring more self-regulation skills and carry more load—a ratio that shifts with age. A four- to six-year-old can already understand "Mom needs quiet for a few minutes." B. Lourenco tells her kids she's taking a break and will be back—this normalizes her need and teaches them "Mom is a separate person from me."

— B. Lourenco
23:48

Stop blaming yourself first; then find people who can hold you

Asked what to say to newly diagnosed or questioning parents, B. Lourenco offers two things. First: stop shaming and blaming yourself. Put ADHD on the human timeline—it's not a fundamental error. Diversity itself is why the human species endured. Second: build a real, shame-free, non-judgmental network. Call someone trusted and say "I'm about to break, give me ten minutes"; have them supervise the kid even by phone, or video-link a distant friend to watch for a while. Needing help doesn't mean you're broken; it means you're human.

— B. Lourenco
26:28

Should society change for 5 percent of people

Jen closes with a memory: Barkley was asked, "Do we really reshape the whole society for 5 percent of the population?" His answer was a cold, scornful "No." Jen's is the opposite: yes—because designs that serve a marginalized group usually make life smoother for everyone. A routine that helps an ADHD kid get out the door on time usually helps the whole family's morning flow.

Glossary

neuroaffirming
A stance treating ADHD as brain diversity rather than a defect to be corrected.
interest-based nervous system
A nervous system driven by interest rather than obligation to direct attention.
