The Cuban Missile Crisis Almost Sparked Nuclear War—One Soviet Officer's Dissenting Vote Stopped It

On Soviet nuclear submarine B-59, two of three officers agreed to launch a nuclear torpedo—only Arkhipov dissented, and his single vote held veto power under Soviet protocol, blocking nuclear war's trigger.

Television won the debate, but polling barely budged

The first televised presidential debate in American history drew 70 million viewers. The visual contrast was stark—Nixon looked haggard, his suit ill-fitting, unshaven with stubble prominent on camera; Kennedy appeared evenly tanned and composed. But Frederick cautions: if you read only the transcript, Nixon actually acquiesced to Kennedy multiple times and didn't lose as decisively as remembered. Television transformed the image of the presidency voters "saw," not the substance of the debate itself. After four debates, the race remained deadlocked; the TV effect has been somewhat magnified in hindsight.

14:32

Nixon publicly conceded, privately contested the outcome

Kennedy won with only 112,000 votes—one of America's closest presidential elections. Public memory casts Nixon as a statesman gracefully accepting defeat, telling reporters the nation couldn't withstand a constitutional crisis. But Frederick reveals that Republicans did mount vote-verification efforts in swing states like Illinois, Texas, and New Jersey; Nixon likely knew of and tacitly approved these, but ultimately didn't push the challenge to its conclusion.

The Bay of Pigs fiasco—Kennedy bore responsibility he couldn't shake

1,400 CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs; intelligence completely misjudged whether locals would rise up to help overthrow Castro. The operation failed catastrophically. Kennedy's defenders note he inherited the plan from Eisenhower and was pressed by the military and CIA to execute it. But Frederick notes Kennedy had advisors warning it was madness—he approved it anyway. What actually deserves credit: he refused the military's pleas to commit U.S. Air Force to salvage the operation, preventing further escalation.

32:39

Vienna summit: Khrushchev lectured Kennedy like a schoolmaster

Two months after the Bay of Pigs, Kennedy met Khrushchev in Vienna expecting rational negotiation with the Soviet leader. He'd prepared exhaustively, like cramming for an exam. But Khrushchev adopted an aggressively bullying posture, catching Kennedy off guard, leaving him outmatched in response. Even Kennedy admitted afterward his performance was poor. The backdrop: Khrushchev had preferred facing anyone but Nixon, and faced internal pressure from East German population flight. He used the meeting to test how much spine this young American president actually had.

38:51

In the missile crisis, he alone resisted the war room's military logic

U-2 reconnaissance photos showed Soviet missiles deployed in Cuba. Kennedy convened ExComm—the National Security Council's executive committee—to decide response. The crisis's first days saw Kennedy himself leaning toward military action; the military and most civilian advisors pushed hard for air strikes. But as discussion deepened, he pivoted. Any military action, in any form, carried too much risk. He shifted toward a political solution: a naval quarantine, not invasion. This stance was established early in the crisis, not a last-minute retreat forced by circumstances.

— Frederick Logevall
43:08

Two letters, one public and one secret—exchanged to end the crisis

Evening of October 26, Khrushchev sent an emotional letter proposing: if America pledged not to invade Cuba, Soviets would withdraw the missiles. Next morning came a second letter, more hardline, demanding America also remove its missiles from Turkey. Conventional wisdom holds Kennedy used the "Trollope ploy"—answering only the first, softer letter and pretending not to receive the second. But Frederick reveals the outcome actually accepted both letters' demands: the public statement said only that Soviets yielded and withdrew Cuban missiles, but Kennedy privately agreed to withdraw Turkish missiles—a secret deal never mentioned publicly.

— Frederick Logevall
45:11

One Soviet officer's dissenting vote blocked nuclear war

On Black Saturday, American destroyers dropped depth charges on Soviet submarine B-59. The boat's commander, Savitsky, believed World War III had begun and ordered preparation to fire a 15-kiloton nuclear torpedo at U.S. warships. Under Soviet protocol, firing required unanimous agreement from three officers aboard. Two consented; only Arkhipov dissented. The nuclear attack was never executed. Frederick argues the crisis didn't spiral into nuclear war because both Kennedy and Khrushchev possessed the capacity to think from the other side's perspective. But this incident reveals the entire process was riddled with countless contingent nodes where things could spiral out of control.

— Frederick Logevall
56:50

Not until 1963 did he first call civil rights a moral issue

From 1962 to '63, street conflict in the civil rights movement escalated. In the James Meredith case—his fight to enter the University of Mississippi—Kennedy was forced to deploy federal marshals to escort him to campus; the images circulated nationwide. Frederick emphasizes Kennedy previously urged civil rights leaders to "wait a little longer," walking a tightrope between Southern and Black voters. Only in June 1963, in an Oval Office address, did he first frame civil rights as a "moral issue." This shift also involved diplomatic calculus—Mississippi's images had damaged American "soft power" abroad, especially in competing for Third World support.
