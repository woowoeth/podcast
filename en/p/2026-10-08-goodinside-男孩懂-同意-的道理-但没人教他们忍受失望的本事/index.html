Boys understand the principle of consent, but no one teaches them to endure disappointment

Knowing you should respect ‘no’ is only the start. Boys truly lack the emotional skill to absorb rejection, read when someone hesitates, and trust their own compass when their peers don't care.

1:04

Knowing the answer is not the same as being able to act

Many parents assume that once a boy understands the rule—respect when someone says no, speak up when a friend does wrong—he'll follow it. But knowing and being able to reach for that knowledge in a moment of high emotion are entirely different things, the way parents know they shouldn't yell at kids but do it anyway when triggered. When a boy enters a high-stakes situation involving consent, he doesn't bring knowledge; he brings emotional patterns built over years of repetition: how he tolerates disappointment, how sensitive he is to others' emotional cues, whether he checks in with himself or with his peers when uncertain. These patterns are what actually determine behavior.

— Dr. Becky
12:33

Setting boundaries is the parent's work; disappointment is the child's

The first key pattern: tell your child ‘no’ with real conviction, then allow him to feel sad about it. Each time a parent refuses—whether one more episode or a late party—because they genuinely believe it's right, the child is learning something beneath the surface about how relationships work: what happens when I want something and someone won't give it to me? If the parent keeps soothing the sadness, the child learns my pain is your job to fix. If the parent holds the boundary while accepting the child's sadness, the child learns disappointment hurts but I survive it, and this is not your fault. That second lesson is the foundation for later absorbing sexual rejection without treating it as a debt you owe me.

— Dr. Becky
19:53

The dangerous logic that grows behind ‘you promised’

When a child's disappointment escalates into blame—you ruined my whole night, you promised so you have to—this is significant. It's a belief forming: my feelings are a problem you created, and you must solve it. It seems harmless in a five-year-old but not at twenty. The answer isn't scolding him for blaming you, and it's not giving in to make the feeling stop. It's holding the boundary with fewer words while naming what's underneath: you wanted me to say yes, and you're really disappointed. She points out this is the childhood version of the twisted logic that shows up later in dating: ‘you said you'd sleep with me so you owe me.’

— Dr. Becky
25:00

When a child interrupts, use it to teach him to read others

The second key pattern: help a boy notice other people's faces, bodies, emotional shifts—not just his own needs. When he interrupts your call, instead of just ‘don't interrupt,’ ask: when you came in I was talking, how much availability did that show I had for what you're asking? When he teases his sister who was laughing but stops, looks down, ask what you noticed. Help him translate her stopped laugh as ‘please stop.’ This practice reading non-verbal signals runs on the same neural circuit later required to sense that a partner was invested but isn't anymore in an intimate moment.

— Dr. Becky
30:23

When your child says he doesn't want to go, don't rush to persuade

The third key pattern: help a boy trust that inner voice saying ‘this doesn't feel right,’ even when it means standing apart. She gives an example: Friday night everyone's going to a party with no adults, probably with alcohol. His child says he doesn't want to. The parent's first instinct is usually ‘what's wrong, everyone's going, have fun’—which inadvertently weakens the kid's instinct. She asks herself: am I undermining the exact voice I most want him to listen to when he's away from home later? A child's gut feeling doesn't have to control the decision, but it's real and deserves curiosity, not persuasion.

— Dr. Becky
35:42

Prepare a sentence your child can actually say out loud

Asking a child ‘what would you do if someone said something bad about a girl’ and having him answer ‘I'd speak up’ isn't enough. Follow up: what would make this hard? If he can't articulate the obstacle, he probably won't actually do it. She recommends practicing real dialogue together—and genuinely welcoming it if he rejects the words the parent suggests—until he lands on something he'd actually say, even if it's a curse word. Also essential: give him a backup plan. If he freezes in the moment and says nothing, that's not weakness or badness; telling someone later or pulling a friend aside both count as follow-up actions.

— Dr. Becky
40:50

What you practice at home must extend into institutions

Sexual violence is not a parenting problem alone, so it can't be solved through parenting alone. Boys are shaped continuously by culture, systems, and institutions. She mentions a university president discussing bystander training; someone raised an observation she keeps returning to: if we can have a designated driver, why not a designated upstander—one person that night specifically responsible for noticing what's happening and stepping in, protecting everyone? The three capacities you build at home—enduring not getting what you want, noticing other people, trusting that hesitant inner voice—must be reinforced by schools, universities, coaches, and teams. Otherwise the patterns childhood built get reversed by the environment when these boys become young adults.

— Dr. Becky

Glossary

dissonance
The instinctive feeling a child gets when encountering something that conflicts with family experience—a sense that something isn't right
bystander training
Training to teach people to actively intervene when witnessing another person in danger or being wronged
designated upstander
Drawing on the concept of ‘designated driver,’ one person assigned that evening to watch, intervene, and protect everyone
