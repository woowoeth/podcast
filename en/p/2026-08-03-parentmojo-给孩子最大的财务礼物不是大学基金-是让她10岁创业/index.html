The biggest financial gift you can give your kid isn't a college fund — it's getting her to start a business at 10

A 10-year-old earned $759 for retirement through pet sitting. This isn't pushing kids early — it's using real responsibility to build systems thinking, boundaries, persistence and financial freedom.

1:59

For a kid's money to go into a retirement account, it has to be earned

Jen's opening conclusion: the most powerful financial gift you can give your child isn't a college fund, it's letting them create their own income around age 10. Her daughter Carys earned $600 in her first month pet sitting and put $759 of it into a Roth IRA. Because she started early, at a conservative 10% annual return that money is projected to exceed $112,000 by retirement. The point isn't the amount — it's that it's real earned income, so it can go into a retirement account that only accepts work income.

5:32

Soft skills can't be taught, only forced out by real problems

Organization, boundaries, persistence — none of these come from a parent nagging. Carys couldn't remember what each pet ate, where the keys were hidden, or what the security code was, so she designed her own intake form. When she hit her limit for the day, she'd say plainly, "I don't have the capacity." Facing a fearful dog, she could sit on the floor ignoring it, waiting nearly 40 minutes while the dog tested its way back and forth, until she could take it out. These abilities appear only when she's solving a real problem she cares about, not completing a task a parent assigned.

— Jen Lumanlan
14:43

What's a deficit in the classroom is an advantage in business

Carys is suspected to have dyslexia and struggles with reading and writing, but caring for animals and scheduling doesn't require fluent literacy at all. She can pour intense focus into a dog she's interested in — a trait treated as a defect in a traditional classroom that becomes a strength in real service. Kids with ADHD aren't incapable of focus; they just need a subject they genuinely care about. Autistic kids' sensitivity to order and structure can translate directly into reliability clients can see. School keeps telling them "you can't," while the business offers a different set of coordinates for judging them.

— Jen Lumanlan
16:34

A mistake at 10 is learning; at 16 it's being immature

At this age kids haven't yet been domesticated by the adult world's "this isn't allowed" inertia, and they're already capable of handling calendars, commitments and money. Jen observes: when a 10-year-old messes up, adults say "still learning"; at 16, they're expected to "know better." Kids charge less, so clients are quicker to give them a chance — and when adults hire a kid they're often investing in their growth, not just buying a service. That goodwill leaves a buffer for the learning curve.

— Jen Lumanlan
28:15

The moment a parent steps in, the business becomes the parent's

Jen's principle is to be a "guide on the side," not a "sage on the stage." For the business to belong to the child, you can't schedule for her, write her scripts, or build her systems. She only asks questions when her daughter is stuck — like "what would help you remember this information" — then lets Carys design the form herself. She steps in only when she sees overload signals: lip-biting, slumped shoulders, restlessness. When her daughter entered a client's security code wrong twice, she held back from reminding her and let the kid learn to carry the form with her.

— Jen Lumanlan
40:55

The bigger danger is raising a kid who can't judge for herself

Safety isn't a reason to stop a kid from doing things. Carys's website contact form copies Jen; Jen accompanies her fully before taking on a new client; before entering a house she confirms no one else is inside, and leaves immediately if she sees someone unexpected; she carries a walkie-talkie while working and doesn't take large dogs or dogs that lunge on the leash. What Jen fears more is raising a child who can't make independent judgments, can't talk to strangers, and won't ask for help when in trouble. Judgment practiced in low-risk settings is the real safety net.

— Jen Lumanlan
43:43

Even her busiest day is only a few hours, because she can turn down jobs

Jen walks through an unusually busy Thursday: first to a client's house to feed the cat and give 0.4ml of medication; then a midday walk for a student's dog, about 30 minutes; in the afternoon, confirming a meet and greet with a new client and connecting with another local dog walker for mutual referrals; in the evening, walking a professor's puppy and the student's dog. Client payments go into Jen's Venmo, and Carys decides how much goes toward the freeze-dryer and how much into the retirement account. Most days are far lighter, because she can decline time slots and tasks she doesn't like.

— Jen Lumanlan
52:57

Starting isn't about selling — it's about noticing your neighbors' problems first

Jen's startup advice isn't to sell; it's to observe for a week: look with your kid at what problems the neighbors have, what the kid is interested in, and what they want but can't afford. Then run just one small trial — helping one neighbor once — to test whether the kid enjoys it and whether the neighbor finds it valuable. The parent's job is to ask questions, not give answers, to resist the urge to take over, and to let the kid be inefficient. Even if no business comes of it, the experience and skills gained are a win.

— Jen Lumanlan

Glossary

Roth IRA
A U.S. retirement account you can only fund with earned income; a kid with work income can open one too.
unschooling
Not following a fixed curriculum; letting the child learn from their own interests. Jen's family does this.
sliding scale pricing
Charging within a range based on what the client can pay; Carys's rate is $10–30 per day.
guide on the side
The opposite of "sage on the stage": asking questions so the child finds their own answers instead of doing it for them.
intake form
A form used at the first meeting to record pet details, key locations and security codes, item by item.
