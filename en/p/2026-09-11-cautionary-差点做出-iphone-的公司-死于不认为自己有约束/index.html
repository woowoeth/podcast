Cautionary Tales with Tim Harford 2026-09-11

The company that almost built the iPhone died because it didn't think it had any constraints

General Magic had the biggest industry alliance in history, more money than it could spend, and the best engineers in the world — and sold 3,000 devices in six months. It lacked neither money nor talent. What it lacked was any sense that it had constraints.

The iPhone was imagined in 1989

While Peratt was in Apple's Advanced Technology Group, he used a big red notebook to describe the information and communication product of the future: it should bring personal satisfaction like jewelry, have perceived value even when unused, offer the comfort of a touchstone, the feel of a shell, the magic of a crystal. What he drew was a smooth rectangle, no buttons, only a touchscreen, able to serve as a phone, a messaging device, a portable TV and a game console, and able to book plane tickets. It was 1989: most cell phones were the size of a brick, Apple's new portable computer weighed 16 pounds, and the World Wide Web did not yet exist. He called the idea a pocket crystal, imagining people carrying it everywhere — "if your house caught fire, you'd think, first save the family, then grab my pocket crystal." He told Bill Atkinson about the idea, and Atkinson brought in Andy Hertzfeld, two of the core programmers behind the original Macintosh.

9:40

A company with no product can still make Wall Street line up

General Magic had no profits, no revenue, not even a product — only an idea — and it issued stock to ordinary retail investors. Goldman Sachs investment bankers shoved each other to grab allocations; one banker asked, "What's the price? Would taking off my shirt be enough?" and then actually did it, standing half-naked in the conference room. The stock doubled immediately after listing. Every partner in the alliance had already put in millions of dollars each. As one vice president in the book told David Epstein, they had so much money they never had to worry about making payroll — what startup never has to worry about making payroll? That is the key mechanism: enough money that you never have to make a trade-off.

— Tim Harford
12:46

The tech was so good it made a partner's department obsolete on the spot

Two young engineers flew to Japan to show a hardware prototype to Mitsubishi. The demo had barely started when the host suddenly called in a group of employees in work clothes, apologized, and asked them to start over — because this thing had no modem, it was all implemented in software. A groan came from the audience, and a manager named Kato slumped forward and banged his head on the table. The host apologized again: "You just made Kato's department obsolete." This technology made answering machines, arcades, books, keyboards and mice obsolete. At the same time they built the precursor to USB cables, a new programming language called Telescript, called portable networking the cloud, put skeuomorphic icons on a touchscreen, and built a store where you could buy applications — back when nobody called it an app store.

— Tim Harford
17:55

They built the future but not a product

The first device running the General Magic operating system was Sony's Magic Link in 1994. Behind the gorgeous graphics it was hard to use, shipping with a 200-page manual; it was the size of a hardcover book, its grayscale monochrome touchscreen only the size of a postcard, and it ran on old-fashioned AA batteries that drained fast. There was no Wi-Fi and no Bluetooth, only an old-style telephone jack on the body — sending messages and making calls both required sitting at a desk and plugging in a phone line. It did not even have a built-in phone. In today's purchasing power, about $1,600. Sony sold 3,000 units in the first six months, mostly to friends and family of General Magic employees. A few years later, Apple sold about 300,000 iPhones on day one. In 1995, Motorola's Envoy had wireless communication, but cost nearly twice as much as the Magic Link.

20:00

eBay's idea was called the dumbest thing a colleague had ever heard

Pierre, a technical support engineer at General Magic, built a webpage on the side: list the stuff sitting unused at home and let other people bid on it. He named the site AuctionWeb, showed it to colleagues, and asked whether he could spin it out as a company, or whether General Magic would invest. One colleague's response was typical: this is the dumbest idea I've ever heard — you want strangers to trust each other on the internet? Good luck, we're not interested. The quiet man was Pierre Omidyar, and the site was eBay, which later made him a billionaire. It is also the epitome of General Magic missing the Web — people inside reminded management every day, and management always found a reason to ignore it.

— Tim Harford
24:06

Missing the Web was embarrassing, but it wasn't the cause of death

In the documentary, former employees talk about missing the World Wide Web with laughter and sighs mixed together, but Tim Harford argues that this failure is a distraction. When the Magic Link launched in 1994, the Web was still a curiosity; even if it had been Web-compatible, that would not have been enough to make someone spend the equivalent of $3,000 on a Motorola Envoy. The second explanation is "too early": by the time the iPhone launched in 2007, memory prices had fallen 1,000-fold from 1994, a typical microprocessor had 500 times the computing power, and there was Wi-Fi, 3G and a Web with real content. That technology gap is real, but one technology commentator in the documentary says General Magic could never have succeeded no matter what it did, and Tim says: that sounds plausible, but he's wrong.

— Tim Harford
29:19

They had time to write the calendar back to the start of astronomical time

Epstein's argument is that General Magic had no constraints at all: the best hardware and software engineers, excellent design and marketing, a charismatic prophet-like leader, plus Apple's investment, millions from each alliance partner, and retail investors' money. With both talent and time, why compromise? Just go build the pocket crystal — even if early-1990s technology simply could not build it. The consequence was that nobody was forced to figure out what they were actually doing: when they described the target market they said Joe Sixpack, meaning everyone. So the product was stuffed with an instrument panel, a big walking-lemon emoji, and a coin-flip animation. The coin flip came from co-founder Andy Hertzfeld, a man who at the time clearly felt he had all the time in the world. The calendar originally covered only 1904 to 2096, a few lines of code, but it was first changed to the year 1 AD, then to the start of astronomical time.

— Tim Harford
33:33

Tony Fadell asked only two questions

Tony Fadell used to call every day begging General Magic for a job, and he was heartbroken when the company collapsed, but he learned something. At his next company, he focused on just two things: given today's technology, what is new, exciting and possible? The answer was MP3s and ever-cheaper hard drives, and combining the two could produce a digital music player that made Walkmans and CD players obsolete. The second question was what deadline to set for himself. The answer: it's January 2001, we ship before Christmas. The company he joined was Apple, and what shipped a few weeks before Christmas on time was the iPod — doing one thing beautifully. That restraint paved the way for the next project, the pocket crystal that was actually built: the iPhone. His colleague Andy Rubin later founded Android, and others co-founded LinkedIn, ran Google Search, and led Apple's AI, Safari and Apple Watch.

Glossary

Pocket Crystal
The buttonless touchscreen portable device Peratt envisioned in 1989, the prototype of the iPhone.
Telescript
A new programming language developed by General Magic to let portable devices communicate with each other over a network.
skeuomorphic
Icon design that represents functions with the appearance of real objects, such as clicking a folder to see files.
Joe Sixpack
American slang for the ordinary man on the street; General Magic used it to describe its target market.
