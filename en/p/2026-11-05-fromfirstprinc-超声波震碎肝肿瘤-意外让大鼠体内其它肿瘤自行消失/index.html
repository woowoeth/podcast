Ultrasound Shattered Rat Liver Tumors; Other Untreated Tumors Vanished

In rat liver tumor experiments, 9 of 11 animals with partially ultrasound-destroyed tumors not only showed no metastasis—untreated residual lesions vanished entirely; the mechanism was released antigens activating systemic immune response.

One Cardiologist's Challenge Forced an Impossible Invention

When Zhen Xu first joined the University of Michigan lab, pediatric cardiologist Ahmad Rudomirsky gave a seminar describing how a patient died on the operating table during congenital heart surgery. He challenged the lab: could you invent a technique that performs true surgery without incisions—he emphasized true surgery because microwave, radiofrequency, and ultrasound heating ablation only cook tissue; they cannot truly remove it, useless to him. This seminar became the origin of what would later be called histotripsy; Zhen Xu decided on the spot to make it her doctoral dissertation, though she had no idea how.

— Zhen Xu
5:26

Cavitation Was Dismissed as Uncontrollable for 50 Years

Cavitation—ultrasound causing bubbles in tissue to expand and contract, mechanically tearing cells—had been observed for nearly 50 years, yet the field believed it could only be controlled by injecting bubbles, otherwise it was random and harmful, like when air in lungs ruptures during imaging. Histotripsy's breakthrough was injecting nothing at all, using only natural nanoscale gas particles in body tissue: these bubbles have surface tension reaching 200 to 300 atmospheres, extremely resistant to cavitation. Precisely because lungs are themselves massive air bubbles, histotripsy still cannot treat lung cancer.

— Zhen Xu
14:54

Silencing the Device Paradoxically Unlocked Tissue Liquefaction

Zhen Xu, with no ultrasound therapy background, followed advisor Charles King's advice to "explore, don't read papers." She found a slaughterhouse in the phone book and went weekly to scavenge discarded pig hearts for experiments, trying every parameter but failing repeatedly, until a colleague complained the equipment was too loud. She did the opposite: raised the pulse repetition frequency above 20 kHz, beyond human hearing—the tradeoff was individual pulse energy actually went down. It was this quieter configuration, chosen for silence not strength, that made pig heart tissue produce a smoke-like phenomenon, creating a hole in one minute—the discovery moment of histotripsy in 2002.

— Zhen Xu
29:27

A Decade Nobody Funded; Only NIH Sustained the Work

After discovering the phenomenon in 2002, Zhen Xu spent nearly a decade—using high-speed cameras to capture microsecond, micrometer-scale bubble activity—before understanding the mechanism. She was blunt: no rational person would fund this kind of work, neither companies nor venture capital would pay for such fundamental research with no visible applications, only NIH funding sustained it. From the initial 2002 discovery to FDA approval for liver cancer treatment in 2023, the journey took 21 years.

— Zhen Xu
38:52

A Student's Personal Tragedy Redirected the Research

The turn to liver cancer came from a request by doctoral student Eli Vlaisavljevich—his mother died of liver cancer when he was young. At the time, the entire field worried that mechanically destroying tumor cells would promote metastasis, so the team designed a rat experiment: intentionally treating only 50–75% of liver tumors, simulating worst-case scenarios, then monitoring metastasis long-term. Control group rats all died within two or three weeks from aggressive hepatocellular carcinoma, but of 11 treated rats, 9 not only saw no tumor spread—even untreated residual tumors vanished completely, nowhere to be found.

— Zhen Xu
42:03

Mechanically Destroying Tumors Unexpectedly Activated Systemic Immunity

The unexpected finding prompted the team to collaborate with tumor immunologist Cliff Cho to investigate, revealing the mechanism: cancer hides itself by suppressing local immune response, while histotripsy converts tumor cells into liquid debris and ruptures cell membranes, releasing previously sequestered tumor antigens into circulation. The body then searches and attacks matching antigens not just at the treatment site but throughout the body—essentially replicating immunotherapy without injecting any drug—also explaining why treating one tumor shrank untreated lesions elsewhere. Zhen Xu emphasized this effect varies by individual, and the team is still exploring how to optimize parameters to amplify it.

— Zhen Xu
47:31

HOPE4LIVER Trial: 90% of Treated Tumors Stayed Gone at One Year

The multicenter clinical trial HOPE4LIVER was the direct basis for FDA approval of histotripsy for liver cancer treatment. Data showed low adverse-event rates; at 30-day follow-up, imaging-confirmed ablation success rate was 96%; at one-year follow-up, local tumor control rate was 90%. Because it's minimally invasive, doctors cannot directly confirm it visually like open surgery, so the entire procedure relies on imaging guidance—doctors outline the tumor on the image, software plans the robotic arm's scanning path accordingly, the doctor confirms and clicks start, and the robot completes treatment automatically, while bubbles from tissue destruction themselves serve as natural ultrasound contrast, letting doctors watch treatment in real time.

— Zhen Xu
1:03:08

$40 Million Federal Funding Became a $3.75 Billion Company

When accepting the Golden Goose Award, Zhen Xu released a set of figures: over roughly 20 years, NIH invested about $40 million funding histotripsy research—early on, no company or investor would fund such fundamental research with no visible applications. Today FDA has approved it for liver cancer treatment, over 5,000 patients have received treatment, HistoSonics is valued at $3.75 billion with 500 employees, and was named by Time magazine as one of the ten most impactful healthcare companies. Her conclusion: federal research funding is the fundamental driver of America's technological innovation leadership.

— Zhen Xu

Glossary

histotripsy
A technique using focused ultrasound to create cavitation bubbles within tissue, mechanically pulverizing lesions without incisions.
cavitation
The violent expansion and collapse of nanoscale gas bubbles in tissue caused by ultrasound, mechanically disrupting cell membranes.
HIFU (High-Intensity Focused Ultrasound)
A therapy using high-intensity focused ultrasound to heat and thermally destroy tissue.
diffraction limit
The smallest physical size to which a focused ultrasound beam can be narrowed; histotripsy overcame this via threshold effects.
Golden Goose Award
Award recognizing initially unconventional federal research funding that ultimately produces major social impact.
