There Are No Sub-$100B Niches in Payments

There are no sub-$100B niches in payments

Max Levchin says payments is ‘by any imaginable measure the largest market in the world’ — any slice you think is narrowest or most contrarian still adds up to $100 billion. But Alex Rampell adds a counterintuitive mechanism: once the amount becomes extremely large, the take rate becomes extremely small. You can't charge 2% on a $40 trillion transfer. So large payments have enormous volume but often aren't large revenue opportunities; the real large revenue opportunities are at the small, high-frequency end. The only exception is B2B payments — everyone thinks they have a clever B2B payments idea, and it happens to be the exception to this rule.

— Max Levchin / Alex Rampell
4:04

Visa's network has a hard 2.5-second ceiling

Max points out that Visa and MasterCard have a hard 2.5-second limit on the interaction between the network, the issuing bank, the merchant, and the acquiring bank; if it times out, the transaction is retried or simply cancelled. This means offline card swipes leave almost no room for any ‘clever innovation’ — online e-commerce can run fraud checks before submitting the card number, but offline, once the card is handed over, you have only 2.5 seconds. Apple Pay and Google Pay matter because they built a secure enclave into the chip, doing the ‘I already know your card’ part in advance, thereby shifting the entire timeline. What genuinely surprised Max: Visa and MasterCard have still not introduced a new standard saying ‘actually it doesn't have to be 2.5 seconds, it could be 15 seconds, let's go have a bunch of issuing banks bid to give you better credit terms.’

— Max Levchin
10:13

Payment innovation has no ‘just okay’ outcome

Max tells a failure case he witnessed firsthand: before PayPal, MasterCard and a gas station network built a ‘magic wand’ that you could wave next to a gas station to fuel up and complete payment. He thought at the time, ‘My God, this will obviously replace credit cards,’ but it didn't. The rule he distilled from it: payment innovation has a critical mass that isn't obvious a priori but is clear in hindsight; fall short and you die — there is no ‘just okay’ middle outcome. Either everyone must have your widget, your network, or you go into the dustbin of history. That magic wand was only a little faster than the credit card in your pocket, not much faster, and the credit card just worked. Amazon also shut down palm payments at Whole Foods; Max says he loved it and used it every time he stayed at a hotel, but it was actually slower — just fun.

— Max Levchin
20:40

The pajama problem and ‘paying with identity’

Affirm's starting point was a concrete image: you're upstairs in your pajamas, you want to buy something, and your credit card is downstairs — how do you pay? Alex's reasoning: phones are proliferating, social networks are proliferating, so maybe there could be a new credit mechanism, returning to the 1800s general store model: you don't have cash on you, but the shopkeeper knows you and says, ‘Max, I trust you, settle up next time.’ Today, if you're at a Walmart in person, the greeter has no idea who you are and can't do the general store thing; but if you have 500 friends on Facebook and have uploaded a thousand photos, you're probably a low credit risk. The key is ‘you're not looking for credit’ — people actively seeking credit (searching Google for ‘broke, need money, credit’) are often bad credit risks, whereas the general store model serves people who aren't looking for credit, who just didn't bring their wallet.

— Alex Rampell / Max Levchin
33:08

A 30% conversion lift is when they found PMF

Affirm's stint with 1-800-Flowers never went smoothly: Amit was never satisfied with the conversion rate and said explicitly, ‘You're cannibalizing my credit card volume and charging more than a credit card — this is a dumb idea.’ The turning point came from Beautylish — its only difference was putting the line ‘you can pay in three installments’ upstream in the shopping flow, telling customers while they were picking out shampoo and perfume, and conversion immediately rose 30%. Max says that was the moment they understood: this isn't an alternative payment method, nor is it solving the pajama problem; it's solving ‘my budget is only this much, but if I can pay in installments, my budget expands.’ They then turned this playbook into a sales offensive, finding a batch of small DTC brands that didn't care whether they were charged 1%, 5%, or 12%, because what these people wanted was to grow revenue.

— Max Levchin
39:34

Every mattress company read the same HBR article

Max says the Harvard Business Review article from around the time Casper was founded was read by all these entrepreneurs: people replace their mattress every seven years, and if you're the company selling that mattress, this is the most important thing in the world — miss it and you wait another seven years — and mattress gross margins are astonishingly high. So within four or five years a bunch of companies sprang up, with the logic: either get you to shorten the replacement cycle from seven years to six, or get you to decide this mattress is the best, coolest one, that pops out of a box. The result was a saturated mattress industry, but it also created a huge premium on ‘getting you to buy a mattress at any cost’ — ‘I'll give you a three-year zero-interest loan, provided by Affirm’ became a very worthwhile marketing cost. Alex adds that mattress margins are extremely high, so tolerance for MDR is much greater, and nobody wants to pull out money for a $1,200 mattress while in their pajamas; once it's split into 30 installments, conversion skyrockets.

— Max Levchin / Alex Rampell
40:37

True zero interest is anger at fake zero interest

Max says he will remain angry until this product is legislated out of existence: the ‘open a store credit card, zero interest’ signs in department stores — that zero has an asterisk, meaning it assumes you keep spending over the next 12 or 24 months; if you pay a penny less or a day late, interest is retroactive to the day you got the card — you spent $1,000, and two years later you wake up owing $3,000. This is called a deferred interest credit card. Affirm's true zero interest comes directly from that anger: a month late, a year late, the price doesn't change; Affirm's zero never has an asterisk. This is also the origin of their not charging late fees and not doing deferred interest — ‘you decide to do three years with us, you will never be screwed, you will never get a negative surprise.’

— Max Levchin
43:43

Affirm's customer acquisition cost is negative

Alex says that as a VC, looking at 90% of consumer companies, he'd rather buy Google or Facebook stock, because those companies' customers are bought from them. And the coolest thing about Affirm in his eyes: it has negative customer acquisition cost — it gets paid to acquire a customer. The mechanism is B2B2C: Casper, the friendly white ghost mattress company, doesn't want to personally send you a dunning notice saying ‘get off my mattress, you haven't paid me back’; it wants a third party to do that. Alex says he tried B2B2C at TrialPay, but Zynga and Netflix didn't want him owning their customers; with Affirm, merchants actually want Affirm to own the customer, because supporting a product, handling unhappy customers, and tech support are already burdens — a one-year loan requires 12 reminders, a three-and-a-half-year loan requires 39 — and merchants are happy to hand off that communication burden.

— Alex Rampell
52:52

Agents will buy goods, but won't pick your clothes

Max says he's not that optimistic about agentic shopping, but very optimistic about agentic payments. The idea that ‘robots will buy our Friday night clothes for us’ is wrong — we want to know how something looks on us before the robot drops it at the door. But the credit card, ‘the best user interface ever,’ may finally be up for renegotiation, because an agent is indeed smarter than a piece of plastic, or even a piece of plastic with a rewritable chip. Alex partly agrees but draws a boundary: when researching what to buy, AI is just another tool — you ask friends and you ask AI; but when you already know which SKU you want and just want the lowest price, that's where the agent truly takes over. He cites CamelCamelCamel as an example — a top-100 US website, but people earning over $100,000 a year have probably never heard of it; it serves the ‘time is worth more than money’ crowd.

— Max Levchin / Alex Rampell

Glossary

MDR / merchant discount rate
The rate a merchant pays to accept a payment, because cash has to be advanced to the merchant.
APR / annual percentage rate
The annualized cost a consumer pays to borrow, distinct from the merchant-side MDR.
deferred interest
A credit card term where, during a zero-interest promo period, paying a penny less or a day late makes interest retroactive to the day of purchase.
B2B2C
A platform first serves merchants, who then reach consumers; Affirm uses this to own the end customer.
secure enclave
An isolated secure area inside a chip; Apple/Google Pay use it to pre-validate card information.
factoring
Selling receivables outright for cash; because it's a sale rather than a loan, it isn't subject to credit laws.
