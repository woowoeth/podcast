The key to secure attachment is reunion, not separation

In the Strange Situation, the child is briefly left in an unfamiliar room, and crying at separation matters far less than the reaction at reunion: whether the child actively approaches, seeks contact, and can go back to playing once "refuelled." Steele says securely attached children have a strategy — they know what to do when they feel bad. Insecure attachment also comes with strategies, just worse ones: avoidant children look calm but their cortisol keeps rising; ambivalent children are picked up and then want down, unable to return to exploration.

22:44

Avoidant and ambivalent are the best solutions the child could devise

Steele stresses that avoidant is not coldness: by nine to twelve months the child has learned "no one comes when I'm upset," so it pushes the distress away and plays with toys; ambivalent children amplify emotion to win closeness. Both are organized strategies, the best defence available under the circumstances. Sarah adds: it doesn't work, but it's the best thing the child can come up with. That explains why blaming a child for being "too clingy" or "not clingy enough" is wrong — it was adapted.

25:58

Disorganized attachment is "fright without solution"

Mary Main used fright without solution to describe disorganized attachment: the child desperately needs this attachment figure to protect it and is also afraid of this person, and with two irreconcilable facts holding at once, behaviour breaks down — the child moves away when the parent returns, freezes, huddles in a corner. Steele warns the term has been overused by the public, drifting from its originally strict observational definition. It is not a strategy the child chose; it is the system shutting down.

28:01

Even in secure attachment, attuned moments are under half

Steele gives a counterintuitive number: even for securely attached mothers and children, genuinely "attuned" moments are under 50%. The child learns precisely from the mismatches — mum didn't catch it, so the child finds a way to pull her back. In Tronick's still face experiment, the infant reaches out, smiles, calls to bring mum back. But with too many mismatches, the child gives up and turns to the defensive modes of avoidance or ambivalence. So perfection isn't the goal; repair is.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
30:01

Co-sleeping until seven won't buy you secure attachment

Steele names the "recipe-style" practices in attachment parenting directly — co-sleeping until five or seven, for instance — saying they have no one-to-one correspondence with secure attachment, and no such requirement exists in the original literature. What secure attachment actually means is following the child's lead, making the child feel you hold them in mind, and responding promptly especially when they are upset. She also criticises "pre-emptive gratification": rushing to feed a child before it has noticed it is hungry erases the child's agency to read its own state and find its own solution.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
39:18

A child's attachment type can be predicted before birth

The London Parent-Child Project ran the Adult Attachment Interview with 100 couples in late pregnancy, then the Strange Situation with the mother at 12 months and with the father at 18 months. Result: from how parents talked about their own childhood before the baby was born alone, the child's attachment type could be predicted at 12 months (for mothers) and 18 months (for fathers). Steele showed the results to Bowlby, and Bowlby said they couldn't even predict the weather, yet you can predict something this subtle.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
40:18

What breaks the cycle isn't being unharmed, it's being able to tell it clearly

The study grouped the sample by childhood adversity (parent hospitalisation, unemployment over three months, low socioeconomic status, and so on). The key finding: parents who had experienced adversity but could narrate their childhood in a way that references mental states — understanding what was going on in their parents' minds back then — had a significantly higher rate of securely attached children; among parents who had experienced adversity without that capacity, the vast majority of children were insecurely attached. Steele sums up secure attachment in two words, flexibility and coherence: does your story have a beginning, a middle and an end, and does it hold up to questioning.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
45:27

To be the one who breaks the cycle, learn to observe yourself first

Steele's concrete advice: get familiar with the basic concepts of attachment, learn to observe yourself, and especially watch your own triggers — work out what makes you so sad, so angry at this moment that should be lovely. She doesn't think yelling at a child is a good approach, but short of cases that constitute abuse, stepping away for thirty seconds and saying "Mummy is very angry right now, I need a moment" is itself modelling for the child that emotions have strategies for being handled. She also suggests finding a clinician to see your own patterns clearly with, because people are pattern-generating creatures, and the attachment system is essentially affect regulation.

Glossary

Strange Situation
A roughly 20-minute laboratory procedure designed by Mary Ainsworth that observes infant attachment types through brief separation and reunion.
Adult Attachment Interview
An interview developed by Mary Main that assesses a parent's attachment representations through the way they narrate their childhood experiences.
reflective functioning
The capacity to understand the mental states (thoughts, feelings, intentions) behind one's own and others' behaviour; it is trainable.
good enough mother
Donald Winnicott's concept: responding sensitively most of the time is enough; perfection is not required.
still face
Designed by Tronick: the mother suddenly goes blank-faced, and the infant's attempts to bring the interaction back are observed.
