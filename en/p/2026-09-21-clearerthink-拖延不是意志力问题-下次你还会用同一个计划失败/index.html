Procrastination isn't a willpower problem: you'll fail again next time with the same plan

Procrastination is delaying despite knowing it hurts you, and about one in five adults does it chronically. Anxiety, distraction, burnout and perfectionism each need a different fix, but they share one premise: change nothing about the plan and nothing about the result changes.

The definition of procrastination has reluctance baked into it

Itamar's definition is: we put off a task even though we don't intend to put it off, and we know it's bad for us. So "I deliberately decided to do it later and I feel fine about that" is not procrastination. The grey zone is: what if you think delaying is good for you but it's actually harmful? His test is "could you have been expected to know it was better for you." About 20% of adults procrastinate chronically, and among students it's 50%. He stresses that procrastination doesn't only happen to people who don't care — people who are highly conscientious, genuinely productive, and sincerely want to make an impact procrastinate too.

3:27

Procrastination is several types, not one thing

Treating procrastination as a single phenomenon makes two mistakes at once: you fail to see that it happens to people other than students, and you can't deal with it in practice. Because a student who procrastinates from distraction, an executive who procrastinates from burnout, and an artist who procrastinates from perfectionism have different drivers and need different solutions. In his book Itamar distinguishes the anxious type, the pulled-away-by-external-temptation type, the burnout type, and a category he calls zigzaggers — closer to ADHD and impulsivity, switching back and forth between internal rather than external distractions.

— Itamar Shatz
5:00

Anxiety cuts both ways: fear of the task versus fear of the consequences

The mechanism by which anxiety causes procrastination is: doing this hurts, the brain says I don't want to hurt, so you push it away. But anxiety also has an adaptive side — if what you're anxious about isn't the task itself but the consequences of not finishing it, a little anxiety pushes you to act. The key variables are intensity and self-efficacy: a little anxiety may motivate you, a lot of anxiety paralyzes you no matter what it's aimed at; and if you also feel you're incapable of finishing, anxiety turns into paralysis more easily. Itamar adds one caveat: even when anxiety does push you, it's a dangerous source of motivation, because it also brings stress and a worse experience.

— Itamar Shatz
9:25

The biggest mistake is believing you'll try harder next time

Itamar says the biggest mistake people make when tackling procrastination is falling into the self-control fallacy: believing that next time, if you just try harder and summon more willpower, you'll get it done. The absurdity is that we watch it fail over and over, and over and over we believe again. The reasons include the bias blind spot (failing to recognize your own biases) and the empathy gap (predicting, in a calm state, what you'll do in a hot emotional state). The real harm of this belief is that it offers a superficially minimal silver bullet, which stops you from looking for what actually works. Spencer's addition: change nothing, and nothing changes.

— Itamar Shatz
12:45

Design for your future self as if they were someone else

Spencer's approach: don't assume that the you of two weeks from now will get it done on willpower; instead ask "what would help a person very much like me get this done in two weeks" — set reminders, make social commitments, add rewards. Itamar says this is exactly an illustration of his core claim: procrastination isn't a time management problem, and even excellent time management can fail; what you should do is distance management. Calling yourself by the second or third person, or using your name instead of "I", or asking "what advice would I give a friend", all create psychological distance and help you enter wise reasoning, seeing through the situation and sidestepping bias.

— Itamar Shatz
16:13

Anxious type: decatastrophize, or do it anxious

For the anxious procrastinator, Itamar's first choice is to decatastrophize: catastrophizing means overestimating both the probability that something bad happens and the harm it does to you. For example, "there's a typo in my proposal, so the agent will blacklist me, and tell everyone else to blacklist me too." The move is to ask: how likely is this to happen? If it did happen, how bad would the impact be? If it happened, how would I carry on? Spencer offers an almost opposite strategy: notice that this task makes me anxious, sit inside that feeling, feel it, and then do it anyway — he calls it metacognitive awareness. Itamar files it under the umbrella of mindfulness and self-compassion.

— Itamar Shatz
21:39

Procrastination is short-term emotional repair, and it reinforces itself

Spencer points out that anxiety creates anxiety: the moment you say "I'm not doing it now", the anxiety usually drops immediately, and you're learning "procrastinating makes me feel better", forming a reinforcement loop. Doing the opposite can unwind it — doing it while anxious functions like an exposure. Itamar says procrastination here is a defense mechanism: because you feel pressure or anxiety about what you should be doing, you push it away to avoid the painful emotion. It makes you feel better in the short term and worse in the long term, because you haven't solved the problem — you've only arranged to face it again and again later, or to face worse consequences.

— Itamar Shatz
26:39

The burnout fix is to do less

Itamar admits this is the last thing burned-out people want to hear: if you're burned out because you're doing too much, or doing things that drain you, you probably need to do less and take more deliberate care of yourself — concretely, deciding more deliberately what to do and, more importantly, what not to do. He describes a loop: you're used to performing well, burnout makes your performance drop, so you work even harder, then burn out more, and it gets worse and worse. He calls this "borrowing health and productivity from your future self." He also notes that burnout doesn't come only from hours: reasonable hours with high-pressure, meaningless, or otherwise draining tasks will burn you out just the same.

— Itamar Shatz
39:30

The correlation between perfectionism and procrastination is near zero

Itamar says this correlation is more contested in the literature than intuition would suggest, because perfectionism isn't a single dimension: some of it is expecting a lot of yourself, some is feeling others expect a lot of you, some is genuinely wanting to do well, some is fearing mistakes. The fear-of-mistakes kind correlates more strongly with procrastination, while "wanting to do well" actually pushes you to finish on time. Looked at separately, the different types cancel each other out, and combined the correlation is near zero. But he stresses the number carries little information: if you're stuck in the maladaptive side of perfectionism, other people's positive perfectionism won't help you. He also points to a hidden form: revising over and over and never handing it in is also procrastination.

— Itamar Shatz
46:17

Two big theories: temporal motivation and emotion regulation

Itamar lays out the two dominant theories of procrastination today. One is Piers Steel's Procrastination Equation, based on temporal motivation theory, coming from behavioral economics: the further away an outcome is, the more we discount it, whether it's the reward for acting or the consequences of delaying; hyperbolic discounting means the biggest drop-off is between "now" and "the future", while a year from now and a year and a day from now are nearly identical. The other is emotion regulation theory, also called short-term mood repair theory, coming from psychology: it's about "I want to feel good right now", and it uses temporal disjunction to describe the gulf between the present self and the future self. The two reach similar conclusions by different routes.

— Itamar Shatz
51:50

Both theories are right, and both are incomplete

Itamar says he believes both, and the newest theories try to merge them into a single framework, such as the temporal decision model, which tries to put temporal motivation theory and emotion regulation theory into the same equation: discounting of future outcomes, self-efficacy, and the degree of delay all matter, and so do emotion and the desire to feel good right now. He quotes George Box to the effect that models are never perfect, but current theory already explains a substantial share of behavior, and merging them fills in a lot of gaps. He also flags an unsolved problem: we keep treating procrastination as a single construct, and once you compress out the richness, effects tend to cancel out or get masked.

— Itamar Shatz

Glossary

self-control fallacy
The belief that next time you'll get it done if you just try harder and summon more willpower, which stops you from looking for what actually works.
decatastrophize
Asking how likely the bad thing is, how bad the impact would be, and how you'd cope afterward, to deflate an overestimated fear.
zigzagger
Itamar's term for a type of procrastination driven by ADHD and impulsivity, switching constantly among internal distractions.
temporal motivation theory
Explains procrastination from behavioral economics: the further away an outcome, the more it's discounted, with the biggest drop-off between now and the future.
temptation bundling
Pairing something you want to do with something you enjoy and that takes no mental effort, to lower the task's aversiveness.
