Procrastination Is Not a Willpower Problem: You'll Fail With the Same Plan Next Time

Procrastination is delaying despite knowing it's harmful, and about 20% of adults do it chronically. Anxiety, distraction, burnout and perfectionism each need different fixes, but they share one premise: without changing the plan, the result won't change.

The definition of procrastination hides reluctance

Itamar's definition is: we delay something even though we don't intend to delay it, and we know it's bad for us. So 'deliberately deciding to do it later and feeling fine about it' is not procrastination. The gray area: what if you think delaying is good for you but it's actually harmful? His test is 'could you have expected this to be better for you.' About 20% of adults procrastinate chronically, and among students it's 50%. He stresses that procrastination doesn't only happen to people who don't care; people high in conscientiousness, genuinely productive, who really want to make an impact, procrastinate too.

— Itamar Shatz
3:27

Procrastination is many types, not one thing

Treating procrastination as a single phenomenon makes two mistakes at once: you fail to see it happening to people outside student populations, and you can't address it in practice. Because a student who procrastinates from distraction, an executive who procrastinates from burnout, and an artist who procrastinates from perfectionism have different drivers and need different solutions. In his book Itamar distinguishes anxiety-driven, externally tempted, and burnout-driven types, plus one he calls the zigzagger — closer to ADHD and impulsivity, switching back and forth between internal rather than external distractions.

— Itamar Shatz
5:00

Anxiety has two faces: fear of the task or fear of the consequences

The mechanism by which anxiety causes procrastination is: doing this hurts, the brain says I don't want to hurt, so you put it off. But anxiety also has an adaptive side — if what you're anxious about isn't the task itself but the consequences of not finishing it, a little anxiety actually pushes you to act. The key variables are intensity and self-efficacy: a little anxiety may motivate you, a lot of anxiety paralyzes you no matter what it's aimed at; and if you also feel you're incapable of finishing, anxiety more easily turns into paralysis. Itamar adds: even when anxiety drives you, it's a dangerous source of motivation, because it brings stress and a worse experience along with it.

— Itamar Shatz
9:25

The biggest mistake is believing you'll try harder next time

Itamar says the biggest mistake people make when tackling procrastination is falling into the self-control fallacy: believing that next time, if you just try harder and use more willpower, you'll do it. The absurdity is that we see it fail again and again, yet believe it again and again. Reasons include the bias blind spot (failing to recognize your own biases) and the empathy gap (predicting, in a calm state, what you'll do when you're in a hot emotional state). The real harm of this belief is that it offers a superficially minimal silver bullet, which stops you from looking for what actually works. Spencer's addition: if nothing changes, the result won't change.

— Itamar Shatz
12:45

Design for your future self as if they were someone else

Spencer's approach: don't assume that the you of two weeks from now will get it done on willpower; instead ask 'for someone a lot like me, what would help them get this done in two weeks' — set reminders, make social commitments, add rewards. Itamar says this is exactly an illustration of his core claim: procrastination is not a time management problem, even excellent time management can fail, what you should do is distance management. Calling yourself in the second or third person, or using your name instead of 'I', or asking 'what advice would I give a friend', all create psychological distance and help you enter wise reasoning, see through the situation and bypass biases.

— Itamar Shatz
16:13

Anxiety type: decatastrophize, or do it anxious

For anxiety-driven procrastinators, Itamar's first choice is to decatastrophize: catastrophizing is overestimating the probability that something bad will happen and also overestimating how much it will hurt you. For example, 'there's a typo in my proposal, so the agent will blacklist me, and tell everyone to blacklist me too.' The method is to ask: how likely is this to happen? If it did happen, how big would the impact be? If it happened, how could I move on? Spencer offers an almost opposite strategy: notice that this task makes me anxious, sit in that feeling, feel it, and then do it anyway, which he calls metacognitive awareness. Itamar places it under the umbrella of mindfulness and self-compassion.

— Itamar Shatz
21:39

Procrastination is short-term emotional repair, and it self-reinforces

Spencer points out that anxiety creates anxiety: once you say 'I won't do it now,' the anxiety usually drops immediately, and you're learning that 'procrastinating makes me feel better,' forming a reinforcement loop. Doing the opposite can undo it — doing it while anxious is effectively an exposure. Itamar says procrastination here is a defense mechanism: because you feel pressure or anxiety about what you should do, you push it away to avoid the painful emotion. It makes you feel better in the short term and worse in the long term, because you haven't solved the problem, you've just made yourself face it repeatedly later, or face worse consequences.

— Itamar Shatz
26:39

The burnout fix is to do less

Itamar admits this is the last thing burned-out people want to hear: if you're burned out because you're doing too much, doing things that drain you, you probably need to do less and take care of yourself more consciously — specifically, more deliberately deciding what to do and, more importantly, what not to do. He describes a loop: you're used to performing well, burnout makes your performance worse, so you work harder, then you burn out more, and it gets worse and worse. He calls this 'borrowing health and productivity from your future self.' He also notes burnout doesn't only come from hours: reasonable hours but high-pressure, meaningless, or otherwise draining tasks can also burn you out.

— Itamar Shatz
39:30

Perfectionism's correlation with procrastination is near zero

Itamar says this correlation is more contested in the literature than intuition would suggest, because perfectionism isn't a single dimension: some of it is expecting a lot of yourself, some is feeling others expect a lot of you, some is genuinely wanting to do well, some is fearing mistakes. The fear-of-mistakes kind correlates more strongly with procrastination, while 'wanting to do well' actually pushes you to finish on time. Looked at separately, the correlations of different types cancel each other out, and combined they're near zero. But he stresses the number carries little information: if you're stuck in the maladaptive side of perfectionism, other people's positive perfectionism won't help you. He also points to a hidden form: repeatedly revising and delaying handing it in is also procrastination.

— Itamar Shatz
46:17

Two big theories: temporal motivation and emotion regulation

Itamar introduces the two current major theories of procrastination. One is Piers Steel's Procrastination Equation, based on temporal motivation theory, from a behavioral economics angle: the further away an outcome is, the more we discount it, whether it's the reward for acting or the consequences of delaying; hyperbolic discounting means the biggest drop is between 'now' and 'the future,' while a year from now and a year and a day from now are almost identical. The other is emotion regulation theory, also called short-term mood repair theory, from a psychology angle, about 'I want to feel good right now,' using temporal disjunction to describe the gap between the present self and the future self. The two reach similar conclusions by different paths.

— Itamar Shatz
51:50

Both theories are right, but neither is complete

Itamar says he believes both, and the newest theories try to merge them into a single framework, such as the temporal decision model, which attempts to plug temporal motivation theory and emotion regulation theory into the same equation: discounting of future outcomes, self-efficacy, and delay all matter, and emotion and the desire to 'feel good right now' matter just as much. He quotes George Box to say models are never perfect, but current theories already explain a substantial portion of behavior, and merging them fills in quite a few gaps. He also points to an unsolved problem: we keep treating procrastination as a single construct, and once you compress out the richness, effects often cancel each other out or get masked.

— Itamar Shatz

Glossary

self-control fallacy
The belief that next time, if you just try harder and use more willpower, you'll do it, which stops you from looking for what actually works.
decatastrophize
Asking about the probability of something bad happening, how bad it would be, and how you'd cope afterward, to deflate an overestimated fear.
zigzagger
Itamar's term for a type of procrastination driven by ADHD and impulsivity, constantly switching between internal distractions.
temporal motivation theory
Explains procrastination from behavioral economics: the further away an outcome, the more it's discounted, with the biggest drop between now and the future.
temptation bundling
Pairing something you want to do with something you enjoy and that takes little effort, to reduce the aversiveness of the task itself.
