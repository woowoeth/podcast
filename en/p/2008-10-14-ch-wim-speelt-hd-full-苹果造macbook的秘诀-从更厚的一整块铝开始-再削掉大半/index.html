Carving thick aluminum down makes lighter MacBooks

Apple discovered that machining a single component from thick aluminum is lighter and stronger than assembling multiple thin pieces; the technique later enabled the entry MacBook to drop its price by $700.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:00

Mac growth came from six compounding factors, not a single hit

Tim Cook attributed Mac's consecutive quarters of growth at two to three times the market's pace to six factors: superior hardware and software (Leopard versus Vista), Boot Camp compatibility that eliminated switching anxiety, Vista's own failure opening doors for Apple, the Mac versus PC advertising campaign, and nearly 250 stores where half of customers were buying a Mac for the first time. The result: Mac captured over 31% of U.S. retail computer revenue—one dollar of every three spent on a computer went to Macintosh.

— Tim Cook
12:18

MacBook Pro's strength came from internal structure, not the shell

Johnny Ive took apart the 15-inch MacBook Pro then in production and showed that the visible aluminum shell contributed little to structural rigidity. Real load-bearing came from an internal magnesium die-cast frame assembled from multiple parts, plus a series of reinforcement plates and internal structural frames welded underneath the palmrest. This multi-part assembly architecture was exactly what the unibody design would replace entirely.

— Johnny Ive
14:21

To build lighter, Apple started with thicker aluminum

Apple broke from the old pattern—starting with thin aluminum sheet and bolting on reinforcement—by reversing it: begin with a solid aluminum block over two and a half pounds, machine away material bit by bit with CNC, and end with a single piece weighing just a quarter pound yet extraordinarily rigid. That one part carried the entire MacBook Air's structure. This process later extended to unibody shells on the new generation of notebooks.

— Johnny Ive
17:28

NVIDIA's desktop graphics chip moved to notebooks with Apple

NVIDIA approached Apple wanting to put a chipset and powerful GPU on a desktop-focused chip, but Apple asked them to adapt it for notebooks. After months of collaboration, they produced the GeForce 9400M: 70% of the die was GPU, 16 parallel graphics cores, 54 gigaflops of performance. In testing it beat the previous integrated graphics by as much as 5x; in real game scenarios, 3x to 6x faster.

— Steve Jobs
38:19

Entry-level MacBook got Pro features and lost $700 in price

Apple research found that MacBook users wanted three things—metal chassis, faster graphics, LED backlit screen—all features the MacBook Pro already had. So the new MacBook got the unibody aluminum body, GeForce 9400M graphics, and LED screen, but the starting price dropped from what you'd have to pay for Pro to get these features to $1,299—$700 lower, a 35% cut—aiming straight at the market's sweet spot.

— Steve Jobs
45:42

Unibody aluminum: extrude like pasta, then mill thirteen times

The promotional video compared unibody fabrication to making pasta: start with a solid aluminum block through an extrusion process like pressing noodles, then run it through 13 separate milling operations to transform solid metal into a finished part with all assembly features. The narration emphasized that even this consumer-level MacBook's internal precision was more meticulous than its exterior suggested—"more beautiful inside than outside" being the real mark of craftsmanship.

50:00

Environmental goals required supply chain redesign, not just label compliance

Apple broke environmental goals into each stage: remove arsenic from glass, mercury from displays, BFR and PVC from internal cabling, targeting EPEAT Gold certification. More specifically in packaging: shrink the box to fit pallets more densely, use less airplane cargo space, burn less fuel—directly cutting carbon footprint. MacBook Pro packaging shrank 37%, MacBook packaging 42%.

Glossary

unibody
A single structural component machined from one block of metal, replacing assemblies of multiple bolted parts.
EPEAT Gold
The U.S. EPA's highest environmental rating for electronics, requiring strict standards on materials and manufacturing.
