Modern scholars reject Weber's thesis linking Protestant ethics to capitalism

Max Weber argued capitalism's spirit stemmed from Calvinist predestination theology, but modern economic historians largely reject this. Instead, Dutch culture—freed from feudal constraints and prizing innovation—likely fostered both Calvinist theology and a flexible specialized economy in parallel, not as cause and effect. Yet Protestantism did aid capitalism in other ways: eliminating Catholic holidays increased working days; emphasis on education raised literacy rates; after independence, the Dutch Republic built 5 universities at once.

Dutch merchants were spendthrifts, not the ascetics Weber imagined

Contemporary foreign visitors claimed the Dutch were so thrifty they reused eggshells; Weber's theory presumed Calvinist asceticism drove capital accumulation. Historian Simon Schama contradicted this in 'The Embarrassment of Riches', offering abundant counterexamples: luxury furniture, expensive Rembrandt paintings, even a lottery's fourth-place prize of 1000 in cash plus a silver bread basket. Schama concluded that Dutch capital possessed an almost universal immunity to disasters afflicting the rest of the world—merchants never hesitated to enjoy its fruits.

— Peter Adamson
9:17

Dutch cleanliness came from cheese production, not Protestant doctrine

Schama accepted another of Weber's claims: Protestant culture and cleanliness arose together, domestic labor compared to spiritual purification. But research on Dutch cleaning habits found the practice predated the Reformation—half of farm families produced butter or cheese, and fines for unclean dairy production existed from the 15th century onward. Cleanliness was near-sacred, but driven by dairy economics, not faith.

— Peter Adamson
11:17

The Dutch state operated as a company managing bourgeois affairs

A study of Dutch capitalism directly quoted Marx's Communist Manifesto: ‘a modern government is merely a committee managing the common affairs of the entire bourgeoisie’—applied perfectly to the Dutch Republic. One need not wait for Marx: after the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War erupted in 1781, a political pamphlet already compared the entire nation to an enterprise: ‘the people formed one great society, and rulers, magistrates, officials, and princes were merely this company's or society's directors, managers, and stewards’.

— Peter Adamson
13:18

De la Court brothers argued: tax landowners, not merchants, because merchants can flee

Opposing the mercantilism then popular in France and England—treating trade as zero-sum, emphasizing exports over imports—Dutch thought favored free trade, naturally advantageous to an already-dominant economy. Brothers Jan and Pieter de la Court, from a Leiden merchant family, advocated abolishing guilds, eliminating manufacturing profit taxes, and taxing landowners and consumers instead; their reasoning was that merchants could relocate their business to lower-tax cities, while landowners and consumers could not move. They called this democracy—but defined ‘people’ to include only property owners, excluding the poor, women, and children.

— Peter Adamson
18:21

The VOC mirrored the Dutch Republic in structure and governance

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was history's first joint-stock company founded on large-scale initial investment, with a 17-member board drawn from various cities—nearly half from Amsterdam—mirroring the Dutch Republic's federal political architecture: each regional chamber retained independent capital and operations while following the federal board's overall directives. Its Atlantic sister company, the WIC, was far less successful, defeated in Brazil by Spain, Portugal, and England; the VOC built a vast trading empire in Persia, India, Ceylon, Siam, Taiwan, Japan, and Indonesia.

— Peter Adamson
20:21

The Dutch abolished slavery at home but maintained it in colonies until 1863

The Dutch homeland prohibited slavery, taking the free market's ‘freedom’ literally, but colonies operated under entirely different rules: 1770s Surinam was roughly 90 percent enslaved; the VOC committed massacres of entire indigenous populations in the Banda Islands. The Netherlands was the last major European colonial power to abolish overseas slavery, not until 1863. Liberal thinkers like the de la Court brothers raised no objection—research shows colonies were treated as another world within Dutch discourse, where domestic standards of culture, liberty, and civilization did not apply.

— Peter Adamson
22:21

Grotius's VOC work laid the theoretical foundation for international law

The Dutch global trading empire raised a cascade of legal questions: on what basis could one claim occupied Indonesian land? What justified violence at sea? Could a private company like the VOC declare war on foreign powers? Hugo Grotius provided the most systematic answers—in a sense, VOC's resident legal theorist, though his influence far transcended that company. The concept of the ‘high seas’ itself originated from his writings.

Glossary

VOC
Dutch East India Company; history's first large-scale joint-stock company, holding semi-official monopoly rights and authority to wage war abroad.
WIC
Dutch West India Company; handled Atlantic colonial operations and slave trading, far less successful than the VOC.
mercantilism
Economic policy favoring exports over imports and treating trade as zero-sum competition.
amour propre
A concept from French moral philosophy, considered the source of all human virtue and vice.
