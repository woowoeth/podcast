China Cut 5 Million Barrels a Day of Imports. Why Didn't the Iran Oil Apocalypse Arrive?

The Iran war was once forecast to push oil to $200 a barrel, and the catastrophe never came — mainly not because of the United States, but because China quietly cut crude imports by 5 million barrels a day. That discretion can save the market, and it can also be turned into a geopolitical weapon.

First a 60% price spike, then a coordinated squeeze

Arnab warns against treating the conclusion as "nothing happened": early in the crisis oil rose nearly 60%, and physical delivery prices in some regions spiked. What actually pulled prices back from the brink was several interventions working at once — SPR releases, East-West Pipeline bypass flows, and China's import cuts. The "gamma-squeeze-style" spike the market feared most did not materialise, and the reason was not any single lever but several forces lining up together.

10:52

China's 5 mbd import disappearance was the biggest variable

Rory publicly called $200 a barrel when the crisis broke, on the logic that filling the Hormuz outage would require demand destruction on a scale never seen in human history (5-7 mbd), and that known SPR and pipeline capacity could not possibly cover it. Then China unilaterally cut crude imports by 5 mbd — equivalent to a large collective OPEC production cut — and closed two-thirds of the shortfall in Asian spot markets. More critically, no domestic liquidity indicator in China matched that contraction — no mobility lockdown — which fits no historical pattern of demand destruction.

— Rory
13:04

The China demand mystery: refineries down 3 mbd, but inventories never hit bottom

China has no official inventory data, so Rory cross-checks refinery runs, customs data and satellite photos of floating-roof tanks: imports fell 5 mbd while refinery runs fell only 3 mbd, meaning roughly 2 mbd of crude is unaccounted for; visible commercial tank levels are still above March 1. He suspects what was actually drawn down is invisible underground strategic reserves and refined-product strategic stocks — China's official figure for underground cavern capacity is 131 million barrels, and the real number may be larger. On top of that, Beijing let domestic fuel prices rise only about 30%, which crushed refinery margins but preserved household mobility.

— Rory
23:48

This time the US SPR used an "exchange", not a release

Arnab unpacks the Energy Department's operation: this was not a traditional drawdown but an exchange — oil is lent to traders to put into the market now, and the traders promise to return the same volume of crude later plus a premium, with the premium being repaid from this November all the way to 2028. DOE also had the traders taking the oil bid against each other, so whoever promised the higher premium got the spot barrels. This mechanism is more creative than any SPR operation he has seen before, and it shows "the reserve is worth investing in" — but he states plainly that the biggest credit for oil not spiralling out of control belongs to China.

— Arnab
29:37

Four motives: altruism, a deal, self-preservation, or a drill

Rory concedes that "why" is more unsettling than "how", and lays out four hypotheses: first, altruistic maintenance of East Asian and European markets; second, a backroom deal struck when Trump visited Beijing; third, domestic economic priority — any repeat of Covid lockdown costs is unacceptable; fourth, treating the Hormuz outage as a Malacca-squeeze rehearsal, testing whether China's energy system can sustain itself if its sea lanes are cut. Jordan thinks the simplest explanation is the most credible: Beijing's governing priority is always to avoid domestic economic collapse. The four explanations do not converge, but they all point to the same fact — China holds the world's largest energy-policy discretion.

— Rory
50:13

America can't rely on shale alone: market leverage is too slow

Rory and Arnab's shared judgment: North America really is an energy superpower in the market sense, but that is private market power, not government discretion; Beijing's and OPEC's government discretion can "slam the wheel" in a crisis, while market mechanisms always need more time. They therefore recommend treating the SPR as a two-way tool — not only selling in shortages but also buying in gluts — and using policy incentives to raise the number of DUCs (drilled but uncompleted wells), compressing the start-up time for emergency response to about 4 weeks. Otherwise, when the next crisis comes, the US will again be able to count only on China "showing mercy".

— Rory
53:29

India and the Gulf are racing to build strategic reserves

Rory observes that countries without strategic reserves paid the highest price in this crisis, India most typically: one-fifth to one-quarter of its oil demand is LPG cooking fuel, which is more rigid than transport. The Indian government has confirmed with ADNOC a joint strategic reserve of about 50 million barrels. The Gulf states, meanwhile, are accelerating expansion of the East-West Pipeline, the Fujairah line and product pipelines, and increasing spending on drones and air defence. But pipelines bypassing Hormuz will always cost more than a free waterway; this kind of infrastructure is essentially expensive strategic insurance and will not generate quick commercial returns.

Glossary

SPR Exchange
Not a sale but a loan of reserve oil to traders, who promise to return it later with a premium.
DUCs
Shale wells already drilled but not yet hydraulically fractured, which can be brought online quickly as flexible capacity.
Apparent demand
Domestic demand inferred from refinery output plus or minus net trade volumes; it excludes inventory changes and contains a black box.
Malacca Dilemma
China's energy imports depend heavily on the Strait of Malacca, leaving it exposed to a supply cutoff if the strait is blockaded.
