Protestantism didn't banish medieval ghosts—it just swapped them for deadlier demons

Protestants dismissed ghosts as Catholic superstition, but they didn't deny the supernatural—they reinterpreted it, swapping debt-collecting spirits for far more sinister demons. James VI's *Daemonologie* of 1597 is the proof.

The oldest surviving text ever written is a ghost story

The earliest written work that survives is the Epic of Gilgamesh—which opens with Enkidu descending to the underworld and returning to describe the realm of the dead. Ghost stories didn't come later as narrative entertainment. They arrived with writing itself. The first thing humans did once they could write was record apparitions.

3:02

Ghosts existed to collect unpaid debts, never just to frighten

Medieval ghost stories follow a practical pattern: unresolved business. Someone died owing money, or was buried improperly, or a property dispute went unsettled. Pliny the Elder recorded the story of Athenodorus, a Stoic philosopher, who rented a famously haunted mansion cheaply. When a ghost appeared dragging chains, Athenodorus simply asked it to wait—he had a philosophy treatise to finish. The next morning, servants found skeletal remains still bound by those same chains. Properly buried, the ghost ceased to appear. The message was blunt: in crowded cities, the unburied dead spread plague. Ghosts were a practical problem to solve.

7:13

Medieval purgatory doctrine converted ghosts from terrors into spiritual creditors

The medieval doctrine of purgatory transformed everything. Death was no longer binary—heaven or hell. Instead there was purgatory, a temporary hell with a time limit. Ghost stories shifted accordingly. Now the dead returned not merely to demand proper burial or payment, but to ask the living to commission masses, to return stolen mills, to settle debts. These stories taught moral lessons, yes, but they also offered something precious: the living could still help the dead reduce their suffering in purgatory. Ghosts became a form of transaction between worlds.

— Eleanor Janega
12:29

Medieval ghost tales that served no moral purpose proved most enduring

Twelve ghost stories from Byland Abbey, recorded in the early 15th century and later edited by medieval scholar M.R. James, include many with clear morals—tales of stolen inheritances being recovered, or ill-gotten land returned. But the strangest were the purposeless ones. One woman, for example, claimed to have caught a ghost and carried it on her back into a room full of men, announcing ‘I've caught a ghost.’ No moral instruction. No theological point. Pure strangeness. These suggest ghost stories weren't always vehicles for moral teaching. Sometimes they survived simply because they were curious enough to record.

— Eleanor Janega
15:59

Even emperors admitted to supernatural terror without attempting moral lessons

Charles IV, the Holy Roman Emperor, wrote what was ostensibly a political autobiography. Yet he inserted a private aside: one night in his Prague residence, he heard footsteps. Cups flew across the room and shattered against walls. He and his attendants prayed. The next day, servants found a cup genuinely broken. The story carries no moral instruction, teaches no theological lesson. It exists only to record that even at the pinnacle of power, one admits to fear. Because such stories serve no political narrative, few survive to reach us.

— Eleanor Janega
20:00

Protestantism reinterpreted ghosts as demons—making them far more threatening

During the Reformation, Protestant critics attacked ghost stories as Catholic superstition—a scam by priests to extract donations through manufactured terror. But Protestants didn't deny the supernatural itself. They reinterpreted it. James VI, who would become James I of England, published *Daemonologie* in 1597, arguing that hauntings near graveyards weren't restless spirits—they were demons drawn to corpses. A ghost might remind you to pay a debt; a demon posed genuine danger. The Protestant solution wasn't skepticism. It was theological horror more alarming than anything the Catholics had proposed.

— Eleanor Janega
22:03

Enlightenment scientists hunted ghosts through empirical methods, not skeptical dismissal

The scientific revolution didn't eliminate belief in ghosts. It formalized the hunt for proof. Researchers collected accounts from across Europe, conducted stakeouts at supposedly haunted locations, treated the supernatural as a natural phenomenon awaiting discovery. The logic was straightforward: we discovered oxygen; why couldn't we discover ghosts? John Locke warned against letting servants frighten children with ghost stories, dismissing them as old-woman's superstition. But he also argued that adults should revisit the question using reason. The boundary between science and superstition was drawn precisely through repeated investigation: is this a ghost, or isn't it?

— Eleanor Janega
25:11

Mass death and grief made spiritualist fraud irresistible to the bereaved

The Victorian era romanticized a vanished rural past, before industrialization. Ghosts became proof that the human soul transcended machinery and material progress. Then came World War I and the influenza pandemic, which destroyed millions. Desperate to reach the dead, grief-stricken masses flooded spiritualist séances—and the coffers of fraudulent mediums. The magician Houdini turned amateur ghost-buster, traveling to expose séance charlatans and their illusions. The post-war hunger to contact the dead proved more powerful than skepticism could resist.
