Mac Icons Aren't About Pixels, They're About Metaphor

Draw less, and users can put themselves into the icon. The 16×16 black-and-white constraint isn't a design flaw—it's the constraint that forces good ideas.

1:05

The hard part of design is the idea; form follows on its own

The opening gives the methodology: Paul Rand told Susan Kare that design should be 'meaningful and memorable,' and the key to achieving that is to have a good idea first—form follows naturally. Susan says she didn't come from a computer background; before Apple, she was in Arkansas welding a life-size wild boar sculpture. Because she had no baggage about pixels or graphics, she treated icons as a communication problem. She stresses that Paul Rand's words still ring true in today's AI-product context.

— Susan Kare
7:28

Constraints aren't the enemy of creativity; they're its precondition

The original Macintosh icon spec was only 16×16 pixels, black and white. Susan Kare's attitude: constraints are not the opposite of creativity—first recognize the constraint, then get excited about it, rather than complain about not having a thousand colors. To keep text from looking jagged in such a small grid, she used horizontal, vertical, and 45-degree diagonal lines to regularize the letterforms. The fewer pixels, the more you're forced to reduce an object to just the bit that makes it recognizable.

— Susan Kare
9:35

Proportional letters made Mac text far better-looking

At the time, text on many devices was monospaced: M was squeezed, I was stretched. The Mac was one of the few machines that could do proportional fonts, where each letter takes width according to its shape, so she immediately saw that 'this looks much better.' She designed Chicago at 9 pixels high for the system, and New York and Geneva along the lines of Times and Helvetica. The original names were after small towns near Philadelphia where she and Andy grew up; Steve Jobs saw them and said: city names aren't bad, but at least use world-class cities.

— Susan Kare
17:04

The more detailed the icon, the less users can enter it

She uses Scott McCloud's comic theory to explain why icons can't be fully drawn: a face with more detail looks like a specific person; drawn minimally, each user can project their own expression onto it. Same with a pencil: a simple pencil can represent 'writing,' while a metallic pencil with highlights is a specific model that not everyone uses. She believes an icon needs only one or two salient features, like the two children on a school sign not needing plaid lunchboxes or shoelaces.

— Susan Kare
22:11

Drawing the actual product into an icon guarantees obsolescence

When making the ImageWriter printer icon, she drew real details like perforated paper because that's what the printer actually looked like; but in hindsight she thinks 'too much detail' was a mistake—a simpler version would have let the symbol live longer. The 3.5-inch floppy disk as the 'save' icon is the same lesson: even a universal product becomes obsolete. Her alternative is to use metaphor and draw only enough detail to be understood.

— Susan Kare
31:52

Virtual gifts sell on cuteness, not price

She did one-dollar virtual gifts for Facebook: a new gift went on sale every midnight, and within about 15 minutes you could tell from sales whether it would be a hit. Susan initially thought drawing expensive items like diamond earrings or a Rolex would make spending $1 feel worthwhile, but the real pattern was that cute beats everything: hearts, teddy bears, and kiss marks were the best sellers, and limited-edition penguins sold out every time. Buying a joke or an adorable moment is more immediate than the fantasy of an expensive object.

— Susan Kare
38:24

Good designers don't pitch; they quote one price

Steve Jobs initially wanted a competition for the NeXT logo: invite five firms or five people, pay each $15,000, and continue with the winner. But Paul Rand said: you'll pay me $100,000, I'll make just one logo, and you'll like it. And that's exactly what happened. Rand opposed spending a lot of money to turn an unrelated random symbol into a brand; he preferred to work on the brand name itself—like putting NeXT in a box, and giving UPS a logo his daughter immediately said was 'a gift.'

— Susan Kare
48:58

Bringing only one option to the boss forces a no

Andy Hertzfeld taught her how to show options to Steve Jobs: don't bring just one thing, because with only one option he can only say he doesn't like it, which might push you to do better; bring several options so he can engage, point out that one is bad, and still pick one. She treats this as a general method for showing creative work to decision-makers—leave room for judgment, so a single piece of feedback doesn't become a total rejection.

— Susan Kare

Glossary

iconographer
Susan's self-described job title; treating 'drawing icons' as a craft of communication.
bitmap graphics
Images stored as a grid of pixels; early Mac icons were 16×16 bitmaps.
proportionally spaced fonts
Characters take different widths according to their shape, rather than each being the same width.
cap height
In type metrics, the distance from the baseline to the top of capital letters.
Letraset
A dry-transfer lettering tool used to stick letters onto paper before computer typesetting.
desktop metaphor
The metaphorical system that uses desktop objects like files, folders, and trash can to convey computer operations.
