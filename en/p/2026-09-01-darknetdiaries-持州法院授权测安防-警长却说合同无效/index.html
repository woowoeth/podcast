A State Court Authorized the Security Test. The Sheriff Said the Contract Was Void.

A state court's authorization letter carries no weight over a county courthouse building, so a contracted security test becomes burglary. Even after every charge is dropped, the arrest record stays on the books forever — the justice system can pay money, but it cannot restore a clean record.

A verbal agreement is no authorization at all

The rules document was only 28 pages, which makes it easy to assume the written contract had locked everything down. But Gary says the truly detailed agreements were almost all made verbally over the phone; if it had all been written down, the contract would have run a hundred pages and nobody could have signed it. That compromise planted the mine directly: when they later tested the Dallas County Courthouse, the verbally agreed scope did not cover that building — so procedurally they walked into a building that was "not authorized." The sheriff's later claim of "no authorization" had its roots not in technology but in a contract that could not cover every spoken conversation.

35:18

The building's owner outranks the authorization letter

Before the sheriff arrived they were still confident, because the contract said in black and white that the state court had authorized it. But the sheriff's only response was: they don't own this courthouse, and I don't care whether you have a contract. Only then did Gary and Justin understand that a building's right of use and its contractual rights are separate; however formal a contract signed by the state court, it cannot make door decisions on behalf of the county court that owns the building. The mood flipped from relaxed to arrest on the spot, and they had not even been worried before, because no tester in the industry had ever been prosecuted. This time was different.

The judge heard the truth as a lie

The judge told Gary, ‘You must think I'm stupid’ — her reasoning being: if the state government had authorized this test, she, as a state employee, should have known; she didn't know, so it could only be a lie. Gary later said this judge faced criminals' lies all year long and could no longer tell what the truth sounded like. A reasonable but dangerous logic: in a courtroom full of lies, an alibi-style contract looks even more fake. Professional reports and legal process all failed in this scenario.

— Gary
1:03:39

Dropping the charges does not erase the record

On January 30, 2020, all charges were dropped, but both men's criminal records will forever say they were arrested on burglary charges. Justin says they were deprived of their rights without due process — in many US background checks, an arrest record is just as conspicuous as a conviction. A dismissal only means no prosecution; it cannot erase the collateral consequences of that arrest: job hunting, passing customs, clearing security screening — this stretch follows them. The clean record they won by fighting the criminal case was really only half won.

— Justin
1:17:45

The government refuses to own its mistakes through immunity

Holding anyone accountable was far harder than they imagined. They wanted to sue the sheriff, the prosecutor and the judge, but the system put up two walls: qualified immunity protects individuals acting in their official capacity, and sovereign immunity says you cannot sue the government without its consent. The state court dismissed first — because the state government was the defendant; the federal court kicked the case back to the state, and the state dismissed it again. This is not a judge deciding a case; it is a system with no entry point for "the government was wrong." The only thing victims can sue is the office, not the person sitting in it; the sheriff personally does not have to pay a cent. Judicial checks and balances here became an idle adjective.

— JACK
1:31:56

You have to win the judicial lottery to get a little dignity back

Almost every wrongful arrest case dies in the same place: over 95% are dismissed on qualified immunity, and most of the rest end in zero damages or a government win. To get more than fifty thousand dollars in compensation, the odds are like winning the lottery, unless you were wrongly locked up for weeks or even months. Gary and Justin were held only one night, yet their evidence was airtight, and they eventually got a rare public statement at the arbitration: it was not a question of whether they would win, but of how much they could get. Near trial, the county offered three hundred thousand dollars each, six hundred thousand total, to settle. But for them, that money cannot buy back the half a life already written into the record.

Glossary

qualified immunity
Protects government workers from being sued over acts performed in their official role, unless those acts clearly violate the Constitution or the law.
sovereign immunity
The legal principle that a private party cannot sue the government without the government's own consent.
