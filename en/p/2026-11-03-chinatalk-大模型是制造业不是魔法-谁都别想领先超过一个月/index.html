Large models are manufacturing, not magic: no lead lasts more than a month

AI skills follow a seesaw: better coding, weaker prose

Xian Yu stopped using AI for writing in mid-2025—he finds it more satisfying to write himself, and the model simply can't translate original thought into personal voice. Language models can replace programmers but can't capture how a person thinks. The reason is a tradeoff in training: vendors pour resources into agentic programming datasets at the expense of writing datasets—‘it's like a whack-a-mole game’. By September 2026, coding ability kept improving while creative writing and the ability to express human thought fell, and noticeably so.

— Xian Yu
14:38

College students paid $10 to find the real ranking

In the past six months, ByteDance's Seedance series dominated video models globally. Xian Yu and his partner wanted a more interesting way to evaluate models than tech benchmarks, so they built wild prompts from memes popular on Bilibili and TikTok, had different models generate videos, then hired college students (about 300 of them) to rate the results for 10 yuan each. When H3, which MiniMax had just open-sourced, outranked Seedance 2.0 and 2.5 in their meme test—contradicting the narrative that ‘Seedance is comprehensively dominant’—Xian Yu seriously considered using technical methods to suppress H3's ranking but ultimately didn't. He thinks H3 won because it was optimized for tasks ordinary users care about (image-to-video, effects), while Seedance targets professional film studios.

23:53

When competitors complain, read it as insecurity

In July, Xian Yu published an article saying MiniMax's base model M3 had fallen behind competitors like Qwen and GLM because it hadn't been updated in four or five months. MiniMax, sensitive to ‘lagging behind’ getting out (it's public, stock price matters), filed a complaint. Xian Yu rewrote the piece as satire: ‘MiniMax is advancing so fast, we apologize to MiniMax’. Coincidentally, the day the apology went live, MiniMax's recruiter added his partner on WeChat asking about joining. This told Xian Yu the complaint was personal, not the company's official stance. Later MiniMax invited his partner to discuss technology—the relationship improved, and Xian Yu wore a MiniMax shirt to record this episode.

China's AI narrative cycles every three months, copying Silicon Valley

Chinese AI startups rotate through themes on a three-month cycle, entirely following Silicon Valley's rhythm: whatever the Valley embraces, someone in China builds a clone project to raise money. After Manus popularized the autonomous agent narrative in early 2025, waves of people entered AI applications, but most lacked real user insight—pure self-serving entrepreneurship. The ‘users build agents’narrative had a moment too, but those products mostly died because product building is far harder than TikTok clips; ordinary users can't participate. By early this year, the torch passed to ‘world models’—all because Li Feifei and others are working on them, so dollar-based VCs want domestic copy-cats.

— Xian Yu
36:08

Most ‘world models’projects are repackaged video models

Xian Yu thinks the world model wave is largely storytelling: you know if an AI app works in one or two months, and if it doesn't retain users, it dies. But world models take three years to validate, and demos don't really matter—people are buying the narrative and upside. Most domestic ‘world models’projects take Alibaba's open-source video model One from a year ago, fine-tune it into digital humans or real-time generation, and wrap it in grander language. Hype has already cooled in the past few months, but after MiniMax open-sourced H3, these storytelling projects came back alive—H3 digital humans look much better than One, so they can show new demos and keep pitching.

— Xian Yu
42:21

China's AI industry doesn't invent terms because it lacks confidence

There's an obvious pattern: Chinese AI barely invents terminology—the trendiest terms like RSI (recursive self-improvement) are all American. Company names are mostly English too (DeepSeek, MiniMax). It's not a technology problem, Xian Yu says; it's a confidence problem. The logic is ‘whatever Silicon Valley does, we build a replacement’, and funding sources matter: dollar-based LPs prefer the story of ‘cloning hot Valley trends, but cheaper and more efficient’. He points out that when investors see the same benchmark, they nearly always believe it if you tell them OpenAI did it over a Chinese company doing it. But he admits inventing terms isn't always good—the domestic model leap forward over the past few months might exist precisely because people were heads-down shipping, not debating grand concepts.

— Xian Yu
49:29

Large models follow manufacturing logic: no technical secrets survive

Xian Yu sees the large model business as fundamentally manufacturing, with no truly proprietary technical secrets. How to build datasets and scale is a small circle—model makers hire from each other constantly, so there are no secrets. Compute isn't a secret either; anyone can stack cards. Give a model more data and compute (production inputs) and you get a stronger model. This chain needs no breakthrough innovation, just engineering to push scaling laws. It's the same logic as solar panels, phones, and electric cars—you can't name fundamental technical differences between EV makers, yet market share still shifts. Competition is brutal: any model's lead rarely lasts two months, usually just one. China's GLM 5.3, Qwen 3.8 Max, and K3 all ship within a month, landing in the same ability band.

— Xian Yu
1:01:43

More money flowing in, crazier the people get

Xian Yu wraps up by noting that most people he knows are normal—just a bit unusual personality-wise. But AI is flooded with ‘so many liars, narcissists (NPD), and people doing who-knows-what’, and these people actually raise money and become industry stars. Half-joking, he suggests someone should study mental-health metrics in AI: rates of NPD or ADHD probably skew absurdly high. By contrast, ordinary AI app founders raise money at valuations under a few tens of millions of dollars; for model makers, Kimi's latest round values it at $5 billion. His conclusion: the industry has taken in so much hot money, yet the people are so odd—hot money and oddness are probably positively correlated.

— Xian Yu

Glossary

benchmark
A standardized test set used to compare the capabilities of different AI models side by side.
RSI (recursive self-improvement)
A proposed technical path where a model iteratively improves itself to achieve capability jumps.
scaling law
The principle that trading more data, compute, and parameters yields corresponding improvements in model capability.
product-market fit
The state where a product is genuinely needed by its target users and they keep choosing to use it.
