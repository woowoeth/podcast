49ers Injuries Blamed on a Substation? The Dose Is Off by 40x, and the Data Doesn't Fit Either

The conspiracy theory claiming a substation's electromagnetic waves caused the 49ers' injury plague gets debunked point by point by a radiation expert and an injury database: the dose is far below safety limits, the cited papers don't qualify, the 2014 expansion was a different station, and the data shows they aren't even the worst off.

9:00

The guy who wrote router firmware pinned the injuries on a power station

Bay Area resident Peter Cowan heard former 49ers player John Feliciano half-jokingly blame the injuries on the "power station" next to the practice field on the radio, and couldn't let the idea go. He had written Wi-Fi router firmware for wireless networking companies early in his career, and had also suspected electromagnetic waves after experiencing symptoms like panic attacks and insomnia himself. He measured readings of 8.5-9 milligauss on site with a gaussmeter, and even 50 milligauss near a feeder line, from which he derived a causal chain of "electromagnetic waves → oxidative stress → weakened collagen → tendon rupture," and posted it on X, setting the internet ablaze.

26:19

50 milligauss is still 40x away from danger

Ken Karipidis, an expert at Australia's radiation protection agency, explains that the extremely low frequency electromagnetic waves emitted by substations and household appliances induce weak currents in the body, but an environmental reading of 50 milligauss is far below the public limit of 2000 milligauss. The safety limit itself has a large margin; you'd need to reach above 50000 milligauss to possibly stimulate the retina into seeing "stars," and hundreds of thousands of milligauss to trigger nerve twitching. Ken says this dose level is nothing to worry about at all.

— Ken Karipidis
36:21

The two papers he cited don't even qualify as a hypothesis

Both human studies Peter cited don't hold up to scrutiny. The Indian "substation worker oxidative stress" study used job titles instead of actual exposure dose, and the abstract says 142 people while the table has only 130, with the sample tested for oxidative stress dropping further to 117 — the numbers don't match; the other compared power plant workers with hospital administrative staff and concluded depression and burnout were linked to electromagnetic fields, yet completely failed to rule out confounders like shift schedules and work stress. Ken's verdict: this kind of paper shouldn't even be used as a hypothesis.

— Ken Karipidis
45:00

Childhood leukemia: about 6 cases out of 13 million

The oft-cited online claim that "the WHO classifies electromagnetic waves as a possible carcinogen" is true, but that was the International Agency for Research on Cancer's classification from over twenty years ago. Scientists subsequently did a large amount of research worldwide and found the association between electromagnetic fields and childhood leukemia to be weak and unstable, estimated to explain 1-4% of cases; German researchers roughly estimated that out of 13 million children, only about 6 cases might be related. The research found no association between electromagnetic fields and adult leukemia or other cancers.

— Wendy Zuckerman
48:10

The 2014 expansion was the substation a mile away

The widely circulated claim that "injuries began exploding after the substation was expanded in 2014" contains a fundamental error. In Silicon Valley Power's documents, the production team found that what was expanded in 2014 was a substation called "Mission Substation," about a mile from the stadium, not the one immediately adjacent to Levi's Stadium and the practice field (Northern Receiving Station); nor is there any obvious inflection point in 2014 on the power curve chart in the presentation. Peter himself later admitted he had gotten one thing wrong.

53:58

The 49ers don't have the most injuries in the league

Zach Binney, who runs the FTN Fantasy injury database, checked NFL injury reports since 2007 and found: looking only at injury counts, the 49ers are just slightly above average, not the most; looking at games lost to injury, the Giants rank first over the past decade and the 49ers second. Only when you adjust the metric to count only games lost by starters or key players do the 49ers barely come out "worst," and even then not by much. This is far from the "far worse than the league" narrative.

— Zachary Binnie
57:09

More like three straight days of practice, plus bad luck

Even after adjusting the metric, there are still confounders hidden in the statistics: the team insisted on "three consecutive days of high-intensity practice" during 2025 training camp, and head coach Kyle Shanahan has now switched to two days; beyond that, there's pure randomness. Psychologists call it a "deep story" — people need a single target to blame for comfort, but the data doesn't support that narrative. With 32 teams, 16 are bound to be in the bottom half; injuries may just be fate striking at random.

An emotionally persuasive but not necessarily true collective narrative that helps people attribute complex phenomena to a single target.
