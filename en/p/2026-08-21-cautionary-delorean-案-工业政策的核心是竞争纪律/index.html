The DeLorean Case: Don't Bet Industrial Policy on a Single Company

DeLorean built its factory on roughly $100 million in UK government subsidies, then slid into an $18 million fraud and an FBI cocaine sting. The real lesson of industrial policy isn't whether to subsidise, but to keep multiple competitors alive, use export markets as discipline, and never bet on a single company.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:36

Big-company rules can't stop options-package smuggling

General Motors had an explicit rule: big engines don't go into mid-size cars, because they're too fast, too much like race cars, and invite antitrust scrutiny. The loophole DeLorean found was that the rule only blocked new models; put the big engine in as an option on an existing model and nobody could veto it. So in 1964 you could pay $300 to put a V8 racing engine in the Pontiac Tempest, a grandma car. He borrowed Ferrari's Gran Turismo Omologato name for the option package, GTO, later called the first great American muscle car, and rode it all the way to GM vice president. The lesson: no matter how many rules a big company has, it can't stop innovation smuggled in as "existing product plus option."

17:50

A new carmaker can only start with a $100,000 sports car

Car building has brutal economies of scale: design, testing and plant construction cost astronomical sums, the first car costs a terrifying amount, and only then does each subsequent car get cheaper. That makes it extremely hard for a new brand to enter; the US had gone about fifty years without a successful new car company (the last was former GM executive Walter Chrysler). DeLorean's solution was to sell a high-priced sports car, clearing the scale threshold without spreading costs over too many units — the distinctive gull-wing doors were the justification for the premium. This is exactly the same trick Elon Musk later used: Tesla's first car was a $100,000 sports car, deposits collected first, then cheaper cars for volume. Same strategy, different ending; the problem was in the execution that followed.

22:57

What governments buy by subsidising carmakers isn't jobs, it's supply chains

Why are governments willing to pour money into car companies? On the surface, jobs: manufacturing work is stable, doesn't require a university degree, and supports a whole ecosystem of suppliers. Deeper down, it's indirect support: the Chinese EV story isn't just cars, it's battery technology and rare-earth supply chains — though the host also admits he can't quite explain what rare earths actually are — materials that are also the basis for drones, missiles and high-end electronics. So supporting the auto industry means indirectly sustaining an entire strategic supply chain. That's the premise that let DeLorean get money, and the reason governments are still generous to carmakers today.

29:03

Once the money is in, the company holds the government hostage

Building the plant in Northern Ireland was far harder than imagined: barbed wire, body searches, construction delays. In 1980 DeLorean came back for more money, and a government audit found the US office had overspent by millions, with internal recommendations to cut. DeLorean's response: $2.5 million is peanuts, don't try to nickel-and-dime me; I don't run a small-time company, and if the government is unhappy I'll shut down next Friday. The government gave more money anyway, mostly loan guarantees. Why? The money was already sunk, and it had promised thousands of jobs for Northern Ireland — sunk costs made it impossible to walk away. When industrial policy bets on a single company, the company ends up holding the government's leverage.

36:22

What turned his business failure into a criminal case wasn't the cocaine

What pushed DeLorean from business failure into criminal scandal wasn't the bag of cocaine in the hotel room, it was GPD Services. The company was registered in Panama, but its cheques went to a numbered post office box, number 33, in Geneva. DeLorean transferred roughly $18 million to it in total. The investigation found GPD's founder was Lotus chief Colin Chapman — Lotus had indeed done design work for DeLorean, but the design fees were paid separately, and this money never entered Lotus's accounts. The prosecutors' version: DeLorean pocketed about $8 million of it to buy a Utah company that made snowcats. Lotus's CFO pleaded guilty to fraud and was sentenced to three years; the judge called it "naked, egregious and large-scale" fraud.

45:35

China's success came from backing dozens, not one

China is often cited as proof that industrial policy works: the government poured huge sums into the auto industry, and now it has powerful car, battery and solar industries, having essentially displaced Germany's solar industry. But there's one key difference between China's approach and the DeLorean case: China backed dozens of carmakers at once rather than betting on one big company; every region had its own carmaker, and they fought each other domestically first. That internal competition preserved discipline and avoided a situation where "one person holds the government hostage." The host's inference: the more sensible move for the UK government back then would have been to build another, different car plant in Manchester, Birmingham or outside London, and let them compete, rather than building one factory and betting on one man.

46:35

The hard part of industrial policy isn't picking right, it's daring to stop

The real difficulty of industrial policy, Tim sums up using his former boss, economist John Kay: "disciplined pluralism." Capitalism works not through the textbook price equilibrium and competitive equilibrium, but by satisfying two things at once: pluralism — you never know which idea will work, maybe gull-wing doors, maybe EVs, maybe not cars at all but e-bikes, so you leave room for many possibilities; and discipline — when an idea is proven unworkable, you have to be able to say "this is a bad idea, stop." In the DeLorean case the UK government lost on both counts: it bet on one project (no pluralism) and, because of sunk costs, refused to stop (no discipline). What many East Asian economies did during their growth phase was use export markets as the discipline: the government could subsidise, but the product had to be sold abroad, to someone willing to buy it.

Glossary

gull-wing doors
Doors that open upward rather than pulling outward, shaped like a seagull's wings; DeLorean's signature design.
entrapment
When law enforcement sets a trap to induce a suspect to commit a crime; a defence in common-law systems for arguing innocence.
disciplined pluralism
A phrase coined by John Kay: accommodate many business ideas while cutting losses on bad ones in time.
snowcat
A tracked vehicle for snow, used to clear and groom ski slopes.
