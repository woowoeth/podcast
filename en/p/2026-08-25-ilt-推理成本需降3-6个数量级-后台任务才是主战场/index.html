Backend Agents Will Eat 90% of Compute; Inference Optimization Should Trade Latency for Throughput

The next wave of AI inference isn't chat but backend agents, which will eat 90% of compute. Sale Research optimizes for throughput over latency, accepting even 80% availability in exchange for dropping price by orders of magnitude, and argues Nvidia's per-watt advantage is overrated.

3:33

Inference companies all chase latency; agents need endurance

Frames Sale Research as a "token factory": providing tokens for open-source large language models at prices unmatched on the market, and supporting agent building. They also offer Sale Box — a long-running agent VM in the cloud, designed for agents that run for hours, days, or even weeks. The company's theme is "abundance": delivering intelligence to as many people as possible at a sustainable cost. The rise of open-source models is a major tailwind, and customers want control and sovereignty over their intelligence. Existing inference companies all focus on low-latency inference, but agents need more persistent, longer-horizon tasks.

— Neil Movva
8:00

Backend tasks will eventually be 90%, because there's no human in the loop

Predicts that by the end of this year, backend and real-time workloads will each be 50%, but over the long run backend tasks will be 90%. The reason is that backend tasks have no human in the loop, so token consumption is unbounded, whereas human attention is limited. Tasks like deep research and cybersecurity are better suited to running in the background; Parallel Web Systems wants to index the entire internet and monitor changes in real time, a task that requires intelligence at massive scale. The backend market is unbounded — that's the decisive advantage.

— Neil Movva
20:10

Chatbots are pushing GPUs off the optimal path

GPUs are throughput machines that like running at full load, but interactive chatbots need low latency, forcing GPUs off the optimal path. The most profound change over the next year is the shift from chatbots to proactive or backend agents, at which point building a throughput-centric stack makes more sense. The best latency is no latency — when you wake up, the work is already done overnight. This shift will change the optimization target of the entire inference system.

— Neil Movva
36:48

The internet was a one-time subsidy; data is no longer free

The internet was a one-time data subsidy: roughly 30 trillion tokens of high-quality text, and about 300 trillion under a broad definition, and models have already seen the entire internet multiple times. Future data comes from models improving themselves on verifiable tasks, using reinforcement learning environments (such as coding and math problems) to let models iterate autonomously. Random human feedback is now worthless, because models have surpassed the average human and need expert-level feedback. Data is no longer free — it's the new bottleneck on scalability.

— Neil Movva
50:55

Inference doesn't need giant data centers; 1MW is enough

Building a 100,000-GPU data center is far harder than building a 10,000-GPU one. In the US, building a 100MW data center is already difficult, 1GW is nearly impossible, 10MW is barely feasible, and 1MW is ample. The market still assumes centralized large data centers are needed, but inference workloads suit distributed 1MW data centers, and he's willing to buy these small compute pools. He'd even accept 95% or even 80% availability, because backend agents are insensitive to latency; a robust control plane migrates around single points of failure, and what you get in return is extreme cost-performance.

— Neil Movva
1:07:19

Distillation can't be stopped; the only moat left is speed of staying ahead

If users own the outputs of their interactions with AI and publish them to GitHub, distillation is inevitable over the long run. Information diffusion can't be prevented; the only question is speed. So how long the value of being 3 to 6 months ahead can last is the key question. Enterprises adopt slowly — many are still on Opus 4.6 or 4.7 and won't quickly adopt the frontier. This means a model's moat lies in the speed of continuously staying ahead, not in absolute capability.

— Neil Movva
1:09:28

Bypass HBM; offload the KV cache to flash

The view that most makes his friends think he has "three heads and six arms" is bypassing HBM and offloading even more aggressively to flash. He's keen on changing model architectures to make offloading the KV cache to flash more efficient. The inference community designs systems around serving at 1 to 10 tokens per second — that's the north star. At OpenAI's pricing, a trillion tokens costs at least $5 million, requiring 3 to 6 orders of magnitude of cost improvement. The world's demand for intelligence will always exist; the challenge is how the product community provides the entry point.

— Neil Movva
1:11:34

Lose TSMC and the worst case is only a 2x per-watt gap

From Hopper to Blackwell to Rubin, the per-watt performance improvement in Bfloat16 multiplication isn't large; from TSMC 5nm to 4nm to 3nm, the per-watt performance change isn't significant either. Even if TSMC were lost, the West's best process (such as Intel) would at worst be a 2x per-watt gap — far less severe than imagined. Chip founders must understand 3 to 5 supply chain bottlenecks: TSMC wafer capacity, HBM capacity, advanced packaging, and possibly power. Design should be spiky, choosing directions Nvidia hasn't prioritized, such as betting on an HBM shortage.

— Neil Movva

Glossary

KV cache
Memory that caches keys and values in Transformer attention, avoiding recomputation during generation.
HBM
Packaged together with the GPU, providing extremely high memory bandwidth but limited capacity.
SRAM
Extremely fast but small in capacity, typically used as on-chip cache.
Tensor Core
A hardware unit on the GPU specifically accelerating matrix multiplication, the compute core of deep learning.
Harness
An agent framework or toolchain built around a model; here it refers to user-defined agent workflows.
