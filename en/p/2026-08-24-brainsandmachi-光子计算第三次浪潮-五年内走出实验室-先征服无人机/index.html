Photonics' Third Wave: Out of the Lab in Five Years, Conquering Drones First

Photons can't interact the way electrons do, yet wavelength multiplexing delivers massive parallelism and ultra-low latency. All-optical neural networks could land within five years — and the first stop isn't servers, it's drones and real-time control.

Photons don't interact, yet they compute in parallel

Photons don't interact with each other as easily as electrons do, which has long been the reason photonics was used only for communication, not computation. But Patti points out that this "weakness" is precisely what yields two big advantages: extremely high parallelism and extremely high speed. Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) stuffs multiple light signals of different colors into the same fiber, directly multiplying the amount of information a single chip can process. In other words, it's not that photons can't compute — they compute a different way, using parallelism to compensate for the lack of interaction.

— Patti Stabile
7:16

All-optical neurons have already been realized

Patti's team built an "all-optical" implementation: neurons, inter-layer connections, and nonlinear activation are all done in the optical domain using semiconductor optical amplifiers. The signal goes from the first layer's input to the last layer's output without ever returning to the electrical domain, and without needing intermediate memory access. This breaks the limitation of early designs, where photons only handled inter-layer connections and neurons still had to be implemented electronically. The biggest beneficiary of all-optical computing is ultra-low latency — the time for light to make one loop around the chip is far shorter than the time for an electronic circuit to access memory.

— Patti Stabile
13:25

Optical amplifier noise compression is a hidden advantage

The nonlinear activation of semiconductor optical amplifiers doesn't just perform the threshold function; it has another often-overlooked role: compressing noise and regenerating the signal. This means that as the network scales up, accuracy doesn't degrade layer by layer — a key to guaranteeing scalability, not just "computing fast." The downside is size and power consumption. Patti admits the amplifiers are current-driven and power-hungry; switching to voltage-driven components like microring resonators could cut power consumption by one to two orders of magnitude, and that's already being planned.

— Patti Stabile
21:30

Products within five years, a third wave

Patti judges that photonic computing is in its third wave. The first wave passed long ago, the second receded, and in recent years it has heated up again on AI compute demand. Her prediction: a real product prototype will appear within three to five years, and the first stop won't be replacing data centers' big compute engines, but real-time control scenarios like drones and autonomous driving that need ultra-low latency, light weight, and low power — the optical signal enters the chip from the sensor, inference is done directly in the optical domain, driving the next action, with the entire loop completed in an extremely short time.

— Patti Stabile
29:32

Photonics is the neural network's communication backbone

Ralph offers a key judgment: photonics' greatest value isn't in fully replacing electronic computation, but in serving as the communication backbone of neuromorphic networks. He recalls David A.B. Miller's famous line that "the brain fits in a pizza box," and argues that optical interconnects are the core driver of large-scale chip interconnection. He in fact questions the necessity of all-optical computing: rather than pursuing opticalization of every layer, it's better to first let photons carry the data transmission load between boards and between racks, with a small amount of embedded neural processing already being transformative enough.

— Sunny Baines
36:32

The power bill only works at scale

Ralph clarifies a power-consumption logic that's easy to misread: it's not that every photonic device is more efficient than its electronic counterpart, but that system-level efficiency improves as scale rises. The initial laser injection is a fixed cost; the larger the compute volume, the lower the amortized power per operation, and the higher the accuracy. Doing photonics at small scale is a disaster; only at large scale can it constitute an advantage. But he cautions that photonics lags neuromorphic engineering as a whole by about ten years, and the infrastructure and toolchain aren't yet mature.

— Ralph
42:33

Don't compare photonics to conventional digital

Ralph sharply criticizes the way comparisons are made in the literature: if a photonic chip is only compared on power and speed against "off-the-shelf conventional digital architectures," that's an unfair fight. The real comparison should be against analog neuromorphic and digital neuromorphic, which are equally emerging technologies. Giulia adds a more solid example: MIT's photonic processor for 6G wireless signal classification is 100 times faster than digital approaches, with a latency of just 120 nanoseconds — but Ralph points out this precisely proves photonics is best at communication, not computation itself.

Glossary

WDM (Wavelength Division Multiplexing)
Using different colors of light to transmit multiple signals simultaneously in the same fiber, multiplying information throughput.
Semiconductor Optical Amplifier (SOA)
A device that directly amplifies an optical signal without converting it to electrical, serving in this scheme as both linear and nonlinear operation.
McCulloch-Pitts neuron model
The most basic mathematical model of a neuron, comprising two steps: linear weighted summation and nonlinear threshold activation.
Indium Phosphide (InP)
A semiconductor material platform capable of integrating lasers, detectors, and optical amplifiers on the same chip, used for photonic integrated circuits.
Femtojoule per operation
A unit measuring energy consumption per operation; 1 femtojoule equals 10 to the negative 15th joules, the target energy-efficiency range for photonic computing.
TDM (Time Division Multiplexing)
Transmitting different signals in turn in time slices within the same physical channel; stacking it with WDM can further expand communication capacity.
