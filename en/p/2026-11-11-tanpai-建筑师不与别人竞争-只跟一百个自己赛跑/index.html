Architects don't compete with others, only with a hundred versions of themselves

She says she never felt like she was racing the rat race because she had no one to race against in the first place—architect Qi Shanshan's (戚山山) vitality doesn't come from self-discipline, but from asking herself first whether she actually likes something before deciding to do it.

Female team members, measuring with their bodies, built truer than blueprints

The construction site of the Tengchong An zhi Ruo Su project was staffed almost entirely by women from the village. They threw bricks up along more than thirty meters and over one hundred steps, relaying materials up the stairs. Qi Shanshan discovered the villagers couldn't read blueprints, so she told them: ‘Listen to your body—turn when your body tells you to.’ They laid bricks by feel. When finished and checked against the blueprints, it was nearly identical. The sections built precisely according to specification, by contrast, looked stiff and lacked spirit.

7:06

Brilliance comes from deviation and error, never from mere precision

She says she's always fought against the rigid rule of ‘must be perfectly horizontal and vertical.’ Why must stairs go straight up, then make a 180-degree turn? No one walks that way—mountain paths are walked on a slant. She believes all the beautiful moments come from error, deviation, misalignment—from the non-dogmatic places, not from precision itself. Refined technique is the foundation, but what truly moves people comes from respecting nature's irregularity, not from rules imposed by people.

— Qi Shanshan
10:06

Fear you direct at yourself is more powerful than external punishment

Her father disciplined her harshly: he'd strike her fingers when she played a wrong note, beat her even for a score of 99 out of 100, saying it was an attitude problem. After she moved to the US at 14, she finally escaped direct supervision. But she wrote her father's name on a piece of paper and taped it on her desk. Whenever she thought of getting up to play, she'd see the name and scare herself back into studying. She'd transformed his external strictness into a self-monitoring mechanism she maintained herself, not into simple consequence-free abandon.

— Qi Shanshan
17:11

How you carry yourself determines what energy the world sends back

Her mother was a dancer. Caring for several elderly relatives and her injured father at once, physically depleted to the point of collapse, ‘she stood right up, took a lipstick from her pocket, applied it, and went back to work’—because ‘no one was watching.’ Her logic: if you go to work without makeup and looking miserable, people see you as pitiful, and the feedback they send you is negative energy. With lipstick on, they see vitality, and the feedback that comes back is positive energy. ‘So in our family it became the norm: don't complain, don't spiral—and happiness just naturally follows.’

— Qi Shanshan
20:13

Working all night for satisfaction is different from racing against others

Harvard's architecture program kept her working from 9 AM to 5 AM. When tired, she'd run for half an hour and come back to keep working. She completed her degree before the phrase ‘racing the rat race’ became common, and she never felt like she was doing it. Knocking off at 5 AM, even exhausted, she was happy—she'd stopped actively because she was satisfied, not because she was forced to. She says those years didn't train her in design skills, but in ‘how to find satisfaction.’

— Qi Shanshan
27:40

One hundred meters of dining hall became the seed of rural transformation

In a poor village deep in the Qinling Mountains near Hanzhong, girls couldn't attend school because their families couldn't afford lunch fees. The school found a solution: join the soccer team, get free lunch. With no construction land available, the team converted a narrow hundred-meter-long old stable into a cafeteria, using food-service practices from train cars to have children line up in two queues. The team later made it to the provincial squad. The training camp brought parents for coaching, creating needs for housing and hotels, which drove expansion of the fields, reconstruction of the primary school, and a visitor center. Over six or seven years, one cafeteria rolled into complete rural revival, driven by a group of young girls who had no idea they'd changed their village.

— Qi Shanshan
1:05:32

Rest you've approved but don't actually want isn't rest, it's procrastination

The host reflected on her habit of thinking ‘I have this already—how do I add that to it?’ Qi Shanshan pointed out: this is playing it safe. You've approved downtime and fitness as ‘wonderful things’ in your head, but they're not things you actually want to do, which is why they're at the bottom of your to-do list, not the first thing you want to do when you wake up. Her logic isn't wanting-it-all—it's: ‘What do I actually love doing most?’ Then put that first in your day.

— Qi Shanshan
1:11:34

AI makes renderings beautiful; humans do the work clients actually need

She doesn't oppose using AI—when she and her colleagues learned architecture twenty years ago, the parametric design they used was already a programming tool. What she opposes is blind hope—outsourcing all the analysis and thinking you should be doing yourself to AI, then not using the time saved to do something more valuable. She says the truly irreplaceable work is face-to-face communication with clients: where is their confusion, what do they want to become? That's deep work requiring you to feel the relationship between people and space on site. AI in the studio handles only surface-level work: making renderings beautiful.

— Qi Shanshan
