Sports betting repackaged as swaps dodges state gambling rules

Kalshi's sports betting contracts cover the same matches and point spreads, but because they're structured as federally regulated swaps, they escape state gambling law—the Supreme Court is about to rule whether this holds up.

Kalshi turned a prediction market into a sports betting platform

Kalshi started with the classic liberal-economist vision of prediction markets—betting on election results, economic trends, and military outcomes, using informed traders and retail speculation to function as a ‘world truth machine.’ But retail users inevitably wanted to bet on sports, not politics or policy. The result: Kalshi became a sports betting platform in practice, a reality all internal staff acknowledged and found somewhat embarrassing.

5:40

A single legal definition turned all sports betting into swaps

Kalshi dodges state gambling regulation through a 2010 Dodd-Frank definition of swaps: any contract whose payout depends on whether an event occurs and has economic consequences counts as a swap, regulated by CFTC and sold to retail only through regulated exchanges. The problem: sports matches obviously have economic consequences. By this logic, every state-legal sports betting app sold to retail violates federal law. Several appeals courts have already ruled this interpretation too extreme and sided against Kalshi.

— Matt
6:50

The CFTC's emergency rule carved out an exception for Kalshi alone

To escape this logical trap, the CFTC rushed through a rule last week: casino-style gambling, including sports betting, doesn't count as swaps and isn't CFTC-regulated. But Kalshi's sports contracts do count. Both let you bet on the same spreads, over-unders, and parlays. The distinction: Kalshi's bets are matched peer-to-peer in a market and can be traded, while normal sports betting is one-to-many against the house. Legally sound, but as a market structure, it doesn't look clean.

— Matt
16:50

The Supreme Court ruling might actually free Kalshi from sports betting

Episode speculation: Kalshi may not actually want to keep sports betting. After years of flow from sports bettors, the company has built up election and economic prediction contracts—real financial derivatives. If the Supreme Court rules out sports contracts, Kalshi may already be positioned to survive without them. The metaphor: a child who can't control himself asking adults to set boundaries. ‘Please release me from this demon.’

— Matt
18:10

Goldman Sachs made more money from lending than from trading

Goldman's equities division earned more this quarter from prime brokerage—lending to institutions—than from intermediation—market-making and trading. This reflects Wall Street's structural shift: trading itself is increasingly electronic and no longer relationship-dependent. Jane Street and Citadel Securities have taken over the traditional trading business. Banks instead focus on providing massive leverage to hedge funds and private credit firms, earning stable interest and fees rather than directional bets. The core skill shifted from trading talent back to client relationship management.

— Matt
23:10

Banks didn't eliminate risk—they just moved it to a different seat

Banks no longer hold large positions in AI stocks or aggressive LBO lending directly. But by providing leverage to hedge funds, private credit firms, and market-makers, they stand behind the same trades—just with better seniority. If those institutions blow up (as Archegos did), banks lose money too. The improvement is that banks now sit further back in the capital structure and at lower risk, while real money—longer-lock-up hedge fund and private credit capital—takes the directional risk. This is described as ‘strict improvement.’

— Matt
27:30

Martin Shkreli is banned for life from 'influencing' drug companies

Shkreli was convicted of securities fraud and his pharmaceutical company faced FTC antitrust penalties. The judgment bars him for life from owning or buying pharma stock, and from directly or indirectly influencing or controlling any drug company's management or business—a scope broad enough to include his Twitter comments. This week, the FTC and seven state attorneys general asked the court for contempt charges, alleging he's continued shorting pharma stocks and attacking them on Twitter. The vote passed 1-0 because the other Democratic commissioner has been removed and not replaced.

— Matt
33:50

Whether short-selling counts as 'holding' stock divided listeners

Shkreli is banned from ‘acquiring or holding’ pharma stock but has been shorting it anyway. The original argument: shorting doesn't count as ‘hold’ because you own a negative position, never actually hold the stock. Listeners pushed back hard, pointing out that shorting requires borrowing the stock first—even momentarily, that's ‘holding.’ The episode stands by the original logic but concedes that the moment you buy back to close the short, you arguably ‘acquire’ the stock, and that's where legal risk may actually live.

— Matt

Glossary

Swap
Financial contract whose payout depends on whether an event occurs; regulated by CFTC, sold to retail only through regulated exchanges.
Prime brokerage
Banks provide leverage financing and trade settlement services to hedge funds and algorithmic trading firms.
Intermediation
Traditional trading business where banks buy and sell securities for profit and take directional risk.
Mention markets
Prediction market contracts betting on whether a specific person will say a specific phrase in a particular context.
