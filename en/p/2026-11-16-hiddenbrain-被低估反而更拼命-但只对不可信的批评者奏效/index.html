Underestimation Fuels Performance—But Only from Critics You Don't Trust

Research shows employees perform better when they feel underestimated—but only if the critic lacks credibility; otherwise the same low expectations drag them down.

His Scout Report Wrote Him Off as Hopeless

Before the 2000 NFL draft, Brady's scout report was filled with negative assessments: poor physique, lack of mobility, a weak arm, easy to read. As a result, he was drafted in the sixth round, 199th overall—with six quarterbacks selected ahead of him. The report became something repeatedly referenced throughout his career as damaging evidence, and serves as this episode's introduction to the phenomenon of underestimation actually spurring performance.

Being Underestimated Leads to Better Overall Job Performance

Samir Nurmohamed surveyed about one thousand new employees at a consumer goods company and found that the more underestimated people felt by others, the higher their supervisors rated their job performance. This contradicts the classic conclusion about the Pygmalion Effect / Golem Effect (high expectations lead to better performance, low expectations lead to worse performance), prompting him to further investigate the mechanism behind this in laboratory experiments.

— Samir Nurmohamed
24:56

The Critical Factor: Whether You Trust the Rater

During peer review, editors pressed Nurmohamed to explain: why didn't his conclusion—that underestimation actually motivates—contradict the previous finding that low expectations harm performance (self-fulfilling prophecy)? He found the distinction lies in evaluator credibility. In past research, low expectations typically came from authority figures with experience and power; in his experiments, those delivering the underestimation were usually strangers or researchers he'd just met, with information asymmetry. High-credibility criticism gets internalized as self-fulfilling prophecy; low-credibility criticism triggers reactance—a drive to prove the other person wrong.

— Samir Nurmohamed
36:24

One Hour of Narrative Practice Builds Psychological Immunity

Nurmohamed partnered with a reemployment center for a field experiment: job seekers were randomly assigned either to recall and write down an underdog story—‘others didn't believe in me, but I proved them wrong’—or to write about ‘others always believed in me’. Weeks later, those who wrote the underdog narrative showed less confidence damage when facing job discrimination and were more likely to actually land employment afterward. One hour of narrative practice effectively provided a psychological vaccine against discrimination.

— Samir Nurmohamed
38:26

Being Second Choice Prevents Seeking Feedback

Another study found that people who learned they were ‘backups’ (the other person was the first choice, only selecting them when the preferred candidate left) experienced the same ‘underestimated’ feeling, yet saw no positive effect from the underdog phenomenon. Instead, they actively avoided seeking feedback from colleagues and superiors. The reason: they felt they hadn't truly been accepted by the team, with lower social integration. This sense of alienation outweighed the drive to ‘prove others wrong’.

— Samir Nurmohamed
52:31

The Hero's Journey Breaks Down Into Seven Elements

Ben Rogers distilled Joseph Campbell's hero's journey theory from seventeen steps into seven elements: an obscure protagonist, a turning point in life circumstances, the mission or adventure that follows, allies encountered, external or internal challenges faced, the transformation that results, and finally returning with the fruits of victory, benefiting others and leaving a legacy. This framework doesn't require identical story details—only consistent underlying structure. Whether ancient myth or an ordinary person's life, any story can be renarrated through these seven steps.

— Ben Rogers
1:19:29

The Hero's Journey Narrative Fails for Chronic Pain Patients

In testing the hero's journey intervention on chronic pain patients, participants on average reported more meaningful lives, but this research group showed the highest dropout rate—roughly ten to fifteen percent of people directly stated the narrative didn't apply to their experience, that they hadn't experienced ‘transformation’. Rogers theorizes that chronic illness patients have been stripped of agency over their lives, making it difficult to renarrate their experience as a protagonist with control.

— Ben Rogers
1:22:37

The Hero's Journey Is Cultural Narrative—and Potentially a Cage

The hero's journey has long used a male protagonist as the default template. Many marginalized people either feel their life experience doesn't align with this mainstream narrative, or are told they're not allowed to tell such stories. This alienation intensifies loneliness. To address this, the team deliberately redesigned the intervention: instead of first asking ‘Are you a hero?’, they had people list concrete elements from their lives, then showed them how these fit the framework—bypassing the psychological barrier of ‘Am I worthy of the word hero?’

— Ben Rogers

Glossary

underdog effect
The tendency to perform better and work harder when others underestimate you.
Pygmalion Effect / Golem Effect
High expectations elevate performance; low expectations diminish it—a self-fulfilling prophecy.
reactance
The oppositional response when personal freedom is perceived as threatened.
hero's journey
Campbell's narrative framework: an ordinary person faces challenges, undergoes transformation, and returns changed.
Reflected Best Self Exercise
An exercise where associates provide feedback on your core strengths, revealing unrecognized advantages.
