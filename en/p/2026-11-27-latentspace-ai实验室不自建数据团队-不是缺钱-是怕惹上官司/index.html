Labs willingly pay more for external data than building internal teams

A data-labeling company scaled from $15M to $100M annually with zero venture capital and maintained profitability, showing that frontier labs prefer external data suppliers to internalizing the work. The real barriers: litigation risk and the complexity of managing a 6,000-person supply chain.

G2I scaled to $100M revenue in one year—zero VC, still profitable

Gabe Greenberg revealed G2I hit $15M last year, expects $100M this year, with zero fundraising and profitability intact—possibly tripling again next year. Its five or six customers are all frontier labs; the company avoids non-frontier labs, though other funded labs are beginning to come with varying budgets to purchase data.

— Gabe Greenberg
1:56

Only one frontier lab built mature internal data infrastructure

Gabe pointed out that only one lab has built data infrastructure and team maturity far beyond others—with mature processes for managing researchers and vendors alike. Other labs mostly still rely on off-the-shelf data, slowly shifting to deep partnerships with external providers because benchmarks have gotten harder, requiring granular data refinement from the instruction stage.

— Gabe Greenberg
3:24

Task complexity shifted from mass contractors to demanding senior engineers

In 2023 and 2024, projects routinely deployed 6,000 mid-level engineers at modest rates—more gig work. Now they require staff-level senior engineers; dataset construction is highly complex, involving subjective taste judgments, moving away from pure headcount-stacking. Robotics data collection is now repeating this same labor-intensity climb from scratch.

— Gabe Greenberg
3:49

SWE-bench became standard through timing, not technical superiority

Gabe disclosed a little-known fact: SWE-bench, used as a top-tier industry benchmark for over a year, began as an undergrad project with significant instruction-design flaws from the start. It became standard because first-mover advantage attracted sufficient attention, not because of technical excellence.

— Gabe Greenberg
6:58

Task difficulty must land in a narrow band between useless and unevaluable

Designing trainable tasks requires hitting a tight band: too hard and it becomes an eval; too easy and training value drops to zero. The ideal sits just a few steps beyond current model capability. Gabe called it both science and art—requiring engineers to multitask with poker-player precision.

— Gabe Greenberg
15:23

A 6,000-person funnel harbors real risks but only two mega-suppliers exist

Gabe acknowledged the industry faces concrete hazards: a 500-person project typically feeds a 6,000-person funnel, where North Korean labor, supply chain integrity, and CISO strength are legitimate concerns. The industry has already seen contractors working for competing clients simultaneously. Yet even when problems emerge, vendors don't necessarily lose clients—only two or three $1B+ scale vendors exist as substitutes.

— Gabe Greenberg
18:39

Non-exclusive data is cheaper and more attractive than exclusive rights

Counterintuitively, not all labs demand exclusive data. When the same off-the-shelf dataset sells to four customers, per-customer price drops; exclusivity to one buyer commands a premium. Most labs don't care if their data goes to competitors because for researchers, justifying "how much this budget buys" matters more than secrecy—only the top one or two labs truly care about exclusivity.

— Gabe Greenberg
22:02

Rising data budgets show labs trade compute efficiency for time-to-market

Gabe observed post-training spending rising sharply, with data costs climbing from 2-3% to 4-6% of total compute budgets—he even believes there's a case for 50%, since better data wastes less compute. But labs face time pressure: idle compute burns resources, so they accept "good enough" data rather than waiting for "best-in-class."

— Gabe Greenberg
27:32

Robotics and 3D models fastest-growing; CAD and chip design severely undersupplied

Looking ahead, Gabe sees robotics and 3D world models as the fastest-growing segments; three years ago, contracts for these lasted three to six months, but now six-month minimums are routine. Law, finance, and computer use offer substantial headroom. Meanwhile, non-software engineering tasks like CAD, mechanical design, and chip design remain severely under-supplied.

— Gabe Greenberg
