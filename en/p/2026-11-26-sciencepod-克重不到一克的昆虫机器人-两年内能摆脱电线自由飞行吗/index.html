Sub-gram insect robots—can they break free of power cords within two years?

A National University of Defense Technology team built an insect robot that can fly untethered for four minutes, but it still can only control altitude and cannot turn. The field is stuck between ‘can fly’ and ‘can be useful’.

Even the CIA's Cold War insect robot was defeated by a stray breeze

During the Cold War, CIA engineers built a dragonfly-painted insect-sized robot equipped with a micro gas engine and eavesdropping equipment that could glide considerable distances. Yet even the slightest crosswind would completely blow it off course. The Insectothopter project ultimately failed. The field didn't seriously restart until the late 1980s.

3:03

Scale down to insects and physics becomes an entirely different game

At insect scale, gravity and inertia—crucial forces for large drones—become negligible, while static electricity and surface tension grow dominant. Air becomes so viscous that if you shrank to fly-size, walking would feel like wading through maple syrup. You cannot simply scale down large-drone components proportionally; the entire design logic must restart from scratch.

— Fi Jacobs
9:26

Direct wing drive cuts dead weight and friction for the Chinese team

Harvard's RoboBee and MIT's Softfly both use indirect drive—artificial muscles don't attach directly to wings, which amplifies motion range and reduces stress on fragile piezoelectric actuators. The National University of Defense Technology team does the opposite: direct attachment to test how far piezoelectric actuators can be pushed. This eliminates extra parts, reduces weight, cuts friction losses, and feeds all power directly into wing-flapping—the key to its cable-free flight.

— Fi Jacobs
10:26

Four minutes of untethered flight—but steering remains out of reach

The wireless insect robot's body is nearly weightless; almost all heft comes from battery and control systems. It achieves continuous flight for four minutes. But it can only control altitude; directional control is still lacking. The team's approach: achieve untethered flight first, solve controllability next. This exposes the gap between ‘can fly’ and ‘can be useful’ that currently defines the field.

— Fi Jacobs
13:36

MIT optimistically says two years, but batteries remain the deeper problem

Harvard postdoc Elio Chilita thinks wireless flight in two years is probably achievable, but flying longer than a few minutes will take far more time—because how batteries work at the fundamental level needs to change, something no robotics lab can solve alone. By contrast, MIT's Kevin Chen was the most optimistic interviewee, believing two years is feasible.

— Fi Jacobs
24:03

Supplementing during pregnancy isn't enough; lactation demands continued nutrition

Three intervention studies of undernourished pregnant women in low-income regions showed that locally-sourced balanced energy-protein supplements raised B vitamins and micronutrients in milk, but had little effect on total protein, fat, carbohydrates, or the milk microbiome and oligosaccharides. A critical policy implication: many programs supplement only during pregnancy, then stop after birth. This research shows continued supplementation during lactation matters equally, because it actually changes milk composition.

— Megan Azad
28:09

Breast health research was neglected so long it weakened cancer science

Milk and breast health were chronically underfunded research fields, and this neglect harmed breast cancer science itself—without understanding how healthy breast tissue works during normal lactation, understanding cancer remains limited. An intriguing correlation: each additional month a woman breastfeeds over her lifetime reduces breast cancer risk, but science is only beginning to understand why.

— Megan Azad
29:09

Eight biomarkers distilled from thousands predict the breast's overall health status

From thousands of milk compounds measured, researchers selected eight biomarkers that both respond to nutrition interventions and correlate with infant growth. The underlying proteins relate to female reproductive secretion, mammary tissue remodeling, milk synthesis, and inflammation signals. Collectively they signal not just what's in the milk, but how healthy the gland producing it truly is.

— Megan Azad

Glossary

piezoelectric actuator
Ceramic thin sheets that deform when charged, mimicking insect muscle to drive wing motion.
dielectric elastomer actuator
Soft, durable polymer actuator like rubber that resists impact but demands more power.
resilin
Shock-absorbing protein in insect wing hinges that recovers energy from wing-flapping.
