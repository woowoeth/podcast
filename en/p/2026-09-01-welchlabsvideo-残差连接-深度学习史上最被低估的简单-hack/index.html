Residual Connections: Deep Learning's Most Underrated Simple Hack

ResNet is the most cited paper of the 21st century, but its real contribution isn't making networks deeper — it's inventing the residual stream, a kind of working memory that lets a model store and edit information, and that fundamentally changed how we understand neural networks.

ResNetDeep LearningTransformerWorking MemoryVision ModelsArchitecture
This episode uses clear animations and experiments to explain why residual connections work, how they gave rise to the Transformer, and the evidence that the residual stream acts as working memory. Suited to anyone who wants to understand the essence of modern AI architecture.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:07

Deeper networks perform worse, not better

In early 2015, Sun Jian's team used careful initialization to push a network to 30 layers, but performance got worse than 14 layers (error rate 16.59% vs 13.34%). In theory, a 30-layer model could match a 14-layer one at minimum via identity mappings, but the optimizer couldn't find such a solution. This hinted the problem wasn't network capacity but the training process itself.

15:18

Shattered gradients are the root disease of deep networks

By visualizing the loss landscape, they found that as depth increased, the loss surface of early layers became extremely rugged and gradient directions turned as random as white noise — the ‘shattered gradients problem’. Gradients are the core learning signal; once they fail, deep networks can't be trained effectively.

17:18

Residual connections: an almost absurdly simple fix

Sun Jian's team's solution was laughably simple: between every two layers, add the input tensor directly to the output, forming a skip connection. This gave activations and gradients a direct path, effectively eliminating the shattered gradients problem. A 74-layer network with skip connections jumped from 38.9% to 72.6% accuracy, sweeping the major 2015 vision competitions.

20:19

ResNet overturned hierarchical representation theory

A Cornell team found that deleting or even shuffling ResNet's layers had little effect on performance. This contradicted the then-mainstream hierarchical representation theory — which held that each layer builds on the previous one, so deleting any layer should be catastrophic. It forced the field to rethink: ResNet may not be learning hierarchical features but iteratively refining a ‘residual stream’ that runs through the whole network.

26:26

The residual stream: modern AI's hidden backbone

Straighten out the skip connections and you see an uninterrupted stream of data from input to output, with each layer merely making incremental edits on top. This ‘residual stream’ later became the core design of the Transformer and the cornerstone of modern AI. But note: follow-up work shows ResNet learns both hierarchical representations and refines the residual stream — the two can coexist.

29:28

The residual stream is the model's working memory

A Meta team found that in a vision Transformer's residual stream, activations at certain positions were unusually large and concentrated in unimportant regions of the image. They hypothesized the model used these positions as ‘registers’ to aggregate global information. Experiments confirmed it: the embeddings at these high-activation positions performed astonishingly well on image classification (85.2% vs 10.8%), and after adding extra ‘register tokens’, the model no longer occupied image positions.

Glossary

Residual stream
The continuous path of data flowing through a network from input to output, with each layer making only incremental edits.
Skip connection
The operation of adding the input tensor directly to a layer's output, giving gradients a shortcut.
Register tokens
Extra embedding vectors added to store global information, avoiding the need to occupy image positions.
Shattered gradients problem
Increasing depth makes gradients as random as white noise, rendering training ineffective.
