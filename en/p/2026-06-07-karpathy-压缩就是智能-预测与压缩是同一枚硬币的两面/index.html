Compression Is Intelligence: Prediction and Compression Are Two Sides of the Same Coin

Information theory says prediction and compression are mathematically equivalent, so the pretraining objective of a language model can be re-understood as building the most efficient text compressor — not merely predicting the next token.

This is part one of a trilogy and covers only the mathematical foundations of the compression limit, with no engineering details and no product judgments; information density is moderate. Suited to people who want to build information-theory intuition, not to people who want industry judgments.

The pretraining objective is really compression

Karpathy points out that large language model pretraining is usually described as ‘next token prediction’, using cross-entropy loss, and the term cross-entropy itself comes from information theory. One result of information theory is that prediction and compression are mathematically equivalent — two sides of the same coin. So you can restate the pretraining objective entirely as: not predicting the next token, but building the most efficient text compressor. The value of this restatement is that it makes clearer what cross-entropy actually is and why it is used.

3:05

The robot example: probability determines code length

A warm-up with a robot far away on the moon: the commands are only up, down, left, right, but the distribution is uneven — half are up, a quarter down, an eighth left, an eighth right, and each command is independent. The naive student gives every command a fixed two bits, averaging 2 bits; the smart student uses a variable-length code: 0 for up, 10 for down, 110 for left, 111 for right, for a weighted average of 1.75 bits. The key constraint is that no codeword may be a prefix of another codeword — this is called a prefix-free code — so that the robot can decode unambiguously.

— Andrej Karpathy
10:13

The output of perfect compression looks like random noise

A third, ‘impractical’ student proposes: random noise is incompressible, so the bitstream produced by a perfect compression algorithm should be indistinguishable from random noise. If the compressed n-bit bitstream really looks like random noise, then all 2 to the n messages must be equally likely, each with probability 1/2^n. Working backwards, a message that takes n bits under a perfect scheme means its probability is 2 to the minus n. Take the log and negate it, and you get n = -log₂(p). This negative-log expression is the fundamental formula of information theory, and Shannon's definition of ‘the information content of an event’.

— Andrej Karpathy
17:19

Information content is the lower bound on compression

Perfect compression is not always possible, because the probabilities of real messages are usually not integer powers of 2. The more general statement is: the information content of a message gives a lower bound on how short it can be compressed — at least when averaged over all possible messages. You can absolutely overfit a compression algorithm to one specific case. In the robot example the probabilities are too clean, all powers of 2, so the information content of each symbol is exactly an integer number of bits; but in natural language the probability of each letter depends heavily on context, and none of them are powers of 2, so the computed information content is all fractional.

— Andrej Karpathy
21:27

Shannon had his wife guess the next letter

Shannon started with n-gram statistics, but long strings simply do not appear in a corpus, so the statistics break down. So he switched models: his wife Betty. Shannon took a book and had Betty guess the next letter one at a time; when she guessed wrong he wrote down the correct letter, and when she guessed right he drew a dash. The transcribed text has fewer letters but the same information content — because if he could replicate a Betty, he would only need to give her this reduced text to restore the original. In a 1950 paper he changed to recording how many guesses were needed to get it right, and from that inferred the implicit probabilities the human brain assigns to real letters. When he analysed language he was not purely doing data analysis; he was probing an underlying language model, namely the brain of his subject.

— Andrej Karpathy
24:29

Entropy is the average information per symbol

Entropy asks for the average information content per symbol in a signal. Because under perfect compression codeword length equals information content, entropy is essentially asking about the compression limit, giving a lower bound on how efficiently a signal can be compressed. For any probability distribution, entropy is the sum over all p of p times negative log₂(p). Visually, draw the probability distribution as stacked horizontal bars whose widths are the probabilities, put a rectangle on each bar with height equal to negative log₂(p), and entropy is the sum of the areas of all the rectangles. The more uniform the distribution the higher the entropy, the more skewed the lower, and the more kinds of symbols the higher too.

— Andrej Karpathy
28:35

The noiseless coding theorem: the limit is achievable

A more precise understanding of entropy is: it describes the minimum number of bits per symbol needed to encode a message drawn from this distribution. This is the central theorem of Shannon's 1948 paper that founded information theory — the noiseless coding theorem. It says no encoding can be more efficient than this limit, and, more strongly, Shannon proved you can always get arbitrarily close to it. This expression only applies when every symbol follows the same distribution; natural language does not satisfy that, and needs the more general entropy rate, i.e. an average over all possible messages — but that is almost never something you can compute cleanly.

— Andrej Karpathy
29:36

English is about one bit per character

Shannon estimated the entropy of English at roughly one bit per character, provided the subject has at least 100 preceding letters of context. That number is crazy, because it means English can be compressed down to a single yes/no answer per character. Karpathy says part three will show that algorithm: if you are allowed to use a high-quality language model for encoding and decoding, in practice you can get quite close to this limit. He also previews that part two will cover cross-entropy and its use in training large language models, plus distilling large models into small ones, and why GZIP can recover structure across different languages.

Glossary

prefix-free code
An encoding in which no codeword is a prefix of another codeword, guaranteeing unambiguous decoding.
noiseless coding theorem
Shannon's 1948 proof that entropy is the compression limit, and that it can be approached arbitrarily closely.
entropy rate
The average information content per symbol in a stochastic process, applicable when the symbol distribution is not fixed.
cross-entropy loss
The loss function used for language model pretraining, whose name comes from information theory.
