Two-year-old defiance isn't a broken bond—it's emotion doing repair work

When toddlers say no to everything—getting in the car, bathing, holding hands at crossings—it's because they've weathered massive changes with no sense of control; the answer isn't more choices but tighter, earlier boundaries.

Emotional storms are not signals of relationship breakdown

Her daughter experienced housing moves, then a new daycare within three weeks. Janet notes that same-aged and older children create more uncertainty than adults do—adding another source of stress. This is a ‘transition crisis,’ not a relationship problem. Better housing or warmer caregiving wouldn't prevent it. The trigger is loss of familiarity, regardless of circumstances.

— Janet Lansbury
6:11

Children who hold it together most need permission to collapse

Janet distinguishes two response patterns: fragile children break down directly, while determined, sensitive children ‘hold, hold, hold’—using continuous resistance to fight the out-of-control feeling. When parents worry about resistance and treat it as a red flag, they undermine the firmness these children need to release fear and sadness. Firmness is the container that enables the release.

— Janet Lansbury
9:22

During transitions, tighten boundaries rather than expand choices

The parent has been minimizing restrictions and offering choices, but Janet names a counterintuitive rule: in high-stakes transitions—boarding the car, crossing streets—children actually need fewer choices and earlier, clearer boundaries, not more autonomy. Set boundaries earlier and the resistance drops, because she won't have to wrestle alone with that feeling of lost control.

— Janet Lansbury
11:25

Set a window, then step in directly; no open delays

Specific tactics: at boarding time, offer a few seconds to choose, then directly buckle her in without prolonging the request loop. If she stands in the stroller, lift her without repeated verbal requests. When she heads the wrong way or toward danger, hold her hand or pick her up. Early intervention, more protection. At this stage, it won't hurt her—it's exactly what she needs.

— Janet Lansbury
14:28

Let kids choose bathing, but allow bedtime meltdowns

Bathing is a voluntary activity. If she refuses, wiping off works fine—let her choose. But stop dangerous or excessive behavior immediately by lifting her out. Bedtime is different. Children often save a full day's accumulated frustrations for this moment. Parents shouldn't add more books or extend delays to soothe. Instead, clearly allow the breakdown—this is exactly when she most needs to release.

— Janet Lansbury
15:31

Managing conflict with firmness and empathy strengthens the bond

The parent worried ‘the relationship will revolve around these conflicts.’ Janet's answer: when you meet intense feeling with both firmness and empathy, conflict actually fortifies rather than erodes the bond. It's not a warm, close moment. It's something deeper: the child confirming ‘I can have a huge feeling, and you're still here keeping me safe.’ It's one of the most connective things that happens between you.

— Janet Lansbury
17:34

Dramatic tears at separation signal secure attachment, not anxiety

Mom started a new job; dad now does daycare drop-off. Her goodbye to mom is intensely theatrical. Janet reframes this: when a child releases strong emotion at parting, it means there's enough safety with that parent to express pain. Dad may not see this intensity because the child experiences separations differently with him. This dramatic release is worth reading as a vote of confidence, not as evidence separation went wrong.

— Janet Lansbury
22:50

Asking for water and the bathroom is stalling the crash

The second letter: son goes from calm to suddenly ‘flipping a switch’ at bedtime, then repeatedly requesting water, the bathroom, finally collapsing in tears wanting to leave the room. Parent isn't sure whether reading more books soothes or teaches ‘emotions are scary; we can't handle them.’ Janet's suggestion: weave those routine requests into the presleep sequence, then you can confidently refuse extras. Then receive the tears as healthy release. The calmer parents are with this as normal, the faster it passes.
