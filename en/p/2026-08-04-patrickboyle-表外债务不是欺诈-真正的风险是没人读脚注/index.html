Big Tech's Off-Balance-Sheet Debt Isn't Fraud, It's That Nobody Reads the Footnotes

The so-called $1.65 trillion in hidden debt is not Enron-style fraud but long-term leases and purchase commitments for data centers not yet in service — legal, but buried in the footnotes; the real danger is AI giants telling their story through mutual financing and adjusted earnings.

$1.65 trillion isn't hidden debt, it's stuff not yet delivered

The $1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet debt reported by Nikkei is mostly long-term GPU purchase agreements and leases on data centers not yet in service. Under standard accounting rules, when goods haven't been delivered or buildings aren't running, you don't have to record a balance-sheet liability — you just disclose it in the footnotes, just as signing a two-year phone contract doesn't mean booking a $1,200 liability that day. Meta added $233 billion in commitments last quarter, of which $96 billion in leases will move onto the balance sheet once the data centers come into service. So this isn't Enron-style fraud, it's a timing gap.

5:12

Willing to borrow but not to sell stock means management really believes

If management believed it could turn $1 into $5, it would tend to borrow rather than sell equity, so as to keep the excess profit for itself; only when uncertain about the outlook would it bring in outside shareholders. Alphabet completed nearly $85 billion in equity financing in June, the largest ever, with Berkshire investing $10 billion and buying in at roughly a 6% discount — a company that normally doesn't pay a premium for a story. The signal isn't the method of financing but the record scale of simultaneously using debt, equity and convertibles: the company thinks there's something behind the massive spending worth fighting for.

8:22

Ignoring depreciation assumes equipment never wears out

Tech giants prefer to talk EBITDA, but Munger advised reading EBITDA as BS earnings. Depreciation is reverse float: cash goes out first, the expense shows up later; ignoring depreciation assumes equipment never wears out. In the latest earnings season, the four big hyperscalers together generated only $7 billion in free cash flow, and Alphabet went cash-flow negative for the first time since going public. Stock-based compensation shouldn't simply be added back either — it's barter: issuing shares directly is as much a cost as selling shares to pay salaries. To offset dilution, companies have to buy back on a fixed schedule and can't wait for a low price.

11:29

When they default you lose the customer and the money you lent

Nvidia is pushing more than $750 billion in AI deals: $250 billion of support for OpenAI leases, then financing for $350 billion of chip purchases, plus $5 billion invested in Ilya Sutskever's startup; Google is providing $35 billion in lease support for Anthropic; SoftBank has committed $65 billion to OpenAI and borrowed $40 billion in bridge loans. The giants are investing in each other and buying from each other. It's like carmakers lending customers money to buy cars, but it's also old-fashioned vendor financing. The risk is that on default you lose both the customer and the money you lent the customer.

— Patrick Boyle
15:46

Everyone is priced as a winner, which is mathematically impossible

Aswath Damodaran and Bradford Cornell call it the big market delusion: every investor treats the company they're betting on as the future winner, but add up the money each is expected to earn and the number exceeds the market itself — mathematically impossible. The Economist estimates about $900 billion of AI buildout spending this year, more than $400 billion of it borrowed; earning that back would require roughly $2.5 trillion a year in AI revenue, more than the entire global tech industry generates today. Actual adoption is modest: US executives use AI about 100 minutes a week on average, and median enterprise spend per person is just $10.66 a month.

20:06

93% of SpaceX's market space comes from AI, not rockets

In its IPO prospectus, SpaceX attributed $26.5 trillion of its $28.5 trillion TAM — 93% — to AI/Grok, leaving about $2 trillion for actual businesses like rockets and satellites. Analysts then put out an $800 target price, implying a market cap above $10 trillion, while the company's revenue last year was under $19 billion. The reason: SpaceX disclosed roughly $235 billion in spending commitments through 2030, and IPO proceeds cover only a small part — the roughly $170 billion gap has to be filled by repeatedly issuing stock and debt over many years, which is exactly Wall Street's underwriting feast. Analysts at 18 underwriting banks published bullish calls at almost the same time, and only one independent firm gave a sell rating.

— Patrick Boyle
26:17

Burying debt on page 83 is a rational bet

Public doesn't mean usable. Robert Bloomfield's incomplete revelation hypothesis holds that the harder information is to extract, the less fully it gets priced; putting debt on page 83 of a 200-page document, scattered across four footnotes, is choosing that most people won't look. Richard Sloan found in 1996 that the market treats cash earnings and accruals the same, even though accruals are less persistent. Limits of arbitrage then explain that someone who reads the footnotes can short, but may be blown up before the market corrects, so smart money often stays away from hype stocks. Companies burying inconvenient numbers in the footnotes is a rational bet: it disclosed them, it's legal, and it often works.

— Patrick Boyle
30:36

The AI winner is the person paying $20 a month

This round of "hidden debt" panic isn't the discovery of the next Enron; it's people finally reading the footnotes and realizing the true scale of spending. AI isn't fraud, it's obviously useful, but the biggest returns don't go to the giants burning hundreds of billions — they go to the founders using AI to start one-person businesses at about $20 a month. For the renter it's a great deal; for the side that spent enormous sums building and is still waiting for $20 monthly fees to accumulate into $2.5 trillion in annual revenue, it's terrible. As Jamie Dimon said: AI may pay off like the internet, but not on the timetable you expect.

Glossary

off-balance-sheet vehicle
A special-purpose entity that keeps debt off a company's books, though it still must be disclosed in the footnotes.
vendor financing
A seller lending to a buyer so the buyer can purchase its product, locking in the customer while taking on default risk.
big market delusion
Investors price every company in a new sector as the eventual winner, pushing total valuation beyond the truly addressable market.
incomplete revelation hypothesis
The harder information is to extract, the less fully the market prices it; facts buried in hard-to-read footnotes get undervalued.
limits of arbitrage
Even when mispricing is spotted, shorting can blow up because the market stays irrational, keeping smart money out.
