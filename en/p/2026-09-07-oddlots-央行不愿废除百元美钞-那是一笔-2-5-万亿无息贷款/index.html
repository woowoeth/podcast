Central Banks Won't Kill the $100 Bill: It's a $2.5 Trillion Interest-Free Loan

Cash use is declining, yet the dollar in circulation hits new highs. Central banks have an incentive to keep printing $100 bills: this money is effectively a $2.5 trillion interest-free loan to the U.S. government, at the cost of enabling global money launderers.

Money laundering scale hasn't changed in 30 years

The most commonly cited estimate for money laundering is 2% to 5% of global GDP annually, which at today's roughly $100 trillion GDP would be $2 trillion to $5 trillion. This figure comes from a rough estimate by former IMF chief Michel Camdessus in the late 1990s, and no one has since provided a better answer. Oliver reminds us that the more alarming point is that this percentage hasn't changed in three decades: decades of anti-money laundering efforts haven't shrunk the criminal economy; they've only barely kept it from growing faster than the overall economy.

— Oliver Bullough
9:10

Banks spend $200 billion a year on AML with near-zero effect

Banks complaining about KYC costs isn't just whining. The 1970 Bank Secrecy Act in the U.S. and the 1989 FATF global norms forced banks to hire compliance teams and install AI monitoring software. LexisNexis estimates global AML compliance costs around $200 billion a year—enough to end world hunger and provide clean water to everyone. But the system generates massive numbers of suspicious activity reports, and launderers still stay ahead of regulators. The problem isn't that banks aren't trying; it's that the whole system is costly, has weak feedback, and almost no one is held accountable for results.

— Oliver Bullough
12:14

Trade-based laundering is an order of magnitude bigger than cash smuggling

Cash smuggling makes bank-based laundering look small, but the bigger chunk doesn't involve money at all: value is hidden in goods. Mexico sees about $25 billion in cash inflows annually, while U.S. exports of John Deere and Caterpillar equipment become the counterpart for drug cartels; European luxury goods flow to China, and from China drugs and goods are imported, with triangular trade settled through misinvoicing. Global Financial Integrity estimates trade-based laundering at around $1 trillion a year. A million-dollar watch worn through customs is far easier than moving a million in cash.

17:18

The Vancouver model: Chinese cash demand meets drug cash supply

China's每人每年 only $50,000 forex conversion quota, yet wealthy Chinese have huge spending needs abroad. The Vancouver model matches this demand with drug cartels' dollar cash supply: cash bags are handed to Chinese gamblers outside Canadian casinos, the money is lost at the tables, and the gamblers owe a debt. This debt is offset by RMB transactions in China and subsequent drug sales. Viewed alone, the gambling looks like a loss, but across the whole chain, it's a triangular settlement that simultaneously repatriates drug funds and moves Chinese capital out. The model later expanded globally, with luxury goods, daily necessities, and drugs all serving as vehicles for moving value.

— Oliver Bullough
23:25

VAT loophole lets the same goods repeatedly extract 20%

The EU's zero-rated cross-border transactions were meant to avoid tax authorities auditing each other, but they became an ATM: goods entering the UK from Ireland don't incur VAT, and when sold to a related shell company, 20% is added; that shell exports the goods back to Ireland and claims a refund from the UK tax authority for the 20% never paid. This circular trade is called carousel fraud. The UK largely curbed it with multi-agency crackdowns, but fraudsters moved to mainland Europe, where it now totals about €50 billion a year, spreading from Lithuania to Portugal. Criminal organizations have no upper limit on competition: the more they cooperate, the bigger they get.

— Oliver Bullough
27:27

No one uses cash, yet central banks print record amounts

In the digital payment era, cash transactions are only about 9% in the UK and 13% in the US, so logically paper currency supply should shrink. After Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey raised the banknote paradox in 2009, the phenomenon intensified: UK currency in circulation doubled, and the US is near $2.5 trillion. Central banks attribute this to 'store of value,' but that doesn't hold—surveys show the average American adult has only a few hundred dollars on hand, while per capita currency in circulation exceeds $7,000. The Fed estimates about 65% of US banknotes are abroad, but that just pushes the question across borders: no one knows who actually holds these notes.

— Oliver Bullough
32:31

The $100 bill is a $2.5 trillion interest-free loan to the government

Seigniorage isn't simply profit from printing money: a $100 bill costs 9 cents to make, but it isn't 'sold' to you; you can redeem it for goods at face value anytime, so public cash holdings are essentially an interest-free loan. With nearly $2.5 trillion in circulation, the U.S. government gets interest-free funds equivalent to about 6% of its national debt; federal interest payments are about 19% of the budget, and if that $2.5 trillion had to pay interest, it would add about another percentage point. That's why central banks don't want to hear about abolishing the bill.

— Oliver Bullough
47:51

AML incentives are inverted: more reports, less crime prosecuted

Suspicious activity reports were meant to be real-time intelligence for law enforcement, but reality is the opposite: banks hire thousands of compliance officers to file reports, law enforcement lacks resources to read them, and most reports go into a black hole; banks can face billion-dollar fines for missing reports, but get no reward for doing it right. Side effects include debanking—many people suspected, often Muslims, get cut off from financial institutions. The guests' solution isn't hiring more compliance officers but generating fewer reports, actually reading them, and genuinely prosecuting financial criminals, raising the cost of laundering rather than drowning everyone in paperwork.

Glossary

seigniorage
Profit from issuing currency; in modern terms, it can be seen as an interest-free loan from currency holders to the government.
trade-based money laundering
Hiding and transferring illicit funds through misinvoicing, transshipment of high-value goods, and similar methods.
Vancouver model
A laundering cycle using casinos and debt to match Chinese wealthy individuals' cash demand with drug cartels' cash supply.
banknote paradox
The phenomenon where daily cash usage declines but the total value of banknotes in circulation keeps hitting new highs.
SAR (Suspicious Activity Report)
A form financial institutions file with law enforcement about suspected money laundering or criminal transactions; currently so numerous that no one reads them.
