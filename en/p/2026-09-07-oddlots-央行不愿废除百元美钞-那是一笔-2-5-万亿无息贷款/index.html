Central Banks Won't Kill the $100 Bill: It's a $2.5 Trillion Interest-Free Loan

Cash use keeps falling, yet currency in circulation keeps hitting records. Central banks have an incentive to keep printing $100 bills: the money is effectively a $2.5 trillion interest-free loan to the U.S. government, and the price is convenience for money launderers worldwide.

The scale of money laundering hasn't changed in thirty years

The most commonly used estimate of money laundering is 2% to 5% of global GDP per year, which at today's roughly $100 trillion GDP is $2 trillion to $5 trillion. That number comes from a rough estimate by former IMF managing director Michel Camdessus in the late 1990s, and no one has come up with a better answer since. Oliver warns that the more alarming fact is that this ratio hasn't moved in thirty years: decades of anti-money-laundering action have not shrunk the criminal economy, only barely held it back from growing faster than the overall economy.

— Oliver Bullough
9:10

Banks spend $200 billion a year on AML for near-zero effect

When banks complain that KYC is too expensive, it isn't just whining. The U.S. passed the Bank Secrecy Act in 1970, and in 1989 the FATF turned anti-money-laundering into a global norm, forcing banks to hire compliance teams and install AI monitoring software. LexisNexis estimates global AML compliance costs about $200 billion a year, enough to solve world hunger and provide clean water for everyone. Yet the system generates a huge volume of suspicious activity reports every year, and launderers still run ahead of regulators. The problem isn't that banks aren't trying; it's that the whole system is expensive, weakly feedback-driven, and almost no one is accountable for outcomes.

— Oliver Bullough
12:14

Trade-based laundering is an order of magnitude bigger than cash smuggling

Cash smuggling makes money laundering inside the banking system look small by comparison, but the bigger chunk never touches money at all: value is hidden in goods. About $25 billion in cash flows into Mexico each year, while John Deere and Caterpillar farm equipment exported from the U.S. becomes the consideration cartels get back; European luxury goods flow to China, and drugs and goods are imported back in China's direction, with the triangle settled through misinvoicing. Global Financial Integrity estimates this kind of trade-based laundering at about $1 trillion a year. A million-dollar watch on your wrist through customs is far easier than moving a million in cash.

— Oliver Bullough
17:18

The Vancouver model: Chinese cash demand meets cartel supply

Each person in China has only a $50,000 annual foreign-exchange quota, yet wealthy Chinese have plenty of demand to spend abroad. The Vancouver model matches that demand with the dollar cash supply from drug cartels: bags of cash outside Canadian casinos are handed to Chinese gamblers, the money is lost at the tables, and the gamblers run up gambling debts; that debt is offset on the other end by an RMB transaction inside China and the drug sales that follow. Looked at as just the gambling, it seems like a loss; looked at across the whole chain, it is a triangular settlement that simultaneously recycles cartel money and moves Chinese capital out. The model later spread worldwide, with luxury goods, consumer goods and drugs all serving as vehicles for moving value.

— Oliver Bullough
23:25

A VAT loophole lets the same goods repeatedly extract 20%

Zero-rated cross-border transactions in the EU were meant to keep national tax authorities from having to audit each other, but they became an ATM: goods entering the UK from Ireland don't have to charge VAT, and when sold to an affiliated shell company 20% is added; that shell company then re-exports the goods back to Ireland and can claim a refund from UK tax authorities for the 20% that was never paid. Repeat the loop, and that's why it's called carousel fraud. The UK once largely suppressed it through a multi-agency crackdown, and the fraudsters then moved to continental Europe, where it now runs about €50 billion a year, spreading from Lithuania all the way to Portugal. Criminal organizations have no ceiling on cooperation: the more they cooperate, the bigger they get.

— Oliver Bullough
27:27

Nobody uses cash, yet central banks are printing record notes

In the digital-payments era, cash transactions are down to about 9% in the UK and about 13% in the U.S., so by ordinary logic the supply of paper money should be shrinking. After Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey raised the banknote paradox in 2009, the phenomenon only intensified: the value of UK notes in circulation doubled, and the U.S. is approaching $2.5 trillion. Central banks blaming it on "store of value" doesn't hold up — surveys show the average American adult carries only a bit over $400, while per-capita U.S. currency in circulation works out to more than $7,000. The Fed estimates about 65% of U.S. bills are abroad, but that just pushes the question past the border: no one knows whose hands these bills are actually sitting in.

— Oliver Bullough
32:31

The $100 bill is a $2.5 trillion interest-free loan to the government

Seigniorage can't simply be understood as the profit from printing money: a $100 bill costs 9 cents, but it isn't "sold" to you — you can always exchange it back for goods at face value, so the cash the public holds is essentially an interest-free loan. With close to $2.5 trillion in U.S. currency in circulation, the U.S. government gets interest-free funding equal to about 6% of the national debt; federal interest payments are now about 19% of the budget, and if that $2.5 trillion also had to pay interest, you'd add roughly another percentage point. That is exactly why central banks don't want to hear anyone discuss abolishing cash.

— Oliver Bullough
47:51

AML incentives are inverted: the more reports, the less crime gets policed

Suspicious activity reports were meant to be real-time intelligence for law enforcement, but reality is the exact opposite: banks hire thousands of compliance officers to fill out reports, law enforcement has no resources to read them, and most reports fall into a black hole; a bank that misses a report can face billion-dollar fines, while doing it right earns no reward. The side effects include debanking — large numbers of people who come under suspicion, many of them Muslim, get cut off from financial institutions. The guest's solution isn't to hire more compliance officers, but to generate fewer reports, actually read them, and genuinely prosecute financial criminals — raise the cost of money laundering instead of drowning everyone in paperwork.

— Oliver Bullough

Glossary

seigniorage
The profit from issuing currency; in modern terms it can be seen as an interest-free loan the government gets from currency holders.
trade-based money laundering
Hiding and moving illicit funds through misinvoicing, transshipping high-value goods and similar methods.
Vancouver model
A laundering cycle that uses casinos and debt to match wealthy Chinese demand for cash with drug cartels' supply of cash.
banknote paradox
The phenomenon in which everyday cash use keeps falling while the total value of notes in circulation keeps hitting new highs.
SAR (Suspicious Activity Report)
A form financial institutions file with law enforcement to report suspected money laundering or criminal transactions; there are now so many that no one reads them.
