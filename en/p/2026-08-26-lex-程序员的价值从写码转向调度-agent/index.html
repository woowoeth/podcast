Programmer Value Shifts to Directing Agents: DHH Wrote an Entire Product with AI

DHH has written all of Omachi's code with AI and found a counterintuitive conclusion: agents don't need verbose instructions, they need to run in parallel; what's truly scarce is judgment, aesthetics, and architectural constraints.

This episode isn't a bystander's commentary, but a first-hand report from Rails creator DHH on rebuilding a commercial product with AI: complete with workflows, cost figures, and architectural lessons.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

9:27

AI poses no existential threat to programmers

DHH feels only excitement and optimism about AI's role in programming, not existential threat. He quotes Lenin — 'There are decades where nothing happens, and weeks where decades happen' — saying he's seen decades of progress in the past nine months. He positions himself as someone who has lived through both the internet and AI transitions, calling that experience rare and fortunate. This framing explains why all his subsequent judgments rest on the premise that 'change is real.'

— DHH
13:50

The watershed in AI coding is usability, not intelligence

Opus 4.5, released on November 24, 2025, he calls a watershed for AI programming. He recalls that the first time he used it, the output quality was startlingly close to code he'd write himself, making him lean back and think 'what just happened.' He stresses that the key improvement isn't just intelligence but usability: AI can use tools and check its own work, so it can accomplish genuinely meaningful tasks. This provides direct evidence for the later claim that 'AI is better than the average programmer.'

— DHH
22:02

In legacy codebases, AI must be led by senior programmers

Basecamp 5 is 37signals' first truly AI-accelerated product, but early on, letting designers vibe code directly led to numerous PRs that broke the system architecture, eventually requiring manual cleanup. DHH's conclusion isn't that AI is unreliable, but that on existing large codebases, preserving architectural integrity requires experienced programmers to guide the AI. For teams about to integrate AI into legacy code, this is a concrete operational warning.

— DHH
31:43

No longer settle for generic tools; build only the 5% you need

DHH shares how he used AI to write a writing app in C++ and Qt in 20 minutes as a replacement for Tapura, fully switching within two days. He believes in 'building only the 5% you need': AI lets individual developers easily create customized software without settling for generic tools. He also suggests publishing such small tools to GitHub, where AI automatically manages the repo, writes the README, and handles releases. This is a concrete example of 'personal software' moving from slogan to daily practice.

— DHH
1:02:41

The more detailed the instructions, the worse the agent performs

A counterintuitive finding: over-specified instructions actually hurt agent performance. DHH cites Claude Code's Opus 5, whose system prompt was reduced by 80%, because agents no longer need as much human instruction and can even be harmed by over-specification. He analogizes to the 'pointy-haired boss' — those who think they know more and demand micromanagement actually drag down performance. The right approach is to give the agent enough ambiguity to explore the best solution on its own. This directly challenges the mainstream intuition that 'the more detailed the prompt, the better.'

— DHH
1:38:16

Flow in the agent era comes from parallelism, not immersion

Agentic programming requires a different toolset, with the core shift from single-threaded to parallel. Deep immersion is flow when hand-writing code; but agents are both too fast and too slow, requiring waiting, so you must run multiple agents simultaneously. To that end, DHH wrote Herder — essentially tmux plus agent notifications, ringing when an agent finishes or needs a decision. A month ago he felt a single machine wasn't fast enough, so he bought several Geekom mini PCs, put them in a closet, and networked them via Tailscale; he can now run about 16 threads in parallel. From the perspective of 'maintaining flow,' he argues that parallel scheduling isn't optional but the inevitable form of programming in the agent era.

— DHH
2:35:00

The strongest model isn't necessarily the one to use; it's over ten times pricier

DHH offers his current model selection judgment: the best model is Fable, followed by Opus 5, but the gap is small. He tested by 'translating a Python library to Rust': Fable was fastest (45 minutes) but cost $550; Sonnet and Grok 4.6 cost much less (about $40-55) but took longer. The conclusion: you can't just look at the 'strongest model'; you must trade off speed, cost, and capability per task. He also relays Shopify CTO's research — agent-reviewed PRs cause fewer issues in production.

— DHH
3:09:33

AI eliminates bullshit jobs before programmers

DHH cites David Graeber's Bullshit Jobs research: a UK survey found about 30% of workers believe their jobs are worthless. He sticks with small companies and no VC, partly because he doubts the efficiency of large-scale programmer collaboration. He thinks the current wave of layoffs is more a hangover from pandemic-era overhiring, with AI as an excuse, but it will indeed eliminate some roles. He also uses an F1 analogy for the future: tens of thousands of people doing something with no intrinsic value, yet we're willing to spend billions — after AI liberates humanity, there may be more 'frivolous' but real professions.

— DHH

Glossary

vibe coding
A programming style where software requirements are handed to an AI agent without the human reviewing every line of implementation.
agentic engineering
An engineering approach centered on AI agents, accomplished through task definition, scheduling, and review.
harness
A tool layer that runs and manages AI agents, such as Claude Code.
fast takeoff
The hypothesis that AI capabilities will surge dramatically in a short period, bringing disruptive change.
bullshit jobs
A concept by anthropologist David Graeber referring to jobs that workers themselves consider worthless.
