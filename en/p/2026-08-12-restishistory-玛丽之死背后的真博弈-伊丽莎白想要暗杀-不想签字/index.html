Elizabeth Wanted Mary Assassinated, Then Was Forced to Sign the Execution

Elizabeth I did not want a public execution of Mary, hoping someone would kill her privately under the Bond of Association; the Privy Council insisted on legal process, and in the end, after the Queen signed, they defied her wishes and sent the death warrant to Fotheringhay overnight.

What Mary waited for was not freedom but bait in a beer barrel

Gifford's plan was to pass messages via beer barrels: letters for Mary were put in a waterproof container, inserted through the bunghole of a beer barrel, carried to Chartley, and taken out by Mary's steward. The system worked both ways, so Mary could reply. But Gifford had already been turned by Walsingham, and the whole "French embassy channel" was watched from behind the scenes by Phelippes. Mary thought she was rebuilding contact with the outside world; in fact every letter was opened, deciphered, and sealed back up exactly as before. What she waited for was not freedom but carefully designed bait.

28:50

The codebreaker drew a gallows on the transcript, not a prediction but a marker

Mary's dictated reply contained the coded reference to the six gentlemen, which was exactly what Phelippes wanted. After deciphering Mary's secret letter, Phelippes copied it into English, drew a gallows on the transcript, and called it the "bloody letter." He was not predicting the outcome; he was marking the key node in the chain of evidence. Babington and his fellow conspirators were kept in the dark from beginning to end; when Mary was taken out of the house on August 11 to go stag hunting, she still thought the men arriving might be rescuers, but in fact it was Walsingham closing the net. Letters, decipherment, gallows: this process turned Mary from "someone implicated in a conspiracy" into "the person who approved the assassination of the Queen."

38:39

An empty throne is more oppressive than a queen in the room

In the presence chamber at Fotheringhay, Elizabeth did not appear, but her empty throne stood high under the canopy, looking down over the whole trial. Lord Burghley sat in the presiding seat; he had waited decades for this moment. Mary had very little room to manoeuvre: she was not an English subject but an anointed monarch, and she refused to recognise the court's legitimacy. Burghley sent delegations to persuade her again and again, and finally Christopher Hatton got her to enter with the line "if you are innocent, avoiding trial will only damage your honour." The moment she walked in, she found herself facing not the law but a stage of power where the ending had already been decided.

— Tom
45:00

Walsingham's answer denied everything and answered nothing

In court Mary accused Walsingham of tampering with her letters — which was in fact true. His reply was diplomatic-grade phrasing: "I call God and the whole world to witness that as a private person I have done nothing unbecoming an honest man, nor as a minister of state have I done anything unworthy of my place." The sentence denied everything in principle while responding to no specific charge. Stephen Alford called it "a masterpiece of phrasing that says everything and nothing." Mary then turned to Burghley and said he was indeed her enemy, and Burghley shot back: "I am the enemy of Queen Elizabeth's enemies." This exchange was no longer a legal debate but a showdown of power.

— Tom
53:59

The Puritans refused assassination because justice must be seen

After signing, Elizabeth still did not want the death warrant carried out; what she wanted was for someone to privately "shorten Mary's life" under the oath of the Bond of Association. Walsingham, ill, wrote to her keeper Paulet to convey the Queen's displeasure. Paulet's refusal is crucial: "I will never let my conscience sink so low, nor leave such a stain to posterity — to shed blood without law and warrant." For this Elizabeth called him "dainty." This explains why the Privy Council did not choose assassination: these men were hardline Puritans, yet they firmly believed justice must be seen, and that an execution must follow legal process and carry God's approval. Assassination was politically cheap but religiously unacceptable.

— Tom
1:03:03

Elizabeth won the law, Mary won the narrative

Mary took off her black velvet outer gown, revealing a crimson silk petticoat and undergarments — the colour of a Catholic martyr. The Protestants present completely lost their composure, and all the official reports omit this scene; only the six Catholic waiting women around her spread the details. The night before, she had written her last letter to Henry III, saying she was condemned for the Catholic faith and for her claim to the succession, but after her death she was forbidden to declare that she died "for the Catholic faith." The flash of red was the visual version of that letter: making the execution look, in public opinion, like religious persecution. Elizabeth won the law, Mary won the narrative.

— Tom
1:05:14

Three axe blows, a wig and a dog under the skirt: the execution became a farce

The executioner's first blow landed on the back of her head, and Mary cried out in agony, "Lord Jesus, receive my soul"; only the second blow reached her neck, and still did not sever it, so the executioner had to use the blade to separate the head like chopping meat. When he lifted the head to show it, as custom demanded, there was only a wig in his hand — Mary's head had already rolled to the edge of the scaffold. The waiting women were then led away, and the executioner found under Mary's skirt a Scottish terrier she had kept, howling in the pool of blood. These turns made the execution an embarrassing farce, and they also show that Mary performed to her own script right up to death: she wanted not only martyrdom but to leave her enemies humiliated.

Glossary

Bond of Association
A bond signed by English Protestants in 1584, promising that Mary could be put to death if she took part in an assassination of Elizabeth.
Act for the Queen's Safety
A 1585 act of Parliament providing that a claimant to the throne implicated in rebellion, invasion or assassination would be tried and executed for treason.
presence chamber
The room in which a monarch received subjects; at Mary's trial an empty throne was placed in the chamber to symbolise the presence of royal authority.
Star Chamber
The judicial body of the Privy Council at Westminster, which gave final judgment in Mary's case and declared her guilty.
cipher
The encrypted writing Mary used to communicate with the outside world; Phelippes was responsible for deciphering it and sealing the letters back up as before.
