Skipping grades beats lock-step progression

Liang Jianzhang sees himself as a beneficiary of accelerated learning: as a ninth-grader he focused on computer competitions, then self-studied high school mathematics, physics, and chemistry, and entered Fudan's youth class at fifteen, bypassing high school entirely. He therefore argues that gifted students should compress middle school, high school, and even college into accelerated paths—subjects like physics need only two passes, not three; the efficiency problem isn't the curriculum but the gaokao system locking everyone into the same pace.

35:32

Ctrip's four founders meshed in less than six months

Liang Jianzhang, Ji Qi, Shen Nanpeng, and Fan Min didn't have clearly defined roles initially and experienced friction, but the meshing period lasted only three to five months—soon Ji Qi handled external relations, Shen Nanpeng became CFO and handled fundraising, Liang Jianzhang focused on internal management and IT, and Fan Min brought hospitality-industry management experience. He attributes Ctrip's early success to two things: Six Sigma–style standardized processes that refined call-center scripts, and process and data management that achieved ticket distribution across all channels—airports, train stations, and aircraft.

— Liang Jianzhang
47:44

3+2 hybrid didn't hurt productivity because only Ctrip does it

After the pandemic, Ctrip ran a 3+2 hybrid-office experiment for engineers and other specialist roles and found no decline in code output or overall performance—employee satisfaction and loyalty rose noticeably. But Liang Jianzhang admits this partly depended on Ctrip being the only large Chinese company doing it—employees who compared themselves horizontally felt happier, and this may not generalize. Silicon Valley founders later privately told Luo Yonghao that while they publicly support hybrid work, they privately believe efficiency does fall; some admitted to playing basketball on remote days, only to encounter coworkers doing the same on the court.

1:02:56

Mobile Internet's lag forced a company-wide pivot

Around 2012, Ctrip lagged visibly in mobile development, lost market share, and faced price wars. Liang Jianzhang ended his academic career and returned as CEO. He created an independent mobile division, pulled in the best engineers, personally drove app development, and mandated that all product discussions use phone screenshots rather than desktop screenshots. In six months to a year, Ctrip's app went from lagging to industry-leading, and the organization shifted from centralized to divisional to accelerate iteration.

— Liang Jianzhang
1:16:09

Ctrip went global eight years ago, not multi-category

Liang Jianzhang believed travel shouldn't stay confined to mainland China but should expand one category globally—a completely different path from Alibaba, Tencent, and Meituan's continuous category expansion domestically. Eight years ago, Ctrip acquired the Trip.com domain, first targeting Hong Kong and Taiwan, then Asia. Today it ranks first in Singapore and Malaysia, approaches first in South Korea, and leads in Japan flights. But he admits travel's supply chain is deeply local, and Ctrip still hasn't fully overtaken Booking's Agoda across Asia.

— Liang Jianzhang
1:47:52

Demographic and economic experts' knowledge mismatch

When Liang Jianzhang studied innovation, he found Japan's shift from innovation boom to decline tracked closely with population decline. Looking at China's data, he discovered a puzzle: demographers hold the real data but think fewer people is good; economists readily accept population-shortage logic but lack accurate data. At the time, the public and policymakers still thought the one-child policy posed no problem.

— Liang Jianzhang
2:14:52

Families without children freeload off families with children

Liang Jianzhang argues childbearing creates externalities: one child's future pension and innovation benefits the entire society, but childbearing's cost falls entirely on the family—so families who don't or rarely have children are freeloading off large families. He admits the claim easily provokes backlash (the "birth-encouragement bureau director" joke stems from this), but he insists it's the fundamental logic for government to provide real cash subsidies, not simply moral appeals to young people.

— Liang Jianzhang
3:07:44

Innovation shouldn't go to AI—it lacks mortality

Liang Jianzhang says ChatGPT's biggest philosophical impact is this: in theory, neural connections can replicate nearly all human abilities. But he firmly believes technology innovation shouldn't be dominated by AI—AI's creativity inevitably brings uncontrollable hallucinations, whereas human ethical judgment springs from "having birth and death, instinctively wanting descendants to continue"—something AI can only simulate, never truly possess. When managing risk, he favors multiple small AIs that check each other, with human-verifiable intermediate steps, not a single black-box large model.

— Liang Jianzhang
