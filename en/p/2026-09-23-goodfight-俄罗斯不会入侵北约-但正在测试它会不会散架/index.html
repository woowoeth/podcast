Russia Won't Invade NATO, but It Is Testing Whether NATO Will Fall Apart

Russia has no capacity for a ground offensive; its real goal is to corrode NATO's political cohesion — using drones, parcel bombs and the grey zone to force the US to expose its divisions in a crisis.

No ground-invasion risk, but hybrid-war escalation

Joshi says plainly: there is no risk of a Russian conventional ground offensive against NATO, this is not the kind of alarm it was in January 2022. The entire security apparatus of Estonia and Finland exists to detect exactly that kind of movement, and every port, naval base and forest route in western Russia is covered with intelligence and technical sensors. But another risk is rising: Russia's tolerance for hybrid operations below the Article V threshold is increasing. They have already used drones against Ukrainian cargo planes that might be bound for Ukraine, put explosives in DHL parcels in Leipzig, and sabotaged power plants and railways across Europe. The question is: would they actually fire missiles at Polish facilities? Joshi thinks Russia is still quite cautious and wants to avoid dragging NATO into a broader conflict, but that debate is not settled in Europe.

Poland sounds the loudest alarm, the Baltics say it's fine

The most alarming signal this week came from Poland. Tusk said intelligence indicated Russia might attack Poland and other NATO allies — not a conventional ground attack, but drones and missiles landing on NATO territory that Russia could portray as accidents. But Latvia's leadership, including the defence minister and the president, said there is no reason to panic and no elevated operational risk. Estonia also said it sees no risk of Russia aggressing in that way. Joshi says he has spoken to intelligence officials in multiple countries and there is no reason to fear a ground offensive. The disagreement is itself the information: Poland is raising the alarm, the Baltic states are cooling things down, and Russia is watching who believes whom.

Russia's real goal is to corrode NATO cohesion

Joshi points to Russia's political objective: not to conquer NATO territory or occupy Western Europe, but to weaken and break apart NATO's unity. The mechanism works like this: if a drone lands on Polish territory and blows up a warehouse, Russia says it was an accident, and then Trump says, "I spoke to Putin and he assured me it was an accident" — that lays the alliance's internal divisions bare. Under the Biden administration, Jake Sullivan would call the Russians and say that if this continues, you are risking us joining the war on Ukraine's side, and we will retaliate. But under the Trump administration, does Russia really believe Trump would step into a crisis the same way? So Russia has an opportunity not only to retaliate against attacks on Russia, but to test NATO's cohesion and its boundaries.

19:40

Trump on Ukraine: criticises in public, shares intelligence in private

Joshi says Trump's support for Ukraine is quite limited, far below Biden's. The US has effectively let weapons aid lapse; Ukraine desperately needs Patriot interceptor missiles, but because the US has used up so much in the Iran war, it has given Ukraine almost no interceptors recently. Diplomatically, the administration leans toward Russia and expects Ukraine to make concessions. But there are two exceptions: first, the new Graham bill, which the president has agreed to, would impose new sanctions on Russian entities and force Trump to levy tariffs on countries that buy Russian oil and do not reduce their purchases — in effect China and India, and possibly Central European countries. Second, Trump is quietly, not publicly, providing intelligence to Ukraine to help it strike Russian energy facilities. That is the strange reversal: Trump criticises Ukraine overall, but secret assistance is still happening.

26:10

The Strait of Hormuz is still half-closed

The Trump administration insists this is not a war; Trump calls it an "excursion." But the Strait of Hormuz remains largely closed, with oil traffic far below pre-war levels. There is no deal with Iran, no resolution. There are no formal limits on Iran's nuclear programme and no formal commitment to reopen Hormuz. Trump faces the midterms in a month and desperately needs to avoid a sharp escalation of the war that would send oil prices soaring. But politically he cannot make the concessions to Iran needed for a deal, because that would be too humiliating, too obviously equivalent to defeat, and would look extremely bad before the election. So it is a stalemate: Iran has considerable control over the strait, with occasional clashes — the US bombs Iranian coastal missile bases, Iran strikes tankers trying to pass through Hormuz.

Houthis used Claude to improve ballistic missiles

Joshi tells a shocking detail: when Anthropic published its report on harmful uses of Claude, one of the examples was Houthis using Claude to improve their ballistic missile programme. This group, widely seen as technologically primitive, is using the most advanced AI on Earth to improve the guidance systems of the ballistic missiles they fire at Middle East shipping. Mounk adds that being able — at least until being cut off — to access the most advanced American technology to strike America and America's allies is itself a fascinating story. This connects the Ukraine and Russia discussion with the Houthi discussion: in modern war, defensive capability dominates on the ground, but offensive capability dominates in the air.

38:20

Perception is the primary problem on the battlefield

Joshi offers a unified framework to explain why ground defence dominates while air strikes remain effective: it is all about perception. To hit something, you first need to perceive it — find it on the battlefield, then fire at it. That is much harder than it sounds. Perception is the primary problem on the battlefield; actually firing is secondary. On the Ukrainian front line, this is a sensor-saturated environment: small drones everywhere, thermal cameras detecting movement at night, radar penetrating foliage, satellites targeting the area. Anything that moves gets hit; it is a kill zone. But you cannot perceive everywhere equally easily. Drones can see ten kilometres behind the battlefield, but fifty kilometres back, satellite coverage is patchy. It is like the fog of war in a real-time strategy game — you send units out and the fog lifts, but where you have not been remains fog. That is why Iran can still launch ballistic missiles, and why Houthis can fire missiles from the mountains of Yemen into the Red Sea.

Glossary

Article V
NATO's collective defence clause: an attack on one member is considered an attack on all.
hybrid warfare
Mixed methods below the threshold of conventional war, including cyberattacks, sabotage and disinformation.

The chokepoint from the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden, a critical node for global oil shipping.
