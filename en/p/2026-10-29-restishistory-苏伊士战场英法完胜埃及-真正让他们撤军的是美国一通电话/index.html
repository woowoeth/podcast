Anglo-French military victory in Suez, cut short by American financial pressure

9:20

Three-Power Conspiracy: Britain, France, and Israel Plot War at a Secret Paris Villa

Selwyn Lloyd, Britain's Foreign Secretary, disguised himself as a flu patient and traveled to a villa in Sèvres, a Paris suburb—a former Resistance headquarters—where French PM Guy Mollet and FM Christian Pineau were waiting. In the adjacent room sat Israeli PM Ben-Gurion, the one-eyed Dayan, and future PM Peres. After a fish dinner, they finalized the plan by midnight: Israel would strike Sinai first; Britain and France would follow with an ultimatum demanding both sides withdraw, giving them a pretext for military intervention. Lloyd harbored doubts but didn't dare voice them because Eden had already committed.

16:39

Macmillan's Gamble: A Military Victory Will Restore Confidence in Sterling

Chancellor Harold Macmillan assured the Cabinet he understood Eisenhower and that Americans would stay neutral. Yet simultaneously, the Treasury and his own chief economic advisor warned that sterling reserves were under pressure and American support was essential—any unilateral action was unthinkable. Macmillan inverted the logic entirely. He believed that decisive military action to seize Suez would actually restore market confidence in the pound. He became one of the Cabinet's most hawkish voices—a prediction later proved completely backwards, as military victory and currency collapse turned out to be cause and effect, not opposites.

24:05

Two Days to Destroy Egypt's Air Force, But Political Victory Remains Out of Reach

After Nasser rejected the ultimatum, RAF and Navy aircraft launched from Cyprus and three carriers. In three days they destroyed nearly all of Egypt's air force—260 Soviet-made jets—and lost only 3 pilots. Israeli forces were crossing Sinai toward the canal; Egyptian forces retreating to the Nile Delta; Egyptian officers debating whether to wage guerrilla war or surrender. But Britain and France never destroyed Cairo Radio. Nasser kept broadcasting Churchill-style defiance speeches to his nation. The Arab world rallied behind him. The more Britain and France won militarily, the more their political calculations collapsed.

31:16

Oil Embargo and UN Isolation Strike Simultaneously

Saudi Arabia imposed an oil embargo. From December 1956 to May 1957, Britain had to ration petrol—each person got 200 miles of driving per month. Canada's PM wrote a private letter condemning Eden for using war as a smokescreen for Soviet brutality in Hungary. At the UN, Dulles himself introduced a ceasefire resolution; only Britain, France, Israel, Australia, and New Zealand voted against. Britain and France were utterly isolated from the world while Soviet tanks crushed the Hungarian Revolution—both stories fought for the front page.

48:02

Tank Columns at the Canal's Edge; One American Word Halts the Advance

British tank columns rolled unopposed 23 miles south of Port Said. Soldiers didn't dare believe how easily they were winning, drinking whiskey as they advanced—then the lead tank stopped. Command had received orders: the Americans said to halt. The real pressure was sterling. Treasury officials told Macmillan the reserves couldn't hold; he had to call Washington immediately. The US Treasury replied: if you call ceasefire by midnight, we'll support an IMF loan. Simultaneously, Britain's UN ambassador reported America was preparing oil-sanctions resolutions. Within hours, even Macmillan, initially the war's most vocal hawk, shifted to support ceasefire. The Cabinet voted to halt the entire operation.

56:12

Eden Lies in Parliament, Then Vacations in Jamaica While Troops Withdraw

Troops withdrew completely by December 3—a humiliation for the Conservative Party. At his last Commons appearance before Christmas, Eden was directly asked whether he had pre-coordinated with Israel and France. He took a step fatal to a British prime minister: he lied publicly, denying any plans to attack or any advance knowledge. Years later, former cabinet secretary Nutting published his memoir and exposed the truth. But everyone knew it was a lie at the time. Even worse: while his troops still withdrew from Port Said, Eden accepted an invitation to Ian Fleming's estate in Jamaica for a holiday—a public-relations catastrophe. Churchill's son Randolph even wrote that Hitler never vacationed in Jamaica while trapped in Stalingrad.

1:01:29

Even Victory Would Have Left Them with an Untenable Endgame

The host points out that even if Britain and France had won, their end-state made no sense. Two years earlier, Britain had withdrawn from administering the canal because it was too expensive to maintain; retaking it just meant returning to square one. France's dream of overthrowing Nasser and occupying Cairo and Alexandria was pure fantasy. France was already losing in Algeria, had just lost in Vietnam, and yet imagined it could conquer Egypt. The only explanation is colonial muscle memory—the ghost of past power, not a sober assessment of current strength.

1:11:58

The Real Legacy: Not Empire's End, But Submission to America

The common narrative says Suez ended the British Empire and inflicted national trauma—both wrong. India and Africa had already left; the empire was already shrinking; Suez just made it visible. In 1959, Macmillan ran for reelection alongside Suez-tainted figures and won a majority of over 100 seats. Voters cared more about the new washing machine they could buy than about Suez. The real shift happened inside the Foreign Office. One internal report concluded: never again without American support. From Macmillan forward, every prime minister enshrined alignment with American foreign policy as the first principle of British diplomacy—which, ironically, also made it harder for Britain to commit fully to European integration.
