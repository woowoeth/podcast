You Can Raise a Securely Attached Child Even If Your Own Childhood Wasn't Safe

What determines a child's attachment style isn't what their parents lived through, but whether those parents can narrate that experience coherently and understand their own and others' mental states — a capacity that can be trained.

17:07

Separation doesn't matter; reunion does

In the Strange Situation, the child is briefly left in an unfamiliar room — whether they cry or fuss is not actually the key indicator. Steele states plainly that behavior at separation is "important, but far less important than at reunion" — the moment the parent returns, whether the child approaches you, looks at you, clings to you for a "refuel" is what the classification rests on. Secure children have a strategy: they know what to do when distressed, and cortisol drops after contact with the caregiver. Avoidant children look calm "like a little cucumber," crawling off to play with toys, but studies measure their cortisol rising just the same — and it doesn't come back down. So "looks fine" does not equal fine.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
20:42

Avoidance is the best solution the child could devise

Avoidant attachment isn't coldness; it's a defensive strategy formed by nine to twelve months of age. Steele says these children have already learned, from the interactions observed in their homes: when I'm distressed, the caregiver probably won't come, or I can't turn to him — I have to carry it myself. So they push away the pain of "I can only rely on myself" and turn to spinning a top. Sarah Bren adds the key point: this is still a defense attempting to reduce stress, it just doesn't work. Steele agrees — "this is the best defense they could come up with." It's an organized strategy, not chaos.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
25:58

The core of disorganized attachment is "fright without solution"

Mary Main summed up disorganized attachment in three words: fright without solution. The child is pulled by two forces at once — desperately wanting the caregiver to protect them, and desperately fearing that person — and the two incompatible facts can't be reconciled, so behavior collapses: when the parent returns, the child walks toward the door instead, freezes, huddles in a corner. Steele cautions that the term is now overused by the general public, far removed from its original set of clear behavioral observation criteria. She also stresses this isn't the child consciously choosing "I'll use this strategy" — it's the best choice that infant could make at that moment.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
28:01

Even in secure attachment, attunement happens less than half the time

Steele offers a data point that loosens the grip on many parents: even in securely attached parent-child pairs, the proportion of "in-sync moments" is below 50%. It's precisely in the mismatches that children learn — mom didn't catch it, and the infant is forced to think "wait, where is she? How do I get her back?" Tronick's still-face experiment is the extreme version: the mother suddenly goes blank, and the infant reaches out, smiles, calls out trying to pull her back. But if there are too many mismatches, the child gives up and shifts toward defensive patterns closer to insecure attachment. So the question isn't whether you miss — it's how much you miss.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
30:01

Co-sleeping until age seven buys you no secure attachment

Steele directly calls out popular parenting methods: co-sleeping with a child until age five or seven has "no one-to-one correspondence" with raising a securely attached child. She files this perfection-chasing trend under attachment parenting — some of its ideas are reasonable, but as a whole it pushes parents toward the goal of "perfect," and perfect "isn't in the original writings of Bowlby, Ainsworth, Main, or mainstream attachment theory at all." What actually works is small everyday interactions: following the child's lead, letting the child feel you're nearby and that you hold them in mind. She also warns against the reverse: rushing in to feed a child before they've even registered they're hungry erases the child's chance to recognize their own internal states and figure out their own solutions.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
40:18

Breaking the intergenerational cycle depends on how you tell the story

The London Parent-Child Project conducted Adult Attachment Interviews with 100 couples in late pregnancy, then ran the Strange Situation with the mother at 12 months and with the father at 18 months. Result: how parents narrated their own childhood before the child was born predicted the child's attachment classification at 12 months (for mothers) and 18 months (for fathers). More crucial is the stratification: people who had experienced adversity (parent hospitalization, low income, unemployment over three months, etc.) but could talk about their parents' mental states, thoughts, and feelings at the time had a significantly higher probability of a securely attached child; those with the same adversity but without that capacity had overwhelmingly insecure children. Steele's conclusion: what matters isn't what happened in your childhood, but how you understand those experiences.

— Dr. Miriam Steele
41:19

Two words for secure attachment: flexibility and coherence

Steele distills secure attachment into two words: flexibility and coherence. Coherence means — does your story have a beginning, middle, and end? Does it hold up against the challenging questions of the Adult Attachment Interview? She gives a typical mismatch: you say your mother was loving, protective, wonderful, but when asked for an example, you describe a school building or your garden. When the overall narrative and the specific details don't line up, that's incoherence. Flexibility shows in a system that adjusts as the child grows: what an infant needs, what a school-age child needs, what an adolescent needs are completely different, and secure parents can pivot — the child says they want to read on their own, and you think "how do I support that while it remains part of our closeness."

— Dr. Miriam Steele
45:27

When you lose control, step away for thirty seconds first

For parents who want to be "cycle breakers," Steele's practical advice is: learn to observe yourself, especially to recognize your own triggers, and think through "what is making me so sad, so angry at this moment that should be beautiful." She explicitly doesn't think yelling back is a good approach, but short of anything that could be called abuse, stepping back often helps — even just thirty seconds, saying "Mommy is really upset right now, I need a moment" — which itself tells the child: we all have moments of falling apart, we're not superhuman, but there's a strategy for handling it. She also suggests finding a clinician to help identify your own patterns, because "we are pattern-making creatures," and the attachment system is essentially the regulation of emotional states.

— Dr. Miriam Steele

Glossary

Strange Situation
A roughly 20-minute laboratory procedure designed by Mary Ainsworth that classifies infant attachment through brief separation and reunion.
Adult Attachment Interview
An interview that activates the attachment system by probing childhood experiences; what's assessed is the coherence of the telling, not the experiences themselves.
reflective functioning
The capacity to understand the thoughts, feelings, and intentions behind one's own and others' behavior, considered trainable.
good enough mother
Winnicott's concept: being responsive most of the time is enough; you needn't be 100% perfect.
still face
Tronick's experiment: the mother suddenly goes blank, and researchers observe how the infant tries to pull her back into interaction.
