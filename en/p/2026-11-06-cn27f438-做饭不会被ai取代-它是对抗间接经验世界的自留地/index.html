Cooking won't be replaced by AI: it's a reservation against the world of indirect experience

Professional cooking and shrinking home cooking are irreversible trends, but taste cannot be replicated by data—so the kitchen stove becomes the last place where people can keep direct, embodied presence in the world of concepts.

4:17

The ‘firewood’in basics conceals a complete survival skillset

Li Yun points out that modern people struggle to understand why ancients put ‘firewood’ first in ‘firewood, rice, oil, salt’—today, lighting a stove is just turning a knob, but anciently, knowing which firewood catches easily was a skill even four- or five-year-olds had to master. Meng Qingyan adds his childhood camping experience: without alcohol blocks, using newspaper to kindle the firewood was exhausting, showing how the preliminary steps modern people skip cost the ancients tremendous effort.

— Li Yun
9:41

Even Zhou royal kitchens had full-time cloth washers

Li Yun describes from the Rites of Zhou the division of labor in royal kitchens—three thousand years ago, Zhou courts employed a position called ‘秘人’ (cloth keepers) solely to clean the cloth covering food. He uses this to show that the king's court was where civilization accumulated densest, where eating was most elaborate and meticulous, and thus where dietary practices left behind recordable traces, while ordinary people's eating habits remain obscure for want of documentation.

— Li Yun
21:33

Home cooking will shrink but professional cooking won't disappear

Meng Qingyan raises that food delivery platforms created ‘an unprecedented separation of eating from cooking in human history’—anciently, food and cooking costs consumed 70-90% of income, but that share has collapsed. Li Yun concludes: by pure efficiency of nutrition and satiation, home cooking ‘is a bad deal,’ so it will keep shrinking, but as long as people eat, cooking itself—social and professional cooking—won't vanish.

— Li Yun/Meng Qingyan
24:06

Why people cook differs; there is no single answer

Meng Qingyan explains his motive is ‘瘫嘴’—food delivery tastes monotonous day after day, so he cooks to break the taste monotony and to recreate his parents' cooking from childhood. He notes that another friend, He Bi (何必), cooks for an opposite reason: He Bi's mind is typically scattered, but cooking requires his full attention—inattention burns the dish. They conclude: each person's reason for cooking differs, which is precisely how modern life requires people to find their own meaning.

— Meng Qingyan
31:28

Would robots taking over cooking completely be good?

Li Yun poses a hypothesis: if humanoid robots mastered all cooking skills and entirely replaced humans at cooking, would that be desirable? His answer is no—the more advanced AI becomes, the more human embodied ability and sensation gets ‘carved away,’ and people might end up living narrower lives. He presses further: AI can read and speak, but ‘can it recognize taste?’ Even if AI judges flavor, that judgment bears no necessary relation to whether ‘I’ myself can taste that flavor.

— Li Yun
36:27

Spice level quantifies, but what spice tastes like to each person doesn't

They discuss the limits of AI and data-driven cooking: temperature, knife thickness, salt by the gram can be precisely replicated, but once ‘spice’ is assigned a value of one to one hundred, different people feel completely different things eating the same spicy dish—Li Yun's spice tolerance clearly exceeds Meng Qingyan's. Meng extends this deeper: when he cooks saltier, friends still eat it and taste ‘heavy mouth,’ because ‘my cooking is me’—the cook brings mood and their day's state into the food, and that is precisely cooking's most unstandardizable part, as proved by a Tianjin small restaurant owner's remark: ‘if my mood is bad, maybe I didn't perform well.’

— Meng Qingyan
39:57

Cooking is the reservation against the world of indirect experience

Meng Qingyan proposes: history is the future. From history's trend of labor specialization and delegated convenience, the process is irreversible, but cooking's meaning might lie precisely in preserving human embodied presence—the direct connection between person and world, nature, and time. He cites unfollowing travel influencers as an example: when video capture technology is so strong that visiting in person doesn't compare to the screen, this challenge to embodied presence becomes increasingly common, and cooking as the most accessible direct experience in daily life might become the smallest unit of resistance against this trend.

— Meng Qingyan
44:49

Neither embrace nor resist AI—it becomes infrastructure

Meng Qingyan responds to He Bi's earlier question whether he is a ‘embracer’ or ‘resister’: he is neither, because AI technology will, like DOS becoming Windows or handwriting becoming Word, gradually become life's default and infrastructure over time—this cannot be resisted. Meanwhile, he has followed many cooks' short videos, especially how avant-garde chefs' imaginative approaches often make him think ‘you can do it like that?,’ showing that AI and new tools can open more techniques and imagination for cooking, not just threaten embodied presence.

— Meng Qingyan
