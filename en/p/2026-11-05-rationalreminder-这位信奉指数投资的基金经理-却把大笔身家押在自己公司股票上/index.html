18:23

Smart investors hire advisors to save stress, not save money

Simon Berube, a cardiologist, fully understands passive investing evidence and theoretically could DIY. But he says that as complexity rises—family, insurance, multiple accounts, multiple entities—‘time, errors, and mistakes compound exponentially’and small oversights become big problems decades later. What he needs isn't judgment being replaced—he still constantly asks ‘where's the evidence’—but what Brady calls ‘expert knowledge, integration, and oversight’. This shows that evidence-literate people still pay for advisors, not because advisors pick funds, but because they integrate all the moving pieces without error.

— Simon Berube
34:00

Income and happiness correlate at only 0.09

Ben cites 2018 research: the satiation point for life satisfaction in North America was around $105,000 in that sample, equivalent to approximately $137,000 in today's purchasing power; the satiation point for daily emotional happiness was lower, around $85,000. But subsequent 2021 and 2023 research overturned the ‘satiation point’narrative—for most people, happiness rises steadily with income, only the already-unhappy hit a plateau. Even so, the correlation coefficient between income and happiness is only 0.09—the median happiness gap between households earning $15,000 and $250,000 is only 5 points on a 100-point scale.

— Ben
40:12

His online critics forced him to read academic research

During his master's, Ben briefly sold insurance and actively managed funds. When he admitted in a media interview that he invested in Fidelity Dividend Plus Fund, the comments section ridiculed him harshly. This shock drove him to research active funds for the first time—tracing back to 1968, decades of evidence consistently showed active funds couldn't beat the index, the opposite of what the industry taught him. Later, learning the Fama-French three-factor model in class, he excitedly called Dimensional about using their products but was told he wasn't qualified to sell them; he briefly despaired and considered returning home to find engineering work. Weeks later, Dimensional called back and introduced him to Cameron Passmore, an Ottawa advisor using their products—leading him to join PWL.

1:03:13

Same evidence led them to different portfolio allocations

Ben's main holding is Dimensional Global Equity Portfolio, 100% stocks, zero bonds; Dan holds a 70% stocks / 30% bonds ETF mix. Both accept the same evidence—the Cederberg paper Dan cites also supports a global allocation of roughly one-third domestic stocks—but different risk tolerance led to different final allocations. Dan's principle: ‘take exactly the risk you need, no more and no less’. He did financial planning and found that 70/30 suffices for his retirement spending needs; additional stock exposure buys only ‘a bigger portfolio but not necessarily more happiness’.

— Dan
1:10:34

He chose to stake his net worth on company equity

Ben's private equity isn't a fund but equity in PWL (later acquired by OneDigital) that he holds directly—no fund manager, no layered fees. He admits it violates the conventional wisdom ‘don't concentrate in employer stock’and admits this is the ‘idiosyncratic risk’he actively wanted, because he's an actual contributor to company growth, not a salaried bystander. Dan adds that what matters isn't the percentage of holdings relative to net worth, but whether you've already earmarked enough for ‘how much diversification your financial goals require’—the framework they used helping Shopify employees make concentration decisions: first determine how much diversification your goals need, then do whatever you want with the rest.

— Ben
1:16:41

Both agree homebuying was their worst financial decision

Both Ben and Dan bought homes during the 2020 pandemic and both admit it was the worst investment they've made from a purely financial perspective—Ben says if he hadn't bought then and had kept the money in liquid assets to buy more PWL equity, the difference would be ‘a very large number’; Dan says if he'd invested his down payment in the bull market of the past six years, his portfolio would be 50% to 60% larger. But neither regrets it: homes lock in long-term housing costs, like a long-term bond tied to that specific home's living expenses, and both genuinely like their homes.

— Ben
1:23:01

100% stocks on paper but actually holding a cash buffer

Ben says his liquid investment portfolio, aside from private equity and his home, is entirely a 100% stocks fund—but he admits his high-yield savings account holds cash equal to roughly 5% of liquid assets, more than he needs daily, purely to ‘not worry’. Dan points out this is an overlooked detail: many who claim to be ‘100% stocks’aren't, because as long as you keep emergency cash, your real stock exposure is already discounted.

— Ben
1:24:08

Suboptimal decisions can still be rational ones

At the show's end, Dan distinguishes ‘suboptimal’from ‘irrational’: homebuying, keeping cash, betting on employer equity are none of them theoretically optimal financial choices, but as long as you've thought through the tradeoffs and opportunity costs before deciding, they're still rational. Ben adds that the show's original intent was always to first explain what's theoretically optimal, then honestly explain why people diverge from it—because a good life doesn't depend only on an optimal portfolio, and ‘an optimal portfolio’itself has no consensus definition.

— Dan

Glossary

benign neglect
A hands-off approach to one's portfolio, intervening infrequently and rebalancing only when necessary.
idiosyncratic risk
Risk tied to a single company or asset, impossible to eliminate through diversification.
covered call fund
A fund that sells call options to generate current income but sacrifices upside potential.
income satiation
The income level above which subjective happiness no longer noticeably increases.
Fama-French five-factor model
An academic pricing model using factors like size, value, and profitability to explain stock return differences.
