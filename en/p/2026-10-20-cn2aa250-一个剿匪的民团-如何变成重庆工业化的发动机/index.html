How a bandit-suppressing militia became the engine of Chongqing's industrialization

Chongqing's later military-industrial DNA didn't originate in wartime—Lu Zuofu transformed a militia bureau charged with bandit suppression into a prototype local government that built railways and established scientific institutions.

5:01

Civilization reached the mountains twice: relay pattern, not rupture

The author argues that southwest mountain regions experienced two successive waves of civilizational transformation rather than a clean break. The first came during Ming-Qing: American crops (corn, sweet potatoes) arrived with central Chinese migration, enabling mountain agriculture and spurring new trades in rice wine and tea. The second wave was Lu Zuofu's industrial modernization in Beibei. Both depended on institutions like militia bureaus that maintained order and enabled economic development, though their technical scope differed. The author calls this ‘creative destruction’—the pattern runs deep beneath today's ‘Chinese-style modernization’ discourse.

— Wang Guo
16:10

A Qing-era gentry scholar wielded republican theory against warlords

Hu Nanxian (胡南先) (b. 1868), first director of the Gorge Defense Bureau, typified pre-Qing scholar-gentry: he wrote classical Chinese. Yet his methods were startlingly modern. When warlord Liu Xiang (刘湘) ordered the militia bureau's tax revenue abolished, Hu refused—three times. His weapon wasn't traditional gentry rhetoric but pure republican theory: ‘The Republic is a nation of its people,’ ‘sovereignty belongs to the people.’ He repackaged local fiscal autonomy as constitutional rights. He won each time, preserving the tax base that later funded Lu Zuofu's coal mines and railways. Without Hu's success in protecting this revenue, Lu's industrial vision would have starved at the start.

— Wang Guo
23:16

A countryside version of the May Fourth movement took shape in Luzhou

After joining the Young China Association in Chengdu in 1919, Lu Zuofu returned to Luzhou with new ideas. He worked with warlord Yang Sen (杨森) as education director, but created something radically different from the urban May Fourth protests playing out in coastal cities. He brought literacy campaigns, popular science, and museums to farmers—a ‘rural May Fourth.’ When Yang Sen offered him the education ministry, Lu refused. Instead, he demanded just one folk education center. It housed a museum, zoo, and even a Western symphony orchestra, designed to expose rural populations to ‘new things.’ This experience convinced him that educational salvation required concrete institutions, not political office.

— Wang Guo
36:33

Coal miners returned home to farm when the agricultural season demanded it

Beibei had mined coal since the Kangxi era—over 200 years by the 1930s. Most accessible surface and riverside coal was exhausted; what remained lay deeper inland, requiring the 1928 Beichuan Railway (Sichuan's first fully completed railway, built before the chronically unfinished Chuanhan Railway) to haul it out. Yet the last miles—from mine shaft to rail station, station to riverbank—ran entirely on human porters. This apparent backwardness actually solved a structural crisis: mining jobs were seasonal. Miners farmed during planting season, descended to pits during slack months. In harvest they were farmers; in winter they were workers. This hybrid identity broke the Malthusian bottleneck that threatened mountain regions: surplus population with no land to till might otherwise become bandits.

— Wang Guo
40:59

Most of Beibei's new institutions were transplanted from temples

When Lu Zuofu pushed industrial development and modern education, he didn't build anew. Instead, he conscripted existing temples and guild halls—spaces left by immigrant society for worship and mutual aid—converting them into modern functions. A contemporary newspaper ran a piece titled ‘All of Beibei's Enterprises Come from Temples’: Dongyue Temple became a power plant; some temple served as the Defense Bureau's offices; a Prince's Temple housed yet another institution. The names stayed; the functions inverted entirely, from ritual and mutual support to modern administration and production. It was secularization so quiet it seemed invisible.

— Wang Guo
49:49

He grasped Japan's invisible invasion before the fighting began

Before the September 18 Incident, Lu toured Dalian and Port Arthur to observe how the South Manchuria Railway managed territorial development—mining museums, railway administration, resource surveys—all operating with startling coordination. He recognized it as ‘invisible invasion.’ Back in Beibei, he ordered the Defense Bureau to study the Northeast crisis through morning briefings and reading circles debating the national emergency. But his response always returned to building Beibei itself—nationalism as construction rather than mere exclusion or war-mongering. Chiang Kai-shek, by contrast, didn't grasp the crisis until 1932, when Weng Wenhao (翁文灏) briefed him on Northeast coal and iron reserves. Chiang's diary recorded his deep regret at having researched too late.

— Wang Guo
58:55

He succeeded because he was a native, not an outside expert

The author compares Lu with two contemporary rural reconstruction figures. Liang Shuming (from Guangxi) undertook rural reconstruction in Zouping, Shandong, relying on his disciples and outside intellectuals—producing cultural friction and peasant complaints that ‘pig feed was finer than human food.’ Yan Yangchu (晏阳初) drew Rockefeller Foundation backing, operating in a different register. Lu differed fundamentally: he was Beibei-born. His initial coal capital came through gifts and loans from classmates, teachers, relatives. Once these people lent money, the venture became entangled with their interests—they had skin in the game. Being a ‘local’ rather than a missionary modernizer determined whether rural reconstruction could actually take root.

— Wang Guo
1:04:58

Wartime migration assembled China's industrial heartland

The Minsheng (民生公司) was often narrated as a shipping company; it was actually an industrial empire with 60–70 subsidiaries—everything from screw factories to coal operations, coal washing, power plants, ship construction and repair. The episode deploys Pomeranz's logic on the Industrial Revolution: coal and industry centers coincide. China's tragedy was misalignment—traditional industry in Jiangnan, energy sources in the west. War reversed this by accident: coastal industry evacuated to Chongqing, suddenly co-locating both centers. This conjunction didn't merely serve wartime emergency; it was the true genesis of later Jiangnan Motors, Changan Automobiles, and decades later, the Third Front construction program. Not a wartime accident but the structural foundation for postwar industrial continuity.

— Wang Guo
