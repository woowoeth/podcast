He Cooked the Chickpea Pasta Into Mush, Then Survived by Telling People to Steep It Like Tea

Banza's first scaled-up production run failed across the board — 20,000 boxes of pasta fell apart the moment they hit boiling water. They didn't destroy them. They slapped on a sticker that said ‘steep it like tea’ and shipped — because not shipping meant losing the retailer forever.

5:14

The starting point wasn't a business insight, it was a sinus problem

In college Brian's fridge held nothing but hummus, food was just ‘fuel’ to him, and he cycled between antibiotics and steroids for so long that surgery was nearly on the table. It was his father, an orthodontist, who got him to try an elimination diet, and that's how he found that wheat-based foods triggered his sinus inflammation — one beer and the next day he couldn't breathe. The ENT told him: I don't know what you're talking about, but if it works, keep doing it. That private problem became the product definition: high-protein, high-fiber, gluten-free pasta, doing what Chobani did for yogurt.

10:26

The first recipe had chia in it and was too sticky to look at

He bought Bob's Red Mill chickpea flour on Amazon and followed blogs for combinations. Version one was chickpeas plus eggs plus chia — the chia made it so sticky the result was ugly. No rolling pin, so he used a wine bottle; no pasta cutter, so he used a knife, dusting flour on the table as he cut to keep it from sticking. Version two dropped the chia, leaving just chickpeas and eggs, and was clearly better, but to make it usable for more people he added back tapioca starch and xanthan gum and committed to a vegan route. His first capital expenditure was an Atlas Mercado hand-crank pasta machine, about $30 on Amazon. He got up at 5 a.m. every day to make pasta, put on country music, and his roommates thought he was insane.

— Brian Rudolph
20:49

Going from 200 pounds an hour to 1,000 multiplied the mistakes too

They first found a tiny gluten-free factory in Michigan that could only do 150 to 200 pounds an hour — wildly inefficient, but they could learn as they went. To get into 190 Meijer stores and 2 Eataly locations, that capacity wasn't enough, so they found what was then the largest gluten-free pasta factory in the country, outside Los Angeles. The problem: that plant had only ever run rice and corn pasta, never legumes. Scaled up to roughly 1,000 pounds an hour, the pasta looked fine coming out and turned into total mush the moment it was boiled. Each trial run was 8,000 to 10,000 pounds, and a failure meant the die costs and ingredient costs were gone, and they had only a little over $100,000 on hand.

— Brian Rudolph
26:02

He got thrown out of the factory for asking the operator

After several failed trial runs, Brian ignored the other side's objections and went straight to the line operator to ask what was actually going wrong. The worker said ‘this corn smells weird,’ and he answered: that's not corn, we're not making corn pasta today, it's chickpeas. He realized on the spot that communication had broken down completely, and right after that he was escorted out of the plant. At the same time the distributor was threatening him: if you don't fulfill this purchase order, you will never work with this retailer again. He later estimated the shipment would cost nearly every dollar they had.

— Brian Rudolph
28:07

The fix was don't boil it — steep it like tea

With nowhere left to turn they started testing: boil one minute, two minutes, three minutes, then stop boiling altogether — bring water to a boil, turn off the heat, and let the pasta sit in the hot water. What came out of steeping was better than what came out of boiling, or at least it didn't fall apart. Brian says he wasn't proud of the product, and it bothered him to sell it under his own brand, but he knew it was a life-or-death question. The packaging was already printed, so they made an extra sticker reading ‘Banza is easier to cook than regular pasta — steep it like tea,’ and had people hand-apply it to 20,000 boxes. Once that shipment went out, they immediately pulled out of the big plant and went back to the small one in Michigan.

— Brian Rudolph
35:18

The box is orange because every pasta box was blue

They noticed that nearly every pasta package on the shelf at the time was blue — Brian put it as ‘you'd think they colluded.’ Orange contrasts most strongly with blue, and orange was also the color he'd loved since watching Nickelodeon as a kid, so it became the brand color. The packaging copy involved trade-offs too: putting ‘chickpeas’ in the most prominent spot would scare off a lot of people, and so would gluten-free, so the gluten-free certification went on the side while the front led with high protein and high fiber — making consumers feel this was ‘a product for me’ rather than ‘not a product for me.’

— Brian Rudolph
41:31

The glyphosate report cut weekly sales by a third

Moms Across America published a report saying glyphosate residue had been detected in Banza products. Brian saw the Google alert during a one-on-one meeting; he says his heart sank and his mind jumped twenty steps ahead. He wanted to verify first, so he put out only a statement saying ‘we are investigating’ — which was nowhere near enough for the most panicked people. The lab results were delayed again and again, dragging on for three weeks. The final published result was 98% below the allegation, but not zero — still a very low level, and below the EWG's risk benchmark. He decided to make the supply chain 100% traceable and joined the Detox Project's certification program.

— Brian Rudolph
52:15

The natural channel recovered far more slowly than conventional

The sales recovery was a mixed bag: some channels came back better than before, but the natural channel recovered much more slowly — and that was exactly the customer base with the best repeat purchase rates and the highest sales per store. Brian says it actually makes sense, because natural-channel consumers are the most susceptible to that kind of information. What he does now: he keeps alerts on for anywhere Banza and glyphosate are mentioned, and personally goes onto Reddit to reply and add context, with the goal of making it impossible for the story to be seen without its context. He also admits he should have acknowledged what was happening faster and more visibly at the time.

— Brian Rudolph
