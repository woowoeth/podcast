Patches Can't Keep Pace With Vulnerabilities: AI Puts State-Level Hacking in Everyone's Hands

CrowdStrike CEO says AI is exploding vulnerability discovery and auto-chaining attacks; defenders' patching cadence has hit a sound barrier, and the attack-defense imbalance is shifting from theory to everyday outages.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:07

Frontier AI models refused to help defenders

George Kurtz saw the Hugging Face autonomous agent attack as a turning point, but not for the reason most assumed. The real shock was not the end-to-end autonomous attack itself, but that frontier models would not comply with what defenders needed them to do—forcing defenders to turn to Chinese models just to understand what had happened. His conclusion: frontier AI capabilities must reach defenders, otherwise the attack-defense asymmetry persists forever.

— George Kurtz
11:15

Nation-state hacking power flows down to everyone

Kurtz describes the "Pyramid of Pain," which stratifies attackers by sophistication: hacktivists at the base, organized cybercrime in the middle, nation-states at the peak. AI agents have shattered that pyramid. Nation-state-grade capabilities are now descending to the bottom tier—activists. This multiplies adversaries and democratizes destructive power. With cybercrime already a $20 billion industry, everyone now starts from the same level.

— George Kurtz
16:21

One year of CVE discoveries rivals a quarter century of history

Kurtz presents CVE counts: this year's one-year total equals 25% of all CVEs ever discovered. The past eight months have seen near-monthly doublings. This is not simply because more code exists. The bigger driver is AI excavating old, never-before-discovered vulnerabilities. One example: a PHP vulnerability traceable to around 2004.

— George Kurtz
21:22

AI chains harmless bugs into exploitable breaches

AI's most dangerous capability is not finding individual flaws—it is linking multiple isolated, low-risk vulnerabilities into a chain that achieves remote code execution. That means complete system takeover and remote control. Game over. This compositional ability is nearly impossible for humans to execute through brute force or experience alone, but AI executes it effortlessly.

— George Kurtz
24:25

Patch releases have hit the sound barrier

Here is Kurtz's core metaphor: vulnerability discovery grows exponentially, patch creation grows exponentially, but actual patch deployment speed has hit a "sound barrier." This is why companies now preemptively shut down services to install patches—choosing to disrupt business rather than face breach. The industry is paying the bill for 25 to 30 years of accumulated technical debt; that bill has come due.

— George Kurtz
31:30

An open-source attack framework breaches targets in hours

Artex, an AI-driven attack framework built by someone in China, chains together multiple AI agents and sub-agents to execute reconnaissance, discover vulnerabilities, generate exploits, escalate privileges, and steal data—all in sequence. When the Korean banking system was attacked, the framework ran against 200+ institutions simultaneously, with each breach taking under 3 hours. The author deleted the project, but over 12000 Artex forks are now in circulation.

— George Kurtz
44:37

Attackers steal session tokens instead of passwords

Here is the mechanism of "login is easier than breaking in." Multifactor authentication creates a false sense of security, but attackers actually steal session tokens—the credential issued after you check "trust this device for 30 days." Holding a session token means the attacker is already authenticated. Next, they breach your Outlook inbox and set auto-rules routing critical emails (transfers, sensitive communications) to folders you never read. Then they impersonate you.

— George Kurtz
46:38

North Korean workers ship stolen company laptops to U.S. farms

Kurtz recounts an early case CrowdStrike disclosed years ago: North Korean operators, hired for remote positions, received laptops shipped to a fake U.S. address—about 80 devices were recovered at a Texas woman's home—then remote-controlled them via VPN from North Korea. This has scaled into an entire industry. One detection technique: when suspecting an employee is North Korean, have them repeat a phrase like "Kim Jong Un is a fat pig" on video. Refusal exposes their identity.

— George Kurtz
