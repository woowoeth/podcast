The price crisis requires anchoring energy, food, and rent prices—not tax cuts

Economist Isabella Weber argues that calming price anxiety requires not tax cuts or laissez-faire competition, but short-term price floors on essentials like energy, food, and rent, alongside public-sector-led long-term supply building.

6:14

The essence of price anxiety is economic insecurity

Citizens of the wealthiest nations still protest in the streets over unaffordable food, housing, and energy—not a psychological problem but genuine economic insecurity. When democracies fail to guarantee basic needs, people naturally say ‘we don't trust these people’. That distrust is the core of today's liberal democracy crisis.

— Isabella Weber
7:16

CPI is a plutocratic index that obscures real pain

CPI is weighted by spending: someone spending $300,000 annually counts ten times more than someone spending $30,000. A luxury fridge price drop lowers CPI, but rent and staple price rises get diluted. Bottom 20% saw incomes rise about 30% but electricity prices jumped 40%—meaning essentials inflation hits the poor far harder than overall CPI indicates.

— Isabella Weber
10:20

Germany treats baseline natural gas access as a quasi-right

Germany's energy-crisis gas-price cap uses nonlinear pricing: each resident gets a guaranteed baseline—enough for heating, cooking, and bathing—locked at a set price; usage above that is market-priced. Such short-term price floors should buy time to rebuild public infrastructure like electrical grids. Electricity, as a natural monopoly, should be built and operated by the public sector.

— Isabella Weber
14:24

Mexico holds down food prices through government-supermarket negotiation

Mexico's government selected a basket of food essentials and negotiated with four or five major supermarket chains to keep the basket price stable, with the president holding regular press conferences on corporate compliance. Weber sees this as redefining government—not ‘helpless’, but negotiating on behalf of consumers and holding businesses accountable. That's essential to rebuilding democratic trust.

— Isabella Weber
18:28

Rate hikes to counter oil shocks undermine renewable energy investment

She questions the logic of raising rates to counter oil shocks. Real wages have already declined; rate hikes further depress labor markets while pushing up financing costs. Yet renewables are precisely the kind of industry with high upfront capital needs and low operating costs—extremely sensitive to interest rates. If price stabilization didn't depend on rate hikes, there would be more room for supply-side building.

— Isabella Weber
21:30

Oil windfall profits flow half to the richest one percent

Weber's team studied 2022 fossil fuel windfall profits and found that 50% flowed to the richest 1%, while the bottom 50% captured roughly 1%. Such windfall waves fundamentally represent redistribution from bottom to top, further intensifying already-extreme inequality and steadily eroding public trust in democratic institutions.

— Isabella Weber
34:41

Chinese pork prices boom and bust; grain prices stay stable

Weber notes that Chinese economists joke CPI is essentially ‘China's pork price index’—pork price swings heavily drive the number. After African swine fever, government subsidies rebuilt large-scale hog farms, creating overcapacity and then collapse, producing cyclical booms and busts. Storable grains, by contrast, have been managed far more successfully—evidence that intervention's effectiveness depends entirely on sector-specific factors.

— Isabella Weber
39:48

State capacity must rebuild starting with potholes and garbage

After neoliberalism's decades of outsourcing and de-capacitation, the state has ‘gone stupid’, she argues. Rebuilding execution capacity means starting with achievable wins—she cites the Mamdani government beginning with pothole repair and garbage collection. This both restores public trust and rebuilds officials' confidence in their own governing capacity.

— Isabella Weber

Glossary

plutocratic index
A price index weighted by spending volume: higher spenders carry greater weight in the index.
Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR)
The U.S. government's crude oil reserve system, used to moderate oil price volatility.
