Dan Snow's History Hit 2026-09-28

An explorer built fame on a fake Viking tribe story, then sent colonists to a barren island

Stefansson became a world-headline explorer through a fabricated Viking lost-tribe story, then used that prestige to send colonists to Wrangel Island with no animals—four died as a result.

He became famous overnight through a fabricated Viking tribe discovery

Stefansson was born William Stephenson but changed his name to Vilhjalmur Stefansson after becoming obsessed with Viking culture. In 1911, he traveled to the Arctic to find a legendary lost tribe and returned two years later with sensational news: he had discovered a ‘lost tribe’ of blonde, blue-eyed people who appeared to be descendants of Viking colonists. Global media covered the story extensively, and he became one of the early 20th century's most famous explorers overnight. Decades later, in 2007, scientists used DNA testing to confirm that this Inuit group had no blood relation to Vikings whatsoever—his entire claim to fame was fabricated from the beginning.

7:08

The real reason for Arctic exploration was controlling future aircraft fueling stations

Stefansson persuaded the Canadian government to provide him with nearly unlimited funding for the expedition—not for pure scientific exploration. He was friends with the Wright brothers and believed that future flights from London to Japan would have to cross the Arctic. Early aircraft needed refueling every few hundred miles, so whoever controlled the landing points on Arctic islands would control the strategic chokepoints of future aviation—he called these points ‘airports’. This reveals that this expedition, flying the banner of empire, was actually a geopolitical gamble laid out in advance.

14:22

The flagship, trapped in pack ice, drifted away with all the crew aboard

The flagship Karluk became trapped in Arctic pack ice just weeks after departure. Pack ice is not stationary but rotates slowly around the North Pole, drifting. Being trapped in it meant the ship would be carried into a barren sea from which escape could take years. Stefansson thought the ship was only temporarily stopped, so he went ashore with a few companions to hunt musk oxen for supplies. A few days of warming weather loosened the ice, and the ship drifted away with all the scientists and crew aboard into the unknown. Command fell to Captain Bartlett, and Stefansson became completely separated from the expedition.

21:46

Ice ridges reached 100 feet high; the island held no food or animals

After abandoning the ship, crew members spotted an island (Wrangel Island) on the horizon—their only hope—but 80 miles of pack ice lay between them. The pressure ridges blocking their path rose as high as 100 feet and were about 50 feet thick, like a stone wall. They could only hack their way across with ice axes. Worse yet, after nearly dying from frostbite, snow blindness, and falling through ice, they reached the island only to find it had no animals and no food source—the real survival ordeal was just beginning.

— Giles Milton
27:58

The captain crossed the Arctic for help; frostbite victims lost limbs to tin cans

Captain Bartlett, accompanied by an Inuit guide, trekked across the frozen sea to Siberia, then walked for miles along the coast until finding a settlement. He finally boarded a ship to Alaska and sent out a distress signal. When the rescue ship reached Wrangel Island in the summer of 1914, the crew was severely frostbitten and starving. Multiple crew members had to have fingers and toes amputated due to frostbite—with no anesthetic and no surgical instruments, they were simply crushed off with tin cans.

— Giles Milton
36:23

The colonization plan recruited just four young men and a dressmaker

To legally establish sovereignty over Wrangel Island under international law, Stefansson needed actual ‘colonists’ to live there permanently. He promised to return the following summer with supplies and more settlers. He managed to recruit only four young men, plus Ada Blackjack, an Inuit single mother who took a job as a seamstress because her son needed hospital care for tuberculosis—she had grown up in town, never hunted or fished, and had no wilderness survival experience. The resupply ship Stefansson promised never appeared the next summer.

— Giles Milton
43:51

The rescue leader accused the explorer of willful murder; both fought through the press

After two years of silence, Harold Noice led a rescue team to the island while simultaneously securing exclusive reporting rights with dozens of newspapers. When he arrived, all four male colonists had died; only Ada Blackjack survived. Noice immediately accused Stefansson in the press of knowingly sending the colonists to their deaths despite knowing the island had no food and extreme cold—essentially accusing him of negligent homicide. Stefansson wielded his reputation to strike back, forcing Noice to sign a legal document retracting his claims. The two fought publicly across newspapers worldwide.

— Giles Milton
49:59

The sole survivor lived into her eighties, haunted by the experience to the end

Ada Blackjack was eventually rescued alive, her son's tuberculosis also healed, and she lived into her eighties. But the island experience haunted all the survivors. Another survivor, William McKinley, later witnessed the horrors of war in the trenches of Flanders during World War I, yet he said none of those experiences compared to the ordeal on Wrangel Island—because in the trenches there was at least mutual support among comrades, while on the island everyone had to fend for themselves in a desperate struggle for survival.
