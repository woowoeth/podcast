Cisco bragged it could see global demand in real time—what it saw was customers' duplicate orders

Cisco's inventory surged 87% and 58.8% in two successive quarters. The cause was not demand explosion but customers doubling orders to grab scarce inventory, which the 'real-time supply chain' amplified and passed upstream unchanged.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

9:27

An e-commerce site built Cisco's false 'virtual company' identity

Cisco Connection Online launched in 1996 on Sun hardware running Solaris. Within its first five months, it generated $75 million in sales. By 1998, web orders accounted for over 65% of Cisco's total sales. Customers received real-time quotes, configured orders, and the system flagged errors—new orders routed automatically to the relevant sales rep. Annual FedEx costs dropped by roughly $500,000. The platform won CIO Magazine's award three years running and became Cisco's showcase for 'how the Internet transforms business.'

12:31

Cisco employees never handled their own products

By the late 1990s, Cisco outsourced 70–80% of manufacturing to contract manufacturers like Jabil and Solectron, officially reducing costs by roughly 30%. Cisco built an extranet letting these third-party vendors view orders and specifications in real time; finished products shipped directly to customers. Ideally, no Cisco employee touched the hardware. One analyst compared the structure to the Japanese keiretsu—nominally independent firms functioning as a managed in-house factory network.

13:36

Quarterly closings compressed from weeks to hours

CFO Larry Carter told Harvard Business Review that Cisco could close a quarter's accounting in hours instead of weeks. Their 'virtual close' system, backed by real-time financial dashboards, let executives pull same-day global sales figures sliced by region, product, and customer—enabling 'daily tactical adjustments.' CEO John Chambers branded this 'virtual supply chain' as possessing unlimited capacity, extreme reliability, and unmatched agility. This real-time system, which they promoted as their competitive edge, later became the pipeline that amplified false demand signals.

16:41

Cisco's packet-switching bet made it an unchallenged market leader

In 1998, Cisco bet that Internet infrastructure would converge around packet-switching networks treating data, voice, and video as unified data packets sent over fiber. Lucent, still reliant on legacy circuit equipment, tried grafting the new technology on top—a failed approach. Nortel spent $9 billion acquiring Bay Networks to catch up, but integration proved difficult. Cisco alone owned the most lucrative segment—Internet data networking—and became the biggest beneficiary of the fiber-optics boom that followed.

22:51

Suppliers warned caution while Cisco doubled down

In early 2000, delivery times for some Cisco equipment stretched to 4–6 months. Capacity shortages cost Cisco an estimated 10% of prior-year revenue. Cisco's response: it forecast 60% revenue growth for the new fiscal year and signed long-term purchase commitments with manufacturers and component suppliers. Solectron's internal forecasts showed the market softening; they advised Cisco to cut back on component orders. Cisco pushed back: e-commerce was under 5% of revenue; other customers were still growing. More parts were needed. Management overruled its manufacturing partner's caution.

24:53

Record revenue masked a financing collapse

In Q3 2000 (the October quarter), Cisco posted record 65% year-over-year revenue growth. Management insisted they saw no slowdown signs, but the stock had already dropped 32% from its March peak. Telecom startups' equity and debt financing was in freefall: first eight months of 2000 averaged $5.6 billion in monthly fundraising; September fell to $1.2 billion, October to $900 million, November to $700 million. Xilinx, which supplies FPGA chips for routers, cut its internal growth forecast from 16–20% down to 10%. Cisco's leadership noticed the decline but characterized it as 'consolidation'—believing market shakeout would leave only stronger telecom customers.

26:57

Customers doubled and tripled orders chasing scarce supplies

Earlier that year, customers began doubling and even tripling their orders to secure supply amid months-long delivery delays. Cisco's own sales staff sometimes added 'buffer' numbers to make orders look more important. The same customer might place identical orders with multiple distributors. These layered-up demand signals, taken at face value, became massive component orders to upstream suppliers. By late September, a $3.8 billion backlog that was supposed to be a safety cushion evaporated.

28:00

Layoffs and write-downs—cash reserves saved Cisco's supply chain

In April 2001, Cisco acknowledged revenue would decline 30% quarter-over-quarter. The company laid off 8,500 employees and took approximately $2.5 billion in charges against inventory stockpiles at Jabil and Solectron (actual write-down: $2.2 billion). Some inventory found buyers; some was held for potential market recovery; the rest, custom-built for Cisco and no longer needed, had to be scrapped. In the same quarter, Solectron posted 85% revenue growth while laying off 8,200 workers. JDS Uniphase cut 16,000 jobs and saw its stock price collapse from $130+ to $7. Cisco had sufficient cash reserves to survive the downturn and maintain its supply-chain relationships. Most competitors did not.

Glossary

Bullwhip effect
Small fluctuations at retail compound into exponentially larger swings upstream in the supply chain.
Virtual close
Using real-time financial systems to compress quarter-end accounting from weeks to hours.
Extranet
A private network connecting an enterprise with external suppliers and manufacturers to share real-time order and specification data.
Keiretsu
Nominally independent Japanese firms operating as a coordinated business group; here used as an analogy for Cisco's outsourcing structure.
Vendor financing
When equipment makers invest in or extend credit to customers to enable those customers to buy their products.
Contract manufacturer
Third-party factory contracted to manufacture products on behalf of a brand company, like Jabil or Solectron for Cisco.
