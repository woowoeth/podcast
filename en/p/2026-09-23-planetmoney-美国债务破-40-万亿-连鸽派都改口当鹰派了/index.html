US Debt Tops $40 Trillion: Even the Doves Are Turning Hawkish

The 2010 paper claiming growth halves once debt exceeds 90% of GDP was used by politicians as a red line for over a decade; now even the dove economist who argued for bold spending calls herself a hawk, but no one can say where that line is.

2:05

The real fuse on the debt snowball is interest

The textbook worry isn't the debt itself, but that interest costs feed on themselves: pay more interest, the deficit grows; a bigger deficit pushes debt higher; next period's interest is even bigger, and so on compounding worse and worse. The other path is investors losing their appetite for Treasuries, forcing the government to keep raising rates to attract buyers, making the snowball roll faster. Neither of these feared outcomes happened after the financial crisis, and in hindsight the people who argued the government should spend more were on the right side of history — Karen Dynan even thinks it should have spent more and the recession could have been shorter.

— Karen Dynan
8:21

The 90% line was read into the paper

The 2010 six-page paper by Reinhart and Rogoff, "Growth in a Time of Debt," studied decades of debt data from 20 advanced economies and concluded that when debt reaches 90% of GDP, growth is only half its normal level. But what the paper actually did was sort countries into four buckets — low, medium, high, and very high — with the very high bucket containing countries with debt far above 90%, and technically it said that this bucket, on average and over long periods, was associated with lower growth. Politicians and the media read it as a red line: "cross 90% and the economy crashes." The paper never said that.

11:28

An Excel error ignited the austerity fight

The debate the paper sparked raged in blogs and newspapers, with calls for austerity and belt-tightening on one side and warnings not to kill the recovery and stop spending now on the other. Later, more debt-friendly economists pointed out a major error in the Excel spreadsheet Reinhart and Rogoff used, and the paper's final sentence said that since their finding was wrong, the austerity agendas in Europe and the US should be reassessed. Correcting the error weakened the claim everyone had seized on, but didn't overturn it — the updated paper still showed high debt generally associated with slower growth, just less steeply.

— Andrea Presbitero
13:41

Low growth can also cause high debt

High debt being correlated with low growth doesn't mean high debt causes low growth. Andrea Presbitero points out the causality could run the other way: when the economy underperforms, policymakers want to stimulate it, which means public consumption and public investment, and one way to pay for that is borrowing — borrowing pushes debt up, so you see low growth and high debt in the data at the same time. He and his coauthors ultimately reached the same conclusion as most: based on the empirical evidence, there's no definitive answer, and causality may run both ways. Moreover, the debt threshold varies by country — it could be 90%, 45%, or 100% — and depends on who the creditors are, whether they're domestic or foreign investors, and whether the debt is short-term or long-term.

— Andrea Presbitero
17:50

Zero rates shelved the whole issue

What makes high debt destructive isn't the debt itself but the interest paid on it. After the financial crisis, global rates fell to near zero and stayed there for years, so many macroeconomists thought maybe there was no need to worry much about debt, and attention drifted away from the topic. Then the world changed twice: the pandemic and the spending that followed pushed debt even higher, and more importantly, rates rose again. Today the cost of holding a lot of debt is much higher for the US and other countries than five or six years ago, and the shelved question is relevant again.

19:57

Rogoff says 91% and you're doomed is a misreading

Ken Rogoff is still annoyed by how the paper was interpreted. He says 90% was just their highest bucket, and they never said that at 91% you suddenly go to hell — that's like a 55 mph speed limit and you drive 56, so you'll crash the next minute. He thinks that interpretation was used and distorted polemically. But he also stresses that you can't therefore say there's no threshold and any level of debt is fine — that's crazy too. His basic intuition hasn't changed: constantly leveraging up is playing with fire, and the US trajectory needs adjusting, not just debt but also Social Security and Medicare.

— Kenneth Rogoff
25:22

The dove who argued for spending has flipped

Two years later, debt has grown by more than $4 trillion, the large tax cuts Congress passed in 2025 further reduced revenue, and interest alone costs over $1 trillion a year, a record. Karen Dynan, who once thought spending was worth the risk, now says, "I'm a debt hawk now," because changes in financial markets, especially Treasury borrowing rates, make her think this is trickier than she thought two years ago. She describes it as walking on ice and comforting yourself that it hasn't cracked yet. Last week the 10-year Treasury rate hit its highest level since before the Great Recession, and the CBO projects debt will exceed 120% of GDP by 2036, higher than at any point in US history including World War II.

— Karen Dynan
28:32

Rogoff puts the odds of a big problem above 50%

Rogoff thinks the biggest misconception is believing rates will stay low forever, and that if they're high for a while, it's just a nightmare that will pass. He says he thinks the probability of something very serious happening is greater than 50% — not the end of the world, a debt crisis isn't the end of the world, but something very significant will happen and we're not prepared. He also says we need to reflect on how wrong everyone was and for how long, because that belief hasn't disappeared — mainstream opinion leaders think basically the same now as they did then. As for the red line, neither economist can draw it, but both agree the solution is spending cuts, tax increases, or both, and that looks impossible to push through.

— Kenneth Rogoff

Glossary

deficit hawk / deficit dove
Hawks advocate cutting deficits and austerity; doves believe in spending boldly to stimulate the economy when necessary.
crowding out
Government borrowing sucks up capital, reducing private investment (building factories, R&D) and thus dragging down growth.
debt to GDP ratio
A country's total debt as a percentage of its annual output, a common measure of debt burden.
Treasury bonds
IOUs issued by the US government to borrow money, considered one of the safest investments in the world.
