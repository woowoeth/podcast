Asking questions is not about getting a yes — it's about giving the other person the safety to say no

An FBI negotiator says people feel safest when they can say no — asking ‘do you oppose this’ gets genuine cooperation better than ‘would you be willing’, and the same trick works for couples too

Saying "no" is self-protection, not rebellion

When Voss says people feel protected the moment they say "no"—a kind of Pavlovian reflex—because nearly everyone got burned by saying yes as a child (he still keeps a coupon book he paid $25 for that promised $5000 in value). "No" isn't a confrontation signal; it's self-protection. When parents see their child say "no", they need not treat it as rebellion—the child is actually confirming they still have control.

— Chris Voss
6:25

Replace commands with "how" to make kids think

When a child refuses bedtime, saying "I'm in charge" is just a command that won't make them think. Voss suggests "how" and "what" questions—like "if you don't sleep now, how will you do what you want tomorrow?"—which trigger what he calls deep thinking, pulling the child to your side as problem-solvers instead of making you adversaries.

— Chris Voss
12:33

Autonomy matters more deeply than even survival

Maslow puts survival first in the hierarchy of needs, but Voss says from human history and hostage negotiation experience, autonomy is actually first—no enslaved society willingly stays enslaved, America was founded on "give me liberty or give me death." When a child says "no", they're protecting their autonomy. How the parent responds in that moment teaches the child what "control" means—kids learn from what you do, not what you say.

— Chris Voss
15:34

Mirror technique: repeat the last few words, then stay silent for six seconds

The core move is "mirroring"—repeat the last one to three words of what the other person said, with a slight lowered chin to show you're listening, or a slight head tilt with rising tone to show curiosity. Many parents ask "how was your day" or "what did you learn," kids give one-word answers like "fine," because relentless follow-up shows you weren't really listening to the last thing they said. Switch to mirroring plus silence—clients report that after two or three exchanges kids start volunteering more, because the neurotransmitters take at least 6 seconds to work, and you have to resist interrupting.

— Chris Voss
22:43

When a child says "I hate you," mirror back: "You hate me?"

When kids unleash emotion-driven attacks, Voss's move is to mirror first to protect your own state—respond with curious mirroring ("You hate me?") rather than get triggered into argument. He stresses understanding is not agreement, empathy is not approval: first hear what the child is really saying, then you have room to handle the boundary work later—"that's not how we talk"—rather than getting defensive and losing your footing.

— Chris Voss
27:49

"You're right" shuts people up; just "that's right" builds trust

A phrase like "you're right" can satisfy almost anyone enough to stop arguing—it's nearly a universal shut-down tactic people use every week, and both sides know what's happening. But drop the "you" and just say "that's right"—it sounds like you're jointly confirming an objective fact, and it actually builds real attachment to you as the person saying it. Voss says this is how charismatic leaders create devoted followers who ignore contrary evidence: it triggers that same psychological mechanism.

— Chris Voss
37:07

Replace "why" with "what" to remove blame

Almost everyone was trained as a child that "why" means you're being accused of something, so "why did you hit your sister" lands as "you're a bad kid." Voss suggests replacing it with "what led you to do that," which finds the cause without the accusatory tone—and it works exactly the same in hostage negotiation: nobody chooses violence innately, every choice is "the result of a string of failures," and only by understanding that "why" can you unlock the other person's willingness to cooperate.

— Chris Voss
53:44

To get partner buy-in, ask "do you oppose this" not "would you be willing"

If you want a partner who doesn't fully agree with your parenting to watch a video with you, don't ask "can we watch this together"—ask "do you oppose watching this?" It's a deliberately no-oriented question, and it actually gets genuine cooperation, because it preserves the other person's autonomy. Voss says binary questions have powerful psychology: once they answer "no, I don't oppose it," you can follow up with "when would work," and the whole thing moves smoother than trying to persuade them.

— Chris Voss

Glossary

Mirroring
Repeating back the last one to three words of what someone said while staying silent, showing you're genuinely listening.
Labeling
Naming the other person's emotion—"it sounds like you're saying this for a reason"—without judgment.
No-oriented question
A question deliberately designed so the other person can say "no," preserving their sense of control.
