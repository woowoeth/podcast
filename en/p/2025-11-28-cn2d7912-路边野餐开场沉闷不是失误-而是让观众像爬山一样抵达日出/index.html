Strict control over behavior, complete freedom for thought—that shaped his creative life

Bi Gan's grandmother had a strong personality and strict control over life's details—manners, sleep schedules, all had to follow her rules. But when it came to what he wanted to read or play, she never restricted him. Bi Gan says this was a strange phenomenon, and he can't fully explain why, but this 'discipline the body, free the mind' parenting style left him introverted and formal in behavior, yet never considering 'what rules I should follow' in his thinking. He believes without his grandmother, he wouldn't be who he is today.

41:19

He wanted to write a savage review of Tarkovsky—until he understood Stalker

In college, Bi Gan encountered Tarkovsky's Stalker. The gap between the critical consensus ('greatness incarnate') and his complete incomprehension made him suspect the film was flawed, not his understanding. He says the only time in his life he wanted to write a review was to trash this movie—but you have to have some rules about it, you have to finish and understand it first. After nearly two weeks of watching, in the final moments, he was struck: film could use the camera to address ultimate questions, something he'd never thought about cinematically before. He was eating in the cafeteria when it hit him mid-bite.

— Bi Gan
1:01:26

Short films taught him nothing about holding a feature together for sixty interlocking scenes

When making his thesis film Tiger with nearly ten thousand yuan from his aunt, Bi Gan discovered that short-film experience was useless—a short is one or two scenes, while a feature has sixty scenes supporting each other. He couldn't grasp the scale of work, and fell into a half-abandoned state, shooting material he didn't understand. In the end, he threaded together passages of his own poetry as voiceover to connect the disconnected parts, accidentally creating a new texture. The film won an award at Nanjing Film Festival anyway, even though the selection committee said 'the focus isn't even correct.'

— Bi Gan
1:35:53

He shoots real long takes—not because he must, but because the temptation is irresistible

Bi Gan says what makes long takes truly moving isn't the technical metric of 'whether it's shot live'—seamless editing can be invisible to audiences. But once you have the chance to bind time and film together permanently, that temptation is overwhelming for the whole crew. True seamless editing requires a dedicated post-production plan; it's far from just tossing glitches to visual effects. That's why the VFX supervisor on Wild Years actually treasured working with him—because he'd shoot extra material just for visual effects rather than throwing problems at post-production.

— Bi Gan
1:45:00

Honor what audiences don't consciously know they want, not what they say they prefer

Bi Gan explains why Roadside Picnic's slow first-half obstacles can't be simplified: they're a language themselves. When recombined into emotion in the second half, they activate the audience's own complex lived experience, not philosophical 'understanding.' He believes truly respecting the audience means trusting them to assemble the puzzle themselves, not catering to their desire for smooth pacing. It's like aiming for sunrise: the climb is more important and memorable than the view. A cable car straight to the top is something entirely different.

— Bi Gan
2:04:06

One change in how stories reach people multiplied a niche film's audience a hundredfold

Before Roadside Picnic's release, the marketing team predicted 500,000 yuan in ticket sales—'getting it into theaters was already a long shot.' Bi Gan guessed 7 million and was told he'd lost his mind. Opening day brought 1.5 million; it closed with over 6 million. He later realized it wasn't the film that changed—the era did. Traditional journalists migrated en masse to become social-media film critics, establishing a distribution channel that didn't previously exist, suddenly amplifying the odds that a niche art film would be seen.

— Bi Gan
2:32:27

Real film industry is anti-industrial: add time for thinking, not efficiency for shooting faster

When making The Last Night on Earth, Bi Gan first encountered formal industrial workflow and was deeply uncomfortable with it—until making Wild Years did it click: conventional film industry logic is 'cut costs, raise efficiency'—adding workers saves money if it costs less than shooting one more day. But art is the opposite. Time saved through industrialization should go toward deeper thinking and better work, not be silently redirected by the production system toward the next shoot. This realization was the most important lesson in his transition from guerrilla filmmaker to professional.

— Bi Gan
3:16:08

Short video isn't a storytelling problem—it's an addiction architecture that hijacks human nature

Facing the question of whether film is dying and micro-dramas matching film's box office, Bi Gan's view: film's problem is distance between storytellers and audience—fixable. But short video isn't a narrative tool problem; it's an addiction mechanism that's 'perfectly suited to human nature.' He refuses to install TikTok-like apps because once he starts, he'd be glued to it every day. It's objectively unhealthy, yet happening at massive scale.

— Bi Gan
