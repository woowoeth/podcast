Arthur Conan Doyle hated his creation—but Sherlock Holmes's real inspiration was a Scottish surgeon

Conan Doyle once tried to kill off Sherlock Holmes to escape the character, but it was Joseph Bell, the Scottish surgeon who taught him to ‘read patients’ in Edinburgh's anatomy theatre, who gave the detective all his flesh and blood.

6:51

Bell read patient bodies like crime scenes

Bell taught students at Edinburgh Royal Hospital how to discern a patient's origins, profession, and living habits before they spoke—a callus, a stain, a limp were all clues. In 1878, Conan Doyle became his student and witnessed this ‘cold logic’ firsthand. This was the earliest and most direct prototype scene for Sherlock Holmes's deductive method, and the starting point for the whole story.

14:02

Bell accepted female physicians earlier and more fully than Lister did

Bell was an early adopter of antiseptic technique and played a key role in professionalizing nursing in Scotland; he was friends with Florence Nightingale. More striking: Lister himself did not believe women should be doctors or surgeons, but Bell, though he taught male and female students separately, held them to the same high standards—a remarkably progressive stance for the time.

— Lindsay Fitzharris
20:07

The method came from Bell; the cases came from Littlejohn

Police surgeon John Littlejohn was older than Bell and officially responsible for conducting autopsies. While this was his formal role, he also unofficially advised police and pioneered fingerprint identification and court photography. When he recognized Bell's analytical mind, he began informally asking Bell to participate in cases. The two became a ‘detective partnership’ and together solved some of the 19th century's most sensational murders—a collaboration rarely mentioned in earlier Bell biographies.

— Lindsay Fitzharris
24:09

Scientific expert testimony and pseudoscience emerged together in forensic medicine

Before the mid-19th century, doctors could only testify in court if they had directly participated in a case, such as personally conducting an autopsy; afterward, the system changed and doctors could be hired to testify based solely on reviewing case notes, and the ‘expert witness’ was born. But during the same period, pseudosciences flourished—phrenology and ‘optography’, which claimed the eyeball could preserve images of the last thing a dying person saw. The latter has since been proven completely false.

— Lindsay Fitzharris
33:27

Conan Doyle tried to kill Holmes, then publicly revealed Bell's address

Conan Doyle always despised the Sherlock Holmes character and yearned to be seen as a serious writer. He wanted to kill Holmes off after about eighteen months of writing, but was forced to revive him due to public pressure. At nearly the same time, he publicly acknowledged that Bell was the prototype and dedicated the Sherlock Holmes stories to Bell—with his address included, effectively exposing Bell to the public.

— Lindsay Fitzharris
36:32

Jack the Ripper left the public hungry for genius detective stories

As literacy rates rose, the public consumed crime news through ‘penny bloods’ and sensational newspapers. The Jack the Ripper murders of 1888 went unsolved, and the image of ‘bumbling police’ took root. But Bell and Littlejohn, a real-world pair who repeatedly solved difficult cases, fit exactly what the public craved: a ‘master detective’—this was precisely the social soil in which Sherlock Holmes could flourish.

— Lindsay Fitzharris
42:36

In 1993, Conan Doyle's daughter settled the question once and for all

In the 1940s, Conan Doyle's son Adrian had publicly denied that his father was influenced by Bell, despite an article mentioning Bell's influence—even though Conan Doyle himself had acknowledged it multiple times in autobiography, interviews, and on film. It was not until 1993 that Conan Doyle's daughter Jean stated in an interview that it was Joseph Bell who inspired her father to create Sherlock Holmes. "He wrote it in black and white; what more evidence do you need?" she said.

Glossary

optography
The discredited belief that the human eyeball could preserve images of what a dying person last saw, as if recording on photographic film.
Penny Bloods
Cheap serialized crime fiction popular in the Victorian era, driven by sensational plots to build readership.
expert witness
From the mid-19th century onward, a medical professional legally permitted to testify in court based on case notes rather than direct participation in a case.
thief takers
Informal law enforcement agents in early 19th-century England who apprehended criminals for reward money, before the establishment of formal police forces.
