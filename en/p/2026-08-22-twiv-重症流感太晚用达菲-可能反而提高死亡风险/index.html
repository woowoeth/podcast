Tamiflu Too Late in Severe Flu Doubles the Death Risk

Severely ill flu patients who start Tamiflu about five days after symptoms have roughly twice the 90-day death risk of those who don't take it; an mRNA flu vaccine is approved, but only ages 50-64 got traditional approval.

This DRC Ebola outbreak is the deadliest on record

The Africa CDC's wording is heavier than the usual news coverage: the current DRC Ebola outbreak is already the country's deadliest and the fastest-growing on record, with a case fatality rate approaching 50% and roughly one life lost every 30 minutes; there are 5105 confirmed cases and 2420 deaths. The vaccine doesn't prevent infection, but it cuts transmission about 400-fold

A systematic review found 70 vaccinated people who still transmitted measles virus, causing 237 secondary cases and ultimately 812 traced cases; so "the vaccine prevents infection" does not hold absolutely. Daniel's conversion is the key part: an unvaccinated person infects an average of 20 people, whereas among vaccinated people a secondary case appears only about once per 20 people, a roughly 400-fold difference in transmission risk. He repeatedly stresses that the vaccine prevents severe disease, not all infection and transmission, and that what really needs watching is immunity gaps at the school and school-district level, not average vaccination rates.

— Daniel Griffin
21:39

The mRNA flu vaccine is only proven better in ages 50-64

The FDA approved Moderna's mRNA-1010 flu vaccine: traditional approval for ages 50-64, accelerated approval for those over 65. The key evidence is that in the 50-64 group the vaccine reduced disease by 27% relative to standard dose; in those over 65 it only proved "non-inferior to high-dose vaccine," not better, so the approval was conservative. Vincent says plainly that if he fell in the 50-64 range he would choose the mRNA version, and Daniel expects it to be available in late September or early October.

— Daniel Griffin
24:41

The problem with Tamiflu isn't that it doesn't work, it's that it's given too late

A randomized controlled trial of 442 critically ill flu patients showed a 90-day mortality of 13.7% without antivirals, versus 19.8% and 19.4% in the 5-day and 10-day Tamiflu arms, with an adjusted OR of about 2.1 and a 98% posterior probability of harm. Daniel points to the reason: enrollees were on average about 5 days into symptoms and most were already on high-flow oxygen or intubated, so the evidence for antivirals given early, within 48 hours, has not been overturned; the real signal may be that "given too late it doesn't help and may even harm," and that the endpoint should be extended to 90 days.

— Daniel Griffin
33:03

The winter when RSV overwhelmed children's hospitals may not come again

A French national matched cohort enrolled 74,672 infants in 2023 and 175,526 in 2024; RSV lower respiratory tract hospitalization rates were 0.8% in nirsevimab-vaccinated infants versus 2.4% in the unvaccinated group (0.5% vs 1.9% in 2024), with vaccine effectiveness of 66% and 72% respectively; there was no protection in the second year. Another UK study of maternal RSV vaccination during pregnancy found adjusted effectiveness of about 61% against infant hospitalization within 6 months of age. Daniel's hope is very concrete: the scene of children's hospitals being overwhelmed by RSV each winter may be rewritten.

— Daniel Griffin
40:21

In 150,000 pregnant women's data, no miscarriage link can be found

This responds to Republican officials treating old Fauci text messages as evidence that vaccines cause miscarriage. Daniel first gives the background: with or without vaccination, 10%-20% of known pregnancies end in spontaneous miscarriage; a 2022 pooled analysis of 23 studies covering about 118,000 vaccinated pregnant women and a 2023 analysis of 21 studies totaling 150,000 people both found no miscarriage link, and the 2022 analysis actually found a lower risk of stillbirth. The comparison numbers are more striking: nearly one-third of pregnant women infected with COVID in the first half of 2020 were hospitalized, versus only 6% of non-pregnant women of the same age, with a 70% higher death risk.

— Daniel Griffin
43:27

Viral infection isn't just the acute phase, and discharge isn't a cure

The JID commentary dismantles three myths: viral infection is not just an acute respiratory problem — it brings fatigue, brain fog and reduced physical function; it is not only the elderly, the young and the immunocompromised who suffer long-term damage — marathon runners infected with SARS-CoV-2 could not run three miles three weeks later; and third, "after the acute phase we provided the care that was needed" does not hold, since many long COVID specialty clinics in the US have closed because they don't make money. Daniel uses this to repeatedly argue for extending intervention endpoints to 90 days or even further, rather than calling discharge a success.

Glossary

cyclosporiasis
A foodborne parasitic disease acquired from contaminated produce; the oocysts are extremely hard to wash off, and the typical presentation is persistent watery diarrhea.
New World screwworm
Fly larvae that parasitize open wounds of warm-blooded animals and feed on flesh; in recent years it has spread from South America toward North America and also infects pets.
nirsevimab
A long-acting RSV monoclonal antibody given to infants; one shot protects through the entire season without waiting for active immunity to mature.
test-negative case-control
A study design that compares the vaccination history of test-positive and test-negative patients within the same group of hospitalized patients to estimate real-world vaccine effectiveness.
adaptive analysis
A trial with pre-specified rules for looking at results partway through, in order to decide whether to stop enrollment or change subsequent group allocation.
non-medical exemption
Parents keeping a child unvaccinated for school entry on religious or personal-belief grounds, not tied to a medical contraindication.
