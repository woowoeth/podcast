US Treasury yields near 5% — why hasn't the AI narrative broken yet

Money is getting more expensive in the US, yet AI is still inflating while every other industry deflates. This contradiction either gets resolved by a real AI breakthrough that pulls rates down, or by the narrative being falsified — but nobody dares slow down first.

Q3 markets: the exhaustion comes from having no direction

In Q3 the S&P rose 2.1%, the Nasdaq was roughly flat, Japan and Korea fell 8-point-something and 18.5 respectively, the CSI 300 dropped 9.4, ChiNext fell 23 points, and the Hang Seng rose 8 points though Hang Seng Tech remained very weak. Commodities were the bright spot: WTI and Brent both rose 40 points over the quarter, and gold rose 8.5. But the host says his own feeling is ‘a strong sense of exhaustion’ — unlike the first half, when hearing a narrative and watching the market move still made you wonder what opportunity there was, whether to buy or sell, how to allocate. Now it's ‘tired, forget it.’ Ricky's summary: the first half was making money with your heart in your throat; this quarter was adjusting until you were completely drained.

— David Weng
17:13

Three forces pushing US Treasury yields up

A Clock Tower research note attributes the rise in US long-end yields to three forces: first, energy prices rising as the Middle East stalemate drags on; second, resilient US nominal growth — Q2 real GDP growth of about 1.5 plus core PCE inflation of 3.4 gives roughly 5% nominal growth, comparable to the late 1990s, when the combined level of US Treasury yields was around 5%; third, rapid expansion of corporate bond issuance and continued AI infrastructure buildout, all against the backdrop of an already-high US fiscal deficit. The remedies correspond to Middle East de-escalation, tighter macro policy, and a substantial slowdown in the AI investment cycle — but the latter two contradict each other.

— David Weng
21:17

Bond yields are credit pricing, not a buy-sell mismatch

Ricky explains it by going up a dimension: bond yields are fundamentally the cost of funds generated on the basis of credit. The US entered a long period of low rates after the Soviet failure in the late 1980s and early 1990s, because it was the most powerful country in the world, so its borrowing cost should be the lowest in the world. Since 2018 the US has entered the middle-to-late phase of its national fortune cycle, facing more and more doubts, its credit downgraded, so its borrowing cost must rise. In the two-dimensional world you see fewer buyers of Treasuries and more redeemers; in the three-dimensional world you see declining national power driving up borrowing costs. The US decision at the three-dimensional level is to keep pushing on AI, mobilising the whole country for AI buildout while promoting manufacturing reshoring — which sets private capital and state capital competing for money, and everyone's borrowing cost rises.

— Ricky
26:20

Will AI infrastructure repeat the ROA trap of China's big buildout

Ricky compares US AI infrastructure to China's big infrastructure buildout starting in 2008: an economic entity dominated by CAPEX will inevitably see ROA decline — more and more assets, no breakthrough in production technology, so return per unit of asset falls. When ROA drops below the cost of borrowing, an asset crisis follows. The biggest worry now is that AI infrastructure will end up with ROA unable to cover borrowing costs, while borrowing costs are rising and ROA improvement is still not obvious. David Weng adds: what's different this time is that the deficit wasn't this high before — past cycles didn't casually involve trillions, and the current magnitude has exceeded the range of human comprehension.

— Ricky
41:29

The prisoner's dilemma behind calls to slow AI down

This week Dario wrote "We must pace the frontier," Musk reposted it saying he agreed, and Sam Altman also said in an interview that things should slow down, even saying OpenAI isn't in a hurry to IPO this year. A Reuters article on September 9 was titled "Researchers want AI to slow down, but labs can't afford to." Ricky sees three layers: first, right and wrong in the two-dimensional world cannot be overturned in the three-dimensional world — Asimov's Three Laws saw it a hundred years ago, and you can't stop a disaster before it happens; second, a unified alliance of three or more companies is unachievable — the prisoner's dilemma ends with everyone choosing the worst outcome, and it needs an external force, which is the government, but the government will only abandon its own problems when great-power competition produces enough damage and disaster; third, the funding side cannot tolerate such a long verification period — if within a year large models haven't proven they have sufficient application and commercial environments, many funds will face runs or even chain collapses, and the magnitude will certainly be countless times that of the 2008 subprime crisis.

— Ricky
51:38

Large-model revenue $150bn against $200bn of investment

Ricky does the math: total large-model revenue is $150bn, investment is $200bn. Split into frontier models and open-source models: frontier model revenue is about $120bn with $100bn invested; open-source models have $100bn invested and $30bn revenue. Frontier model revenue alone is just so-so, and open-source models are still a long way from actually generating revenue to cover investment — not even profit covering investment. So there's a lot of pressure on whether these companies will still post big profit increases next year. More critical is the second layer: even if corporate earnings still rise by low double digits next year, will the capital market accept it? Because the second derivative is actually declining, and valuations are set based on the second derivative, not on earnings growth. If you can't count on white-collar replacement, the math doesn't work, and it's definitely expensive.

— Ricky
1:00:40

Midterms: Trump losing the House is highly likely

According to BCA research, over more than a century of US history, every midterm election where performance on livelihood issues and inflation was poor has seen the president's party lose the House and Senate with near-certainty. The path given on the show early this year was: by June, hope for a US-Iran resolution, energy costs brought under control, and some fiscal policy support from Trump, and there was hope of holding the Senate. But neither has materialised, so polls suggest Trump losing the House is very likely, with many swing states and minorities like Hispanics — formerly Trump supporters — starting to switch to the Democrats. Trump holding the Senate is probably likely. If he only keeps one chamber of Congress, he becomes a lame duck.

— Ricky
1:25:03

Q4 A-shares have a rally, but don't go too far left

Ricky thinks A-shares can still look forward to a rally in Q4, but it shouldn't be played too far to the left; watch capital flows mainly, and if there's a short-term amplification of volume, it's worth participating. After that rally, switch to full defence and wait for the next, larger breakthrough — for example, something clear on white-collar replacement, or clearer investment next year in the Huawei supply chain and DeepSeek supply chain. If there is a rebound, it will certainly be led by tech, because many sectors have been tried without effect, and only tech can drive the market up. Most fund managers didn't do any work in Q3, busy writing self-criticisms; in Q4 they'll have to do something. David Weng is more conservative: still put cash first, second dividend low-volatility, third a smaller scope of tech like the STAR 50, with a position of maybe two or three points.

— Ricky

Glossary

CAPEX
Spending by a company to acquire, maintain or upgrade physical assets.
ROA
Net income divided by total assets, measuring how much profit each unit of assets generates.
carry trade
Borrowing in a low-interest-rate currency to invest in higher-yielding assets and earn the spread.
hyperscaler
A tech giant that owns large-scale data centres and cloud infrastructure.
second derivative
Here it means the rate of change of earnings growth; valuations are often based on this rather than the absolute growth rate.
