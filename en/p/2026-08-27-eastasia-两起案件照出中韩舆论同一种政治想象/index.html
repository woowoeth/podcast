Korean Murder Cases Reversed: Public Opinion Turns Incidents into Political Labels

The Chinese student case flipped from "Koreans killing Chinese" to a Korean-Chinese suspect; the police officer who took the report in the Jeju four-body case allegedly fabricated the closure. What truly warrants vigilance is not public safety, but how public opinion turns individual cases into political labels.

The crime time was misreported across Chinese media

Many Chinese internet reports said the girl went to Jeong's home at 2 PM on August 20; Korean surveillance shows she actually entered with him at 2 AM on August 20. About 20 hours later, Jeong left wearing her clothes and carrying her suitcase, took a taxi to Dongdaegu Station, turned off her phone, changed back into men's clothes in the restroom, and returned, creating the illusion that the girl had left. Police used this sequence to identify him, and the so-called boyfriend's report did not change the investigation.

27:07

The same Korean-Chinese identity serves both sides' narratives

When the case first broke, many on Chinese internet assumed it was Koreans killing a Chinese person, fearing anti-Chinese hate crimes. After the suspect was revealed to be Korean-Chinese, the narrative shifted to Chinese killing Chinese. Korean right-wingers and some netizens used it to claim Chinese people enter Korea to kill and that the Korean government indulges Chinese people, also saying the suspect's prison time is funded by Korean taxpayers. The same fact is exploited by both sides: Chinese public opinion wants to confirm Korean exclusion of Chinese, while Korean public opinion wants to confirm the threat of Chinese immigration.

— Zhang Zhe
28:07

Calls for deportation are wishful thinking: even a death sentence won't be carried out

Korea theoretically retains the death penalty but has not executed anyone in about 30 years. Even if Jeong is sentenced to death, he will not be executed. Under the principle of territorial jurisdiction, the case is handled by Korea. Extradition to China requires the consent of Korea, China, and the individual himself, and he obviously will not agree. More realistically, he may serve decades in a Korean prison and then be deported, by which time Chinese internet will have long stopped paying attention. Thus, online calls for deportation are more wishful thinking.

— Zhang Zhe
37:40

The closure reason was fabricated by the responding officer

Jang Mi-ran left home on the evening of May 12 and never returned. Three days later, her cohabiting boyfriend reported her missing. The officer who took the report, surnamed Bu, told colleagues he had contacted her, that she was safe and wanted to be alone, and the search was called off. Two months later, a new report was rejected because of the record that she had been found. Later, her family reported to Seoul police, who ordered a reinvestigation, and the body was only discovered on August 24. Records later showed that Officer Bu had never spoken with her; the closure reason was fabricated.

— Zhang Zhe
43:05

This is not a one-off mistake but a habit of closing cases

Officer Bu had been in the missing persons unit for about a year and a half, handling 298 cases. Media reported at least 10 were closed with the method of "person found, wants to be alone." He was also found to have left fingerprints in a women's restroom at the police station and was suspended on August 11. Another case of a 60-year-old man, also handled by him, was similarly written as "the person wants to be alone." The total number of missing person reports in Jeju is not unusual, but in his case, the proportion and method of suspicious closures exceed normal range and require case-by-case review.

— Zhang Zhe
46:25

The suicide theory is hard to accept, but the evidence has already decayed

The body was found at the edge of a palm grove. For over 100 days, farmland next to it was cultivated daily, yet no one noticed. Media reported she hanged herself, but palm trunks are brittle, and the road to the site at night had no streetlights and was full of potholes. Jang Mi-ran had previously said the grove was scary and she never approached it; her home was only 400 meters away. These details make the suicide theory hard to fully accept, but the body was highly decomposed, and beyond a neck fracture, little evidence could be extracted.

— Zhang Zhe
49:27

If police theory holds she died, her phone turned on four days later

If, as police speculate, Jang Mi-ran died the night she disappeared, her phone was last turned on at 9:44 AM on May 16 at Hallim Port, two kilometers away. This signal falls within the two-kilometer radius between her home and the discovery site. More troubling, surveillance after her disappearance could have been checked, but because Officer Bu had already closed the case, by the time the investigation was reopened, the footage had been overwritten, leaving only the initial footage of her leaving home. The missing evidence chain makes it hard to re-investigate and leaves room for various speculations.

— Zhang Zhe
53:32

Conspiracy theories lack evidence; procedural failure is real

Korea ranks first in OECD suicide rate, while its intentional homicide rate is only moderate. Thus, Jeju police's inclination to classify the four bodies as suicide is not entirely baseless given the data and remaining forensic evidence. The problem is that key evidence disappeared due to the initial erroneous closure, and police tend to close cases quickly, which amplifies conspiracy theories. Currently, there is no direct evidence for mafia, murder maniacs, or police protection; the most solid accusation is procedural failure.

— Zhang Zhe
