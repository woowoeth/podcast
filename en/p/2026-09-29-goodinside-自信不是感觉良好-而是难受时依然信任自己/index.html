Confidence isn't feeling good about yourself; it's trusting yourself even when things hurt

Parents rush to fix their child's pain, smooth over outcomes, and soothe inner doubt with external validation—loving instincts that narrow the range of circumstances where children can safely be themselves.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:01

Confidence means opposite things: fitting in to one parent, standing alone to another

In one consultation, parents worry their child clings to them at a birthday party instead of joining in like other kids. In another, a child stays silent during group-text bullying while others pile on, and the child gets suspended along with the bullies. Both sets of parents say their child lacks confidence. But the definition is nearly opposite: one family equates confidence with quickly joining the group; the other sees it as the ability to resist when everyone else is doing something. This contradiction—confidence meaning opposite things depending on context—sets up the entire premise of the conversation.

— Dr. Becky
4:03

Confidence is trusting yourself despite failure, not feeling good about yourself

If you define confidence as feeling good about yourself, parents instinctively move to patch things over, reassure, and shield their child from situations that would feel bad. But children will inevitably fail, be rejected, walk into rooms where nobody knows them. Real confidence isn't "I feel good about myself"—it's "even though I wasn't chosen, even though I performed worst in the room, even though I choked and messed up, I can still be myself." The goal isn't to reduce the discomfort but to widen the range of circumstances where a child can accept themselves. It's about being OK with yourself when things don't feel OK at all.

— Dr. Becky
5:04

Preventing difficulty is modern parenting's deepest hidden trap

This generation has made it easier than ever to make problems disappear: conflict with a teacher gets an email; a child falling behind gets a tutor; boredom gets a screen. None of these moves is wrong in isolation, but together they teach a powerful lesson: "When I hurt, the outside world must change." What gets lost is the experience of staying in the discomfort, asking yourself what you need, and discovering you can handle it. Confidence is built on exactly this kind of experience—the ability to sit with difficulty and find your own way through. And parents are systematically removing it.

— Dr. Becky
7:07

Denying a child's feelings teaches them their own judgment is unreliable

A child says, "I'm bad at soccer." A parent's first reflex is, "No, you're not." Maybe the child is right; maybe they really do perform poorly at soccer. But this response teaches a silent lesson: you need to be good at things in order to feel okay about yourself. A more durable confidence sounds like this: "Yeah, I do kick poorly. And I'm still okay with me as a person." The parent's denial, no matter how well-intentioned, whispers that a child can only feel acceptable when they're performing well—which means their self-worth is always in someone else's hands.

— Dr. Becky
9:09

Paving your child's path trains them to expect rescue, not resilience

Your child doesn't get invited to a sleepover. The urge is to text the other parent, work to get the invitation sent. Your child has friction with a teacher; the instinct is to call and advocate. These acts come from love, but done as reflex—as the habit of actually changing the outcome—they shrink the range of situations a child learns to navigate alone. The real question parents need to ask is not "How do I fix this?" but "Am I paving this particular road, or am I preparing this child for any road?" Because at 21, nobody wants the phone call where their kid asks them to email the professor.

— Dr. Becky
11:10

Real confidence comes from surviving what didn't work out, not winning

Confidence isn't "I can bend circumstances to my will." It's "Most of the time, circumstances don't bend to my will, and I've survived that. The pain of it shrinks over time." This understanding doesn't come from being told, "Not everything goes your way." Kids hear that and forget it by next week. It comes from actually living through "It didn't happen the way I wanted, and nobody came to rescue me." Then discovering: "I'm still here. I can handle this." That lived experience—that track record of weathering disappointment—is where real confidence is built. There's no shortcut.

— Dr. Becky
12:12

Outside voices drown out children's ability to evaluate themselves

"What did you score?" "How did your friends do?" "You're the best player on the team." These questions aren't inherently wrong. The problem is when a child learns to judge themselves only through external metrics—grades, what friends think, how many likes, what coaches and parents say. Shift the question: instead of "You got an A, that's great," ask "Tell me how you studied; what did you get right?" Instead of "You scored two goals," ask "What part of this game made you proudest?" These small moves build an internal evaluation system—a private sense of what you do well—instead of leaving children dependent on an audience's applause.

— Dr. Becky
16:19

Before you comfort your child, ask which problem you're actually solving

When a child is hurting, pause before speaking. Ask yourself: Am I trying to change how they feel? Change what happened? Use something external to make their inner world feel better? A child says they didn't get invited to the sleepover. The practice is: say "Oh." Look at them. Prepare yourself to listen. Tell yourself, "Don't speak, don't speak, don't speak." Create space for them to tell the whole story—not for you to immediately offer comfort or solutions. Often the most generous thing a parent can do is sit with their child's disappointment rather than race to fix it.

— Dr. Becky
