Existence Itself Has No Content: Peirce Overturns Substance-Centered Ontology in Nine Pages

In a nine-page 1868 paper, Peirce uses the criterion that a concept must be able to unify the contents of consciousness to derive being from substance, and then to derive intermediate categories such as quality. Existence itself has no content; it is only the connection of predicate to subject.

This episode reads through Peirce's most condensed nine-page text sentence by sentence, repeatedly comparing it with Kant and Aristotle. Once you understand precision and ground, you can see how he transforms the transcendental deduction into a method for deriving categories.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:04

A concept's only qualification is ‘you can't do without it’

Peirce comes straight to the point: the function of a concept is ‘to reduce the manifold of sense impressions to unity’; a concept is valid if and only if, without introducing it, the contents of consciousness cannot be unified. This is a reworking of Kant's transcendental deduction—not a complete table of transcendental logic, but a reverse verification of ‘you can't do without it.’ Wes points out that Peirce does not, like Aristotle, directly announce that he is doing metaphysics, but instead enters through a shorter introduction, so reading it requires supplying the systematic intention oneself.

10:00

Substance is not a natural kind, only an unspeakable ‘it’

The first universal concept is ‘present in general,’ that is, ‘it.’ It is closest to sensation, but has no intension, no predicates at all, and is merely the mind's pure power of indication. Peirce, in philosophical language, calls it one meaning of substance: substance cannot be predicated of anything, cannot be in a subject. Unlike Aristotle, who understands substance as a natural kind, substance here is a ‘given’ that has not yet been cut in any way, an X in the perceptual flux that can only be pointed to, not spoken.

23:25

Being has no content; it only attaches predicate to subject

The unity of a proposition comes from the connection of predicate and subject, and the being expressed by the copula ‘is’ is precisely the element that completes this connection. Peirce says the concept of being ‘manifestly has no content’—it contains only ‘the connectability of predicate to subject,’ the common point of the two verbs (actually is / will be). Thus substance and being form the two ends: substance has no unity at all, being does all the unifying work. Other categories are still needed in between to fill the gap.

31:26

You can precisify color out of space, but not the reverse

Peirce redefines three kinds of mental separation: discrimination draws boundaries only at the level of meaning; dissociation separates two images when association is absent; precision lies between the two, attending to one element without assuming the other really exists. The key symmetry-breaking: one can precisify color out of space, but cannot precisify space out of color; cannot precisify red out of color. This directionality provides the lever for the subsequent derivation of categories.

44:19

Categories are not derived from a list but from the directionality of abstraction

Peirce turns the asymmetry of precision into a method: to find a universal concept lying between substance and being, see whether a concept can reduce a manifold to unity, or connect an already unified object with another concept. An element that is precisified can be thought before the concept, while a concept can be thought apart from the element. From this direction he derives three intermediate categories—quality, relation, representation—as the connectors between substance and being.

49:10

A property is not an accident of substance but an abstraction first detached

Quality is the first intermediate category moving from being toward substance. The example ‘stove is black’ reveals: to make this proposition, one must first let ‘black’ detach from the stove itself and be grasped as an independent quality; black is a pure abstraction, and when applied to the stove it is hypothetical and fallible. Peirce thus stresses that a property is not an accident adhering to substance, but an abstract item that must be separated out in advance.

58:12

A quality can fail to exist, but existence cannot do without quality

Peirce names this pure abstraction ground: the respect in which two things can agree is the pure abstraction we use to compare them, such as black. The key asymmetry is: ground cannot be precisified out of being—being cannot do without concrete qualities; but being can be precisified out of ground—a given quality need not exist. Ground therefore both connects substance and being and is the formal expression of quality.

Glossary

manifold
The multiplicity of sense impressions; in Kantian philosophy, the manifold content that is given.
precision
A term peculiar to Peirce, referring to the mode of abstraction that attends to one part of an object without assuming the other part, and that has directionality.
ground
For Peirce, the pure abstract quality represented by a predicate, such as black, serving as the basis for agreement among objects.
copula
That is, ‘is,’ the word connecting subject and predicate.
substance
Here Peirce means the ‘present it’ closest to perception, a purely indicative object with no intension.
