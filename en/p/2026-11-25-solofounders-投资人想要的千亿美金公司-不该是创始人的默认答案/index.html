Hundred-billion-dollar companies investors want shouldn't be your default goal

The Solo Founders podcast host argues that assuming you should raise capital is one of founders' worst decisions. Clarify first whether you actually want to build a ten-billion-dollar company, then decide whether to raise funding.

1:01

Prove demand before you worry about system reliability

Validate that people actually want your product before you invest in system reliability. The key question isn't whether your product is perfect—it's how severe failure would be. Death or inconvenience? Most early validation doesn't require production-ready code. Data backup risks exist, but cloud code or codex make those easy to handle. Vibe-coded products typically suffice until you reach paying customers.

— Julian Weisser
5:08

Build early traction on X by liking, not replying

Competition concerns often seem bigger than they are—users rarely truly overlap in something like campground bookings. The real early tactic is simpler: on X, like relevant content instead of replying. Liking isn't spammy; it's genuine interest, and people will check your profile to see what you're doing. Replies are easier to get wrong and look promotional, so skip them.

— Julian Weisser
8:12

Creating new markets beats fighting for existing customers

Consider whether you're replacing an existing service or creating one that never existed. One company compressed $400,000-per-year salary work to $40,000—suddenly, customers who couldn't afford the service could. Customers who pay more are often better (they expect to spend money); but small customers come with less internal politics since no one feels threatened by a new tool. Creating a new market wins on excitement and opportunity.

— Julian Weisser
12:24

Account executive hiring determines your growth speed

One company's data: account executives are the only constraint on growth. Every additional hire delivers quantifiable value. If that's your bottleneck, your funding case is clear; if product development is the constraint, it's more complex. Tactical move: if you have a waitlist, ask them to prepay—it covers hiring costs and proves real customer pull to investors.

— Julian Weisser
15:30

Reject angels who demand the board chairman seat

An anonymous founder described an investor promising funding, accelerator access, and mentorship in exchange for extra equity and a board chairman seat. This is a bright line to refuse. No real accelerator guarantees outcomes—anyone promising one is lying. More fundamentally: you don't have a company yet, just a founder and prototype. Demanding the chairman role now means you're giving away control before anything exists. Run.

— Julian Weisser
17:36

Keep pulling the thread past where you feel satisfied

Think of a good idea as a thread you pull through. If you stop pulling when things feel good, you'll stay at local maxima—decent, but far from your potential. Meanwhile, don't mistake reversible decisions for permanent ones. If something could be tested and reversed in a week, don't spend three weeks deliberating on whether to try it. The learning opportunity is real, and the switching cost is low.

— Julian Weisser
23:50

Defaulting to fundraising is one of founders' worst decisions

Most investors now ask if you can build a $100 billion company. That scale often conflicts with what you actually want. Defaulting to assume you should raise is one of the worst decisions a founder makes. First, clarify: do you want that scale? Or would you prefer your current revenue, compound it, invest elsewhere, and live the life you want? Both are legitimate. The problem isn't preferring one over the other—it's defaulting to raising without asking the question at all.

— Julian Weisser
