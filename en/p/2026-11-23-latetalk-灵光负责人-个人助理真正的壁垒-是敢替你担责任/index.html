Personal assistants' real moat is daring to take responsibility for you

Lingguang (灵光) swaps 'doing things' for 'managing things': a good personal assistant should care more about your affairs than you do, and proactively take responsibility, rather than just passively completing tasks.

7:03

Cloud Code's mishap revealed long-horizon task execution is viable

Last year, Cai Wei's team experimented with Cloud Code to monitor stocks—if prices moved beyond a certain percentage, it would send an alert. She didn't expect success: as an overseas-native model, Cloud Code shouldn't have been optimized for WeChat. Yet it found third-party tools on GitHub, bridged WeChat push notifications, and required only a QR scan and two clicks from users. This unexpected success convinced the team that models' ability to execute long-horizon tasks had reached practical viability—the first key signal driving Lingguang from coding scenarios toward life scenarios.

— Cai Wei
9:04

Manus showed assistants shouldn't be trapped in a chat box

Around the same time, the popularity of Manus taught the team that model capabilities had already broken through the form of conversation itself. Nearly all AI-native applications before this had been trapped within a conversation-box framework; the only differences were question types. Manus made Cai Wei realize that a true assistant should proactively tell you the right thing at the right time and help you do the right thing—not wait for you to request something then act. This became the theoretical foundation for Lingguang's later positioning as a Personal Agent rather than a chatbot appendage.

— Cai Wei
20:09

Building its own agent runtime meant abandoning open-source shortcuts

Initially, Lingguang tried building on top of existing open-source agent frameworks to avoid reinventing the wheel. But those architectures were designed to control a 'super-tool' computer better suited to desktop productivity, with poor extensibility and control on mobile. After a month of experimentation, the team abandoned this approach for self-built Agent Runtime—a system for scheduling when models should be passively triggered, event-triggered, or state-change triggered. This scheduling layer became one of the most time-intensive parts of a six-month development cycle.

— Cai Wei
26:09

Personal assistants need intelligence, integration, and trust—you can't skip any

Cai Wei ranks the factors determining personal assistant experience in this order: intelligence, supply (which services it can connect to and invoke), and trust & security. She sees these not as optional features but as necessary conditions—any significant shortfall makes the product hard for ordinary users to adopt. Intelligence and security the team can refine internally, but supply depends on whether third-party ecosystems will open their APIs. This ecosystem openness is also why domestic Personal Agents are commonly criticized for failing to connect to other companies' services.

— Cai Wei
32:30

Round-the-clock agents stay cost-efficient through off-peak background task scheduling

Outside observers worry that a 24-hour-attending personal assistant would have vastly higher compute costs than ask-and-answer products. Cai Wei disagrees with this accounting. Reactive task products must balance quality and low latency, limiting them to expensive chips. But Personal Agents execute many actions in the background—running ten minutes earlier or later doesn't affect experience—so they can use smaller models and cheaper chips, and optimize GPU utilization through scheduling. She rejects the simple math that 'if queries are 10x higher, costs should be too,' and also questions the statistical basis of Barclays' cost estimates.

— Cai Wei
48:39

Threads, not conversations, are the atomic unit of Lingguang's design

Lingguang chose not to use long conversations as its main interaction layer, instead making 'threads' (or 'items' internally) the core: maintaining a relationship with family, long-term blood pressure management, planning an investment—each is a specific thread to continuously manage, not a chat log. Cai Wei sees this as how to best express Personal Agent distinctiveness now that models can sustain large amounts of context. The team deliberately emphasizes 'managing' rather than 'doing,' to reinforce users' sense of delegation.

— Cai Wei
51:19

Retention matched Muse but users complained it couldn't connect outside

Within a week of launch, Lingguang's retention reached parity with Muse—above the team's beta estimates. The most concentrated complaint was users saying it 'can't break through company silos': users assume Lingguang and Alipay are both part of Ant Group and should access everything in Alipay, but many services actually come from third-party merchants requiring separate authorization. Lingguang won't auto-consume those just because they're in-house—this gap between expectation and reality is what users perceive as 'ecosystem closure,' not actual departmental walls.

— Cai Wei
1:29:43

An agent's real moat is accumulated context, not raw model capability

Cai Wei predicts the market will sustain fewer than ten dominant Personal Agents, possibly fewer than five. The barrier isn't technical—it's three-fold: first, a sufficiently strong assistant has exclusivity; users won't maintain ten assistants in parallel. Second, switching costs: the more an assistant knows your habits, speech patterns, and schedule, the harder it is to switch. Third, two-sided platform effects—better service supply strengthens the assistant, which attracts more supply. She notes that China's major tech companies already have leverage on all three dimensions, leaving Lingguang a lead window of roughly three to six months.

— Cai Wei
