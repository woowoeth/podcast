The Maya Ballgame Was Ritual, Not Sport: The Losing Team Gets Sacrificed Is Made Up

The Mesoamerican ballgame is the earliest rubber-ball team sport in human history, but it was first and foremost a religious ritual: the court re-enacts a creation myth, and it is captured warriors cast as the losing side who might be killed. The idea that the winners were sacrificed was invented later.

This episode uses first-hand archaeological evidence to dismantle two myths about the ballgame: it was not just entertainment, and winning or losing did not equal life or death. It also explains along the way how the ball was made and how gambling could ruin a man.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:33

Rubber-ball sport does not start in Greece or China

Ed says "team sport" may not be the earliest, but the ancestor of rubber-ball team sport is most likely the Mesoamerican ballgame. The Greek Olympics had no ball; China and Persia had ball games, but they only go back to 500 BC; the Maya ballgame is at least a thousand years earlier than those. Current evidence points even earlier: rubber balls from 1600-1700 BC have been found at Olmec sites, and the oldest ball court dates to around 1650 BC.

— Ed Barnhart
4:06

The ballgame was not Maya-only; all of Mesoamerica played it

The ballgame was not unique to the Maya but a shared element of all Mesoamerican cultures. The Maya core area is in what is now Guatemala, extending north to the Yucatán Peninsula and Mexico's Chiapas and Tabasco, and south to half of Honduras; further west were cultures such as the Zapotec and Mixtec. The Classic period peak was around AD 400 to 800, with independent city-states that nonetheless shared religion, calendar, writing and the ballgame.

— Ed Barnhart
10:00

You could only hit the ball with hips, knees, shoulders and chest; the hip throw was the classic strike

According to Aztec rules recorded by the Spanish: players could not use their hands or feet, only hips, knees, shoulders and chest, and the most classic strike was the hip throw. The ball was a solid rubber "super bouncy ball" about six inches in diameter, and being hit by it hurt. The court had a centre line and end lines: if the ball went out over the end line, the other team scored; if you hit the ball through a stone ring high on the side wall, the game ended immediately. Formations had a front court and defenders, similar to modern football.

— Ed Barnhart
18:56

The court was first a mythic site, and only second a playing field

Ed explicitly rejects separating Maya religion from sport. The large stone structures at the centre of Maya courts were first and foremost ceremonial: the Hero Twins play a ballgame against the lords of the underworld in the creation myth, so the court becomes a space that re-enacts the moment of creation. The court at Chichén Itzá is so vast it does not look like it could host a real game, and its stone rings are about 30 feet high; a more reasonable explanation is dancing, sacrifice and symbolic re-enactment. Courts were often built at the lowest point of a city, imitating the underworld.

— Ed Barnhart
39:13

This sport could kill you, or cost you your whole family

The ball was more dangerous than you would think: a Spanish priest witnessed a player struck in the abdomen between his protective gear, coughing blood and dying within an hour, possibly from a ruptured internal organ. The ball was made from natural rubber with sulphur added, and the Maya most likely extracted sulphur from morning glory sap; Ed made one himself using the ancient method, and it is still around 20 years later. Gambling on the game was just as wild: players could bet on themselves, and after a losing streak they could lose their money, their house, and even their whole family into slavery to nobles; if a noble tried to slip away after the match without handing over gifts, the players had the right to bring him down and take his things.

— Ed Barnhart
44:50

The sacrificed-winners story was made up eight years ago

Ed debunks it on the spot: the claim that the winning player was sacrificed was admitted eight years ago by an old man at Chichén Itzá to be something he made up, because people loved hearing it so much. Logically, if the winners were going to die, no one would play well. Those who might actually be sacrificed were captives, arranged to play the "lords of the underworld" side in a ritual re-enactment and killed after losing. He himself excavated a headless body at the centre line of a simple court in Belize, with a turtle shell where the skull should have been, directly echoing the story of the Hero Twins' decapitation.

— Ed Barnhart
55:48

A city of 100,000 has no court; most likely it just has not been recognised

Among the great Mesoamerican cities, Teotihuacan may have reached a peak population of 100,000, yet no certain ball court has been found there, even though murals show people passing a ball; while contemporary sites within 50 kilometres all have courts. Ed thinks this is not "they did not play" but that archaeologists have not yet recognised its form — it could have been a temporary court, built of earth and wood, or simply using a pyramid wall to bounce the ball. This counterexample shows how far our understanding of the ballgame's evolution still has to go.

— Ed Barnhart
59:01

Spain tore down the courts but never truly banned the game

Spanish priests quickly saw through the ballgame's inseparable link to local religion, so they tore down courts and banned the game, hoping to replace it with Christianity. But people kept playing underground, especially in regions like Sinaloa that the Spanish could not be bothered to police, and the ballgame was never truly interrupted. Today there are leagues in Yucatán, Guatemala and Belize, with Maya people re-forming teams to play each other, and Ed hopes that one day it becomes a properly televised event or even an Olympic sport.

— Ed Barnhart

Glossary

Popol Vuh
The sacred creation text of the K'iche' Maya, recording the story of the Hero Twins' ballgame against the lords of the underworld.
Mesoamerica
The pre-Columbian cultural region from central Mexico to northwestern Central America, sharing a calendar, writing and the ballgame tradition.
Hero Twins
The twin ballplayers of Maya myth who defeat the lords of the underworld and become gods of maize and the stars.
yoke
The heavy U-shaped protective gear worn around the waist by Mesoamerican ballplayers, used to strike the rubber ball.
morning glory
A plant whose sap contains sulphur and may have been used by the Maya to vulcanise rubber balls for greater durability.
ball court
The distinctive I-shaped stone structure of Mesoamerica, with sloping walls on either side of a central alley, serving as both playing field and ritual space.
