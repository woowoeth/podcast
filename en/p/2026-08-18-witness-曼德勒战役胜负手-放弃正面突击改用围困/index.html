Mandalay hinged on one call: drop the frontal assault, lay siege

In March 1945 British forces attacked Mandalay in Burma; against Fort Dufferin's thick walls and deep moat, the frontal assault failed. The division commander refused to keep storming it and switched to a siege, and the Japanese eventually abandoned the fort.

1:01

Mandalay was attacked to take Rangoon before the monsoon

In March 1945 the British 14th Army was pursuing retreating Japanese forces through central Burma. Mandalay was Burma's second-largest city and strategically placed; the British believed that taking it would let them liberate the capital, then called Rangoon, before the monsoon arrived in May. The Japanese were dug in at the Mandalay palace fort, which the British called Fort Dufferin. The host explains that this episode works differently from the usual format: instead of finding a survivor to recall the events, it uses the original audio that BBC correspondent Douglas Claverdon recorded at the front line in 1945.

— Tim O'Callaghan
3:04

Eight feet of brick plus a moat left no frontal answer

Standing by the flagstaff at Fort Dufferin, Claverdon describes the defences: the fort is more than a mile square, walled on all four sides in dark red brick roughly 30 feet high, the brickwork 8 feet thick, with earth banked up behind it 30 feet wide at ground level; outside that runs a moat 70 yards wide and 10 or 11 feet deep. On the afternoon of 9 March the Frontier Force Regiment, supported by tanks and artillery, pushed to within 100 yards of the wall, but the Japanese sheltering behind the wall and the moat could not be driven out, and the British could do no more than consolidate the ground they held.

— Douglas Claverdon
4:05

Men cresting the bank became silhouettes against the brick wall

On 10 March Reese, commanding the 19th Indian Division, launched a full assault. A 5.5-inch gun fired directly at the wall from about 250 yards for more than an hour. The BBC recording van pulled up behind a small concrete building 300 to 400 yards outside the north wall, and a senior officer recorded a live commentary from the spot: the troops were going forward, the tanks were firing hard, but the formation was a little bunched. Even so, nothing got in: the Japanese had sited their machine guns close to the waterline of the moat, and the brick wall behind made the British clear silhouettes as they came over the bank, so they were shot down one by one.

— Douglas Claverdon
5:07

The division commander called it a hornets' nest, not worth storming

With the assault beaten, Reese ordered a withdrawal. The next day he told war correspondents that he had never seen braver men than the Frontier Force Regiment; but as he saw it, Fort Dufferin was nothing more than a hornets' nest, and he was not prepared to spend the lives of large numbers of his soldiers on a fort that could only be taken by frontal assault. So for the next two days the 19th Indian Division worked forward step by step through the wrecked houses of the city, while night patrols kept probing the walls for weak points. The tactics shifted here from assault to siege, and what followed proved the choice a sound one.

— Douglas Claverdon
6:08

The siege did not go smoothly: the boat crossing the moat sank

Parked under trees at the roadside, the BBC van recorded an infantry company advancing under enemy fire while it waited for another company to work around from the left. The recordist says he spent most of the time lying underneath the van, wishing he had a steel helmet. On the night of 16 March the 15th Punjab tried to cross the moat by boat and get into the fort, but one boat sank, drowning the leading troops of the second wave; to avoid further casualties the unit pulled back before dawn. This stretch shows a siege going badly, with the Japanese still resisting.

— Douglas Claverdon
7:09

Once the ring closed, the Japanese pulled out that same night

On 19 March the Allies threw a heavy attack at a pagoda commanding the east gate: bombers went in first, then heavy artillery, and then two companies of the Worcestershire Regiment took the objective with tank support, bringing the encirclement of Fort Dufferin close to complete. The Japanese evacuated the fort that night. Mountbatten, Supreme Allied Commander South East Asia, then announced over the radio that Mandalay had fallen, noting that Japanese propaganda had called it a position they would fight to hold; he thanked all the services and the British, American, Indian, African and Chinese troops.

— Douglas Claverdon
8:11

Mandalay's fall was one link in the chain to Japan's surrender

After Mandalay fell, the 14th Army entered the Burmese capital in May 1945, and Burma was then liberated, which is seen as one of the links that pushed Japan to surrender to the Allies in August 1945. The host closes by recommending other oral histories from the same war: Norwegian fishermen rowing small boats across the North Sea to support the resistance, a woman recalling the Japanese invasion of Malaya when she was a little girl, and the Japanese occupation of Shanghai.

— Tim O'Callaghan

Glossary

Fort Dufferin
The British name for the Mandalay palace fort, ringed by high walls and a moat, where Japanese troops held out in March 1945.
19th Indian Division
A divisional formation of the British Indian Army, which led the attack on Mandalay and ultimately laid siege to Fort Dufferin.
Frontier Force Regiment
An infantry regiment of the British Indian Army, which made the first attack on Fort Dufferin on 9 March.
14th Army
The main British army in the Burma theatre, made up of units from Britain, India, Africa and elsewhere.
