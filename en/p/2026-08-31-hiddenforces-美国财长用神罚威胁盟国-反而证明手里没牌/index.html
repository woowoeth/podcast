America's Treasury Secretary Threatens Allies with Divine Wrath, Proving He Has No Cards

Treasury Secretary Bessent portrays America as an irritable god, demanding allies submit as if to Pascal's wager. Farrell sees through the editorial: the louder the threat, the more it proves America lacks the means to follow through, and allies are already hedging.

The Treasury chief casts America as an angry god

In Bessent's Financial Times editorial on 'economic D-Day,' he frames allies' choice as a Pascal's wager. Farrell's read: he's essentially saying you must treat America as God, appeasing this wrathful deity by any means, lest it strike you with lightning. Farrell likens it to a 17th-century Puritan sermon, retitled 'Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God,' with the sinners being the rest of the world. The problem: such pulpit-thumping usually signals a pastor losing his congregation, not strength.

10:18

The louder the shout, the softer the stick

Roosevelt's maxim was 'speak softly and carry a big stick.' Farrell says Bessent reverses it: speak bigly and carry a soft stick. The real test is China—currently the country most capable of supporting Iran—and Bessent won't answer whether America would dare bring its wrath down on China if it keeps buying Iranian oil. Farrell believes markets and nations are betting 'no.' So the editorial reads as an empty threat: the more overblown the language, the more America seems to be bluffing at an empty table.

15:20

Flexible realism equals no strategy at all

Using 'flexible realism' to explain White House behavior is just draping a cloth over chaos. Farrell says the term, unpacked, means only: America does what it wants, and other countries should adapt. What actually drives the White House isn't strategy but a scramble for attention, like Cosimo de' Medici making everyone revolve around him—except Trump does 'a stupid version': to be the center of attention, he'll make decisions that are idiotic in both the short and long term. Other countries respond by cutting losses, not doubling down.

— Henry Farrell
22:21

Ambition-countering-ambition has broken down

Federalist No. 10 offered 'ambition must be made to counteract ambition': let each person's drive for power cancel out the others, grinding toward something close to the public interest. Farrell says this mechanism is dismantled from two ends. One is the Republican Party: internal dissent has nearly vanished—senators won't openly block even when they object—and unified control short-circuits the usual checks. The other is the federal bureaucracy: the Supreme Court embraces the 'unitary executive' theory, inspectors general are fired or cowed, and ordinary civil servants are converted to at-will employees. An utterly incompetent person sits atop the system, and errors amplify into national madness.

— Henry Farrell
31:21

Rebuilding US institutions requires learning to experiment

Farrell doesn't think institutions can return to the old consensus of the early 1990s to 2015, but he also doesn't see America as doomed to disaster. His hopeful example is New York Mayor Mamdani's experiment: building feedback loops between city government and the public, turning people's real troubles into policy, and using social media to show 'I'm listening and acting.' He explicitly links this to Roosevelt's 'bold, persistent experimentation': keep what works, discard what doesn't. The open question is whether America's media and political system can tolerate a politician openly saying 'this path failed, let's try another'—without that tolerance, experiments won't go far.

— Henry Farrell
38:23

The more flexible, the less anyone takes you seriously

The biggest problem with flexible realism is that it destroys an asset that erodes with caprice—credibility. Farrell cites Nobel laureate Thomas Schelling: a nation's most important assets include credibility; promises and threats that can be carried out are what actually work in negotiations. If you change your mind at will, if yesterday's solemn threat vanishes tomorrow, then at some point no one takes you seriously. Farrell sees America in that state today: other nations still fear its capacity to harm, but no longer believe in its capacity for long-term action. His image: a drunk grandfather with a shotgun—everyone humors him to keep him from firing.

— Henry Farrell
46:23

America is smashing its own web with a sledgehammer

In 'Underground Empire,' Farrell and Newman's core argument: globalization is a web of interdependence—supply chains stitched together by thousands of suppliers across dozens of countries, with dollar clearing as the nervous system. America built this system and kept a 'house advantage' for itself, using the threat of cutting off access to discipline others. The Trump administration isn't delicately dismantling the web; it's swinging a sledgehammer at random parts. Farrell's prescription is clear: full decoupling would be catastrophic; the goal isn't independence but resilient interdependence, and major powers must relearn the language of 'restraint.'

— Henry Farrell
48:23

A rare-earth card punctures the myth of invincibility

On last year's 'Liberation Day' tariffs, Bessent publicly said America held all the aces and China had none. Weeks later, China played its rare-earth and critical-minerals card, and America suddenly realized it held a busted flush—a hand that looked fierce but never materialized. Farrell sees this as the moment that sobered Washington; since then, Marco Rubio and others have shown awareness that China can materially damage the US economy. The US and China now live in a fragile balance where each knows 'I can hurt you badly,' but they haven't yet sat down to turn that balance into more stable rules.

Glossary

Pascal's wager
God's existence can't be proven, but believing yields higher expected value; Bessent twists it into 'submitting to America is safer.'
weaponized interdependence
Controlling chokepoints in global networks to coerce others by threatening to cut off their access.
flexible realism
The Trump administration's stated doctrine; Farrell argues it has no content—just 'America does what it wants.'
unitary executive
Theory embraced by the Supreme Court majority: the executive branch only carries out the president's will, weakening internal checks.
neo-royalism
Farrell, citing Abe Newman and Stacey Goddard: dividing the world economy by family and networks rather than national interest.
busted flush
Poker term: a hand one card short of a flush; America discovers its hand is actually worthless.
