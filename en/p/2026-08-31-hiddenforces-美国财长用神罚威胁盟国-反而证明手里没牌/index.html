The US Treasury Secretary Threatens Allies with Divine Wrath, Proving He Has No Cards

Treasury Secretary Bessent portrays America as an irritable god and demands allies submit like Pascal's wager. Farrell sees through this op-ed: the louder the threat, the more it proves the US lacks the means to deliver, and allies have already begun to fend for themselves.

8:17

The Treasury Secretary casts America as an angry god

In his "economic D-Day" op-ed in the Financial Times, Bessent packaged the choice facing allies as Pascal's wager. Farrell's dissection: he is effectively saying you must treat America as God, appease this irritable deity by any means, or it will strike you with lightning. Farrell likens it to a 17th-century Puritan sermon, one that could be retitled "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," except the sinners are the rest of the world. The problem is that this kind of sermonizing is usually the reaction of a preacher who sees his congregation leaving, not a sign of strength.

10:18

The louder the shout, the softer the stick

FDR's line was "speak softly and carry a big stick." Farrell says Bessent's approach is the reverse: speak bigly and carry a soft stick. The real test is China: China is currently the country most capable of supporting Iran, and Bessent simply doesn't want to answer whether the US would dare direct its wrath at China if it keeps buying Iranian oil. Farrell thinks markets and nations are all betting it won't. So the op-ed reads more like an empty threat: the more absolute the language, the more it looks like the US is bluffing at an empty table.

15:20

Flexible realism equals having no strategy at all

Using "flexible realism" to explain White House behavior is throwing a cloth over chaos. Farrell says the term, once unpacked, amounts to one sentence: America does whatever it wants, and other countries had better adjust themselves to it. What actually drives the White House is not strategy but a contest for attention, like Cosimo de' Medici making everyone revolve around him—except Trump does "a stupid version": as long as he can be the center of everyone's attention, he can make decisions that are idiotic in both the long and short term. Other countries' response therefore becomes damage limitation, not continued betting.

22:21

The mechanism of ambition countering ambition has failed

Federalist No. 10 offered the answer of "ambition counteracting ambition": let each person's drive for power cancel out others', grinding toward an equilibrium close to the public interest. Farrell says this mechanism has been dismantled from both ends. One end is the Republican Party: internal dissent has nearly vanished, senators who disagree dare not block openly, and single-party control of all three branches short-circuits the old checks. The other end is federal agencies: the Supreme Court embraces the "unitary executive" theory, inspectors general are fired or intimidated, and ordinary civil servants are converted into at-will employees who can be dismissed at any time. With a thoroughly incompetent person at the top of the system, errors get amplified into nationwide madness.

31:21

Rebuilding US institutions requires relearning trial and error

Farrell doesn't think institutions can return to the old consensus of the early 1990s to 2015, but he also doesn't think America is doomed to slide into disaster. The model he favors is the experiment New York Mayor Mamdani is running: building a feedback loop between city government and the public, turning people's real troubles into policy, and using social media to let the public see "I'm listening, and I'm doing." He deliberately links this style to FDR's "bold, persistent experimentation": keep what works, throw out what doesn't. The real open question is whether America's media and political system can tolerate a politician openly saying "this path didn't work, let's try another"—without that tolerance, experimentation won't get far.

— Henry Farrell
38:23

The more flexible, the less anyone takes you seriously

The biggest problem with flexible realism is that it destroys an asset that depletes with unpredictability: credibility. Farrell cites Nobel economics laureate Thomas Schelling's argument: one of a nation's most important assets is credibility; promises and threats that can be delivered are what actually work in negotiations. If you change your mind at any moment, if a threat sworn to the day before yesterday vanishes tomorrow, then at a certain point no one takes you seriously anymore. Farrell thinks America is in that state today: other countries still fear its capacity to do harm, but no longer believe it has the capacity for long-term action. His image: a drunk grandfather holding a shotgun, and everyone just humors him so he won't fire.

— Henry Farrell
46:23

America is smashing its own web with a sledgehammer

The core question in Farrell and Newman's Underground Empire is: globalization is a web of interdependence—supply chains interlock across dozens of countries and thousands of suppliers, and dollar clearing is the network's nerve center. The US built this system with one hand and reserved "house advantage" for itself with the other, using the threat of cutting off network access to constrain others. The Trump administration's current approach is not to dismantle the web delicately but to swing a sledgehammer semi-randomly at various parts. Farrell's direction is clear: complete decoupling from one another would be a disaster; what countries can do is not become independent but give interdependence resilience, while getting major powers to relearn talking about "restraint."

— Henry Farrell
48:23

One rare-earth card punctures the invincibility narrative

On last year's "tariff big day," Bessent publicly said the US held all A's and China had no cards. Weeks later China played the rare earths and critical minerals card, and the US suddenly realized it was holding a busted flush—seemingly fierce cards that never made a hand. Farrell sees this as the key moment that sobered Washington; since then, in speeches by Marco Rubio and others, you can see the recognition: China has the ability to materially hurt the US economy. The US and China now live in a fragile balance where each knows "I can cripple you," but they haven't truly sat down to negotiate that balance into more stable rules.

Glossary

Pascal's wager
God's existence can't be proven, but believing yields higher expected value; Bessent swaps this for "obeying America is safer."
weaponized interdependence
Coercing other countries into compliance by guarding key nodes of global networks and cutting off their access.
flexible realism
The Trump administration's declared foreign policy line; Farrell sees it as having no real content, just "America does whatever it wants."
unitary executive
The theory embraced by the Supreme Court's majority: the executive branch carries out only the president's will, weakening internal checks within agencies.
neo-royalism
A concept Farrell attributes to Abe Newman and Stacey Goddard: the world economy carved up by family and patronage networks rather than national interests.
busted flush
Poker term: one card short of a flush; America discovers its hand is actually worthless.
