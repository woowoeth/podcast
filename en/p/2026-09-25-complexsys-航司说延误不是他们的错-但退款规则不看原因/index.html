Stranded? Who Pays When Air Travel Breaks

The US automatic-refund rule doesn't care whose fault it was: if a flight is canceled or materially changed, the airline must refund you the original way you paid, regardless of whether it was weather, an ATC failure, or the airline's own mess — "not our fault" doesn't cancel that right.

Flight-disruption compensation is a five-layer waterfall

What you get back after a disruption isn't governed by one rule but by five separate systems stacked on top of each other: mandatory government refunds, voluntary airline commitments, travel insurance, credit-card travel benefits, and discretionary goodwill compensation. Each layer has its own trigger and its own payout mechanism — if one layer denies you or falls short, you move to the next one. Getting rebooked by the airline is not the end of the process.

Who can actually help you is a matter of authority level

Airport staff are stratified by what they're allowed to do: only go to the gate agent for something that can still get this specific flight out on time; to rebook onto another flight with the same airline, the app's self-service tools are faster than a human and draw on essentially the same inventory; for anything genuinely complicated that needs human judgment, go to the lounge or the service desk and find a higher-authority employee — who will usually take a quick question even from a non-member.

In-app chat carries the least authority — use it least

Phone support, especially the elite-tier line, is often staffed domestically and carries more authority; the 24-hour in-app chat, by contrast, is almost always outsourced to an overseas call center, where agents have limited discretion and can only say no or open a ticket when a case doesn't fit the standard script. The automatic-refund rule doesn't ask why the flight broke

The automatic-refund rule — finalized by DOT in April 2024 and written into law by Congress the same year — is deliberately cause-blind: if a flight is canceled, or changes significantly and the passenger declines the rebooking offered, the airline must issue a refund to the original payment method — within 7 business days for card payments, within 20 days for other methods — regardless of whether the cause was weather, an air-traffic-control failure, or the airline's own crew problem, and in theory the passenger shouldn't even need to request it. This right doesn't go away just because the airline says "this wasn't our fault."

— Patrick McKenzie
22:57

Airlines' own pledges only pay out for causes they control

In 2022 the Secretary of Transportation got airlines to publish their own commitments and tracked compliance on flightrights.gov, but this layer is not cause-blind: crew, maintenance, cleaning, fueling, and baggage-loading issues count as "controllable" causes the airline owes you for, while weather, ATC, and ground stops count as "uncontrollable" causes the airline can decline to cover. Offhandedly telling a rebooking agent "you explained the policy and I'm fine with it" can amount to waiving your claim under this layer.

— Patrick McKenzie
26:18

Paid trip-interruption insurance pays out at only 40 cents on the dollar

The trip-interruption insurance sold at flight checkout costs 5% to 10% of the ticket price, but its loss ratio — how much of every dollar in premium comes back out as claims — is only around 40 cents; by comparison, term life insurance runs about 95 cents and property insurance is generally 80 to 90 cents. Most of the gap goes to the sales channel (the airline or the airport insurance kiosk), and the claims process is deliberately made cumbersome to keep payouts down.

— Patrick McKenzie
30:05

Credit-card travel insurance is the better deal

Cards like Amex's premium tier or Chase Sapphire Reserve bundle in delay insurance covering costs like overnight lodging, funded by roughly 3% interchange fees on card swipes — the insurer can afford to offer it because only a small share of cardholders ever actually file a claim. To collect, get a delay-confirmation letter from the airline first, then submit it through the card issuer's claims portal; a check typically arrives a few weeks later.

— Patrick McKenzie
32:37

For goodwill compensation, don't name a number first and don't take miles

Airlines like to fob people off with miles because a mile is only worth about 1.2 to 1.5 cents — 5,000 miles costs the airline roughly $60 but feels generous to the passenger — so ask for a dollar-denominated voucher instead of miles. Don't state a number first; describe your actual losses (lodging, rideshare, meals) and let the agent make the first offer. You can also just ask the agent directly, "what's the most you're authorized to approve?" — nothing in their training says they can't answer that, and they usually remember the number.

Glossary

waterfall
A layered structure in which multiple compensating parties can be claimed against in priority order
misconnect
When the expected arrival time makes it impossible for a passenger to make their originally booked connecting flight
controllable cause
A delay caused by something within the airline's own power and responsibility, such as crew or maintenance issues
geste commercial
Discretionary compensation an airline offers voluntarily to preserve the customer relationship, not because it's required to
loss ratio
The share of premium income that ultimately gets paid back out to policyholders as claims
