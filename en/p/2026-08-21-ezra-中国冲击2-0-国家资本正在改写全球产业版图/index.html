China Shock 2.0: Not Cheap Goods, but Subsidies Crushing Global Industry

Unlike 1.0, China Shock 2.0 is state capital pivoting to frontier manufacturing after the real estate collapse, squeezing the world with subsidies and overcapacity — and AI may be the next battlefield.

2:38

The first shock destroyed communities, not industries

In 2002 Chinese exports surged, but they were concentrated in low-end products like furniture, appliances and clothing. At the time the US assumed these were not industries of the future, but overlooked that they employed a huge number of workers across the South and Midwest. Factory closures drove house prices down, surrounding businesses shrank, and communities fell into broad decline, while policy never produced an answer. This is the baseline for comparison with 2.0: that shock was local, this one is frontier.

— Brad Setser
10:00

America got rich and built welfare; China got rich and subsidised production

China's personal income tax is only about 1% of GDP (the US is about 8%), and there is nothing like the Earned Income Tax Credit; the hukou system means people lose some social rights the moment they leave their birthplace; the financial system is dominated by state-owned banks, and credit follows the Party's goals. The result is a savings rate above 40% of GDP, leaving the state with enormous funds it can direct into the industries it wants to dominate. America got rich and built a welfare system; China got rich and used the money to subsidise production.

— Brad Setser
13:39

Once real estate collapsed, exports were pushed into becoming the growth engine

In 2021 the ‘three red lines’ burst the real estate bubble, and the government instead steered bank credit into advanced manufacturing, especially import-dependent areas. The domestic economy is growing only about 3-4%, with exports contributing 1.5-2 percentage points. Cars, batteries and tunnel boring machines all ramped up, imports stopped growing, and the trade surplus again became the growth engine. Unlike 1.0, this time China stands at the technological frontier and dominates it.

— Brad Setser
19:43

Joint ventures made local automakers fat and lazy; Tesla broke the deadlock

China's auto industry first used a 25% tariff to force Ford, GM and Volkswagen into joint ventures, and parts suppliers moved in with them, forming a world-class supply chain. But the joint ventures left local automakers ‘fat and lazy’ for a long time, so the government made EVs a strategic priority, throwing credit, local subsidies and localisation requirements at them together. Tesla's entry into China came with a requirement for a high share of local procurement, spilling supply chain benefits outward; subsidies required batteries to be made domestically, and in the end the whole industry was set off. This is the classic protection-plus-industrial-policy path.

— Brad Setser
24:01

It is not competition weeding out the weak, it is closed markets squeezing global capacity

China's battery capacity is already several times global demand, and its auto capacity is 55 million vehicles, close to two-thirds of global demand. In industries that already have idle capacity worldwide, China keeps expanding, which necessarily squeezes out capacity elsewhere. Supporters say this is market elimination, but the problem is that a closed market suddenly doubles down on a saturated industry, crushing profits and output around the world. This is not ordinary competition, it is structural squeezing.

— Brad Setser
31:28

China did not really count as manipulation then; now it genuinely comes close

The textbook definition is an undervalued currency plus government intervention. From 2003 to 2012 China fit the definition but was not named, because the currency was appreciating at the time. Today China buys about $50 billion of foreign exchange a month through state-owned banks, $600 billion a year, closer to the manipulation standard than before. Buying foreign exchange is meant to hold down the currency. Trump is not interested, Europe is starting to pay attention, and the issue has gone from a US domestic matter to a global one.

— Brad Setser
42:56

Tariffs too broad, too high and too fast gave China breathing room instead

The first term's tariffs were selective, and 25% was something the economy could bear; the second term opened fire on the whole world, and after China retaliated the rate was pushed to 145%, until even Christmas tree importers stopped. Tariffs on allies like Canadian aluminium are pure self-harm; the highest tariffs ended up falling on daily goods from Southeast Asia, becoming a ‘Walmart tariff’ rather than a strategic tool. The conclusion: too broad, too high, too fast, and instead China was given breathing room.

— Brad Setser
56:53

The assumption that America will always dominate high-end digital services does not hold

The US stock market is propped up by software, finance and AI, but China's open-source models are advancing fast, run at low cost, and face no constraint from local protests against data centres. If the AI market becomes fully competitive, there may no longer be the kind of super-profits Google, Microsoft and Apple once had; US platforms may also be blocked the way search was. Brad argues the assumption that America will always dominate high-end digital services does not hold, and this is a real point of shock.

— Brad Setser
