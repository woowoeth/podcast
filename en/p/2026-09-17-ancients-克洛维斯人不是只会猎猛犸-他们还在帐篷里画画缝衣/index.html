Clovis people didn't just hunt mammoths — they painted and sewed inside tents

Four hearth-centered areas, ochre pigment and bone needles turned up at the LaPrele site in Wyoming, and DNA protein analysis shows the needles were made from red fox and felid bone — the Clovis diet was far more complex than the "mammoth hunter" label suggests.

3:02

Clovis lasted only a few hundred years

Radiocarbon dating pins Clovis culture to roughly 13,100 to 12,750 years ago, a window of a few hundred years just before the end of the Ice Age. The narrowness of that span is itself startling — the Ice Age runs on scales of tens to hundreds of thousands of years, and Clovis is only an instant within it. More crucially, within those few hundred years, highly similar fluted stone spear points appeared across the entire North American continent, from New Mexico to Wyoming, from Washington state to Virginia. That combination of "narrow in time, wide in space" is Clovis's most distinctive feature; in later periods, spear points rapidly regionalized.

— Madeline Mackey
12:21

Clovis was not the first Americans

"Clovis First" theory dominated most of the 20th century: humans followed mammoth herds into the Americas, and Clovis people were the earliest settlers. But finds at sites like Monte Verde (Chile), Paisley Caves (Oregon), Cooper's Ferry (Idaho) and Page-Ladson (Florida) have led most archaeologists to now believe people were living in the Americas thousands of years before Clovis. Mackey says this is one of the fiercest debates in American archaeology, and Pleistocene archaeologists argue about it hard. But she also stresses: Clovis research shouldn't fixate only on the question of "who came first" — there are many questions worth asking about their lives themselves.

— Madeline Mackey
21:01

One chopper changed the whole nature of the site

The LaPrele site was discovered in 1986 when locals found mammoth bones by a creek, and Mackey and collaborators returned to excavate in the 2000s. In the first season, retired archaeologist Rich Adams, while helping everyone fix the screening channel, found a large chopper about 10 meters from the mammoth — a simple tool made by knocking a few flakes off a river cobble. The key point is that it appeared in a position where it geologically shouldn't have: the site was gradually buried by floods, and natural processes were unlikely to have moved such a large cobble there. This tool prompted the team to test a wider area, ultimately finding more than 50,000 flakes, at least four artifact concentration areas, several hundred stone tools and mammoth ivory. LaPrele was no longer just a kill site, but a camp where humans had lived.

— Madeline Mackey
29:15

Hearths and tent walls left fingerprints in the sediment

LaPrele's most striking find is four "hearth-centered areas." In the Block B area, the team found no obvious ash or oxidation traces, but burned bone and flakes concentrated in a small patch, indicating a hearth once stood there. Even more compelling is the spatial analysis: if you look down from above at a tent, the hearth is at the center, surrounded by a high-activity zone, then an open space for walking, and near the tent wall objects pile up — because the wall is a barrier to artifact movement. The artifact distribution in Block B shows exactly this "bullseye" pattern, indicating an indoor space. In the same area, a roughly 1.5-by-2-meter ochre (hematite) pigment scatter was found, matching the wall's position, suggesting people painted by the fire.

— Madeline Mackey
39:25

The bone needles were made from red fox and felid bone

The team used ZooMS (Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry) to analyze bone needles and possible bone beads; the method extracts proteins from extremely small bone samples to identify the animal species. The results were unexpected: many needles were made from hare bone, but one was red fox, and another tested as felid — not a domestic cat, but some wild felid. Mackey notes these animals are all fur-bearing, and their bone thickness may have been just right for making needles. More importantly, these animals were likely caught in traps rather than hunted with spears. This means Clovis people weren't hunting mammoths 24 hours a day; they were also taking more common small prey.

— Madeline Mackey
44:32

Were Clovis people mammoth specialists or omnivores?

About 15 widely accepted Clovis sites in North America coexist with megafauna, mostly proboscideans (mammoths, mastodons, gomphotheres), but also horses and camels. Yet Mackey points out that only about 30 to 40 sites truly define our understanding of Clovis, while thousands of Clovis spear points have been found on the surface, lacking context. This raises a core debate: were they mammoth-hunting specialists, or omnivores with a broader diet? Small animals, fish and plant remains preserve less well and are less often systematically sought. Mackey is studying plant remains, and she notes archaeologists usually look for plants only in hearths, and how many places in the Clovis record have actually been looked at is a problem.

— Madeline Mackey
50:00

Mammoth extinction: did humans push, or was climate the main driver?

The classic "overkill" debate: 35 genera of megafauna went extinct at the end of the Ice Age, and how much was human versus environmental? Mackey says she takes a middle path, believing both contributed. She considers mammoths the strongest candidate for the overkill hypothesis, because mammoth sites are numerous, the animals were large, and their reproductive rate was low, making them more vulnerable to hunting. But she also notes overkill doesn't necessarily mean humans hunted all 35 genera; hunting may have broken the balance through trophic cascades. Interestingly, paleoecologists lean toward "humans did it," while archaeologists lean more toward "the environment did it."

— Madeline Mackey
53:12

Clovis human bone barely exists

Apart from a single ancestral remains attributed to Clovis at the Anzick site in Montana, we have almost no Clovis human bone. Mackey thinks this isn't surprising: population density was extremely low, 13,000-year-old sites are inherently extremely hard to preserve, and these groups were highly mobile, so we don't know their burial practices. She also stresses that Clovis people are the ancestors of millions of Indigenous people in the Americas today, and any related research must proceed on the basis of consultation and collaboration.

— Madeline Mackey

Glossary

Clovis points
Fluted, leaf-shaped stone spear points, the hallmark artifact of Clovis culture.
fluting
The distinctive technique of knocking flakes off the center base of a spear point to form a groove.
ZooMS
A technique that identifies animal species by analyzing bone collagen proteins, requiring only a tiny sample.
ochre
Hematite, a red pigment often used in prehistoric painting and decoration.
hearth-centered areas
Human activity areas centered on a hearth, reflecting camp spatial organization.
overkill debate
The academic debate over whether human hunting caused the extinction of Ice Age megafauna.
