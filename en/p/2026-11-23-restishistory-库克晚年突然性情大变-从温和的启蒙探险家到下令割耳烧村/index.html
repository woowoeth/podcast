Cook's abrupt personality change in his later years: from mild-mannered Enlightenment explorer to ordering villages burned and men flogged

On his first two voyages Cook consistently restrained his crew and advocated for local people; on the third voyage he became impulsive and irritable, ordering 72 lashes for thieves and burning 20 houses and 18 canoes, an aberration that ultimately led to his death on a Hawaiian beach.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

20:39

Cook punished his own men to protect local women

On Tahiti, a butcher threatened to rape local women, and Cook ordered him flogged. This action was consistent with Cook's attitude toward his crew: the navy's main criticism of him was that he was ‘too soft and too indulgent’ toward local people, not too violent. This directly contrasts with later depictions of him as a symbol of European colonial violence.

23:49

London's official instructions recognized locals as legal landowners

The Royal Society's instructions to Cook stated that local people were ‘natural and in the strictest sense of the word, the legal possessors of the several regions they inhabit’. This was not public relations language but formal orders to the captain, showing that 18th-century English colonial planners at least rhetorically sought to distinguish themselves from Spanish colonizers in the manner of Cortés and Pizarro.

— Tom
48:01

Cook held English sailors responsible for corrupting local morals

Cook did not blame local people for ‘low morals’ but instead wrote that Europeans had ‘debauched their morals’, introducing desires and diseases they never needed, destroying the peace their ancestors had long enjoyed. Almost every criticism of Cook leveled today he had already articulated in his own diary.

— Dominic
54:12

Cook's colleague refused vengeance after his crew were eaten

After ten landing soldiers from the accompanying Adventure went missing and were found reduced to bones with only a hand tattooed with ‘TH’, crew members urged bombardment and burning of canoes in retaliation, but Captain Furneaux refused, reasoning that ‘retaliation cannot bring back the dead’, and simply chose to return home.

— Dominic
1:13:07

An exhausted Cook demanded to lead the third expedition

Before the third voyage, everyone knew Cook had spent six of the previous seven years at sea, and his body and emotions showed signs of exhaustion; he should have remained at home as an advisor. But at a dinner one evening he suddenly slammed the table and said ‘I will command this expedition myself’, a decision that ultimately cost him his life.

— Dominic
1:16:19

Cook's unexpected detour signaled the first personality breakdown

On what was originally a planned stop in Tasmania for supplies, Cook suddenly changed course for New Zealand, seen as the first clear signal of Cook's character aberration—a consistently calm, deliberative man becoming impulsive, indecisive, and irritable.

— Dominic
1:18:21

Cook escalated theft punishment to 72 lashes and cutting

Because of frequent theft by locals, Cook abolished the navy-mandated limit of 12 lashes and instead ordered 72 lashes for one man, and carved a cross-shaped wound into another thief's skin until bone showed. The young midshipman Gilbert recorded that Cook's behavior was ‘unbecoming of a european’ and entirely unlike his conduct on the first two voyages.

— Dominic
1:20:25

Missing goats drove Cook to burn villages and canoes

On Eimeo Island, after two goats went missing and were stolen, Cook fell into ‘a rage his officers had never seen’, ordering the burning of 18 canoes, 100+ paddles, and 20 houses in retaliation. Even Gilbert, executing the orders, said ‘we executed the orders very reluctantly’ and expressed incomprehension at how differently Cook behaved than before.

— Dominic
