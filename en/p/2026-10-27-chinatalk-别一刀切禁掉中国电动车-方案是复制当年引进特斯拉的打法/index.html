Instead of blanket-banning Chinese EVs, replicate China's Tesla playbook

China turned Tesla into a teacher for its domestic industry by mandating localized supply chains and separating hardware from software. America should apply the same strategy to Chinese EVs instead of imposing an outright ban.

Corruption once lubricated a broken institutional system

In 2008, China ranked 172nd hardest globally for building permits, yet half the world's construction cranes were in China—proof that formal systems couldn't keep pace and corruption became the workaround to bypass bureaucracy and get things done. This level of corruption was tolerated in the 80s and 90s because it served rapid growth; the real crisis came when conglomerates like Anbang, HNA, and Wanda weaponized the same playbook at scale—funneling assets through hundreds of subsidiary companies, using dark transfers between unlisted firms to hide holdings. This crossed from lubricant to systemic theft.

7:21

When policy shifts, entrepreneurs' horizon collapses from 3 years to 6 months

Rithmire sees Chinese private enterprise cycling through repeated rounds of tolerance followed by crackdown, training owners not to trust institutions but to stay close to the Party and ready to flee. Normally they frame business horizons around the policy environment of the next 3 to 5 years. But the moment they sense a shift, that window compresses to 6 months: move money overseas, deploy the mutual dirt you've accumulated to secure yourself, extract whatever you can before the door closes.

— Meg Rithmire
16:38

The Party refuses both legal constraints and judicial discipline

Legal scholars describe China's system as ‘法制’ (lawfulness) rather than ‘法治’ (rule of law)—the Party doesn't accept rulings from independent courts. Rithmire extends this: China practices what she calls ‘rule by market’ either—the Party won't tolerate being disciplined by market forces. This explains why China still has no genuine bankruptcy framework, no effective minority-shareholder protections. The Party insists on picking winners and losers itself; land, labor, capital—all production factors ultimately flow from Party decisions, not markets.

— Meg Rithmire
22:49

Zombie firms got rebranded: from asset shells to unprofitable makers

The old zombie enterprises were Anbang and Wanda—shells that produced nothing, just shuffled assets around. After resources tilted toward industrial policy, zombie firms evolved into real manufacturers that actually make things. The difference: they don't make money. Paired with the anti-involution campaign—government subsidies flooding in to see who survives—overcapacity crushes margins, forcing genuinely competitive firms like BYD to race to the bottom on marginal products like massage chairs just to move volume.

— Meg Rithmire
27:18

Ordinary households are locked out of the wealth-creation cycle

Real estate and finance were systemically corrupt, but they gave ordinary families a sense that their money was growing. Now resources flow to BYD and DJI, the policy champions—but those firms employ far fewer people. A family can afford an EV, yet can't touch the upside. Rithmire notes that Xi's framing has shifted from ‘eliminate poverty’ to ‘maximize national power’. Personal healthcare and pensions deteriorating in the bargain doesn't appear in his cost-benefit calculus.

— Meg Rithmire
39:49

National security law itself betrays the Party's loss of control

The West assumes the CCP can command any Chinese firm at will. Reality is the inverse. Laws like the National Security Law and Foreign Espionage Law—vaguely worded, almost never cited in court—exist precisely because the Party is unsure it can actually direct private tech companies when it needs to. These laws function as deterrent and backstop, not as day-to-day operating mechanisms. The dynamic between Party and firm is an ongoing dance of negotiation, not a command chain.

— Meg Rithmire
49:10

Her solution: replicate how China once handled Tesla's entry

Rithmire rejects both blanket bans and full openness. She advocates flipping China's Tesla playbook: allow CATL and BYD to build US factories, but mandate localized supply chains, enforce supplier diversity with ongoing audits, and split hardware from software—exactly as Tesla was forced to use Baidu for maps and data residency in China. This preserves security guardrails while importing the know-how America's EV sector needs: the engineering to hit $15,000 EVs.

— Meg Rithmire
1:16:14

American graft is brasher, therefore more corrosive to society

Rithmire contrasts the two systems: in China, you must be discreet—Guo Wengui was already washed up and cornered before he bragged about wealth; normal practice means staying invisible. Now America's problem is Trump minting money with meme coins, doing it openly, the President himself bragging. She argues the ‘everyone's cheating so why shouldn't I’ reflex is more dangerous than the dollar amounts—smaller scale, but it's accelerating erosion of belief in business fairness itself.
