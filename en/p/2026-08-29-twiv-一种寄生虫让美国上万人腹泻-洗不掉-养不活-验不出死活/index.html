A Parasite Is Giving Thousands of Americans Diarrhea: You Can't Wash It Off, Can't Culture It, Can't Tell If It's Alive

The oocysts sickening thousands of Americans resist bleach and rinsing, only become infectious one to two weeks after being shed, and can neither be cultured in the lab nor tested for viability — which is why the outbreak is so hard to cut off.

This episode turns ‘why do outbreaks keep happening’ into a chain of evidence: first the data on thousands of US cases, then the laboratory dead end of no culture and no viability assay, and finally down to how you personally buy lettuce.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:06

The US is in the middle of its largest outbreak

Daniel Griffin opens with the numbers: Michigan has passed 10,000 cases, North Carolina has a separate outbreak of roughly 1,000, and New York City is over 500. He calls it the largest cyclosporiasis outbreak he has seen, and offers his own tabletop clue — the pre-washed chopped romaine his household was eating came from the suspected Taylor Farms. The numbers themselves are not the point; the point is that these cases had to be contaminated two to three weeks earlier. By the time officials confirm an outbreak, the suspect lettuce is often already off the shelves or eaten. This is also the starting point for every ‘why can't we do anything about it’ in the episode.

— Daniel Griffin
11:09

By the time cases appear, the contamination is two or three weeks old

The mechanism is laid out clearly at 11 minutes: oocysts shed in feces are not infectious and must first sporulate in the environment for 7 to 14 days; after a person ingests infectious oocysts, roughly another week passes before illness. So a contamination event only becomes the batch of cases in front of you two to three weeks later. By then, asking patients what they ate a few days ago is aimed at the wrong window; the leftover leaves have already been distributed and eaten, and the physical evidence is gone. This explains why tracing Cyclospora is so hard, and why cases are hard to attribute to a single recipe — it is not that people don't remember, it is that the two-week silent period makes the epidemiological question nearly unsolvable.

— Daniel Griffin
13:11

Rinsing and bleach are both powerless against it

Then comes the set of properties that surprised Vincent: oocysts resist chemical treatments like bleach and ordinary rinsing, and they are ‘sticky’, clinging to the surface of leafy greens. So bagged ‘pre-washed salad’ cannot be assumed safe, and the problem is not how long you run it under water. The more fundamental experimental obstacle is that it currently infects only humans, has no available animal model, and cannot be cultured in vitro; that means Koch's postulates cannot be completed, and there is not even an endpoint for testing whether a disinfection method works. A positive PCR only says nucleic acid is present, not whether the oocysts are alive or dead — something virology taught us long ago. The further you listen, the clearer it becomes why the US has outbreaks every year and finds the source every year without success.

— Daniel Griffin
21:16

It sporulates after leaving the gut, the opposite of Cryptosporidium

Christina comes in to fill out the life history and correct the intuition that oocysts must always wait 7–14 days: its close relative Cryptosporidium is already sporulated when shed and can infect the next person's mouth immediately; Cyclospora sheds immature oocysts that must sit in a warm, moist environment to complete sporulation. The life cycle itself resembles Cryptosporidium's: sporozoites invade the small intestinal epithelium, multiply asexually inside cells into merozoites, and repeatedly infect new epithelial cells; then some go through gametogony, and finally unsporulated oocysts leave in the feces. This design of ‘waiting outside the human body’ is exactly what gives it its opening to spread through the produce supply chain — those two weeks in transport and storage are precisely what carries contamination from the growing region into thousands of homes.

— Christina Nowlin
28:24

They found an effective drug before they identified the pathogen

The history segment is about ‘treatment before pathogen confirmation’, a counterintuitive order of discovery. In the late 1980s, at the CIWEC traveler's clinic in Kathmandu, Nepal, many expatriates and trekkers had diarrhea that would not resolve for weeks; technicians kept seeing a small round body on smears without knowing what it was. With no culture and no animal model, researchers could only try drugs used for coccidian diarrhea, and found TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole/Bactrim) most effective. The pathogen was subsequently described as a new species and named Cyclospora cayetanensis — the species name does not come from Nepal, the place of discovery, but honors the naming author's alma mater, Cayetano Heredia University in Peru.

— Daniel Griffin
35:30

America's imported outbreaks could put down roots

The most alarming part of the second half is whether the US can shift from ‘reimported every year’ to endemic transmission. Currently most US patients have toilets and wash their hands, oocysts go into the sewer, and the transmission chain breaks when the season ends, leaving only next summer's import from endemic regions. But in reality many low-income people live in multigenerational households, and field laborers have even their bathroom breaks dictated by employers, so when it's urgent they go in the field. Once that becomes common enough for oocysts to reach soil and irrigation water, and they get their 7 to 14 days to sporulate, the domestic transmission cycle in the US no longer needs imports to sustain it. Add that frontline clinics may not run expensive molecular stool tests, so hidden cases are underestimated — the speaker considers this a real risk.

— Daniel Griffin
37:33

Symptoms are not just diarrhea — they drag on for two months

The clinical segment translates ‘explosive diarrhea’ into concrete images: watery stools five to fifteen times a day, very large volume, and patients basically have to live next to the toilet. Daniel says the duration is the biggest surprise — it does not pass in two or three days; immunocompetent people can have diarrhea for two months, and immunocompromised people for months. Don't treat it as just acute gastroenteritis: three to six weeks after infection, reactive arthritis can appear, and it can also progress to Guillain-Barré. So when the US really has tens of thousands of cases, the end-of-season public health burden is not only diarrhea clinics but also the delayed bill for neurological and joint complications weeks later.

— Daniel Griffin
48:45

Inactivation can only be judged by looking under a microscope for damage

The finale is the paradox of detection and inactivation methods: the paper mentions UVC irradiation might help, but the way to verify it is still to put oocysts under a microscope and see whether they look damaged — Daniel says this does not give him much confidence, because ‘damaged-looking’ does not equal non-infectious. Molecular tests only look at nucleic acid and likewise do not answer viability; the current genome is even just a draft full of gaps, about 44 Mb and AT-rich — not untestable, just too few people working on it. The more the guests get to the end, the more candid they are: much of the literature's knowledge is extrapolated from close relatives, and the original authors themselves repeatedly say speculate. The real way out may be to grow the oocysts in vitro or in an animal model, or to lean on other Cyclospora species in the same genus.

— Daniel Griffin

Glossary

Cyclospora cayetanensis
The star of this episode: a human coccidian that enters small intestinal cells via contaminated produce and causes watery diarrhea (the species name cayetanensis comes from Cayetano Heredia University in Peru).
oocyst
The resistant form the parasite sheds in feces; it tolerates bleach and adheres to the surface of produce.
sporulation
The process by which an immature oocyst develops in the environment for 7–14 days and produces infectious sporozoites.
Guillain-Barré
An autoimmune peripheral neuropathy that can follow infection, listed in the episode as one of the post-infectious complications of cyclosporiasis.
reactive arthritis
Inflammatory arthritis appearing 3–6 weeks after enteric symptoms resolve, as a post-infectious complication.
