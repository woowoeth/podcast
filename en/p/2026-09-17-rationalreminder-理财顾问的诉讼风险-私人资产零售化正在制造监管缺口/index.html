Litigation Risk for Financial Advisors: The Retailisation of Private Assets Is Creating a Regulatory Gap

Private assets, long confined to accredited investors, are now open to the public without the protections that traditionally accompany public offerings — practices normalised in institutional settings become major litigation risk once ordinary investors enter.

Financial AdvisorsPrivate AssetsLitigation RiskRegulatory GapInvestor Protection
This episode breaks down the professional risk facing financial advisors from a litigator's perspective, with high information density — especially useful for anyone in wealth management, private asset sales, or compliance.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:02

Winning a case depends on evidence, not fault

Taylor says a successful negligence claim almost always requires a failure in the suitability analysis first — KYC information not properly collected, KY product not properly understood. But ‘there was a failure’ does not equal ‘it can be proven.’ He splits this into ‘what people know’ and ‘what people can prove,’ saying the two rarely align perfectly, and it is the latter that ultimately drives the analysis. On evidence, the strongest is when an advisor used the same portfolio for everyone: a bunch of people with identical portfolios but completely different life circumstances — that turns ‘he said, she said’ into ‘he said, she said, she said, he said, they said,’ which is far more persuasive to a judge than one person's unilateral account.

— Matthew Taylor
2:03

The trading record itself is a red flag

Taylor lists a series of behaviours that are almost impossible to rationally explain: concentration above 80%–90% (he has seen it, and in small caps), churning done solely to generate commissions, double dipping on multi-account clients — buying in a commission-based account first, then moving the assets into a fee-based or AUM-based account without justification. There are also trades entered without any confirmation, and off-book communications. He says these are powerful because in most cases the advisor or the firm simply cannot offer a reasonable explanation.

— Matthew Taylor
5:07

Communication going from warm to silent is a danger signal

Taylor describes a not-uncommon pattern: extremely dense communication and attentiveness when courting the account, then only one or two contacts a year once the account is won — the bare minimum of communication. He sees this huge drop-off as a problem in itself. Another signal is that no one talks to you about changed life circumstances — retirement, layoff, death of a breadwinner, having a child — all of which should feed into the recommendation basis. If you look back and think ‘so much happened, and we never talked about whether my needs had changed,’ that does not necessarily mean something went wrong, but it is worth getting a second set of eyes on it.

— Matthew Taylor
7:07

Check the regulatory complaint record before you hire

Taylor says this is one of the strongest pre-hire signals, and it costs almost nothing: if the person is regulated (he says most large dealers are), complaint or investigation records are public information, and checking takes less than 5 minutes. The mechanism he describes is that these claims commonly involve someone who had a small complaint first, was watched closely for a while, then reverted to old behaviour — just on a much larger scale. He also cautions: past results do not necessarily predict the future, but this is a very worthwhile piece of information to factor into the decision.

— Matthew Taylor
8:08

The common thread among victims is vulnerability, not stupidity

Taylor says these investors share strong commonalities, almost always some form of vulnerability: advanced age, lower education level, language barriers — official forms and statements are only in English and French, so clients who struggle to understand rely more on the advisor, and people naturally seek out someone they can communicate with, which adds another layer of asymmetry. Another category is people who suddenly come into money: life insurance payouts, large inheritances — they are simultaneously dealing with a dramatic change in financial circumstances and a negative life event. He has personally advised a lottery winner and has heard colleagues mention similar cases.

— Matthew Taylor
39:28

Fee structures are the next litigation goldmine

Taylor says fees are the second big indicator after disclosure, and there is already a wave of class actions around fees; some fee types can no longer be charged because of legal changes — for example, charging advisory fees in non-advisory accounts. He predicts future litigation will centre on ‘how fees are calculated,’ and he names private asset managers as particularly fertile ground for lawsuits. For those in wealth management, this means the fee basis in product design is itself a legal risk exposure, not just a commercial choice.

— Matthew Taylor
41:29

Pension funds manage litigation claims as an asset

Taylor cites a Stanford Law Review article from twenty years ago titled Letting Billions Slip Through Your Fingers: at the time, many pension funds and other asset managers simply did not participate in securities class action settlements, effectively leaving money on the table — possibly because they had not kept the records needed to participate, or because decision-makers were philosophically opposed to litigation. US pension funds later widely hired monitoring counsel, who are not paid, to flag potential claims, and built into their compliance and committee structures a decision process for ‘whether to participate in litigation, and in what capacity.’ Taylor says Canada has not caught up on this. His key judgment: under fiduciary standards, a claim in litigation is itself an asset, it can be sold, and it should be managed like any other asset.

— Matthew Taylor
47:32

Private assets remove the people who digest information

Taylor groups the problems with private assets into two umbrella issues: less information and less regulation, both by deliberate design. In public markets, the information asymmetry created by the separation of ownership and control is smoothed out by a set of checks: periodic financial reports, periodic audits, third-party verification, regulation, and a large group of people whose profession is to digest and disseminate information — equity analysts track companies, what analysts say is picked up by journalists, and an ordinary person opening the Globe and Mail can at least get a rough sense of what happened last quarter at a TSX company. Private assets strip all of this away: statutory reporting requirements are extremely limited, there is no equivalent level of analyst coverage, and the raw information available is itself far less.

— Matthew Taylor
50:34

You can understand the investment thesis but not the underlying structure

Taylor says the most ‘pernicious’ thing about private assets is this: the investment thesis sounds easy to understand — for example, this fund will roll up veterinary clinics, or hold a commercial real estate portfolio in a certain region — but lift the hood and there may be five to seven different entities involved in managing the fund in different ways: the people managing the money, the manager they hired, the property manager, the management company of the veterinary clinics, each layer charging fees. This is far harder to understand than an ETF with a 6% management fee, but people feel they can understand it, and that is precisely where the danger lies. He also points out that price discovery is another major difficulty: in public markets the price is the transaction price; private assets have no active market, so valuation depends on the manager themselves or someone they hire, and the valuation directly determines how many units you get when you subscribe or redeem — this opens the door to ‘mischief.’

— Matthew Taylor
53:36

IRR and stock returns are not the same thing

Taylor says private asset returns are a stream of cash flows, not something you can directly calculate as a 50% return like buying at 20 and selling at 30. The most commonly reported metric is the internal rate of return, IRR, which attempts to discount the cash flow stream into a single percentage. His judgment: an ordinary person sees that percentage and compares it to the stock percentage, thinking it is apples to apples, when it is actually apples to oranges — the two numbers simply do not line up. IRR also has an anchoring effect — the early returns will continue to influence the number for many years. Add on top a complex fee structure that is hard to understand, and ordinary people are nearly unable to assess how what they hold is performing.

— Matthew Taylor
55:38

Private asset liquidity is illusory

Taylor says the liquidity issue should be front and centre when advisors deal with private assets: these funds can basically be gated or locked, and clients may not understand that they cannot get their money out at any time like redeeming a mutual fund — a normal mutual fund pays out in a day or two, so you can fix a roof or buy a car. Private funds are not like that. He advises that advisors must explain all structural risks clearly and keep a written record, and if they also do planning, the plan must include a contingency for ‘expected cash flows from the private asset side not materialising.’ He says gate news is everywhere right now, and the calls he gets about gates are too numerous to count — this is happening, and it is likely to persist for the foreseeable future.

— Matthew Taylor
59:39

Someone who can only sell private assets will only sell private assets

Taylor suggests clients first ask one question: what is the product shelf this advisor can sell me? In the consultations he handles, most of these products are sold through people who only hold an exempt market licence, and what they can offer may be an entire suite of private asset products. He uses an analogy: walk into a Ferrari dealership and they will certainly try to sell you a Ferrari, even if what you need is a Dodge minivan. He does not think private assets are always unsuitable, but if it is the only thing in your portfolio, it would take very exceptional circumstances to justify — and that may be exactly the situation you are in when facing someone who can only sell this.

— Matthew Taylor
1:01:42

Institutions do not sue; retail investors do

Taylor reduces litigation to an incentive problem. Historically private assets were the domain of sophisticated institutions, and they have many reasons not to sue: ongoing relationships with fund managers they do not want to sour; this was a bad deal but there were good deals in the past and there will be more in the future; someone wants to enter the private equity industry and does not want to be the one who pulls the trigger and damages their career prospects; and in the US they are forced into binding arbitration. These considerations basically all disappear for Canadian retail investors: they do not care about a long-term relationship with the fund manager, they just think ‘this is terrible, I never want to deal with this person again,’ and Canada makes it harder than the US to force retail investors into arbitration, and class action waivers are harder to enforce. So the incentives all point toward more litigation, and he expects Canadian courts to hear more and more of these issues.

— Matthew Taylor
1:04:43

Ontario securities law explicitly allows suing for misrepresentation

Taylor fully agrees with the judgment in the paper by Ludovic Phalippou and William Magnuson — asset classes long confined to accredited investors are now open to the public without the protections that traditionally accompany public offerings, and this retailisation creates a significant regulatory gap: practices normalised in institutional settings (misleading performance metrics, manipulable valuations, opaque fees, limited liquidity, fiduciary duty exemptions) become major litigation risk once ordinary investors enter. Taylor says this paper is required reading in his course, and he believes the situation it describes holds even more strongly in Canada: Ontario securities law has a section that explicitly creates a right of action for misrepresentations in a prospectus, and the prospectus is often part of the sales materials for these products.

— Matthew Taylor
1:06:44

Influencers have no insurance, so winning a judgment gets you nothing

Taylor says the first difficulty with the influencer problem is jurisdiction: video and audio are everywhere, listeners may be in Australia or South Africa, and if something goes wrong, who has authority? Even within Canada, if someone records content in Manitoba, involves an Alberta company, and the trade happens in Ontario, you first have to figure out who handles it. The second difficulty is that private enforcement incentives are limited: being an influencer does not require insurance, anyone can plug in a microphone and start recording, and it is easy to reach a large audience, but that does not mean suing them is worthwhile — they may be completely bankrupt. Canadian regulators have sued several influencers, and when setting fines they did take into account the fact that the person had almost no assets. So for an ordinary person, the first sentence of the conversation is always: can we actually get your money back? Is hiring a lawyer just throwing good money after bad?

— Matthew Taylor
1:10:46

Vaccinate clients with weakened versions of misinformation

Taylor recommends the Ontario Securities Commission's research on investor behaviour and influencer interaction. The research found that among practices that reduce the probability of being harmed by bad advice, advisors proactively addressing misinformation in advance is very effective — you need to talk about it before the client comes to you with questions. The report also gives specific forms, one of which is called inoculation: present misinformation circulating in influencer circles to the client in weakened form, then have the advisor rebut it themselves — essentially one person performing a debate, helping the client build resistance to influencer persuasion. Other effective practices include talking with clients about how to evaluate the information they receive, and about what warning signs to watch for — essentially encouraging critical thinking.

— Matthew Taylor
1:12:49

The regulator's job is not to get your money back

Taylor says investors' recourse after following bad influencer advice is quite limited. In theory, if there is reason to believe the person has assets you can sue, but these claims differ from the class actions discussed earlier, and the evidentiary approach is different — he has not seen many such cases in Canada. You can complain to a securities commission; both the Alberta and BC securities commissions have sued influencers, and the US SEC has too. But the key point: the regulator's job is not to return your money, but to protect the market, impose fines, and restrict the person's participation in the market. So going down this road you should go in with your eyes open, knowing the probability it improves your financial situation is limited. He sees ‘limited recourse’ as one of the biggest problems in this area.

— Matthew Taylor
1:13:49

Education, promotion, and advice are three different things

Taylor's advice for licensed advisors doing online content: disclosure is a legal requirement, and any relationship must be made clear. There is a distinction between education, promotion, and advice — the latter two, advice and promotion, are what get you into trouble. If you are not promoting a specific company and not advising specific trades through your online content, and stay at the level of education helping people understand concepts, you are on the right side of the line. He also cites a regulatory ruling on the form of disclosure: if someone has to expand the box below the video and read five pages of material to see somewhere at the bottom or middle that ‘there is actually a financial relationship,’ that does not count; it should be above the fold, ideally displayed directly on the video screen so the viewer sees it at a glance. Audio content also needs an actual spoken declaration — you cannot expect someone to have a tab open staring at it.

— Matthew Taylor
1:20:56

Canadian investment managers are not automatically fiduciaries

Canada is often described as ‘being a portfolio manager means you are a fiduciary,’ but Matthew points out this does not necessarily hold: courts look at how you actually conduct yourself. If a portfolio manager is taking orders and executing them without exercising discretion, they may not be considered a fiduciary. Conversely, as soon as you hold yourself out as a fiduciary, or join an association that requires signing a fiduciary pledge or best interest pledge, courts are highly likely to hold you to that standard — essentially, ‘acting from a position of trust.’

Glossary

suitability
The regulatory standard for whether a recommendation matches the client's circumstances and objectives.
churning
Frequent buying and selling to generate commissions, harming the client's interests.
double dipping
Moving the same assets between commission-based and fee-based accounts to charge twice.
exempt market
Securities offerings that can be sold to accredited investors without a prospectus.
gate
A fund restricting or suspending investor redemptions, leaving money inaccessible.
IRR
A metric that discounts a series of cash flows into a single percentage return.
