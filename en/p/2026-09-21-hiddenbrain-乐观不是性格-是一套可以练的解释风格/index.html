Optimism Is Not a Personality Trait, It's an Explanatory Style You Can Train

Helplessness is learned, and so is optimism. What decides whether a person gives up is not the blow itself, but whether he explains it as permanent, pervasive, or his own fault.

9:36

The dog doesn't escape because it never learned escape

In Solomon's lab, on day one the dog was strapped in a hammock and given Pavlovian shocks it could not avoid no matter what it did; on day two it was put in a shuttle box where running to the other side would shut off the shock, and the dog just lay there. Seligman's judgment was that what the animal actually learned was not "the tone predicts shock" but "nothing I do matters." That reading collided head-on with behaviorism at the time, because it required the animal to be a cognitive subject capable of thinking and integrating experience across time.

— Martin Seligman
13:50

People can be taught to give up by noise too

Hiroto moved the same design onto humans: on day one, headphones played a harsh noise, and for one group no amount of button-pressing could turn it off while for the other group pressing a button did. On day two came a four-foot "human shuttle box" — hand on one side and the noise sounds, move it to the other side and it stops. People who had been through uncontrollable noise the day before just left their hand in the box. Seligman stresses that the variable doing the work is not the trauma itself but whether there was control over the trauma — and they ran control experiments on exactly this point.

— Martin Seligman
19:00

Helplessness became the model for depression

In 1970 depression was not yet a diagnostic category; there was only "manic-depressive illness." By 1975 the American Psychiatric Association's DSM had for the first time split out "unipolar depression," listing nine symptoms: passivity, cognitive difficulty, sadness, anxiety, sleep problems, food losing its taste, numbness, loss of pleasure, thoughts of death. Seligman compared the nine symptoms against the learned-helplessness literature accumulated by then and found the lab could reproduce eight of them; the only one missing was thoughts of death — presumably because the experiments used noise rather than something more severe. The model went on to become the dominant model of depression, and the drugs that could break helplessness in the lab became mainstream antidepressants.

— Martin Seligman
22:19

Five out of eight give up, one in three does not

Across the whole helplessness literature the effect is statistical: on average five out of eight people or eight rats become helpless, and about a third do not. From the 1970s on Seligman kept asking what was different about that third, and it became the turning point of his career. The answer landed on explanatory style: people who explain bad events as permanent ("I'm stupid"), pervasive ("I'm unlovable"), and their own fault become helpless; people who explain them as temporary, local, and not their fault recover quickly.

— Martin Seligman
26:50

West Point's first week predicts who drops out

The first six weeks at West Point are called Beast Barracks: woken at four in the morning to run ten miles, systematically put through the wringer, with the stated purpose being to "break them," and roughly a third of candidates quit. Seligman's team measured explanatory style on day one, and the ones who dropped out were the pessimists. The same pattern showed up in college grades (optimists exceeded their talent, pessimists fell short of it) and in insurance sales: MetLife lost 50% of new hires within a year and 90% within four years; they hired 140 "special forces" whose optimism-scale scores were off the charts, and this group outsold the whole company and still had 75% on the job after four years, while the ordinary group was down to 10%.

— Martin Seligman
34:17

Agency breaks into three beliefs

Seligman defines agency as the opposite of learned helplessness, made up of three parts: efficacy, the belief that you can accomplish a specific goal right here and now; optimism, the belief that you can accomplish it not only now but in the future and the further future; and imagination, the range of possible goals you can picture — the wider the range, the stronger the agency. Full agency is: I can do it now, I can do it later, and I can innovate across many different situations.

— Martin Seligman
36:22

The Axial Age's answer may have been "I can do it"

Between 1000 and 500 BCE, Greece, the Hebrew world, China, India, and Persia all underwent upheaval: the Buddha, Confucius, and Socrates appeared, and technology, medicine, art, literature, and the first political freedom erupted together. Seligman's hypothesis is that over those five centuries humanity discovered the concept of agency. The evidence is vocabulary: in the Iliad Achilles does not decide to act on his own, Athena tells him to act; a century later in the Odyssey, Odysseus is already a wily, resourceful man who makes his own decisions, and the gods begin to exit the stage. By 500 BCE every culture had produced a large vocabulary of human agency.

— Martin Seligman
38:30

In the Old Testament the verbs of divine command decline

Seligman took the entire Old Testament and counted agency verbs: roughly, in the Pentateuch God commands and people obey, with only a few exceptions like "choose life"; the further you go into the Prophets and the Writings, the more words of human agency appear and the fewer words of divine agency. You can now have AI score each book for agency, and plotted by time it comes out linear — from the Pentateuch to the Babylonian exile around 300 BCE, agency increases, and it correlates linearly with human innovation, population, living standards, political freedom, and technology.

— Martin Seligman
40:36

After Augustine, progress stalled for a thousand years

Progress and agency have not risen steadily across human history. Early Christianity was highly agentic and highly optimistic, but in 383 CE Augustine argued that grace is given arbitrarily by God, that predestination exists, and that there is no human will independent of grace, and this became medieval theology. Seligman says the Middle Ages believed "God acts, man does not," progress stalled accordingly, and only when the Renaissance overturned Augustine's view did progress erupt again. He adds the Chinese side: Western agency is "I can do it," whereas China requires quantifying both "we" agency and "I" agency, and what predicts population, innovation, and GDP is the balance between the two — when state-form "we" agency is extremely high and crushes individual agency, you get stagnation and regression.

— Martin Seligman

Glossary

learned helplessness
After experiencing uncontrollable bad events, learning that "nothing I do matters," so that even in escapable situations afterward you stop trying.
explanatory style
A person's default way of explaining bad events: permanent or temporary, pervasive or local, their own fault or not.
learned optimism
Treating optimism as a trainable explanatory habit rather than an inborn personality trait.
agency
Seligman defines it as the composite of three beliefs: efficacy, optimism, and imagination.
axial age
The period from 1000 to 500 BCE when thought and technology advanced simultaneously in Greece, China, India, Persia, and elsewhere.
