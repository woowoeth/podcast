Women won voting rights through temperance activism

Because saloons were male-only spaces and domestic violence was tied to alcoholism, 19th-century American women fought for temperance as a safety issue, making prohibition their first stepping stone toward suffrage.

Temperance activism was the unexpected catalyst for women's suffrage

Nineteenth-century saloons served almost exclusively men, and farm laborers drank beer as though it were water. Alcoholism directly threatened women and children's safety. This material danger is what first drew women toward temperance activism. When the Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU) grew into the largest women's organization in the world at the time, its president Frances Willard reframed voting rights as ‘a weapon against the tyranny of drunkenness’—turning temperance into one of the few outlets through which women could openly participate in politics. The women's suffrage movement seized this opening.

— Joya Dilberto
17:22

The government handed an unwinnable Prohibition enforcement job to a woman

At age 32, lawyer Mabel Walker Willebrandt was appointed Assistant Attorney General, tasked with enforcing an ‘impossible’ mandate: eliminating bootlegging across America. Historians are blunt about the motive: the Republican Party needed a high-profile woman to placate female voters while dumping an impossible task that would tarnish any political career onto a woman's shoulders. The failure of Prohibition itself became, in a sense, the failure of her political career.

— Joya Dilberto
22:27

After men's anti-Prohibition groups failed, women ended Prohibition

The men's anti-Prohibition groups had been ineffectual. The real force that dismantled Prohibition was an all-women's organization built by Pauline Sabin, an heiress to the Morton Salt fortune. It drew women across class and racial lines and scaled with remarkable speed, powered by Sabin's personal wealth and networks—far outpacing the WCTU's reach. Just before the election, Sabin switched the group's endorsement to Democrat Franklin D. Roosevelt, who promised to repeal Prohibition.

— Joya Dilberto
28:38

AA's template came from a Christian movement, not from peer support groups

In 1935, Bill W., struggling to stay sober, felt the urge to drink and called a nearby church for help, connecting him with Dr. Bob. The two talked for five hours in a friend's living room and found solace—this conversation birthed AA. Both founders claimed they'd never heard of the 19th-century Washingtonians, a peer support organization. What they actually drew from was the Oxford Group, a Christian lifestyle movement that emphasized confession, mutual support, and submission to a higher power. Anonymity, confession-style sharing, and surrender to a higher power—AA's defining practices—all came from Oxford Group.

— Jamie Loftess
31:39

AA's founding text includes a chapter on how wives should manage drunk husbands

The 1939 edition of AA's foundational text, the Big Book, devotes its eighth chapter, ‘To Wives,’ to instructing spouses how to maintain their husband's sobriety without becoming a ‘nagging wet blanket’. The chapter's author, Bill W., went on to have affairs throughout his life. His infidelities acquired their own term within AA—‘13th stepping’—a euphemism for older members exploiting their status to sexually coerce newcomers entering recovery. The victims are almost always women.

— Jamie Loftess
35:41

AA's sobriety chips were never part of the official organization

Many assume the white chip is an original AA ritual from the 1930s, but it appears nowhere in AA's official canon or organizational history. The chips originated in a 1940s local practice, credited to a nun named Ignatia. Working in a hospital with withdrawal patients, she would hand out Sacred Heart medals and tell people they must return the medal before taking their first drink. Religion and women—erased from the official narrative—quietly sustained the program.

— Jamie Loftess
38:42

Black members were allowed only as ‘guests’ for decades

Bill W. was willing to welcome Black alcoholics as early as 1940, but Dr. Bob and other white members objected. Black people could attend only as ‘guests’. A St. Louis all-Black group even hid its existence in 1945 to avoid white members finding out. AA inherited the Washingtonians' rule of staying out of ‘politics’—but this rule was always enforced through a white lens. What counted as a political issue was never neutral.

— Jamie Loftess
46:50

The evidence on AA's effectiveness is contradictory

A controversial earlier study put the success rate of the 12-step program as low as 7 percent. But a 2020 meta-analysis of existing research found that 42 percent of AA members had achieved complete abstinence after one year. The episode's conclusion is modest: what actually works seems to be the community itself—a place you can always go to, where someone will listen, and which has nothing to do with ‘drinking’—rather than any specific theology or procedure.

Glossary

Oxford Group
A Christian lifestyle movement of the early 20th century that emphasized confession, mutual support, and submission to a higher power; AA borrowed its core practices.
13th stepping
AA slang for older members using their status and seniority to sexually coerce newcomers in recovery; victims are almost always women.
Volstead Act
The federal law enforcing Prohibition by criminalizing the production and sale of alcohol in the United States.
WCTU
Women's Christian Temperance Union—the world's largest women's organization in the late 19th and early 20th centuries; the primary force behind temperance activism and women's political mobilization.
