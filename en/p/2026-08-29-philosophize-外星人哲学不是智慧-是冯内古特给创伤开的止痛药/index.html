The alien philosophy isn't wisdom, it's Vonnegut's painkiller for trauma

The Tralfamadorian fatalism, eternalism and quietism are exactly what Billy needs after Dresden — Vonnegut uses this philosophy to demonstrate not a truth, but how a traumatised person uses philosophy as an analgesic.

8:17

Writing a war story is itself a distortion

Vonnegut's friend Mary O'Hare told him that all books about war beautify it as just or heroic, when in fact the people doing the fighting are just a bunch of kids. That made him realise: to write a story you have to compress events into a plot arc with a beginning, middle and end and a moral, and the moment you do that you have already flattened and distorted the absurdity of war into something it wasn't. He thought finding glory or honour in a war zone is only possible when you view the war from a distance; the more you zoom in and focus on the individuals actually doing the fighting, the more you see a bunch of kids and things that have nothing to do with honour — propaganda photos posed in dead people's clothes, helmets used as toilets, being so exhausted you can't remember why you're there.

11:21

Don't look for meaning in tragedy, make something with it

What Vonnegut really cares about is not whether the two sides in a war are morally asymmetric — he says we can and should make that kind of normative judgement. He cares about our reductive narrative impulse in the face of tragedy: once something happens, we want to extract meaning from it, to turn the bad thing into a plot arc where "I was meant to go through this so I could become who I am today". He says sometimes things in the universe simply can't be neatly explained, and that's fine. So he proposes another strategy: rather than spending your limited energy tying a bow around the whole thing and making everything that happened seem necessary, use the thing as fuel and go do something genuinely productive. Both approaches honour what happened in different ways, but only one of them keeps generations from falling into moral confusion and thinking the slaughter of hundreds of thousands of people was glorious.

— Stephen West
16:28

Trauma shatters a person's sense of time

At his eighteenth wedding anniversary party, Billy hears a barbershop quartet and is suddenly hit by an enormous nostalgia for friends he never had; everyone else thinks he's having a heart attack. Afterwards he realises that the mouths and faces of the four singers as they sang took him back to the faces of the German guards coming out of the slaughterhouse and seeing Dresden destroyed for the first time. Scholars generally agree that Billy's uncontrolled jumping through time is a dramatisation of traumatic experience — trauma drags a person into certain moments across time, and he has no control over it whatsoever. This philosophically overturns the common view of identity: normally we say "this person went through something intense in the past and now spends every day trying to recover from it", but Billy doesn't experience his life as a linear plot arc; the past invades the present against his will.

— Stephen West
18:31

Trauma makes it impossible to tell your life as a story

This connects to the argument in Byung-Chul Han's The Crisis of Narration: we understand ourselves across time through stories, and a story that can last and do work has to link past, present and future in a satisfying, meaningful relation. Accept that premise and it follows that when a person's past moments can surface in the present at any moment without warning, the past can't be fully integrated into a story about the self, and so meaning can't be extracted from it or moved past in the usual way. In Vonnegut's words, trauma shatters a person's capacity to have a genuinely chronological sense of self. Through the character of Billy he creates a narrative ontology: to exist with severe trauma means you inhabit a series of fragments rather than possessing a coherent life narrative you can feel stable in.

— Stephen West
23:43

The aliens aren't only talking about determinism

Many people finish the book and say: the aliens are determinists, and their life advice is just the conclusion that follows once you believe events can always be explained by prior causes. But Stephen West stresses that the aliens are also talking about fatalism, eternalism and quietism, and each of these four requires different philosophical arguments to support it, with plenty of different combinations lived out through history. Determinism (causal determinism) is a mechanistic claim about how reality unfolds: given all the conditions at a moment plus the laws of nature, only one future can follow. Fatalism is a modal claim that certain outcomes will happen no matter what; it doesn't care about the mechanism producing the outcome and doesn't necessarily need God or magic — for instance "the universe will eventually collapse into a singularity, and whatever happens in between doesn't matter" is also a local fatalism.

— Stephen West
27:46

Eternalism and quietism each stand on their own

Eternalism is the view that past, present and future moments all exist equally and are equally real; it mainly bears on theoretical physics — if time works that way, the universe is like a four-dimensional block, and differences in time exist like differences in space. But it has ethical implications too: if a person believes all future suffering is as real as present suffering, he may drastically change his current behaviour and prioritise future suffering, because the number of lives not yet lived is far larger. Quietism is the view that the wisest response to reality is to stop trying so hard to intervene in it, usually accompanied by an attitude of acceptance, or at least a sense of withdrawing from the effort to change things. These four positions need not be bound together: you can be a determinist but not a fatalist (like the ancient Stoics), or a fatalist but not a determinist, or a fatalist but not quietist (believing the Titanic must sink but still helping people into the lifeboats), or an eternalist but not a determinist.

— Stephen West
30:49

The alien philosophy is a painkiller, not a truth

Notice that every piece of Tralfamadorian philosophy is exactly what Billy needs after Dresden: fatalism comforts him, letting him believe there was never another Dresden that could have unfolded; eternalism lets him feel that the people who died horribly in the war still exist at other points in time; quietism gives him permission to stop agonising over those terrible moments and thinking about what he could do; and the absence of free will relieves him of ruminating over what he should have done. So what Vonnegut offers is probably not a serious philosophy worth adopting for life, but an excellent coping mechanism that looks philosophically grounded — it can absolutely help people who have been through terrible things, but as a whole-life philosophy it fails. This is also why some readings hold that the entire alien abduction is the invention of a traumatised person whose mental health is declining.

— Stephen West
32:53

If he truly believed it, he'd never have written this book

We have good reason to think Vonnegut himself thought this way, because he wrote Slaughterhouse-Five. He clearly did not think non-intervention is the wisest way to live. He may well have used parts of this philosophy to accept that Dresden had happened, that it can't be changed now, and that there is no clean story that exonerates everyone and makes the whole thing justified. But it's equally clear he thought "because something has already happened, nothing can be changed" is a mistake. If you really lived by Tralfamadorian philosophy, it would be a fine coping mechanism, keeping you insulated and safely away from the difficult moral dimensions of life — but you would never write Slaughterhouse-Five, and you would probably never make anything meaningful out of the bad things that have happened to you. More generally: of any philosophy that attracts us the moment we hear it, how much of the attraction is simply that it lets us cope with the biggest confusion we're currently struggling with?
