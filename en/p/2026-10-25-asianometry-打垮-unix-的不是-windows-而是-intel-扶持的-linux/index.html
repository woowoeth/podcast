What killed UNIX wasn't Windows—it was Intel-backed Linux

UNIX vendors locked in customers through proprietary hardware and operating systems for years; Intel initially opened a crack with Windows NT, but what truly proved lethal was free, open-source, and lock-in-proof Linux.

Complete breakdown of the three-way maneuvering between Intel, Microsoft, and Red Hat, and how server market share shifted step-by-step from UNIX to Lintel—solid details and abundant numbers.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:04

Intel wanted to replicate its PC playbook in servers

Intel had built dominance in consumer PCs through economies of scale—higher volume meant better yields and lower costs, which in turn funded superior manufacturing processes. This margin advantage let Intel outpace AMD and Cyrix decisively. Intel believed the same formula would work for enterprise servers, eyeing the high-margin market held by Sun, IBM, and HP, where RISC chips and proprietary UNIX variants commanded prices in the tens of thousands of dollars.

5:15

Pentium Pro brought x86 within striking distance of RISC for the first time

The 32-bit Pentium Pro, released in late 1995, had 5.5 million transistors and ran at 150-200MHz. In some integer benchmarks it actually edged out the fastest RISC chips of the time—surprising the industry. The R10000 soon reclaimed ground, but Pentium Pro reset expectations about x86's ceiling. Paired with Windows NT 4.0, also released that year, x86 servers could now match RISC performance at a fraction of the price. NT 4.0 revenue grew 85% in 1996 and captured 27% market share.

8:19

The NSP incident revealed Intel's hidden anxieties about Microsoft

Beneath the surface partnership lay tension. In 1995, Intel proposed a Native Signal Processing standard to let CPUs handle audio and video directly, hoping to drive chip upgrades. Microsoft saw this as middleware that bypassed Windows and publicly refused. Bill Gates sent Andy Grove a pointed email. What really unsettled Intel was that Windows NT, by design, ran on multiple platforms—x86, DEC Alpha, PowerPC, MIPS. If NT achieved dominance, Microsoft could easily reduce x86 to just one option among many.

14:42

The GPL saved Linux from UNIX's fragmentation trap

When Linus Torvalds released Linux in 1991, it adopted Richard Stallman's GPL in version 0.12 in 1992. The GPL let anyone read and modify the code, but demanded that distributed derivatives be open-sourced too. This closed off the usual commercial trick of taking code, locking it down, and selling it as a proprietary product. It prevented the fate that befell UNIX—thirty-odd incompatible variants. It also reassured companies like IBM, which deployed hundreds of engineers; they knew their contributions wouldn't be captured. Torvalds later called the GPL decision the smartest he ever made.

22:08

Intel escalated from ignoring Linux to actively supporting it

In early 1998, Fermi National Laboratory and other institutions approached Intel asking for math libraries ported to Linux. When Intel checked with OEM partners, they discovered that nearly 20% of public web servers were already running Linux—a remarkable penetration for a system with nearly zero desktop presence. Andy Grove's response was swift and forceful. He ordered action. When Intel executives met Red Hat, they were confused: Red Hat owned no IP to the core product, anyone could get the code for free—how would it make money? CEO Robert Young used a ketchup metaphor to convince them.

27:12

Intel's Red Hat investment triggered Microsoft's fury

In September 1998, Intel joined Netscape, Benchmark Capital, and Greylock Partners in funding Red Hat, pledging technical support and resident engineers to liaise with the Linux community. Red Hat executives recalled that Microsoft "went crazy." Intel's executives fielded angry calls from Microsoft that weekend. But Microsoft was hamstrung—it faced a May 1998 government monopoly lawsuit and couldn't afford a visible escalation. An understated consequence: a Benchmark partner also sat on Dell's board. In early 1999, the connection brought Dell to become the first major manufacturer to ship Linux preinstalled.

35:29

Linux 2.4 closed the multi-processor gap and the economics finally worked

The 1999 Mindcraft benchmark exposed Linux 2.2's fatal weakness: beyond two CPUs, performance cratered. Windows NT had long supported 4-way, 8-way, even more. By 2001, Linux 2.4 addressed spinlock issues and could effectively scale to 16 or even 32 processors, opening the mid-market door. A Dell server with four Xeon chips running Red Hat Linux cost $24,000 and matched the performance of a Sun Fire UNIX machine worth twice as much. Linux server hardware revenue grew 63% in 2002, while UNIX hardware revenue declined 11%.

40:44

Active Directory, not marketing, was Microsoft's real moat

Against Linux's free assault, Microsoft's truly effective weapon wasn't the "Get the Facts" campaign. It was Active Directory, introduced with Windows 2000—a directory service that unified identity authentication and permissions for every user and device in an organization. Switching to Windows wasn't about swapping an OS anymore; it meant rebuilding your entire organizational identity infrastructure. That friction worked. In 2005, Windows server revenue matched UNIX for the first time. In 2006 it surpassed it. Yet the real victor was Linux, which didn't claw share from Windows so much as it steadily devoured UNIX and exploded in markets Windows never conquered—supercomputing, the web, and later cloud.

Glossary

Lintel
The combination of Linux and Intel, describing the new server architecture paradigm that displaced UNIX+RISC.
GPL
GNU General Public License; an open-source license requiring that derivative works distributed externally be open-sourced equally.
Active Directory
The directory service in Windows networks that centrally manages user and device identity authentication and permissions.
IA-64 / Itanium
Intel's 64-bit server processor architecture originally planned to succeed x86.
distro
Short for Linux distribution; packaging of the Linux kernel with supporting software into a directly-installable system.
