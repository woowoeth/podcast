Open weights are not zero-sum: America should get in the game and do open source itself

Treating open weights as a threat to closed labs gets the economics backwards — it only adds AI use cases, and the real money is in inference, not the model itself.

1:01

Open weights are misread as a zero-sum game

Levie signed the open-weights open letter that Jensen Huang kicked off, and his reading has two layers: first, open weights themselves drive AI progress, spawning more innovation and use cases, since you can train on them and customize them; second, America needs more companies working on open weights. He stresses that open weights are wrongly framed as zero-sum against closed source, when in fact they only increase the number of use cases people have for AI. He also concedes the closed camp has a real argument on safety, but says he is almost unconditionally convinced that more open innovation is good for AI diffusion.

— Aaron Levie
3:03

The ethical line on distillation is very hard to draw

Asked when distillation crosses the line, Levie says he does not know where that line is. His core argument: it is hard to argue on one hand that an AI model can be trained on the public internet, and on the other that another AI model cannot be trained on an AI model's outputs. He also points out that most people never chose whether their data gets trained on — it may just be buried in the fine print of the terms of service. If you accept that the world's general knowledge gets trained into models, then whether that knowledge comes from Reddit or from Anthropic, he cannot see a meaningful difference. He understands labs wanting to do everything to prevent distillation, but that does not mean an ethical line has been crossed.

— Aaron Levie
6:04

Blocking China only makes American AI more expensive

On the worry that the US ecosystem leans too heavily on Chinese open-weight models, Levie concedes the arguments are decent but asks: so what are you going to do? He offers an analogy: someone says the iPhone should sell for five thousand dollars instead of a thousand, because you should not depend on Chinese manufacturing capacity. If you work through the alternative, the conclusion is that America uses very expensive AI and the rest of the world uses very cheap AI, and then you see how competitiveness looks over five to ten years. He thinks the cat is out of the bag, open-weight models exist, China knows how to produce them, and the dynamic cannot be reversed. He sides with Jensen Huang: China will not give up on AI just because it is blocked — it may instead be forced into a different hardware stack, ultimately deployed into sovereign clouds.

— Aaron Levie
12:07

The money in AI is in inference, not the model

Levie thinks the money-making link in AI is inference, and the money ultimately flows to the infrastructure stack. If there were only one or two labs in the market with training secrets fully locked down, maybe you could seal off a few more layers; but America has three to five players who treat leading models as existential, and competitive dynamics will push token costs ever closer to infrastructure cost. His range: markups land at 20%, 30%, 40% of infrastructure cost, not 70%, 80%, 90%. On that premise, if you run the preferred infrastructure for some open model, do its post-training environment, or become the safer brand for enterprises deploying it, you can earn almost as much revenue. He also suggests frontier labs use the previous generation of models for faster open-source follow-ups, which would actually retain more use cases.

— Aaron Levie
15:22

Anthropic not open-sourcing is a safety belief

Why don't labs open-source? Levie splits them into two groups. The first needs a longer time horizon before believing open weights can be monetized: ship a closed model tomorrow, force all traffic through your own API, and on paper you definitely make more; but over a longer horizon, the token cost per task gets pushed down no matter what, and after two or three stages of reasoning closed and open are nearly even, because what ultimately matters is inference cost. The second group, specifically Anthropic, he believes treats this as a major safety risk — not an economic argument about market share, but a fundamental belief that one entity needs to control the flow of tokens in order to prevent prompt injection and to route different query types to different models — things that cannot be done in an open-weights environment.

— Aaron Levie
17:30

Opus 5 improves on two axes at once

Box has been running Opus 5 evals for the past week or two, and Levie says it is a meaningful jump over Opus 4.8, which was already best-in-class at launch. Box handles enterprise data across industries: life sciences, law firms, big banks' documents, contracts, marketing assets, research materials. Enterprises need a model to do two things at once: deep domain understanding (know life sciences, know law) and the horizontal ability to process lots of data, call tools and do analysis. Opus 5 improved on both axes. He also mentions Fable completely crushing 4.8 on some internal coding tasks, an advantage no token-cost gap can make up for, but Fable has a problem: on certain capabilities it downgrades you to 4.8, and it is hard to know in advance whether a safety warning was triggered.

— Aaron Levie
21:36

It is still too early to lock down models

Levie says what Fable currently downgrades and blocks you from doing, if it became the long-term environment, would be unsustainable for the future of AI — people would simply stop using AI. He appreciates the multi-point framework Anthropic has put forward: agreeing on capabilities at different risk levels and testing models. But he presses: who sets this risk framework? How do you confirm these risks are real risks we actually perceive? His conclusion is that given how early it still is, locking these models down is premature.

— Aaron Levie
22:36

AI lets Box take on multi-year projects

Levie remains very bullish on software engineering, and Box uses these models to do far more than before. He describes a mechanism: in the past you either turned down small things because they were not worth it, or turned down big things because they were too hard, so most software projects sat in the middle band of "doable in one to six months." Now multi-year projects are no longer multi-year, a one-week job becomes two hours, so you can solve more of the problems customers have always had, which in turn raises ambition. He says Box now has dozens of projects that would never have started if AI did not exist. His judgment: if you think you no longer need software engineers, your product roadmap is definitely not ambitious enough.

— Aaron Levie
25:42

Model routing will become the enterprise default

Levie admits he is biased toward this conclusion: the more you need multiple models to complete a task or a set of tasks, the more value accrues to the layer that understands the task, gets the data and handles the workflow — the application AI layer where he sits. He names Cognition, Factory, Cursor and Replit as companies that all want a world where "multiple models each excel" — a cost-optimized workhorse model and a super-frontier orchestration model. He counts five credible model developers in America: SpaceX, Google, Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta, all simultaneously pushing down the cost of intelligence and pushing up the frontier. Model routing also eases enterprise analysis paralysis: models keep leapfrogging each other, so enterprises are reluctant to lock into one model family, and the routing layer amounts to saying you do not have to make that choice.

Glossary

open weights
Model parameters that are publicly downloadable, self-deployable and fine-tunable, as opposed to closed models offered only via API.
distillation
Training one model on the outputs of a larger model, transferring capability into a smaller model.
model routing
A middle layer that automatically dispatches requests across multiple models by task, cost or capability.
prompt injection
An attack that hijacks a model's behavior through input content, making it follow unintended instructions.
sovereign cloud
Cloud infrastructure run by a national or local entity that meets data-sovereignty and compliance requirements.
