Moving data centers to space, SpaceX targets million-ton annual orbital capacity within three years

Starship's rapid and complete reusability is the prerequisite for the leap in orbital computing power. SpaceX plans to increase annual orbital transport capacity from about 2,500 tons to million-ton scale within three years, paving the way for space-based AI data centers.

1:05

Humanity barely registers on the Kardashev scale

The Kardashev scale measures civilization's progress by how much energy it can harness: Type I means consuming all of Earth's energy, Type II all of a star's, Type III all of a galaxy's. Musk says humanity hasn't even reached Type I, using less than one trillionth of the Sun's total output. The Sun accounts for 99.86% of the solar system's mass, making Earth a speck by comparison. He joked that reaching one millionth of the Sun's energy would be "a Microsoft-scale achievement" worth celebrating.

— Elon Musk
8:21

Moving data centers to space requires solving three simultaneous bottlenecks

Three factors constrain space computing: sufficient orbital lift capacity (via Starship), sufficient solar power generation (via panels and radiators), and sufficient chip production. Musk quantified this in terawatts—delivering 100 gigawatts or even 1 terawatt of power to orbit requires matching solar capacity and chip manufacturing capability, all three essential.

— Elon Musk
9:25

Without reusable rockets, space settlement remains idle talk

Musk emphasizes that Starship's core breakthrough is "fully and rapidly reusable"—the prerequisite for crossing the Kardashev threshold and extending life to the Moon and Mars. Non-reusable rockets are too expensive; you can't produce enough of them. He compares it to aviation: if you discarded a plane after each flight, the airline industry couldn't exist. The launch tower catches the rocket rather than using landing legs to minimize structural weight.

— Elon Musk
12:31

SpaceX already commands 85 to 90 percent of Earth's orbital lift

Even without Starship, Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy give SpaceX 85-90% of Earth's mass to orbit. China does most of the rest; other countries combine for 5-7%. Once Starship enters service, the goal is increasing annual orbital mass from roughly 2,500 tons to million-ton scale—SpaceX expects to achieve this within about three years.

— Elon Musk
14:50

AI satellites are actually simpler than Starlink satellites

Ian Dahl explains that Starlink satellites require phased-array antennas, parabolic dishes, and extensive laser links—complex engineering. AI satellites are essentially solar panels, radiators, and modest laser links; simpler engineering overall. The first generation targets 150 kilowatts peak power and 120 kilowatts sustained—roughly matching one Nvidia GB300 rack (72 GPUs)—so each satellite is basically a compute cabinet in orbit.

— Ian Dahl
19:01

Orbital data centers have only a few milliseconds of latency

Many assume moving computing to space means high latency, but satellite orbits sit 600-800 kilometers up. Light travels 300 kilometers per millisecond, so round-trip latency is roughly 3 milliseconds—Musk says not worth worrying about. Data flows through laser links or Ka/Ku-band antennas to the Starlink constellation, which relays it back to Earth.

— Elon Musk
23:17

TerraFab is a chipmaking facility ten times larger than Tesla's Texas Gigafactory

Current AI satellites use off-the-shelf Nvidia Rubin, GB300, or TPU chips, but scaling orbital computing by an order of magnitude demands in-house fabrication at TerraFab, occupying roughly 100 million square feet—ten times the size of Tesla Gigafactory Texas. Musk notes that even without novel technology breakthroughs, merely scaling existing chipmaking processes could theoretically produce 1 terawatt of annual chip output.

— Elon Musk
26:21

100 gigawatts in three and a half years is the goal—just the beginning

SpaceX's timeline: reach 1 gigawatt of annual space AI capacity by year-end next year, then increase by an order of magnitude each year thereafter—hitting 10 gigawatts in 2.5 years and 100 gigawatts in 3.5 years, with longer-term ambitions of 1 terawatt (equivalent to twice current U.S. national electricity consumption). Musk stresses this is "a goal we believe we can achieve," not a promise. Scaling three more orders of magnitude from Earth-based launch is unrealistic; the next step involves electromagnetic mass drivers on the Moon to produce and launch satellites in-situ, since the Moon has no atmosphere and one-sixth Earth's gravity—no rockets needed.

— Elon Musk
