Lawrence ordered a massacre of 250 disarmed POWs and wrote it into his battle report

Before the Damascus campaign, Lawrence—allegedly raped in Deraa—suffered psychological collapse and ordered ‘no prisoners’ for the retreating Turkish column: 250 disarmed POWs were subsequently machine-gunned to death.

The Deraa incident is the focal point of mutual accusations among Lawrence biographers

Lawrence was captured by Turkish forces while scouting a railway junction in Deraa; most accounts say he was beaten and gang-raped. In Seven Pillars of Wisdom he devoted nearly two pages to describing his humiliation in strange, almost voyeuristic language, but never explicitly states it was rape. More strangely, despite severe injuries—broken ribs, broken wrist—he managed to crawl out through a window, wander the streets, find a camel and ride fifty miles to safety; this sequence itself strains credibility. Anderson argues what can be confirmed is that what happened in Deraa left him with deep psychological trauma, regardless of the exact nature; afterward he was fundamentally changed.

— Scott Anderson
5:15

The Deraa incident coincided with the week the Balfour Declaration was revealed

Anderson identifies a crucial clue: Lawrence entered Deraa the same week Izvestia exposed the secret Anglo-French agreement to partition Syria and Lloyd George announced that Britain would establish a Jewish homeland in Palestine. Arabs had only suspected betrayal; now they had proof. But Anderson also concedes that Lawrence may not yet have known the Balfour Declaration was public, so the causal chain is uncertain; the Deraa mission was more likely simply a dangerous venture to obtain supplies or scout the railway hub.

— Scott Anderson
7:25

After his ordeal, Lawrence began doing what he had once mocked

Three months after Deraa, at the Battle of Tafileh, Lawrence fought a European-style positional defense he had previously mocked, exposing his troops to massive casualties. He also grew reckless in railroad demolition operations. Physically, he repeatedly contracted malaria and dysentery, fell from camels and broke bones. A man of 5'4", thin as a youth, continued commanding troops through mental and physical collapse.

10:29

Capturing Damascus ahead of the British Army became Lawrence's obsession for a year

After the Battle of Megiddo, the Arab Army and Allenby's British forces advanced on separate routes toward Damascus—the last opportunity to overturn the Sykes-Picot Agreement. Lawrence continuously impressed on Faisal a conviction: if the Arab forces reached the city first, the agreement could be undone and Arabs would retain their sovereignty. This belief sustained his actions over the following year and made the ultimate disillusionment devastating.

— Scott Anderson
11:32

A sheikh who witnessed village atrocities charged alone at enemy forces and was torn apart

At Tafas village, the Arab forces discovered the aftermath of Turkish withdrawal units committing atrocities against women and children. A sheikh, Talal (塔拉勒), who had long fought at Lawrence's side, completely lost control, charged masked toward the Turkish column on horseback, and was torn to pieces. Lawrence immediately ordered: ‘No prisoners—reward whoever kills the most Turks.’ The Arab forces fragmented the withdrawing column into progressively smaller pieces and massacred each segment, sparing even those already on the ground in surrender. Two thousand died.

— Scott Anderson
12:32

Lawrence's own battle report admits he ordered the execution of disarmed POWs

One unit had not received the ‘no prisoners’ order and had captured 250 Turkish soldiers and several German allies; disarmed, they sat by the roadside. Lawrence ordered their execution. His battle report, written that day, states in his own words: he ordered machine gun (Hotchkiss) strafing to ‘finish them.’ Anderson stresses this is not hearsay but Lawrence's own written admission—another focal point of controversy in his life, since his defenders often deny it occurred.

— Scott Anderson
18:54

Allenby deposed Faisal to his face, and Lawrence's immediate resignation was accepted

In Damascus, Allenby told Faisal directly in a six-person meeting that Syria had been promised to France. Faisal walked out in anger—effectively deposed. Lawrence immediately asked Allenby to release him from his position; initially refused, he persisted until Allenby let him go that same afternoon. Lawrence never returned to Damascus; the city he had been shouting about marching toward for over a year became the place of his complete heartbreak.

24:13

The Faisal-Weizmann Agreement contained a nullification clause that is routinely overlooked

In January 1919, two months after the armistice, Lawrence brokered and interpreted an agreement between Faisal and Chaim Weizmann, leader of the Zionist movement: Weizmann acknowledged Arab rights to statehood, Faisal consented to a degree of Jewish immigration. But the agreement carried a crucial nullification clause: ‘if Arabs don't get what they seek, the agreement is void.’ Dripel notes that this condition is routinely deliberately ignored by those claiming ‘Faisal agreed to give the land to the Jews.’

— William Dalrymple
31:39

Historians accuse Lawrence of falsifying Faisal's original Arabic text

Historian George Antonius observed that Lawrence's English translation differed from Faisal's original Arabic text—the translation had been ‘softened and obscured’ to later be wielded as evidence that Arabs had agreed to the Zionist plan. Anderson argues that if this is true, it is significant, because it means Lawrence artificially exaggerated the Arab side's concessions in translation, laying the groundwork for the subsequent Palestinian problem.
