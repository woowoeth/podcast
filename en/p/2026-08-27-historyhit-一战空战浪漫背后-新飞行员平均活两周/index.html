The Air War of WWI Was Not Romance, It Was a Meat Grinder Where Pilots Lasted Two Weeks on Average

At the start of WWI, senior officers dismissed aircraft as a joke; within four years, the sky became a meat grinder of reconnaissance, bombing, and dogfighting, with new pilots in 1917 averaging about two weeks of life.

6:03

Britain's first airplane came from a Wild West showman

At the start of WWI, senior officers still saw cavalry as the main reconnaissance force and were both snobbish and impatient toward aircraft: airships were considered a gentleman's mode of transport, while heavier-than-air machines were dirty, noisy, and required mechanics, dismissed as 'not for our kind.' Britain's first airplane came from American Samuel Cody—he originally came to Britain with a Wild West show, posing as Buffalo Bill's cousin and performing tricks like shooting eggs off his wife's head before turning to aviation. His large aircraft was nicknamed the 'flying birdcage' because it was big enough to hold a canary. Cody later died in a flying accident, which was the norm in early aviation.

12:32

The commander-in-chief only cared if it was cold up there, not about intelligence

The Royal Flying Corps' initial mission was to reconnoiter German movements over France, but the start was almost farcical: two pilots got lost; one landed and nearly was captured, then was recognized and taken to dinner by the mayor; the other was so lost that he flew over a large city before realizing it was Brussels. Yet they soon began reporting genuinely useful intelligence. One pilot sketched what he saw on his knee board and reported to Sir John French, but French was not interested in the intelligence—he only asked if it was cold up there and how far one could see. French's skepticism later proved completely wrong, and aircraft were gradually taken seriously.

— Joshua Levine
14:51

Aircraft changed war with eyes, not machine guns

The most critical air achievement of WWI was not shooting down but seeing. On September 13, observer Atkinson's report twice noted 'trenches are being dug in the fields.' Joshua says this was the beginning of everything—from then on, the Western Front entered trench warfare, and reconnaissance aircraft went from dispensable gadgets to truly essential eyes and ears. Later that same day, Sir John French officially issued a commendation to the flying corps. In other words, aircraft changed war not because of machine guns but because they were the only eyes that could see the whole battlefield.

— Joshua Levine
19:07

Bombing was not designed by generals but tried out by pilots

Bombing and air combat were not designed by generals but discovered by pilots themselves. Initially they dropped steel darts, then grenades, and only later strapped on bombs. One pilot cut a hole in the fuselage floor and lined it with tin to form a chute for dropping bombs, but the airflow compressed the tin and jammed the bomb, leaving the detonator exposed beneath the fuselage. He passed a note to Louis Strange saying, 'We're going to die.' The two chose to land and wait for the explosion, but the tall corn in the field pulled the detonator off, and they emerged unscathed. Louis Strange later died peacefully in bed in old age—such was the daily reality of early flight.

— Joshua Levine
27:15

One mechanical device made every Allied aircraft obsolete overnight

To fire a machine gun through the propeller, the Dutchman Fokker first offered the solution to the Allies, who declined it, so he turned to building aircraft for Germany. His mechanical device allowed bullets to fire only between propeller blades, suddenly enabling German pilots to aim head-on, and all existing Allied aircraft became instantly obsolete—'going up meant certain death.' This moment is comparable to how drones on today's Ukrainian front become obsolete within weeks of arrival—WWI air combat itself was an arms race measured in months, even weeks.

— Joshua Levine
37:24

Average life two weeks, yet with orderlies and a roller-skating rink

Fighter pilots lived a life of extremes. During the 1917 Battle of Arras, new pilots averaged only about two weeks from arrival at the front to death; many were sent into battle with inadequate training, and a condition called 'flying sickness'—a mental breakdown treated with more leniency—emerged. Yet at the same time, they were housed behind the lines with orderlies, could go into town for drinks, had favorite restaurants, and one squadron commander even poured concrete himself to build a roller-skating rink. Comfort and terror coexisted—that was the daily life of a fighter pilot.

— Joshua Levine
48:36

Too-famous aces may have actively chosen when to die

Richthofen and McCudden both wrote down their own air combat rules, such as 'after shooting down an enemy, do not follow him down over his own lines,' because low altitude made you a target for ground rifles and machine guns. Yet these two most elite and symbolic pilots ultimately died by violating their own rules. Joshua does not accept the explanation that 'they thought they were above the rules' and proposes another possibility: because they were so famous, they were kept continuously at the front, and subconsciously knowing they would die sooner or later, they chose to end it at a time and place they could control, rather than wait for the enemy to choose.

— Joshua Levine

Glossary

Pusher
An aircraft with the engine behind the pilot, allowing an unobstructed forward-firing machine gun in the nose.
Tractor
An aircraft with the engine and propeller at the front, usually better performing, but forward-firing guns must shoot through the propeller.
Interrupter gear
A mechanical device that allows a machine gun to fire only between propeller blades; Fokker used it to change air combat.
Scout
The early WWI term for a fighter aircraft, tasked with intercepting enemy planes and protecting one's own reconnaissance aircraft.
Flying circus
A mobile fighter unit commanded by Richthofen, transported by train between sectors of the front.
