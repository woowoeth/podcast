The market is bullish on AI glasses, but this founder says the next two years will be harder

He reckons the current smart-glasses market is only two to three million units, facing squeeze from Apple, Meta, and domestic tech giants—the real inflection point waits for supply chain, content ecosystem, and product to mature together in 2028–2029.

7:02

The faster phones connect us, the more isolated we become

Yang sees phones as solving connection efficiency—you can reach a friend across the world in seconds. But phones created information silos: the faster we connect, the more distant we become from those around us. Many people who chat online fall out of touch. Glasses could bridge this gap. Walking past a stranger, both of you see each other's information in real time, spot shared interests and mutual friends instantly, breaking the social island effect. This was his core principle when designing the glasses product.

— Longsheng Yang
9:02

Social is the killer app for smart glasses

Yang is convinced that social—not translation or photography—is the endgame killer app. His team built a small dating-scene demo: a dozen people wearing glasses at a speed-dating event. The glasses display identity information and the AI guides the conversation, replacing the host or matchmaker role, removing awkwardness between strangers. He frames it as a tiny sample, but one that will scale into daily life and play an ever-larger role in social interaction.

— Longsheng Yang
12:06

They could read microexpressions—and chose not to

When asked whether emotion recognition could detect if someone is lying or assist ‘cold reading,’ Yang directly answered: ‘Technically feasible, but we don't recommend it.’ Co-host Qin Kuang added that over-datafying someone's emotions is risky: ‘If you data-fy everything, it's genuinely scary.’ Yang explained the company's core principle is tech for good: information should assist people, never replace human judgment or reshape social boundaries. Even if microexpression recognition is ready, a single false read shouldn't destroy a relationship—‘You start as a matchmaker and end as a home-wrecker.’

— Longsheng Yang / Kuang Qin
17:07

He picked me: the moment I saw Oculus

Yang was struck by “Black Mirror” in sophomore and junior year, frantically searching for similar products. As graduation neared, Oculus's first headset shipped. When asked if he felt chosen by fate, he said: ‘He picked me—it's just that he told me there seemed to be a path to get close to what I wanted to do.’ He immediately flew from Dalian to Shenzhen, found Chaihuo (柴火) Makerspace, the only place to touch VR then. After his resume was rejected, he showed up in person, happened to meet a manager, and got hired on the spot.

— Longsheng Yang
20:09

Heard the hype cycle five times, still believes

In 2016–17, Yang concluded VR was a home entertainment device, too heavy for mobile carry. He pivoted to AR, seeing it as capable of a larger market and becoming the next-generation terminal. Asked whether ‘year one’ hype—heard at least five times—ever shook his faith, he said: ‘I definitely still believe it, and I believe it even now, because the core need itself hasn't changed.’ The need remains constant: floating screens from sci-fi, social tags visible as you walk down the street. He has never told the press this is the year for glasses.

— Longsheng Yang
36:17

Bet on all-in-one five years ago, never touched modular design

From the first generation, Yang committed to integrated, lightweight design, sacrificing resolution to get there. Many competitors used DLP, LCOS, or external power packs (split-body systems). Inmo ‘never went that route.’ They're among the rare early companies to never accept external power from day one—Yang calls them ‘the all-in-one pioneer.’ That bet was five years ahead of the industry; only in the last year or two have monochrome glasses started clustering around lightweight design.

— Longsheng Yang / Kuang Qin
40:18

Could record a week of life, chose local storage instead

Inmo Future has 18GB of storage, enough to record 7–10 days of content. For privacy reasons, this capability is stored entirely locally and accessed by the consumer alone—never uploaded to the cloud. On whether to build a feature, Yang applies three gates: first, is there a cohort of people who absolutely must buy it? Second, can current tech and supply chain support it, and does cost match what consumers perceive as value? Third, is the return on investment worth the effort? There's also a values line: even if it makes money, he won't touch projects that circle money through hype, like some web3 ventures—‘we'd walk away from any amount of money.’

— Longsheng Yang
55:29

Three curves converge simultaneously in 2028–2029

Yang identifies two major obstacles to mainstream adoption. First, upstream supply chain—chips, optics, batteries all need a meaningful next step. He predicts three years to support a more refined ‘iPhone 4 moment’ product. Second, content ecosystem: major platforms like ByteDance and Alibaba only began laying out agent strategies around glasses this year; he estimates two to three more years to grow a real one. When hardware, supply chain, and content ecosystem mature and intersect at the same moment, the market will experience a shift fundamentally different from phones, compelling real consumers to buy in.

— Longsheng Yang

Glossary

Split-body design / modular system
A smart-glasses design where the frame connects via tether to an external battery or compute pack for power and processing.
Product-market fit
The point when a product achieves real market demand at scale and proves its core proposition to the market.
SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping)
A technique where a device builds real-time spatial maps and locates itself using onboard sensors, without external infrastructure.
Inside-out tracking
Spatial tracking using only the device's onboard sensors, requiring no external base stations or markers.
