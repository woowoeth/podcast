The Number One Fraud Red Flag Isn't Greed — It's Personal Finances Already Out of Control

For 14 straight years, ACFE has ranked "personal financial problems" as the top red flag for financial misconduct; systemic incentives, a critical brain region going offline, and moral overconfidence all let good people cross the line step by step.

Financial ethicsFinancial advisersInvestor protectionFraud red flagsBehavioral financeCFP
One author is a clinical neuropsychologist, the other has 20 years of suing financial advisers; the offender profiles and warning signals here have more screening value than moral slogans.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:04

The test for crossing the line is one sentence: this is not your money

Moira defines financial misconduct as "doing bad things with other people's money": it includes both taking money for yourself and guiding or advising others to deploy funds in ways that aren't in their best interest. She stresses that anyone working in finance must always remember "this is not your money." Reducing the ethical question to fiduciary duty gives all the later warning signals a single baseline for judgment.

— Moira
9:10

The number one red flag for 14 straight years is personal financial problems

Philippa cites ACFE's global report: for 14 consecutive years, the top fraud red flag hasn't been greed but "personal financial problems" — people whose personal finances are out of control, who can't make ends meet, or who are drowning in debt are the most likely to get involved in financial misconduct. The financial industry offers access to other people's money and attracts people who chase wealth, and on top of that people are irrational about money; those three conditions stack into the soil where misconduct breeds.

— Philippa
13:14

When taking advice, the brain is like it's praying: the critical region goes offline

Moira notes that neuroimaging evidence shows that when people receive financial advice, key critical regions of the brain go "offline," in a state similar to the brain during prayer. People feel safe, trusting, open, and this non-critical state heightens vulnerability, especially for those unfamiliar with finance. She gives the example that even hedge fund managers will "sit and wait for the answer" in front of an adviser rather than asking how the product actually works.

— Moira
19:19

Good people start erring through obedience and eagerness to help, not greed

Philippa and Moira break down the path by which good people err: blindly obeying orders, crossing lines out of "misguided helpfulness," then hiding the mistake out of fear, then covering it with a bigger lie. The book's "John" slides from a small slip into lying, then into doubling down on the cover-up. Moira stresses that ethics is not a solo sport but a team sport; if the organizational culture tacitly permits misconduct, it's very hard for an individual to stay clean alone.

— Philippa
37:36

Psychopaths flow into finance disproportionately

Responding to Cameron's concern, Moira says the evidence shows psychopaths flow disproportionately into finance and business because "that's where the money is." She cites Robert Hare's research: successful psychopaths tend to be charming and extremely good at spotting and exploiting other people's desires. The damage they do is large, and they often keep circulating within the industry because colleagues feel a sense of "relief," leaving the problem for the next firm.

— Moira
53:50

Most people don't realize they're making an ethical decision at all

Traditional ethics training always presents clear moral dilemmas in the classroom, but real-world problems tend to arise quietly, so people don't realize they're at a fork in the road. Moira cites Kahneman's "what you see is all there is": people don't proactively question the motives of product providers, don't think about who benefits from a complex product, and don't admit to themselves that they don't actually understand it. The result is that even with ethics training, most of the time they have no idea they're facing an ethical decision.

— Moira
1:02:56

The future wrongdoer is a vulnerable ordinary person, not a bad guy

Philippa lists the profile of the future wrongdoer: early in their career (Evan got pulled into a global fraud one month into the job and was banned for life nine months later), in debt, highly dependent on their boss, people-pleasing, low self-esteem (compensated for with a luxury car), fatigued, family problems, and so on. These signals are rarely used by the industry to screen advisers for their own biases; they aren't some rare evil but the common weaknesses of ordinary people under pressure and vulnerability.

— Philippa
1:09:07

After 20 years of suing, she has never sued a single CFP holder

Philippa practiced law for 20 years and sued many financial professionals, but never once sued a CFP holder. She thinks there's no simple way to pick out a good adviser, but having the certification is a positive signal. She also offers a test: if an adviser only talks about investments and doesn't understand the client's whole life situation, that's just wealth management, not financial planning — the subject of planning is the person, not the assets.

— Philippa
1:23:28

Most people overrate their own morality; humility takes deliberate practice

Moira warns that the "better-than-average effect" is especially pronounced in the moral domain — most people overrate their own moral level. Almost every wrongdoer she interviewed said something like "there but for the grace of God go I," so moral humility needs to be deliberately cultivated and welcomed. The other thing to practice is tolerating discomfort: treat anxiety and discomfort as meaningful data rather than as annoyances to be eliminated quickly.

— Moira

Glossary

ACFE / Association of Certified Fraud Examiners
An international anti-fraud professional body that regularly publishes a global report on occupational fraud.
CFP / Certified Financial Planner
An international financial planning certification requiring comprehensive planning and ethical standards.
