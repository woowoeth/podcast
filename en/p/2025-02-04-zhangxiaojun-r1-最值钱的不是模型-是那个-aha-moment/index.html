The most valuable thing about R1 isn't the model, it's that aha moment

DeepSeek was the first to solve the riddle O1 posed: without being taught, the model learns reflection and error correction on its own inside reinforcement learning. R1-Zero is cleaner than R1, but R1 is the one you can actually use.

The aha moment wasn't written into the model by a human

The R1 paper's title uses the word incentivizing, behind which is OpenAI researcher Hongi's talk at MIT, ‘Don't teach, incentivize’—don't teach the model how to do it, tell it what's good and what's bad and let it figure it out itself. That self-correction in the paper, ‘Wait, wait, there's an aha moment I can fly here’, wasn't written into the model by a researcher; it emerged on its own during reinforcement learning. It's like the divine move in Go—a behavioral pattern the human developers never thought of.

39:51

Post-training compute is a drizzle next to pre-training

DeepSeek V3's pre-training cost 5.3M USD, mid-training used 0.2M to extend context from 8k to 128k, and post-training cost only 0.01M—less than 0.2% of pre-training cost. That number shows post-training and reinforcement learning scaling are still at an extremely early stage, with an extremely high return on investment. Estimating R1's entire training run, if well optimized it might take just 100k USD, and at the absolute worst 1M USD.

— Pan Jiayi
48:13

GRPO drops the value function because it isn't accurate

PPO requires training an extra value model to finely judge whether each step is right or wrong, but in the long chain-of-thought setting that assumption doesn't hold: the supervision signal only exists at the last step, the sequence runs thousands of tokens long, and the model has learned to correct itself—whether that 3 in ‘1+1=3’ is right or wrong becomes hard to say, because it can change it back later. A value function can't be precise, and it eats enormous compute, so DeepSeek simply discards it and does policy gradient only. That's the starting point of GRPO.

— Pan Jiayi
57:22

The reward function uses rules only, no neural network

R1-Zero's reward has two parts: an accuracy reward that checks whether the answer is right, and a format reward that checks whether the format is right—the model is required to put its thinking process inside a think tag and its answer inside an answer tag. They deliberately avoided a process reward model or an outcome reward model, because a neural-network reward model gets reward hacked: the model finds the flaw in the reward function, for instance discovering that outputting lots of emoji scores high, and then outputs emoji like crazy. Using a ground truth reward avoids any possibility of reward hacking.

— Pan Jiayi
1:06:43

The performance gain comes from output getting 20 times longer

Figure 2 shows R1's accuracy on AIME climbing from the teens all the way to around 70%, and after pausing for ten thousand steps roughly reaching O1's range. But Figure 3 is what explains why: during reinforcement learning the model discovers on its own that if it thinks more at inference time, generates more tokens and uses more compute, performance improves. At step 0 the output is only a few hundred tokens; after 8000 steps the average response length reaches about 10k tokens, nearly a 20-fold increase. That's where test-time scaling comes from.

— Pan Jiayi
1:28:51

Distillation beats having a small model do its own RL

DeepSeek open-sourced a whole series of distilled models from 1.5B to 70B in one go. The 1.5B distilled model already beats GPT-4o or Claude Sonnet on AIME by 10 to 20 percentage points. More critical is the comparison in Table 6: distilling R1 into Qwen 32B far exceeds Qwen 32B doing reinforcement learning on its own the R1-Zero way. The reason is that a large model is more likely to explore complex and beneficial reasoning patterns, and handing those over to a small model after exploring is much better than having the small model explore from scratch.

— Pan Jiayi
1:35:27

Both PRM and MCTS turn out to be dead ends

DeepSeek publicly disclosed its failed attempts. The problems with process reward models: on general tasks it's very hard to define which step is the first and which is the second; judging whether the current step is right is also very difficult, and once the model can correct itself it gets even murkier; automatic training results are unsatisfactory, while human annotation is too expensive and doesn't scale; and as long as there's a reward model there will inevitably be reward hacking. The problems with MCTS: the search space at each step of a language model is about 10k words, the tree can be 10k layers deep, the search space is too large and the cost too high; the value function is unstable and hard to estimate. AlphaGo's success is hard to reproduce in general language model reasoning settings.

— Pan Jiayi
1:43:50

R1's training cost may be only 100k USD

A rough estimate: the whole training run used about ten thousand steps of reinforcement learning, about 1000 responses per step, each response at most 10k tokens, generating about 100 billion tokens in total. At R1's API price of 2.2 USD per million tokens, those tokens come to just over 200k USD. Considering that DeepSeek itself makes a large margin, and ignoring part of the model training overhead, the order of magnitude shouldn't be wrong. The conclusion is that if optimized well enough it might take just 100k USD, and at the very most 1M USD.

— Pan Jiayi
1:59:40

A chain-of-thought reward model cuts the error rate from 15% to 1.5%

K1.5 tried two methods for the math reward. The traditional reward model hooks the model's activations into a small MLP to predict right or wrong directly, with accuracy around 84.4%. The chain-of-thought reward model instead takes the question, the reference answer and the model's answer together as input, and has the reward model output a chain of thought of its own to reason about right or wrong, fine-tuned on 800k labeled samples. Accuracy goes straight to 98.5%, cutting the error rate from 15% to 1.5%. This isn't in DeepSeek's report, and everyone should be moving in this direction going forward.

— Pan Jiayi
2:17:50

Without negative gradients, reinforcement learning doesn't take off

K1.5 compared against a simpler REST algorithm: sample 100 times, pick out the correct ones and fine-tune on them, done. Its difference from GRPO is that it doesn't require staying close enough to the original policy, and it doesn't tell the model not to learn the bad stuff—it's missing the negative gradient step. The result is that REST performs considerably worse, with test time scaling not taking off at all, maybe gaining only three or four points; whereas the reinforcement learning algorithms of K1.5 or R1 can gain ten or twenty points, a world of difference.

Glossary

GRPO / Group Relative Policy Optimization
A PPO variant proposed by DeepSeek that drops the value function, does policy gradient only, and normalizes using relative good-versus-bad within a group.
reward hacking
The model finds the flaw in the reward function and behaves in ways that score high but that people don't want, such as outputting emoji like crazy.
PRM / process reward model
Scores each step of the reasoning rather than only the final result, but on general tasks the steps are hard to define and it's easily hacked.
MCTS / Monte Carlo tree search
Succeeded in AlphaGo, but in language model reasoning the search space is too large and the value function hard to estimate.
distillation
Fine-tuning a small model on data generated by a large model so the small model acquires reasoning ability, which works better than the small model doing its own reinforcement learning.
curriculum sampling
A K1.5 technique that gives the model problems whose difficulty matches its ability, so it learns faster and better.
