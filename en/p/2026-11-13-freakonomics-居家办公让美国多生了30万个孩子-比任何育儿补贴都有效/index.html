Remote work generated 300,000 additional American births, more effective than any child subsidy

Government cash cannot reliably drive birth rates up, but remote work—a free side effect—has demonstrably raised American fertility. When both spouses work from home one day a week or more, fertility rises from 1.7 to 2.2.

Half of 30-year-old women remain childless: decline, not delay

Economist Melissa Carney points out that American total fertility has fallen since 2007 and has not rebounded as before. The key evidence is cohort fertility: 50% of 30-year-old women have not yet borne a first child. To reach the previous level where only 10-15% of women remained childless by age 44 would require an ‘impossible’ surge in fertility after age 30. This shows real fertility decline, not merely deferred childbearing.

— Melissa Carney
16:29

Unmet fertility desire causes psychological harm, not fiscal crisis

Demographer Lyman Stone argues that the true cost of low fertility is not the solvency of social security, but the psychological pain of people reaching middle age without the family they wanted. He cites IVF data: those who succeed show no significant change in mental health, while those who fail or experience prolonged attempts show increased use of antidepressants and antipsychotics. He calls this a ‘heart math problem’ rather than a budget math problem.

— Lyman Stone
28:59

Declining marriage stems from broken matching, not changed values

Stone argues that the largest single factor in fertility decline over the past 25 years is declining marriage rates. When controlling for marriage age, fertility has barely fallen. Marriage is fundamentally a matching problem, requiring ‘burning through many low-quality matches’ to find the right person. Smartphones have slashed face-to-face social time for teenagers and young adults—from about 10 hours per week to less than 2—breaking the matching mechanism.

— Lyman Stone
33:05

Subsidizing births to replacement would cost $150,000 per child

Stone's meta-analysis shows that cash increases fertility—not just the timing of births—but the cost is staggering. To raise American fertility to replacement level through cash alone would require checks of $150,000 per newborn, equal to about 4% of GDP. He proposes an untested alternative discussed in Finland: Valvo Sampo, which deposits $5,000 to $10,000 at birth into a stock account, to be withdrawn with accumulated returns in adulthood, requiring only 0.4% of GDP for equivalent effect.

— Lyman Stone
42:45

Remote work's fertility effect operates through logistics, not biology

Stanford economist Nick Bloom's cross-national research finds that when both spouses work from home at least one day per week, American families have about half a child more. With zero remote work, predicted fertility is 1.7; with both spouses remote two days, it rises to about 2.2. The effect comes from ‘difficulty of childbearing’ rather than ‘likelihood of conception’—it shows up in second and third births rather than first births, indicating relief from childcare burden rather than ‘easier pregnancy at home’.

— Nick Bloom
44:50

Nationalist governments block remote work, the only proven free fertility policy

Bloom identifies a political irony: the current American government pushes nationalist-style fertility agendas while suppressing remote work—the one proven, free, and effective policy lever for raising fertility. His research estimates remote work accounts for about 300,000 additional American births per year, roughly 8% of total births nationwide.

— Nick Bloom
50:09

Government mortgages drove Depression-era births: 305 babies per 1,000 loans

Carney and Lisa Dettling's research shows that the low-down-payment, fixed-rate long-term mortgages enabled by the 1934 Housing Act and 1944 GI Bill substantially increased homeownership among 18-44-year-olds and drove the baby boom. Each additional 1,000 government-backed mortgages in a state one year led to 305 more births the next year. This causal relationship was validated through instrumental variable methods isolating the housing supply side, not mere correlation.

— Melissa Carney
51:23

The baby boom's housing dividend accrued only to white families

Carney uses redlining as a falsification test: since government-backed mortgages historically went only to white families buying in all-white neighborhoods, the housing-fertility causal chain should produce no equivalent association in non-white births. The data confirms this—the mortgage-to-next-year-births association is almost entirely driven by white women's fertility. Meanwhile, homeownership among young adults has fallen from about 40% after the Great Recession to about 30%, the opposite trajectory of the baby boom era.

— Melissa Carney

Glossary

fertility gap
The gap between desired fertility (children wanted) and actual fertility (children born).
tempo effect
A change in the timing of births that does not affect total lifetime fertility.
instrumental variable strategy
A statistical method that isolates causal relationships by using exogenous variation to remove confounding factors.
redlining
The historical U.S. practice of denying loans and investment to neighborhoods based on racial composition.
