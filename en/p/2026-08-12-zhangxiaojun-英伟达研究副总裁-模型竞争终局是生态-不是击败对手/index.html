Nvidia's VP of Research: The Endgame for Models Is an Ecosystem, Not Beating Your Rivals

Liu Mingyu: Model capabilities will eventually converge; Nvidia's open-source world model isn't about competition, it's about paving the road. The GPT moment for physical AI hasn't arrived, but the conditions are converging.

At Nvidia, the world model is a product, not a research project

Liu Mingyu describes his title as VP of Research and VP of Cosmos Lab, with a direct team of about 80 to 90 people, and 200 to 300 people across the company working on Cosmos-related efforts. He is the owner of the entire mission, responsible for setting direction. He stresses that this is a very major project inside Nvidia, and that the headcount is because "producing good results" requires that kind of resource. It also shows that the world model has been elevated to an unprecedented level inside Nvidia — not just a research project, but an engineering team with a clear product mission.

1:24:21

A manager's recognition must align with the company's interests

Liu Mingyu says he was promoted a level every year in his first few years at Nvidia, and may be the first person to go from researcher to VP. The reason is that he joined Nvidia early, and when there was no AI talent yet, he helped the company build AI community visibility and explain how AI would affect the company, while also loving to ship — earning trust step by step. Today the team he leads spends several million dollars a day on compute, and the company's willingness to hand him that scale is the result of long-term alignment. He stresses that aligning with the company is very important: a pure researcher wants recognition, but a manager's recognition must align with the company's interests.

1:41:09

After Sora came out, convincing Jensen Huang wasn't hard

Cosmos was decided around March 2024, right after Sora. Liu Mingyu says that before Sora, convincing Jensen Huang was harder; after seeing Sora, it wasn't hard, because Jensen Huang immediately saw the impact on the company. He and a group of generative-model researchers wrote Jensen Huang an email asking to build the world model. Jensen Huang gathered everyone for a meeting and asked each person to go back and think of a product name. Liu Mingyu thought Jensen Huang would come up with a better name and didn't want to waste effort, but Jensen Huang insisted he think about it, because the name is tied to the product positioning. In the end they settled on Cosmos, with the goal not of creation but of solving problems in the physical world.

— Ming-Yu Liu
1:54:47

22 models isn't rich choice, it's slower iteration

In the Cosmos 1 era, Liu Mingyu estimated on his own that satisfying various customer needs might require 22 models. He reported that number in a meeting with Jensen Huang, who responded with the Costco example: when you reduce the number of items on display, sales actually go up, because too much choice confuses consumers. Liu Mingyu also found that he himself couldn't tell when to use A and when to use B, let alone outside users. At the same time, every model needs maintenance, and the more models there are, the slower iteration becomes. So Cosmos 2 to 3 was a big gap: converging from multiple models into one unified world model that combines understanding, prediction, generation and action all together.

— Ming-Yu Liu
2:08:22

The dual tower is only a stage; one tower is enough

Cosmos 3 uses a dual-tower structure: one tower handles discrete information for understanding, the other handles continuous information for generation. Liu Mingyu explains this is a very reasonable choice at this stage, because separating discrete reasoning from continuous generation lets customers do post-training without interference. If everything were connected, a customer adjusting the generation distribution could break understanding. But he says directly that the next goal is simpler: "Why two towers? One tower is enough." They are moving toward a single tower, but must also consider whether it's easy for users to pick up — you can't sacrifice usability for engineering perfection. The final architecture isn't settled yet; it depends on which has better deployment cost.

— Ming-Yu Liu
2:31:45

People who fear mistakes won't make decisions, so give them room to fail

The Cosmos 3 paper has more than 290 authors, fewer than 300. Liu Mingyu believes the core tension in a large team is: you need everyone aligned on the goal, and you need everyone able to make decisions autonomously. If only a few people decide, progress is slow; if everyone decides but goals aren't aligned, it diverges or even turns into horse-racing. His approach is to put a lot of thought into designing how the team works together, making information transparent so everyone has enough information to make the right decision, while also providing room for error, because people who fear mistakes won't dare to decide. He also stresses that before the paper came out, he told the main leaders clearly what the model would look like, so everyone saw the same vision — like imagining the ball going through the net before a basketball free throw.

— Ming-Yu Liu
2:52:41

Not laying people off is slower than layoffs, but it buys employees who speak up

Liu Mingyu says Nvidia has no forced ranking and doesn't lay people off. In his ten years at Nvidia he has never heard of a layoff. He believes that only when employees feel safe and trust the company will they say the things they wouldn't dare say where they fear being cut; if everyone is scrambling to stand out, it's actually harder to unite and cooperate. The downside is that when a technology matures, employees need time to learn new things and transition, which is slower than simply cutting and hiring new people. But what the company gets is employee trust and unity. Jensen Huang has seen so many ups and downs in Silicon Valley that choosing this model is a deliberate decision. Nvidia runs a marathon, relying on laying groundwork early — CUDA, for example, was laid out ten years in advance.

— Ming-Yu Liu
3:07:40

Being behind isn't about conditions; nothing was ever fair

Liu Mingyu describes Jensen Huang as a "hexagonal warrior": he can go deep into register-level computer architecture details and also talk marketing and pricing — all-around. He recalls once explaining being behind a competitor by saying "the setting isn't fair," and Jensen Huang said directly, "Are you kidding me? Nothing is actually fair" — making him understand that research means creating the conditions for the goal, not finding excuses. Jensen Huang's management core is "Mission is the boss": the company is like an organism, and when it needs to move in a direction, the skills of different teams automatically align. He also mentions that Jensen Huang reads a huge number of emails every day, looking for signals and making priority judgments — all of which has influenced his own current working style of switching topics every half hour.

Glossary

Physical AI
AI that can perceive the physical world and take actions that change the state of the world, such as robots and autonomous driving.
World Foundation Model
Cosmos's positioning for itself: providing a more foundational model for all physical AI applications.
Multimodal Transformer
A Transformer architecture that uniformly handles text, video, audio and action signals; the core of Cosmos 3.
Dual-tower
A dual-tower structure that processes discrete understanding signals and continuous generation signals separately; Cosmos 3's stage-specific choice.
