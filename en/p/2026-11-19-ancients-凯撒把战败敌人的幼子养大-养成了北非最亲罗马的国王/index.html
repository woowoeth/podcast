Caesar raised his enemy's young son into Rome's closest North African king

Paraded as a war captive at age two, Juba II was adopted into Caesar's family, raised in Rome, eventually placed by Augustus on the Mauretanian throne, and ruled for forty years without ever drawing Roman suspicion.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

9:09

War captives' children were normally executed, not adopted as family

Juba I sided with Pompey against Caesar, was defeated, and took his own life. His two-year-old son Juba was paraded through Caesar's triumph as a war captive. Standard practice would have enslaved, exiled, or executed him—as happened to Cleopatra's sister and some Celtic chieftains. Instead, Caesar or his great-niece Octavia adopted the boy. Raised in Rome and given the formal name Gaius Julius Juba, he received elite education alongside the future Augustus and Tiberius.

13:11

Caesar adopted his dead enemy's son as the ultimate humiliation

Roman convention dictated that keeping a subject kingdom's heir as hostage ensured future loyalty when returned to rule. But Numidia had already been absorbed into a Roman province—there was no crown to send him back to inherit. Andrew favors a different theory: Juba I had once physically clashed with Caesar in a Senate diplomatic encounter. By adopting his son, Caesar inflicted the ultimate revenge—transforming enemy blood into a vessel of loyalty, erasing the father through the son.

— Andrew
19:22

Augustus crowned Juba king of a realm wholly foreign to him

Juba's ancestors ruled Numidia, in what is now Algeria. Augustus, however, granted him Mauretania—today's Morocco and Algerian coast—a wholly different kingdom, vacant through lack of heirs. Juba had no bloodline connection to this land. Almost immediately, local Berber tribes rebelled, likely because they resented an outsider imposed as their king.

— Andrew
26:41

Ten days' sail from Rome allowed Juba to honor his enemy father

In his new capital Caesarea, Juba erected statues to Augustus, Cleopatra VII, and his own father Juba I—Caesar's enemy. Such duality would have been unthinkable in Rome itself. Andrew attributes this to two factors: the ten-day ocean voyage between Caesarea and Rome ('out of sight, out of mind') and Juba's urgent need to establish historical legitimacy for this new, rootless dynasty by deliberately evoking his dead predecessor.

— Andrew
33:54

Juba sought the Nile's source and discovered a Christmas flower instead

Juba dispatched explorers into the Atlas Mountains searching for the Nile's source—if located within his borders, it would geographically anchor Mauretania to Egypt. They found only a crocodile-inhabited lake, which Juba mistakenly identified as the Nile's true source. The same expedition discovered a plant with toxic sap. Juba named it Euphorbia in honor of his attending physician. That genus survives to this day; its most famous member is the Christmas poinsettia.

— Andrew
34:54

The Canary Islands were named for dogs, not for the birds

Juba's explorers sailed westward into the Atlantic and encountered a chain of islands. Because of the large dogs (Latin: canis) inhabiting them, they named the archipelago the ‘Island of Dogs’—which evolved into the modern Canary Islands (Canarias), etymologically unrelated to the bird. This discovery remains Juba's most widely recognized and enduring legacy.

— Andrew
38:58

Forty years of historical silence proves Juba was a competent ruler

Juba ruled for forty years and died at nearly seventy. Rome habitually eliminated unreliable border vassals, yet his historical record contains almost no negative mentions until the Tacfarinas rebellion. Andrew argues this silence itself is eloquent: a ruler whose administration was so stable and smooth that it generated no scandal, no crisis, and therefore no written record. Paradoxically, the absence of history is evidence of genuine competence.

— Andrew
46:12

Coins exposed Juba's deeper role in Tacfarinas's defeat than texts admit

The Tacfarinas rebellion lasted ten years and earned the epithet ‘desert Hydra’ for its seeming invincibility. Roman texts attribute its suppression almost entirely to Roman generals and the later ruler Ptolemy, with Juba mentioned only in passing. Yet Andrew notes that Juba minted coins during this period bearing Nike (victory goddess) imagery—symbols of military achievement—and their dates often align precisely with moments when Romans claimed to have defeated Tacfarinas. The coins reveal Juba's actual involvement was substantially deeper than the texts concede.

— Andrew
