From 2 pounds a year to 90: how slavery and marketing conquered the human diet

Over two centuries, sugar pushed English per-capita consumption from 2 pounds to 90 pounds—not from natural taste evolution, but from the convergence of enslaved labor, industrialization, and food marketing that built the modern diet.

17:07

Sugar industry relied on enslaved labor from its very beginning

After Columbus's second voyage brought sugarcane to the New World, the indigenous labor force was already decimated by killing and disease. Spanish colonizers began importing African slaves starting in 1503, which directly transformed Caribbean sugar's trajectory. The episode emphasizes: sugar as a global commodity was bound to slavery from the outset—not a problem added later, but the industry's origin point itself.

— Erin Welsh
19:08

One 166-square-mile island consumed 250,000 human lives

After Britain took Barbados in 1625, the 166-square-mile island imported 252,500 enslaved Africans between 1701 and 1810—yet Barbados's entire population in 2023 is only 282,000. French Saint-Domingue in 1789 had 32,000 whites ruling roughly 500,000 enslaved people; years later rebellion erupted, triggering Haitian independence. These numbers show sugar's profit scale and humanitarian cost were equally staggering.

— Erin Welsh
23:17

Sugarcane never escaped forced labor even after abolition

The British West Indies abolished slavery in 1834, French colonies in 1848, the U.S. in 1865—but Cuba not until 1886, Brazil not until 1888, while Cuba supplied 35 to 40 percent of global sugar during 1840–1870. After abolition, freed workers often had nowhere to go; planters kept wages low or imported ultra-cheap labor. Sugar's dependence on coerced work did not vanish with the law.

— Erin Welsh
24:18

Sugar beet's rise took nearly a century to consolidate

Prussian chemist Andreas Marggraf discovered how to extract sucrose from sugar beets as early as 1747, but the technology went unpromoted for decades until France lost Saint-Domingue and urgently needed an alternative sugar source—only then did the sugar beet industry truly take off. In 1840, 95 percent of global sugar came from sugarcane; by 1890, sugar beets had captured two-thirds. The turning point came largely because beets didn't depend on imports or slave labor—they were cheaper and safer.

— Erin Welsh
28:25

Sugar transformed from rare medicine to 90 pounds annually

English per-capita annual sugar consumption was 2 pounds in 1670, 12 pounds by 1730, 24 pounds by 1800, and 90 pounds by 1900—a 45-fold increase over two centuries. More striking: America today's estimates range as low as 75 pounds and as high as 130 pounds; these figures mostly come from sales data and likely undercount the vast amount of added sugar hidden in processed food rather than bought directly.

— Erin Welsh
38:48

Once sugar became universal, eating less became the elite preserve

Sugar was once a wealth display: banquets featured sculptures made from 73,000 pounds of sugar to show off. But once sugar became universally available, eating it no longer conveyed status—instead, eating *less* sugar became the rich person's privilege, while excessive consumption became linked to the poorest. This reversal shows sugar's social meaning completely flipped with supply, not fixed.

— Erin Welsh
46:02

One acre of sugarcane outproduces beef in calories by far

One acre of prime subtropical land can produce over 8 million calories of sugar—the same calories require over 4 acres of potatoes, 9 to 12 acres of wheat, or 135+ acres devoted to raising cattle. This efficiency gap explains why sugar became the cheapest calorie source during industrialization and urbanization, and why it got packed into processed food.

— Erin Welsh
49:03

Household sugar halved while industrial sugar more than tripled

Between 1909 and 1971, sugar Americans bought directly for home use fell from 52.1 pounds per capita to 24.7 pounds—cut almost in half—while industrial sugar in food and beverages exploded from 19.3 to 70.2 pounds per capita. Consumers appeared to actively choose less sugar, yet total added-sugar intake surged; this was an illusion of choice.

Glossary

humoral theory
Ancient medical theory holding that health depends on balance among four bodily humors, with sugar considered heating and moist in nature.
sugar beet
A root crop that became the second major source for extracting sucrose alongside sugarcane starting in the 19th century.
high-fructose corn syrup
A cheap sugar substitute that emerged after U.S. corn subsidies in the 1970s and is now ubiquitous in processed foods.
