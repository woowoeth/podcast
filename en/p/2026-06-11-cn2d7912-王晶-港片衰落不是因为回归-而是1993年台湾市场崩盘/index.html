Hong Kong cinema's decline was caused by Taiwan's 1993 market crash, not the handover

Hong Kong's film decline is commonly blamed on the 1997 handover and content restrictions, but Wong Jing identifies the real turning point in 1993: when Hong Kong filmmakers forced Taiwan to open its market to Hollywood films, they inadvertently destroyed the Taiwan market that had sustained their entire industry.

Organized crime was priced into the cost of doing business

Wong Jing described how organized crime in Hong Kong operated by a simple rule: if you paid the going rate for protection, no one bothered you. Street vendors factored this fee into their operating costs just as supermarkets factor in shoplifting losses. Even at the height of organized crime's influence on the film industry, ordinary citizens reported strong personal safety—Hong Kong consistently ranked among the world's safest cities. The penetration of organized crime into commerce and its impact on ordinary people's lives were entirely separate phenomena.

— Wang Jing
41:47

Hong Kong's film boom was driven by Taiwan's supply shortage

Wong Jing explained the real driver of Hong Kong's film explosion: Taiwan's rules allowed only Hong Kong and Taiwan films, expanding theaters from two to five screens, requiring 200 films annually. Taiwan's domestic industry produced at most 30, leaving 170 to be supplied by Hong Kong. This massive demand drove bidding wars for actors—Liu Dehua commanded 5 million yuan minimum, Chow Yun-fat 8 million. Actor fees and production volume were directly sustained by the Taiwan market, not by Hong Kong's domestic entertainment consumption.

— Wang Jing
1:21:27

He switched to directing because his pregnant wife was running out of money

Wong Jing said he moved from screenwriting to directing at Shaw Brothers because after marriage and buying a house, his savings ran dry just as his wife entered her ninth month of pregnancy. Shaw Brothers offered him a contract—the executive literally parked his sports car under the TVB building and had him sign the contract on the hood—solving his immediate need for delivery expenses. He turned down a more prestigious TV production manager position at TVB simply because Shaw was paying significantly more.

— Wang Jing
1:49:49

Taiwan gangsters divided up stars' schedules month by month

Wong Jing described how during Taiwan's film boom, a few major triad bosses would meet for drinks each January and directly allocate the year's top actors' schedules—January through November—leaving performers with zero autonomy. Liu Dehua's manager confirmed being threatened at gunpoint during negotiations, ultimately settling on a "thirteen days three months from now" compromise. This semi-coercive, semi-negotiated operating model was routine, not an extreme outlier.

— Wang Jing
2:02:27

Wong Jing signed Shu Qi from a magazine photo but initially cast her wrong

Unable to find the right actress, Wong Jing cast Shu Qi on sight from a magazine photo, signing her to six films in a single day. But he was too busy to brief her; on the first day of shooting, they met for the first time and he realized her styling and role assignment were misaligned—starting with the third film, he corrected course. The additional complication: Shu Qi had previously been in a Taiwan film where a director and actor unilaterally rewrote her scenes to add explicit content. Wong Jing's team backed her legal challenge on grounds that her signature as a minor was invalid—she won—and the other party even tried to intimidate her through organized crime channels, though actual confrontation never materialized.

— Wang Jing
2:21:22

The government's 8 million subsidy for first films—nine out of ten directors vanished

Wong Jing said Hong Kong's First Feature Film Scheme offered new directors 8 million Hong Kong dollars to make their debut, with no repayment required whether the film made or lost money. Many treated it as a shortcut to wealth: pocket 2 million, use the rest to shoot something sloppy, and collect whatever the box office returned. He estimates roughly ninety percent of first-time feature directors disappeared after their film, arguing this kind of government support doesn't actually help the film industry—markets should solve it themselves.

— Wang Jing
3:09:00

Hong Kong cinema's real collapse was 1993's Taiwan market crash, not the handover

Wong Jing pinpointed the turning point in 1993: a Taiwan company spent 30 million Hong Kong dollars buying out a Stephen Chow contract and lost heavily. The owner demanded Hong Kong film fees be capped at just over 20 million yuan. When Hong Kong filmmakers went to Taiwan to negotiate, they misread the situation entirely. They collectively demanded that Hollywood's import quota be relaxed instead. The result: Hollywood completely took over the market. The Taiwan market vanished entirely. Wong Jing argues this directly killed the Hong Kong film industry far more than cultural differences or post-handover content restrictions.

— Wang Jing
3:20:08

AI can replace eighty percent of acting, but never genuine emotion

When asked about AI-generated film and performance, Wong Jing was blunt: AI can replace eighty percent of acting at best. Real nuance and genuine emotion have no substitute—unless a truly great actor is willing to sell their entire career's performance data to train the AI. He compared this to how film and television never eliminated Beijing opera: technological iteration replaces some forms, but certain things only real people can convey always hit a boundary beyond which replacement isn't possible.

— Wang Jing
