Baseball's stadium music isn't accompaniment — it's how fans take the game back

Baseball's official historian thinks stadium music is too loud, but it was fans singing Hey Hey Goodbye in unison that rattled the opposing pitcher — music turns spectators into part of the game.

One walk-up song can turn a passerby into a fan

Host Randa Abdel Fattah says she was indifferent to baseball for most of her life, until Mets pitcher Edwin Diaz's walk-up song Narco in 2022 changed that. Diaz is a closer, usually entering at a key moment in the late innings, and the team uses his walk-up song to announce to the opponent: the show's over, this one's ours. The entrance video went viral, the team started handing out plastic trumpets to fans, the mascots Mr. and Mrs. Met pretended to play trumpets, and eventually the team flew the original artist, Timmy Trumpet, in from Australia to perform live. The host says that if you were in the stands at that moment, the feeling was electric.

— Randa Abdel Fattah
6:18

The official historian thinks the ballpark is too loud

MLB's official historian John Thorne says plainly that he doesn't like entrance music. What he misses is the quiet between half-innings — quiet that lets other sounds ring louder and clearer: the strange sound of a bat hitting a baseball, the same sound when Babe Ruth hit, and when Josh Gibson hit; the dull thud of a leather glove catching a fly ball; a coach yelling at a player to keep running and steal second. He thinks nonstop audio, the endless contest for fans' attention, may have robbed a kid who goes to the ballpark to dream of that right.

— John Thorne
10:31

Take Me Out was originally a women's suffrage song

The seventh inning stretch was originally literal: stand up, move your legs, get the blood flowing. It became a musical moment thanks to a 1908 song, Take Me Out to the Ball Game, which was actually about women's suffrage. The protagonist of the original lyrics is Katie Casey, a baseball fan who knows all the players' names, gets upset when the umpire makes a bad call, and when the score is tight knows exactly what to do — she gets the whole crowd to sing along. From this came a whole new genre, the "baseball song," which flooded sheet music, cylinder records and 78s in the 1910s.

— John Thorne
12:35

The 1910s baseball song boom was publishers, not fans

John Thorne points out that the wave of baseball music in the 1910s was less about fans liking it than about publishers liking it — it was essentially a marketing tool to pull people into the ballpark. The evidence is the number of copycats: George M. Cohan released Take Your Girl to the Ball Game almost the same week, followed by imitations like I Want to Go to the Ball Game. But despite all the baseball songs Tin Pan Alley produced, large-scale singing in actual ballparks remained rare, and John says the main reason was technical: sound systems were too primitive, and before radio took hold, a brass band was still the best accompaniment.

— John Thorne
17:47

That 1977 singalong broke the opposing pitcher

On a muggy July night in Chicago in 1977, the White Sox were down two runs to the Kansas City Royals. After the White Sox put runners on first and second, someone in the stands started singing Hey Hey Goodbye, it grew louder, more than 45,000 fans joined in, and the announcers had to raise their voices to be heard over the singing. The White Sox then erupted — bases loaded, bases cleared, four runs in — and the other side was forced to change pitchers. As the pitcher walked off the mound, Chicago fans "saw him off" even louder. Nancy Faust says she was thinking, "Did I hear that right?" because this wasn't the kind of thing you normally heard at a ballpark — it was more like mockery.

— Nancy Faust
23:51

She gave players their songs; fans fed her ideas

In her first years with the White Sox, Nancy Faust still played a set repertoire: the national anthem, Take Me Out to the Ball Game, and the hour when the crowd filed in. In her third year she started improvising, playing whatever she saw. When Dick Allen came up, the crowd would leave the bathrooms and the popcorn stands, so she played the then-popular Jesus Christ Superstar, and when he hit a home run the place went crazy. Fans sitting around her booth started feeding her ideas: one player had worked at a watch counter, the store closed and he got stuck there, so when he came up she played Does Anybody Really Know What Time It Is. She says she was "the soundtrack to whatever was happening," not just scoring the players.

— Nancy Faust
25:52

An opposing pitcher admitted the singing shook his confidence

After that 1977 singalong, one opposing pitcher told reporters: you're standing on the mound, there are men on base, Zisk is in the batter's box, thirty thousand people are singing Kiss It Goodbye — that shakes your confidence. Nancy Faust later read in a book that players recalling those years said they hated hearing that sound, and flinched when they heard na-na-goodbye. And White Sox slugger Richie Zisk said of those same fans: I don't know if they came to see us or we came to see them, but I can tell you it fired us up. Nancy's conclusion: I guess I did have an impact, I just didn't know it at the time.

— Nancy Faust
28:56

She can't read music — it's all by ear

Every song Nancy Faust improvised over her career, she did by ear — she can't read music. Hum a few bars of anything and within seconds she'd play it back on the organ. She retired from the White Sox organist job in 2010, White Sox fan and former President Barack Obama personally wrote to congratulate her on her career, and the booth she occupied for decades is now named after her. She still occasionally comes back for special occasions, like Pope Night, playing Gotta Have Faith and Don't Stop Believing.

— Randa Abdel Fattah

Glossary

walk-up song
A short music clip played in the ballpark when a player comes up, usually chosen by the player himself.
seventh inning stretch
A break around the seventh inning of a baseball game when spectators stand up and move around, later evolving into a singalong moment.
Tin Pan Alley
The cluster of music publishers in early-20th-century New York, shorthand for the popular song industry.
closer
The pitcher on a baseball team who comes in to finish the game at a key moment in the late innings.
