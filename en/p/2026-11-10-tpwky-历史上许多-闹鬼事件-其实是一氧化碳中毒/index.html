Many historical hauntings were actually carbon monoxide poisoning

Carbon monoxide not only outcompetes oxygen for hemoglobin, but also prevents already-bound oxygen from being released — this dual mechanism produces symptoms so bizarre they were historically mistaken for hauntings or mental illness.

Carbon monoxide poisoning is often misdiagnosed as hauntings or psychiatric illness

A Reddit user received sticky notes in unfamiliar handwriting reminding them to do tasks, yet had no memory of writing them, plus severe headaches. Someone suggested not a ghost or the landlord, but CO poisoning causing confusion and memory loss. A detector read 100 ppm. The hosts stress that CO poisoning's worst aspect is that the brain doesn't suspect it immediately—instead it invents other explanations.

12:40

Hemoglobin's affinity for carbon monoxide is over 200 times that of oxygen

Oxygen depends on hemoglobin as a "transport truck" throughout the body. CO outcompetes oxygen for binding—with over 200 times higher affinity. Worse, CO left-shifts the oxygen dissociation curve, making hemoglobin reluctant to release already-bound oxygen. Dual strike: hemoglobin binds less oxygen and releases less oxygen. Tissues become hypoxic.

25:55

Between 10 and 40 percent of CO survivors develop delayed neuropsychiatric symptoms

Between 10 and 40 percent of CO survivors develop delayed neuropsychiatric symptoms: depression, Parkinsonian features, dementia, psychosis. Medicine doesn't know who gets it, why, or whether dose or duration matters. This is why emergency departments often misdiagnose chronic CO exposure as a psychiatric episode.

— Erin Allman Updike
26:58

The only treatment for carbon monoxide poisoning is oxygen

No drug can metabolize carboxyhemoglobin; the only treatment is high-concentration oxygen to displace CO from hemoglobin. At normal oxygen levels, CO has a 4-6 hour half-life in blood; pure oxygen (100%) reduces this to 40-80 minutes; hyperbaric oxygen further reduces it to 15-30 minutes. However, not all hospitals have hyperbaric facilities.

— Erin Allman Updike
41:18

Science magazine exposed a Boston haunting as CO poisoning in 1913

MIT researcher Franz Schneider Jr. was asked to investigate a Boston "haunted" apartment. Residents felt held down at night, paralyzed, saw hallucinations; children were lethargic by day and hard to wake; previous tenants reported identical symptoms—apparently proving the house was cursed. Schneider found an "extremely defective hot air furnace" leaking CO. Nearly identical cases appeared in a 1921 ophthalmology journal article.

46:23

Coal mine canaries detected danger 20 minutes before human collapse

Birds' respiratory systems extract oxygen far more efficiently than mammals, making them highly sensitive to gaseous toxins like CO. They fell from their perches about 20 minutes before humans lost consciousness. After a Welsh mining disaster in 1894, J.S. Haldane formally recommended deploying canaries in mines, though people had used them since the early 1800s. Miners had deep affection for the birds and maintained specialized revival boxes. The practice continued until the 1980s, when electronic detectors replaced them.

51:40

Intentional US CO deaths outnumber accidental deaths by 5 to 10 times

The US experiences approximately 600 accidental CO deaths per year. Intentional deaths—predominantly suicide—are 5 to 10 times higher, reaching up to 15,000 annually. A 2017 study estimated roughly 50,000 CO poisoning cases in the US yearly, with a 1-3% mortality rate. Globally, low- and middle-income countries see significantly higher rates of CO poisoning and death because coal and wood don't burn completely.

— Erin Allman Updike
54:48

The body naturally produces carbon monoxide; now scientists want it as medicine

Normal hemoglobin metabolism produces trace amounts of CO naturally. Scientists realized low-dose CO might work like nitric oxide—acting as a vasodilator, regulating inflammation, possibly functioning as a neurotransmitter. Slow-inhalation formulations are now in phase 1 clinical trials for vascular and inflammatory diseases. But administration methods and precise targeting remain in early-stage exploration.

— Erin Allman Updike

Glossary

carboxyhemoglobin
Hemoglobin bound to carbon monoxide; its concentration is the diagnostic indicator for CO poisoning.
hyperbaric oxygen
Treatment in which patients breathe pure oxygen at pressure above one atmosphere to accelerate clearing CO from the body.
