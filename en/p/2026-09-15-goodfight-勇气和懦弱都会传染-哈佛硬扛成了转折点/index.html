Courage and Cowardice Are Both Contagious, and Harvard's Refusal Became the Turning Point

Courage is not an individual trait but a social phenomenon: people look at what those around them do. The biggest change in Trump's second term is that the people around him went from resistance to surrender, and Harvard's refusal to sign a deal made resistance possible again.

6:30

Courage is a social phenomenon, not an individual trait

Weisberg's core argument is that courage and cowardice are both contagious and highly situation-dependent. He does not believe human nature changed dramatically between 2016 and 2024; what changed is the signals people receive from those around them. Trump's second term is more dangerous partly because he has firmer control over the government and his own team. In the first term there were still large numbers of officials he had appointed resisting from the inside; in the second term there are almost none. When people look around, what they see is not others standing up but others giving way.

— Jacob Weisberg
10:15

The courage of 2016 was actually going along with the crowd

Mounk offers a demystifying reading: the people who looked brave in 2016 were in fact following the social incentives of their own small circles. At the time the political incentive was to accommodate Trump, but the social and cultural incentives of their circles were to oppose him, so people stood up. Eight years later those social and cultural incentives have flipped, and the people who looked brave back then are no longer brave. Weisberg agrees with this mechanism but adds that you cannot entirely erase the element of individual moral choice — it is just that true moral heroes, like Mandela or Havel, are extremely rare.

— Yascha Mounk
17:45

Brad Karp surrendered in five days

Paul Weiss is the law firm most deeply bound to the American liberal legal tradition, involved in civil rights cases since Brown v. Board of Education, and Karp himself was recruited by Ted Sorensen. Trump threatened to bar the firm's lawyers from government buildings, revoke their security clearances, and sue them on discrimination charges. Weisberg points out that all of this was illegal, that the firm had every right to represent its clients, and that if it sought an injunction it would almost certainly have won — firms like Jenner & Block that chose to fight won quickly. But Karp chose to negotiate at the White House, reaching a deal five days after the executive order, promising free pro bono services, abandoning discrimination, and apologizing for things it had never done. It was a very public, very rapid surrender that sent a signal to the entire legal profession.

— Jacob Weisberg
24:30

Megyn Kelly went from being bullied to being a bully

In a 2015 debate Kelly challenged Trump over his comments insulting women, and Trump set Steve Bannon and others on her with threats; she needed full-time security, her children needed security, and people showed up in her building lobby to intimidate her. She held out for a while, then reached a truce with Trump. Weisberg's psychological explanation is this: Kelly wrote in her memoir about being bullied as a child, and she vowed never to bully and never to side with bullies, which explains why she fought Trump so fiercely back then. But the tragedy is that she herself became a bully; her show now spends every day viciously mocking transgender people, gay people, and minorities. Weisberg sees this as Trump's effect on the coarsening of political culture — bullying became a way to succeed.

— Jacob Weisberg
33:00

George W. Bush's silence is an abdication of moral responsibility

In his book Weisberg confronts people with their own principles: law firms should defend the rule of law, journalists should defend journalistic principles, businessmen should defend free markets. George W. Bush represents the non-MAGA Republican tradition; after 9/11 he explicitly defended Muslim Americans and pushed PEPFAR, which is estimated to have saved 25 million people worldwide. He privately loathes Trump, painted an ugly portrait of Trump that he has never shown anyone, and did not vote for Trump in 2016. But he adheres to the tradition that former presidents do not criticize their successors. Weisberg argues that if he had spoken out publicly at key moments — in 2016, after January 6, and in 2024 — he could have had enormous impact. This is the sin of inaction, different from the sins of action of others in the book, but equally an abdication of moral responsibility.

— Jacob Weisberg
41:00

Anti-Trump open letters actually backfired

Mounk reflects on the open letters he helped push while involved with Protect Democracy — joint letters from the national security establishment and former state attorneys general — which got a great deal of media coverage, but which he now thinks accomplished almost nothing. The actual effect was to make many people feel that all these professionals were in fact partisan actors, and that their claims of neutrality and objectivity were just a cover for doing politics. The intent was to persuade conservatives that "this is an issue conservatives should care about too," but the result reinforced the idea that "the only political divide in America is whether you like Trump, these people are all on the other side, and I should distrust them wholesale."

— Yascha Mounk
48:30

Harvard didn't sign because Trump's price was absurd

Harvard at one point looked like it would reach a deal, even though it had the law on its side and could win in court. The government has the power to restrict foreign students and cancel funding, and even with a $50 billion endowment, Harvard did the math: if it fought the government for four years, it might not survive, or it might survive badly damaged, and it wasn't worth it. But the Trump administration's demands were too absurd — oversight of whom Harvard appoints as professors, whom it admits, what it teaches — and Harvard could not sign. As Trump's approval rating fell and universities kept winning in court, Harvard gradually reached a point where it did not have to sign. Weisberg says that if Democrats take the House in the midterms, he would be extremely surprised if Harvard signed a deal.

— Jacob Weisberg
52:00

Yale became the next target, but resistance now has a cost

The government turned to Yale, wanting another conspicuous surrender. The New York Times front page reported that Yale was about to reach a deal, based on allegations of racial discrimination against white and Asian students at its medical and law schools, and that Yale was prepared to pay a fine, sign, and get on with its life. But there was a huge backlash — counter-pressure from faculty, students, alumni, the Connecticut legislature, and Senator Richard Blumenthal. Yale president McInnis now sees that signing has a cost and not signing has a cost, but signing also has a cost. Weisberg agrees with Mounk's judgment: the incentives on the other side are now clearly stronger than a year ago, which makes concessions harder.

— Jacob Weisberg
