Kennedy pursued power before his brother died in war—the tragedy only accelerated his path

Historian Logvall: Kennedy aimed for the presidency in 1942—before his brother died—and the tragedy only accelerated his path, not created it.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:13

His grandfather's political legacy shaped him more than his father's

Jack's political awakening came not from his father Joe Kennedy but from his grandfather Honey Fitz—the legendary Irish-American Boston politician who served as mayor and congressman. The two had starkly different styles: Honey Fitz worked the room with handshakes and baby-kisses; Jack was reserved and never did such things. Yet Logvall credits his sense of public service and political interest partly to his grandfather, not his father.

9:26

Snubbed by Boston's old money, Joe Kennedy vowed to exceed them

Joe Kennedy had money and got into Harvard without real struggle, yet he was scorned by Brahmin-born classmates and blackballed from Final Club. He vowed to outdo them—earn more, seize more power. Logvall sees this hunger for acceptance mixed with resentment echoed in Jack, who later did gain entry to Final Club.

— Frederick Logevall
18:51

Roosevelt sent him to London to get him out of Washington

Through the 1920s Kennedy made his fortune; by the Depression he chased political power and wanted Treasury Secretary, but FDR had other candidates and distrusted him—saw him as an ambitious threat—so posted him as ambassador to Britain instead. The role was traditionally a springboard to the presidency; Kennedy took it as such, never anticipating that the timing would place him at the hinge moment when Europe chose sides.

— Frederick Logevall
32:26

The brothers broke openly over appeasement as war approached

Both brothers deferred to their father, but in 1939 Jack toured Europe, witnessed Nazi Germany firsthand, and carried back to London an urgent cable: war within a week. Shaped by Harvard professors and direct observation, he drifted from his father's appeasement stance; Joe Jr. held the line. A rare public split between them, though both would ultimately fight.

— Frederick Logevall
37:33

PT109 was negligence; the rescue afterward was the real heroism

August 2, 1943, Blackett Strait, Solomon Islands: moonless night. The Japanese destroyer Amagiri cut the PT109 in half. Logvall is direct: letting the boat get rammed involved no heroism—Jack bore some responsibility. But after the collision, he held one severely wounded sailor's life-jacket strap in his teeth and swam towing him for three or four hours to shore, then went back for another, and waited a week for rescue. That was heroism.

— Frederick Logevall
41:40

His father weaponized the shipwreck for the campaign

The PT109 story made Boston papers' front pages, yet Joe Kennedy orchestrated broader coverage, ensuring the 11th District voters knew it. Logvall considers this crucial to Jack's political future. The tale stung Joe Jr.: his thesis unpublished, Jack's now a bestseller, the younger brother a war hero. The eldest's luster was fading.

— Frederick Logevall
42:45

To match his brother's glory, Joe Jr. volunteered for a suicide mission

Joe Jr., groomed from birth as the future president, volunteered in August 1944 for a near-suicide task: pilot an unmanned drone laden with 23,562 pounds of explosives toward a French target, bail before detonation. Logvall speculates (without direct proof) that he wanted to match the younger brother's renown. The aircraft detonated mid-flight before he could jump. Both pilots: never found.

— Frederick Logevall
48:58

Kennedy chose politics before his brother died—historians debate only the timing

To the counterfactual "Would there be President Kennedy if his brother lived?", Logvall answers yes: Jack almost certainly would have entered politics anyway. His evidence: 1942 pillow talk with Inga Arvad, a Danish woman suspected of Nazi ties. She told him he was destined to "go all the way"—to the presidency—before his brother died. Logvall grants that other historians disagree, holding that absent Joe Jr.'s death, Jack might have become a journalist or academic instead.

— Frederick Logevall
