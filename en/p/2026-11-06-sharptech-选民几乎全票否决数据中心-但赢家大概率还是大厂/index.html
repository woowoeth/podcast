Voters overwhelmingly reject data centers, but big tech will likely still win

43 of 44 local referendums against data centers passed; even mayors and lawmakers were recalled. Yet capital, credit, and land advantages remain concentrated in Amazon and Google—the scarcer compute becomes, the better their position.

Even Texas, data centers' last refuge, is now blocking expansion

Texas was long viewed as data centers' last refuge: independent power grid, abundant natural gas, friendly policy environment. But the newly elected governor promised to pause new approvals until the state legislature reviews—and Texas's legislature meets only every two years. This could mean an indefinite halt. More alarming for incumbents: mayors and officials who backed data centers were recalled. Officials now fear losing their seats even without formal referendums, making them politically cautious regardless of policy.

The opposition isn't about facts; it's emotional and ideological

The root objection isn't factual. Like the ‘fake news’ phenomenon, voters don't believe false claims first and then oppose data centers; they harbor resentment toward tech elites first, then hunt for justifications. If one anti-data-center argument is debunked, they simply pivot to another—because what they really oppose is the entire AI-painted future being imposed on them.

— Ben Thompson
9:19

Only people who love advertising will buy into AI's promises

The AI industry will only convince those already conditioned by advertising. Good advertising first convinces you that you have a need, then offers the solution; AI just asserts it replaces workers. Deeper problem: the industry treats human desires as completely modelable and substitutable variables—a planned-economy logic, not an understanding that humans perpetually generate new needs.

— Ben Thompson
20:51

Data centers promise long-term gains, but big tech skips the short-term bills

Loudoun County exemplifies the data center virtuous cycle: local property taxes down, electricity down over a thirty-year arc. But the catch: data centers drive up local electricity costs and stress the grid in their early years. Big tech doesn't subsidize this transition—it demands communities subsidize them instead. For the industry's long-term story to be credible, it must first absorb the short- and medium-term pain it inflicts.

— Ben Thompson
36:27

Sunk launch costs make space data centers suddenly rational

SpaceX's orbital data center concept mirrors shipping's marginal-cost logic: ship depreciation is sunk; if fuel and port fees are covered, sailing even at accounting loss makes sense. SpaceX has already built launch infrastructure for Starlink. Once that capital is treated as ‘already spent, not to be recalculated,’ sending server racks to orbit only requires covering marginal costs—suddenly the calculation shifts from absurd to plausible.

— Ben Thompson
44:49

Compute scarcity turns big tech's existing advantages into overwhelming dominance

In a compute-constrained world—where politics and grid capacity become limiting factors—incumbents dominate. Amazon and Google hold land, power, permits, and community relationships, enabling expansion without starting from zero. Their cash flows are thicker, credit ratings higher, borrowing costs lower. When raising equity, they have real profitable businesses backing the bet. By contrast, all the equity value of Anthropic and OpenAI rests on a single outcome: whether their models succeed.

— Ben Thompson
1:01:23

Google's pitch now echoes Meta's old playbook on scaling then monetizing

Google's latest earnings language mirrors Meta's old pitch on Stories and Reels: first perfect the product, scale it globally, then layer in advertising. Google Search's ads still live in traditional results pages; the AI mode carries almost no advertisements yet. But users query more frequently and spend longer in AI mode. Google's wager: once ads migrate into AI search, it becomes the new cash machine, as Reels did from initial drag to current engine.

— Ben Thompson
1:21:41

The internet economy is shifting from mass scale toward intimate groups

AI makes building individual software easier, but because everyone can build, single vibe-coding apps lack scalable monetization. The real economic value flows to custom-built software for tight-knit groups. The hardest-hit businesses are those built on Google traffic for mass reach—Reddit recognized this shift and abandoned the pageview chase, pivoting instead to maximizing revenue per logged-in user. The internet economy is migrating from mass-scale reach to high-engagement small circles.

— Ben Thompson
