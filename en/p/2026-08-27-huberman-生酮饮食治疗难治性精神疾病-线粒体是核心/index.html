Ketosis for Mental Illness: It's Not About Weight Loss, It's Mitochondria

Psychiatrist Chris Palmer uses ketogenic and low-carb diets to help patients with treatment-resistant mental illness find relief; the key isn't just weight loss but changing brain energy metabolism and neurotransmitters through mitophagy and biogenesis.

3:32

A strict low-fat year made his metabolic disease worse

During his Harvard residency, Palmer was diagnosed with metabolic syndrome: high blood pressure, poor lipid profile, and prediabetes. He strictly followed a low-fat diet and exercise, yet got worse year after year, until his doctor put him on three medications. On a 'last try' mindset, he switched to the Atkins diet, and within three months his metabolic syndrome completely resolved. More unexpectedly, his mood, energy, focus, and sleep dramatically improved—he woke up naturally before his alarm for the first time. This made him question medical dogma and focus on how diet affects mental state.

— Chris Palmer
9:18

Only patients who truly enter ketosis improve

Palmer's patients mostly have treatment-resistant mental illness: they've tried six or more psychiatrists, dozens of medications, years of therapy, and even electroconvulsive therapy without success. He introduced low-carb diets into his clinical practice and found that only patients who entered ketosis showed improvement. In 2016, a 33-year-old man with schizoaffective disorder had daily auditory hallucinations and paranoid delusions, couldn't leave the house, and had tried 17 medications. Within two weeks of a ketogenic diet, he showed significant antidepressant effects; after 6–8 weeks, hallucinations and delusions began to disappear. He eventually lost 160 pounds, completed a certificate program, and lived independently.

— Chris Palmer
13:49

Depression and schizophrenia need different blood ketone levels

Different mental illnesses require different degrees of ketosis. For depression, Palmer typically targets a blood ketone level of ≥0.8 mmol/L; for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, especially chronic and severe cases, he requires ≥1.5 mmol/L. He also emphasizes that not all patients need a ketogenic diet; some only need to cut out highly processed foods high in sugar and fat. This stratified approach turns dietary intervention from a one-size-fits-all into precise metabolic treatment, and explains why some patients respond to simply cutting sugar.

— Chris Palmer
15:57

Ketogenic diet was not a weight-loss diet but a seizure therapy

The ketogenic diet is not a weight-loss diet; it was developed in 1921 by a physician specifically to treat epilepsy, based on the ancient Greek observation that fasting can stop seizures. The problem was that fasting cannot be maintained long-term, so Russell Wilder tried to mimic the fasting state with a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet. Early results were striking: 50% of patients became seizure-free, another 35% had more than a 50% reduction, for a total effectiveness of about 85%. In the 1970s, Johns Hopkins used the ketogenic diet for treatment-resistant epilepsy, with about one-third of patients becoming seizure-free and another third showing clinical improvement. This paved the way for its later use in mental illness.

— Chris Palmer
19:32

Many pathways change, but mitochondria are the real driver

The ketogenic diet has been shown to alter neurotransmitter levels like glutamate, GABA, and adenosine; modulate calcium channels and gene expression; reduce brain inflammation; change the gut microbiome; and improve insulin signaling. But Palmer argues the most critical effect is on mitochondria: ketosis stimulates mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis—the former clears damaged or aged mitochondria, the latter generates new healthy ones. He believes this is the underlying reason the ketogenic diet works for both epilepsy and chronic mental illness, and it provides a target for mechanistic research.

— Chris Palmer
23:09

Mitochondria are not power cords but the cell's motherboard

Palmer uses a counterintuitive analogy: mitochondria are not just power cords supplying energy to the cell; they are the motherboard that allocates resources. They directly participate in the production and release of core neurotransmitters like serotonin, dopamine, glutamate, and acetylcholine. Experiments even show that if you remove mitochondria from a synapse, neurotransmitters cannot be released even if you flood it with ATP. Mitochondria also contain enzymes for synthesizing steroid hormones like cortisol, estrogen, testosterone, and progesterone, and regulate inflammatory switches. Thus, mitochondrial dysfunction can tie together multiple known threads of mental illness.

— Chris Palmer
30:57

Mitochondrial dysfunction may be the unifying cause of aging

Mitophagy is a special branch of autophagy that specifically recognizes and clears defective mitochondria. Fasting, caloric restriction, and fasting-mimicking diets all strongly stimulate this process, which is one of the main current explanations for how caloric restriction extends lifespan. Palmer cites David Sinclair's claim that mitochondrial dysfunction may be the unifying cause of aging and all age-related diseases. So mitophagy is not just a metabolic topic; it may be a key bridge to understanding the shared mechanisms of mental illness and aging.

— Chris Palmer
36:45

For undiagnosed people, cut carbs first, don't jump to keto

For people without a diagnosed mental illness but who feel fatigued or low, Palmer suggests trying short-term carbohydrate restriction rather than jumping straight to a ketogenic diet. He gives an example: a vegetarian who read his book reduced high-carb foods on her own, and within three weeks her anxiety significantly improved and she no longer needed her prescription. But he explicitly warns that patients with severe disorders like bipolar or schizophrenia must not try this on their own; they must adjust medication under medical supervision, because diet-induced metabolic changes can affect drug dosages, and stopping medication on your own is extremely dangerous.

— Chris Palmer

Glossary

Mitophagy
The cellular process of selectively clearing damaged or aged mitochondria, after which new ones are synthesized.
Mitochondrial biogenesis
The process by which cells generate new, healthier mitochondria; a ketogenic diet can stimulate it.
Ketosis
A metabolic state in which blood ketone levels rise and the body shifts from glucose to fat as its primary fuel.
Schizoaffective disorder
A mental illness that combines symptoms of schizophrenia and mood disorders.
