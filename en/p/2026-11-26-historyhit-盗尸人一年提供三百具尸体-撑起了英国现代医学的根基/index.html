Three hundred corpses a year: how grave robbers built modern British medicine

Nineteenth-century English law confined dissection to executed murderers, while medical schools trained thousands of students. Professional body snatchers filled the shortage, supplying over three hundred fresh corpses annually to hospitals.

Four executed criminals per year: Henry VIII set dissection's quota

In 1540, Henry VIII merged surgeons and barbers into a single guild and permitted them to dissect four executed felons annually as compensation. This decree permanently bound ‘being dissected’to ‘being criminal’: law-abiding people would not be dissected, only villains deserved it. This notion persisted through 1752's Murder Act, when dissection formally became an added punishment for murderers.

8:23

Medical schools trained thousands of students but legally could dissect twelve corpses annually

Britain then had roughly eight hundred to one thousand medical students, yet only about twelve people were executed annually for murder. Each student needed at least two or three corpses for practice. The supply-demand ratio was absurdly skewed; law had lagged reality for decades, forcing illegal channels to close the gap.

10:28

Anatomists once robbed graves themselves before hiring professional gangs

Before the 1790s, anatomists and students who wanted corpses had to dig them up themselves at night. But when doctors like William Hunter faced hundreds of students, they couldn't personally rob graves constantly. This spawned professional ‘resurrection men’gangs, turning body snatching into a business.

— Emma
10:38

Stealing the body was legal; stealing the shroud was a crime

English law then didn't recognise the corpse itself as having legal status; possessing a body was only a misdemeanour. But the coffin was property, the burial shroud was property, rings and hairpins were property—damaging the coffin or taking these things constituted theft and criminal damage. Professional body snatchers thus developed a procedure: open only the coffin's top, strip the corpse, leave clothing and jewellery as found, steal only the corpse itself, which ‘counted as’no property.

— Emma
20:09

One victim's name in the grave robber's sales diary

Professional body snatcher Joseph Naples (约瑟夫·内普尔斯) kept a diary that is essentially a sales ledger; he called the dead ‘things’, anatomists called them ‘subjects’. But in one December 1811 entry, he uniquely wrote a name: Mary Rolph (玛丽·罗尔夫), died December 5. Burial records confirm she was interred that day; Naples' gang dug her up on the 12th—less than a week from burial to theft.

— Emma
29:27

Burke and Hare never grave-robbed at all

The most famous ‘body snatchers’, Burke and Hare, never actually robbed graves—they murdered tenants and sold them to anatomist Robert Knox. This became known as ‘burking’, smothering via the nose and mouth. The two killed about seventeen people; when exposed, Hare turned against Burke to escape execution, while Burke was hanged and himself became a ‘subject’on the anatomy table.

— Emma
33:42

The professional grave-robbing leader won price increases by withholding supply

Ben Crouch (本·克劳奇), who monopolised the medical schools' corpse supply, struck when price talks broke down, halting deliveries for roughly four weeks until anatomists agreed to raise rates. This shows that by its late stage, the corpse trade had become a mature business with pricing power and capacity for collective action.

— Emma
44:59

One attempted corpse sale exposed a murderer and forced legislation

An 1828 parliamentary inquiry should have prompted legislation, but it stalled for years due to controversy over whether to take corpses from the poor. Only in 1831, when John Bishop was caught attempting to sell the corpse of Carlos Ferrari—an Italian boy who sold white mice—was the bill revived and swiftly passed, legalising unclaimed and voluntary-donated corpses for dissection.

Glossary

resurrection men
Eighteenth and nineteenth-century professional gangs who specialised in exhuming fresh corpses and selling them to medical schools.
burking
To murder by suffocation, specifically by covering the mouth and nose to cause death without leaving marks—the method of Burke and Hare.
Anatomy Act 1832
English legislation permitting the legal dissection of unclaimed corpses and voluntarily donated bodies for medical study.
