Screens Train Multitasking, Not Learning: The Harder Classrooms Go Digital, the Dumber Kids Get

Since 2010, the harder classrooms have pushed digitalization, the more children's cognition and digital literacy have declined across the board; screens train multitasking, not learning, and AI only makes the errors more invisible.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:56

The more tech you stuff into classrooms, the worse kids learn

Jared defines the "digital illusion" as the default assumption that technology equals learning. Since 2010, schools have introduced edtech on a massive scale, expecting it to deliver more information, unleash creativity, and ditch rote memorization. But the data says otherwise: humans are not good at learning from technology; the more technology is injected into the classroom, the more learning development and cognitive ability decline. It can be a production tool, but it is not a classroom tool.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
6:09

The only skill kids practice at school is multitasking

The first mechanism of learning is focus, and multitasking simultaneously lowers accuracy and speed while pushing memory into secondary networks. But computers are inherently multitasking machines: kids spend over 2,500 hours per year multitasking on computers, and at home doing homework they start getting distracted after an average of 6 minutes; even in a classroom with a teacher watching, there are still 24 to 38 minutes per hour spent offline and off-task. By the 10,000-hour rule, in four years they have mastered only one thing—multitasking.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
8:29

Words on a screen have no page numbers, so memory has no anchor

Human memory relies heavily on spatial location: a book's fixed three-dimensional coordinates become retrieval cues for memory, so after reading you can roughly remember which page a passage was on. On a screen, text flows in from the bottom and out the top, eliminating three-dimensional position, and this entire dimension of memory is discarded. After about four or five minutes the brain signals "I'm not receiving," so the reader stops deep reading and becomes a keyword scanner.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
9:38

The more schools teach computers, the less kids can use them

Using the ICILS international digital literacy test over the decade from 2013 to 2023: the proportion of children explicitly taught how to use computers in school rose by about 600%, while the proportion reaching the minimum digital literacy threshold fell by about 11%. This holds in every country; the more technology is emphasized, the less proficient children become with technology. The key is not whether they can use the tool, but whether they have something to bring to the tool; just as a hammer is meaningful only to a carpenter.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
12:47

Experts use tools to offload, kids use tools to outsource

When experts use tools it is called offloading: they already have enough knowledge to verify the output. A statistician doing calculations by hand takes four hours; a computer produces the result in twenty seconds, but he can immediately judge whether it is right or wrong. When a child uses the same tool it is called outsourcing: they can only input, copy, paste, they don't know the process, they don't know whether the result is good or bad, they just wait for someone else to nod. The result is that they never learn facts and procedures, only dependence on the machine.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
22:02

Personalized learning only works for what you already know

Individualized practice is only effective for "repetitive training of already-learned content," the one place where technology is supported by data. But most classroom time is spent on things not yet learned, which requires self-regulated learning skills, and about 95% of children lack these skills; they most need the teacher to provide unified information and collective discussion. Data actually shows that once truly personalized, most children zone out. Ironically, technology originally promised to eliminate rote memorization, yet the only thing that works is drill-and-kill.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
27:18

AI's errors drift, so you can never catch them

AI will accelerate cognitive decline. Ordinary digital courseware, even if it has errors, has fixed errors that a teacher can spot and correct; AI's errors drift anywhere between 20% and 35%, so if ten children do the same problem, nine get it right and one gets it wrong, and it will never be caught. Someone asked "will people who can use AI replace you?" Jared says the key is that experts use AI; the essence of AI literacy is that you must understand more than the AI to fix the mistakes it makes.

— Jared Cooney Horvath
29:25

The antidote is not inventing a new system, but going back to 2010

Policy is turning: in the US, at the start of the year 3 states had edtech bills, now 31 states do; Victoria, Australia has already banned use for children in grade two and below, and has significantly pulled back in primary schools. Jared says the good news is we don't need to invent a new system, just return to the track that existed in 2010—textbooks, pen and paper, blackboards, and discussion are all still there. The specific advice for parents is to buy a printer: keep home learning on paper as much as possible, and start talking to teachers about "non-essential technology withdrawal."

— Jared Cooney Horvath

Glossary

Ed tech
Refers to various networked teaching devices, software, and online tutoring systems in the classroom.
ICILS
An international test of students' digital skills, administered in 2013 and 2023.
Offloading
Experts use tools to save effort, but because they have enough knowledge, they can verify the output themselves.
Outsourcing
Novices hand over thinking and the learning process to a tool, unable to judge whether the result is right or wrong.
SRLs
Students' ability to plan, monitor, and adjust their own learning process.
Drill-and-kill
A method of "drilling to death" knowledge through massive repetitive practice, often pejorative.
