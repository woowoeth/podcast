The polygraph doesn't test lies, it tests the fear that makes you confess

US courts rejected the polygraph's science, yet allowed police to use it to extract confessions—its value was never detecting lies, but manufacturing confessions and ongoing surveillance through psychological fear.

1:01

First case solved, suspect may never have been guilty

The 1921 Berkeley theft case where police used a polygraph on a female suspect, who panicked and fled, then confessed after days of continued surveillance. The inventor Larson later doubted her guilt—she had psychological issues and the thefts continued afterward. This "first solved case" suggests the polygraph's value wasn't finding the truth, but extracting confession through humiliation and pressure.

9:41

The polygraph was invented to monitor police, not criminals

Created in 1920s Berkeley by August Vollmer (the "father of modern policing") and physiologist John Larson to civilize brutal police practices. The first people systematically polygraphed were Berkeley police themselves—the tool was used for internal accountability to prove police weren't breaking the law, building public trust in the technology before use on suspects.

11:44

Courts banned polygraph evidence while police kept using it in interrogation rooms

In United States v. Frye, a murder suspect confessed then recanted; the polygraph showed innocence but the judge barred the jury from hearing it. The case established the Frye rule: only evidence vetted by the scientific community is admissible in court. But police continued using polygraphs to pressure suspects in interrogation rooms—police are legally permitted to lie and use deception to elicit statements that can later be introduced in court, regardless of the polygraph's scientific validity.

— Ken Alder
14:56

The polygraph became a tool for continuous employee surveillance in corporate America

Leonard Keeler, a colleague of Larson's, brought the polygraph to Chicago in the 1930s and sold testing services to banks and retailers like Walgreens. Found that employees often confessed to small thefts during tests. When management wanted to fire them, Keeler advised against it: knowing they could be polygraphed anytime made these employees the most honest. The tool transformed from a crime-solving instrument into a system of continuous workplace surveillance, spreading through banking, retail, and federal agencies.

— Ken Alder
16:00

Large-scale polygraph screening creates impossible statistical problems

When used at Los Alamos and Oak Ridge on thousands of nuclear weapons project employees, Ken Alder points out that even if the polygraph worked perfectly (it doesn't), mass screening produces enormous false positives—all the innocent people get investigated while actual liars slip through. The tool might have some value for a single suspect, but applied to an entire workforce, the mathematics breaks down entirely.

— Ken Alder
17:01

A Secretary of State threatened resignation to ban workplace polygraphs

From the 1960s onward, unions and the public opposed polygraph abuse; by the 1980s about half the states had banned it in employment. But it remained legal federally. When Reagan wanted to use polygraphs to hunt leakers in government, Secretary of State George Shultz declared it scientifically worthless and threatened to resign. This prompted Congress and Reagan to sign the Employee Polygraph Protection Act in 1988, banning private-sector employee testing, though government security, police, and pharmaceuticals were exempted. Industry scale collapsed from its peak.

— Ken Alder
19:10

The polygraph masks subjectivity with the false objectivity of technology

Ken Alder frames this as a very American story, like the SAT—seemingly machine-scored, objective, and fair, yet SAT is criticized for hidden bias. The polygraph is the same: who determines if someone is lying isn't the machine but the operator's subjective reading of the waveforms. The belief that "a neutral machine can solve social problems" is itself a distinctly American myth.

— Ken Alder
20:14

The polygraph may create nothing more than security theater

Alder concludes the problem isn't just that polygraphs don't detect lies, but that they create "security theater"—like airport metal detectors, giving false reassurance while not actually improving safety. He closes with Montaigne's 450-year-old observation: truth has one face, but liars have a thousand; extracting truth from someone's mind is a game that's hard to win.

— Ken Alder

Glossary

Frye rule
The US precedent establishing that scientific evidence must be validated by the scientific community before admission in court.
Employee Polygraph Protection Act
1988 legislation signed by Reagan that prohibits private-sector use of polygraphs on employees.
security theater
Ritual security measures that create an illusion of safety without actually improving it.
cardiopneumopsychograph
The formal academic name for John Larson's early polygraph device.
