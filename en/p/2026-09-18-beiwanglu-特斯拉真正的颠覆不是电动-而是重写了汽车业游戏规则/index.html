Tesla's real disruption wasn't electric — it rewrote the auto industry's rules

Legacy automakers didn't miss Tesla; they filed it as a new toy in a niche segment. What actually got disrupted was the seven-year model cycle, the dealer system and supply-chain interests — baggage that kept them from turning around.

An executive who spent over a decade at Mercedes, Aston Martin and Jaguar Land Rover explains that what foreign automakers missed wasn't technology, it was the rules. The second half on going global and management is especially valuable.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

11:04

Tesla was filed as a new toy in a niche segment

Freda revisits how Mercedes judged it at the time: legacy automakers didn't miss Tesla, they categorized it as ‘a new product in a niche segment’, a functional replacement for one powertrain. Back then luxury customers said buying a Tesla was a status symbol, an innovation label, and Mercedes' reaction was — you have five or six cars in your garage, adding one more is fine, you won't drift far from the traditional luxury brand. The error in that judgment was failing to see that EVs opened up a new business model and redefined the automobile product. She draws a Kodak analogy: when digital cameras came out, Kodak also thought it might be a new toy for enthusiasts.

— Freda
13:05

Direct sales is only the most superficial change

Freda says Tesla was the first brand to switch from dealer sales to direct sales, the most obvious change from the customer side, but behind it are bigger things: changes in suppliers and the supply chain, and changes in the speed of new-car development. These were only realized later. She stresses that legacy automakers realized Tesla was taking customers and having an effect in the market, but ‘still haven't fully realized whether EV will change the competitive rules of the auto industry’.

— Freda
17:13

Turning a ship of hundreds of thousands is hard in process, not awareness

Freda says legacy automakers weren't unaware that digitalization was needed, but ‘an enterprise of hundreds of thousands, such a big ship turning around to make this change, the process is extremely, extremely difficult’. At the time sales doubled every year, dealers could open 50 new stores a year, and digitization was like a new initiative, a new campaign, done from headquarters to region, but resistance was strong and the process long. She gives the example of websites: many brands' websites are still centrally controlled by headquarters today, while Chinese consumers' online interaction habits are more sophisticated and require more information, and this communication process is extremely long.

— Freda
21:19

Global consistency overrides Chinese consumer needs

Freda explains why headquarters is reluctant to delegate: branding has always been about global consistency. Luxury brands especially insist ‘you can't follow trends, trends may pass in the blink of an eye’, and must stick to brand tonality and a globally consistent look and feel. Websites absolutely won't be delegated; only China-specific platforms like mini-programs can be used by the China team to persuade headquarters. But even then, headquarters needs to be persuaded to let go — every time headquarters visits they ask why the materials headquarters gave you can't be used in China, why you need Xiaohongshu. She says such questions are ‘very dumb’, but the China team still has to persuade them every day.

— Freda
28:30

A century-old rulebook upended by six-month iteration

Freda says the gasoline car industry has been in Germany for over a hundred years, many brands are over a hundred years old, and the rules were set by these players over a century. In the past Chinese, Korean and Japanese brands all entered and played by these rules: car updates were a seven-year generation major facelift and a five-year minor facelift, and that's been the case for decades. But today an EV might come out with a new feature every six months, and won't let your process run, ‘the entire rules of the auto industry are completely disrupted’. Her personal judgment is that legacy automakers haven't realized how revolutionary the change is.

— Freda
31:27

Supply chain and dealers are baggage they can't carry

Freda breaks down why legacy automakers can't turn around: German companies have Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers behind them, with decades of cooperation with the brand, and it's not like you don't need engines this year and can cut them off tomorrow; the dealer system has been formed for so many years, protecting dealer profitability is a rule everyone must follow, and a brand can't say I don't care about five or six hundred dealers, I'll sell directly to consumers myself. But Chinese new EV brands enter as EVs from the start, can set their own rules, can be half dealers half direct sales, ‘without so much baggage’. Legacy automakers carry countless bags, and it's not something that can change overnight.

— Freda
41:41

2023 Shanghai Auto Show: BYD put Yangwang next to Mercedes

Freda says the 2023 Shanghai Auto Show was the first after the pandemic. In the past foreign Global CEOs and global R&D presidents would come to China several times a year, but during those years visas were hard to get and quarantine was required, so visits dropped a lot, causing a huge surprise in 2023. She checked the data: there were about 100, 130, 150 new products in 2023, of which Chinese brands accounted for 100, and 70% were pure electric products. At the auto show, which hall and what position matters a lot; luxury brands are all together, and BBA each bring a luxury brand. But that year, for the first time, BYD put Yangwang next to Mercedes, in the same hall, and began the high-end positioning of independent brands.

— Freda
50:52

Betting on gasoline, hybrid and EV at once — where's the focus?

Freda says this is a huge strategic question she hasn't figured out: EV companies like NIO, Li Auto and XPeng only have EVs and hybrids to invest in, but legacy automakers have gasoline cars, hybrids and EVs, and must also invest in R&D and new product production, with limited capital — where is the focus? Worse, China is in a state of running forward with EVs, while Europe relaxed standards and the US and Canada completely withdrew EV sales subsidies, and only China, such a large market, is running fast. She believes these foreign companies in China today ‘please no one, and do no product well’.

— Freda
1:14:13

Going global, the scarcest talent is someone who can talk to dealer investors

Freda says the first requirement for Chinese brands looking for people overseas is to screen for Chinese who can talk to local dealer investors. The reason is that Chinese companies need local middle management who can both talk to them and understand the Chinese brand's business thinking, and also connect with local dealers and speak their language. But such talent ‘can almost be counted on one hand’ — there are many Chinese, but not many who sit in this position and can communicate with dealer investors as equals. From this she raises a bigger concern: Chinese companies going global also face the headquarters-region relationship, and should not repeat the path foreign companies took in the Chinese market.

— Freda
1:18:28

What Chinese companies lack most is multicultural management and compliance

Freda says the management experience and corporate culture cultivated by foreign companies, especially compliance, are highly valued by both American and German companies, and these have not been trained in other markets and companies. Conversely, what Chinese companies lack most now is actually management, especially multicultural management, and the concept of compliance — respect for different cultures, which is the most basic entry-level thing for running a company, but is not particularly emphasized domestically. So people coming from headquarters don't have this concept in some places, creating a mismatch, with different expectations and different requirements between the local team and headquarters.

— Freda

Glossary

D2C / Direct to Consumer
Brands bypass dealers to sell directly to consumers; Tesla was the first in the auto industry to do this.
DMS / Dealer Management System
Dealer Management System, manual reports and software systems used by legacy automakers to manage dealers.
global consistency
Luxury brands insist on globally consistent brand tonality and visual presentation, hence reluctant to delegate separately for the Chinese market.
Tier 1 / Tier 2
The layers of the automotive supply chain; Tier 1 suppliers deal directly with the brand and have decades-long partnerships.
VinFast
Vietnamese EV brand that tried direct sales in Canada for two years without success, then withdrew and started looking for dealers.
