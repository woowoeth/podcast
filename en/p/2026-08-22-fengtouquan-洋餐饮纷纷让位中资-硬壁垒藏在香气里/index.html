Foreign Food Brands Change Hands to Chinese Owners, and the Real Moat Is in Flavorings

McDonald's, Starbucks, Burger King and other foreign food brands have successively changed hands to Chinese owners; localization is the line between life and death in food service, but upstream in perfumery and fragrance, the four Western giants still hold a monopoly on flavorings — the supply-chain barrier is the real watershed for domestic substitution.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:01

Remote-controlling across the Pacific is worse than buying the brand and hiring a local team

In 2017 McDonald's sold its China business to CITIC Capital and Carlyle, with CITIC taking 52% and McDonald's Global keeping just over 20%; Golden Arches has been Chinese-controlled ever since. The plot reversed in the last two years: McDonald's Global bought its stake back up from 20% to 48%, and Carlyle exited after several years with roughly a six- to seven-fold return. The hosts judge this deal to be the model for every later foreign food-service divestiture — putting up money to buy the brand and then hiring a local operating team is far more effective than directing operations remotely from across the Pacific, and it has become an industry consensus.

7:08

Starbucks handing over control is the watershed of this round

The deals hit their peak in 2026. In February, Yuanfeng Fund injected $350 million into Burger King China for an 83% stake, at an overall valuation of about 2.8 to 2.9 billion RMB; in April, Starbucks headquarters finalized a partnership with Boyu Capital, with headquarters keeping 40% and Boyu taking 60%. The two joke that investment institutions seem to be copying each other — grab Starbucks, then go grab Burger King. What makes the Starbucks deal a landmark is that Seattle gave up control of its China business for the first time, and the new shareholders moved in to take over before the deal even closed.

11:30

Pizza Hut China alone is worth as much as the rest of the world

In June this year, Yum China bought ownership of the Pizza Hut China brand from Yum Brands headquarters for $1.2 billion, going from master franchisee to brand owner. In the same period, Yum Brands dumped Pizza Hut everywhere outside China to a US private equity firm for just $1.5 billion — one Chinese region priced at close to the sum of the entire rest of the world. The hosts argue this shows Yum took the right path: it began localized operations and a separate listing 20 years ago, and did so well that the brand was absorbed internally rather than handed to outsiders.

17:47

Food service has a low entry barrier and an extremely high billion-market-cap barrier

The footnote to "low entry barrier, high barrier to getting big" is market cap: among all Chinese listed food-service companies today, only Yum China stands at the 100 billion HKD level. Mixue Bingcheng and Haidilao have both fallen back to the tens of billions, a level they only exceeded at their peaks. Food service is a massive three-to-five-trillion-yuan track, but 100 billion is an extremely hard threshold to cross, because every brand is being hit by rivals from all directions — without resolute localization, even the most famous company can easily lose.

20:53

Starbucks won't fight a delivery price war; it's doubling down on space

The most visible change at Starbucks after the acquisition is the playlist: English background music once decided uniformly by Seattle headquarters has been replaced with a large amount of Chinese pop, and the choice of songs has been delegated to each store manager — cozy songs for community stores, trendier ones for trendy commercial districts. This year it also opened a themed store in Guangzhou's Yongqingfang focused on a Cantonese intangible-cultural-heritage parlor, bringing Yue opera costumes and stage scenes into the café. The hosts' judgment: Starbucks is not fighting a 9.9-yuan delivery price war, but making its foundation of "spatial experience" more localized.

31:13

Häagen-Dazs lost because a packaged-food company has no food-service DNA

Häagen-Dazs had a localization high point in China: 20 years ago, to sell high-priced ice cream, the China team opened experience stores in malls, using the in-store price of 50 yuan per scoop as an anchor for 30 yuan in supermarkets, and both the packaged business and the stores exploded, reaching nearly 1,000 stores at the peak. But General Mills is a packaged-food company with no food-service DNA; after the team left, the stores gradually fell behind on product, price and model, and were overtaken by Yeren Xiansheng. In the end, General Mills sold the store business to Ningji in June this year, and the announcement did not even state the transaction price; after taking over, Häagen-Dazs has already begun to see push carts and 19.9-yuan promotions.

38:31

Zero delivery share is exactly why Yeren Xiansheng won

The most counterintuitive fact about Yeren Xiansheng: its delivery share is 0, yet it was a standout brand expanding rapidly over the past year. The logic behind giving up delivery is that ice cream consumption happens in social settings like families and couples, and is emotional value rather than functional need — delivery damages the texture and cannot capture that setting. The hosts argue that its conviction in the insight of "a family sharing one ice cream in the mall after dinner" is why it is stronger than its foreign rivals. Next it will push ice cream cakes for birthdays and emotional sharing, using only a few seats in the "nooks and crannies" around its stores, rather than learning from Häagen-Dazs's heavy spending on large, lavishly decorated stores.

54:08

The chokehold is not only in hard tech, but also in scent

The domestic rise of perfume has to be seen across the whole supply chain: brands like Guanxia and Wenxian are merely "assemblers of smell," and the real R&D sits upstream — four major chemical giants, two Swiss, one German and one American, almost monopolize global scent supply, with the largest posting annual revenue of 100 billion RMB. Three barriers keep them standing for a century: a patent wall, with molecular formulas undisclosed; a talent wall, with only a few hundred top perfumers worldwide and more than a decade needed to train one; and a customer wall, since Chanel and Dior will not switch ingredient suppliers to save on cost. The chokehold exists not only in hard tech, but also in scent.
