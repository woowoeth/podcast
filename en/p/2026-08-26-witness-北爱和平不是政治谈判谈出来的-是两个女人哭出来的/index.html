Peace in Northern Ireland wasn't negotiated—it was cried into being by two women

In 1976, a car crash in Belfast killed three young children. Their aunt and a witness launched the Peace People movement, violence in Northern Ireland fell by 70%, and the two women won the Nobel Peace Prize.

Seven years of troop deployment didn't stop the slide into urban guerrilla warfare

The narrator explains that Northern Ireland was already in its seventh year of violent sectarian conflict. On one side were mainly Protestant unionists, who wanted Northern Ireland to remain part of the UK; on the other, mainly Catholic nationalists, who wanted it to join the Republic of Ireland. The British government had sent in troops in 1969, but the situation kept deteriorating. Gerry Graham, who lived in West Belfast, said the community had degenerated to the point of urban guerrilla warfare, where even a normal commute could be caught up in gunfire or riots.

— narration
2:30

In Belfast, you listened to the news to confirm no one you knew was dead

Gerry recalled that around three that afternoon, they heard three or four gunshots at home, sounding very close. The family had long relied on the news to confirm whether anyone they knew had been hurt; at four o'clock the news reported that pedestrians had been caught up in an incident on Finaghy Road North. Gerry said he sometimes had a premonition, and he turned to his wife and said, 'I think it might be Anne.' When he went in, he saw someone sitting with their head in their hands. That, he said, was the beginning of the nightmare.

— Jerry Graham
4:00

The mother took her own life four years later; the funeral was not the end

The next day, Mairead went with the children's father to identify the bodies at the morgue. In an interview, she said she saw two little ones, and the third was gone too; there would be a triple funeral on Friday. She publicly condemned all those who perpetrated violence: those who called themselves Christians but failed to do what God said—'love one another, forgive and forget.' One baby was still in intensive care; the narrator adds that the mother, Anne Maguire, was believed never to have recovered from the trauma of losing her children, and she took her own life four years later.

— narration
6:00

Peace began with door-to-door knocking, not at the negotiating table

Betty Williams, who witnessed the accident, began going door to door collecting signatures condemning violence—that was the start of the Peace People movement. Four days after the tragedy, ten thousand women—both Protestant and Catholic—carried banners from the site of the children's deaths to the cemetery, the first of dozens of peace marches. Mairead described the cross-community mobilization on BBC radio: women came from the Shankill, they came by bus from East Belfast, they came with banners from Turf Lodge.

— narration
7:30

The call for peace was first suspected of siding with the unionists

The movement did not run smoothly. The narrator says they had to withstand suspicion of 'siding with the British unionists.' On BBC footage, Betty, in a room crowded with women, said passionately: 'For seven years, every explosion we repaired, every time someone was shot we cried and we buried, but we cannot go on like this; we must build a future for everyone, whatever their political views—no one can get a future from the barrel of a gun.'

— Betty Williams
9:00

The achievement was not a 70% drop in violence but putting peace on the agenda

The narrator gives the key figure: the Peace People reduced violence by 70%. Violence later flared again, and it was not until the 1998 political negotiations that it truly subsided; but the program quotes Gerry saying the movement's greatest achievement was 'putting the two big words—peace—on the agenda.' In 1976, Mairead and Betty received the Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo City Hall. In 1998, the Belfast Agreement was approved in referendums on both sides, ending the thirty-year conflict in Northern Ireland.

— narration
10:00

The founders died one by one, but the movement did not stop with them

The program closes by recounting the fates of the principals: co-founder and journalist Ciaran McKeown died in 2019 at the age of 76; Betty Williams died in 2020, also at 76. Gerry Graham is now chairman of the Peace People and, with Mairead Corrigan Maguire, continues to advocate for peace globally. The program previews two upcoming episodes: 'The Belfast Agreement' and 'The Belfast Agreement Referendum.'

— narration
