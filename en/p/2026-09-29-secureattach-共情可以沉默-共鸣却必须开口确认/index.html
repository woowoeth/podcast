Empathy needs no voice; resonance demands someone to speak back

Empathy is the feeling you alone have toward your child—it requires no words. Resonance demands you repeatedly confirm 'Do I understand you?' through tone and guessing, and only when your child says 'No, that's not it' are you truly synchronized.

Hands freeze when old trauma surfaces before language can touch it

Sarah Peyton adopted a son who later died of alcohol addiction at 33. When he arrived at 15, she wanted to embrace him but found her arms freezing. During an NVC empathy exercise, she suddenly recalled being three years old and reaching for her mother, only to be coldly rebuffed. That moment of rejection had lodged itself in her nervous system like a fish hook, causing her to instinctively pull back from embracing her child ever since—yet she had no conscious memory of it.

— Sarah Peyton

The amygdala has no clock; old wounds return without warning

Trauma memories are stored in the amygdala, which has no sense of time—it just glues experiences together without a timeline. When a strong memory is triggered, the body relives it: the same shame, the same physical responses return. This explains why your child's identical noise leaves you indifferent one day and enrages you the next—the difference isn't in the child, but in what triggered an old, unnamed memory on that particular day.

— Sarah Peyton
13:36

Empathy stays silent; resonance demands someone to speak back

Sarah Peyton distinguishes between empathy and resonance: empathy is only your one-way feeling toward someone else, requiring no words or conversation; resonance demands back-and-forth—you ask 'Do I understand you?' through tone, expression, and guessing, and the other person answers 'yes' or 'no.' This bidirectional confirmation is what children truly need.

— Sarah Peyton
17:40

Saying 'you're not sad' breaks the nervous system's transmission

When a child expresses an emotion only to have it denied—Sarah Peyton's mother once replied 'You're not sad' when she said 'I'm sad'—it's like installing a broken transmission in the child: the coordination between body and brain that manages stress and restores calm gets destroyed. Without that coordination, the child can only cope by making the nervous system increasingly shut down and cautious about speaking, to handle a world they don't understand and aren't understood in.

— Sarah Peyton
24:56

A truly understanding presence rewires how the vagus nerve reacts

Drawing on James Coan's hand-holding research at the University of Virginia: when someone who truly understands you is present, your brain changes how it responds to stress—the hill becomes less steep, effort feels less effortful, pain hurts less. Conversely, if you can narrate your emotions during an accident—'I'm afraid, I'm angry, I'm numb, I'm worried about my child'—that proves you've been 'put into words' by others many times before. If you can't speak it, that experience locks itself into your amygdala and body, never becoming a narrative you can digest.

— Sarah Peyton
40:43

Three insecure attachments, three root systems, three growth patterns

Sarah Peyton compares the three insecure attachment styles to three root systems: ambivalent attachment like roots with unpredictable watering, sometimes blooming, sometimes withering; avoidant attachment like roots shut off from all nourishment and joy, eventually developing an insulating layer around the nervous system that stops reaching outward; anxious-resistant attachment like roots that must constantly cling to others to get nutrients, creating deeper rather than lighter dependence on others. A child entering daycare where they must compete with 11 peers for attention can be pushed from avoidant to anxious-resistant—the squeaky wheel gets the grease.

— Sarah Peyton
50:17

You can earn secure attachment later, but old patterns persist

Asked whether an insecurely attached parent can still offer their child secure attachment, Sarah Peyton's answer is yes—but not a clean slate. Learning this neurobiology and language, holding warm curiosity toward your child's experience, gives you 'earned secure attachment'—like wrapping your originally avoidant, chaotic, or anxious attachment in a halo of light. The old patterns stay inside, but you can spot them and adjust them with humor, rather than being led by them.

— Sarah Peyton
55:35

When children say 'I won't, but I will,' their needs have been heard

In Inbal Kashtan's book, a three-year-old told to stop stomping said 'I don't want to, but I will'—because 'you don't want me to.' Sarah Peyton initially thought her house would never see this, but after three years of asking her children 'Are you willing?' using nonviolent communication, both her older and younger son said nearly the same words to her on the same day. This shows that when parents' needs are truly taken seriously, children's needs start mattering to their parents—but this shift comes slowly, built year after year.

— Sarah Peyton

Glossary

resonance
A bidirectional process of repeatedly confirming 'Do I understand you?' through language and tone, distinct from one-way empathy
earned secure attachment
Secure attachment achieved in adulthood through learning and practice, different from the innate secure attachment formed in childhood
vagus nerve
The main neural pathway connecting the brain to internal organs, regulating stress response and the return to calm
alarmed aloneness
A nervous-system state of alertness from sensing the absence of a supporting presence, distinct from fear or anger
Panksepp's core emotion circuits
Cross-species emotion and motivation neural circuits identified by neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp, including circuits for grief, anger, seeking, and play
nonviolent communication (NVC)
A communication method centered on observation, feeling, need, and request, developed by Marshall Rosenberg
