Simulating real people is not a reasoning problem: frontier models are only 20–30% accurate

Joon Sung Park's judgment: treating a person as a trainable object requires not a stronger reasoning model but a model that makes the same mistakes the person makes — frontier models hit only 20–30% accuracy predicting behavior in niche populations, and Simile uses two-hour deep interviews plus RCT data to push it to 85%.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:59

Agents fail because they don't know who you are

When he was doing time-machine extrapolation in 2022, simulation and the "extremely personalized personal assistant" were tied for the top two. Joon chose simulation not just out of sci-fi sentiment but out of a sequencing judgment: you send the model to buy dinner, it orders Hawaiian pizza, and you don't eat pineapple — that failure cannot be fixed by stronger execution ability, only by a deeper understanding of "who I am." His bet is that an accurate representation of a person is causally prior to an automated agent. His hot take is that no personal assistant has ever reached the ambition of that line; ChatGPT and Claude do know a fair amount about the user and generate more fitting output, but the key ingredient is still missing.

— Joon Sung Park
9:50

Public models haven't learned human social physics

He offers a test: if the model needs to learn the underlying physics of the world it inhabits — a new social physics — then you must train or post-train; if the physics is already in the model and it only needs to react to an environment, prompting is enough. He thinks public models have not yet learned the full mapping of human social physics, and the reason is data composition: web data is essentially people's "self-exposed attitudinal data," with behavioral data only scattered sparsely through it. The gap between what people say they do online and what they actually do in reality is what he calls humanity's dark knowledge, and it has to be collected anew and fed into the modeling process. OpenClaw's Markdown-memory approach matches the intuition of the 2022 generative agents — clever, but with a ceiling.

— Joon Sung Park
11:21

Data that describes why is the scarcest and the most valuable

The first bucket is deep interviews: just ask "tell me the story of your life" — childhood memories, trauma, first love. This kind of long-tail information gives "a person as a model" its texture, and it acts in ways that are hard to predict. The second bucket is observational behavioral data: transaction data, scrapeable web data, which supplies the base statistics of behavior and is also the easiest to get. The third bucket he considers the most important — data that describes causal mechanisms, that describes "why," coming mainly from RCTs: hold the setup fixed, move one variable, watch how behavior changes. This data is both scarce and critical, because the world is the ground truth and it only happens once. Simile runs a large number of RCTs itself, and uses "the decision has a real cost" as the line between attitude and behavior: what you buy in the experiment really does get shipped to your home.

— Joon Sung Park
14:08

Telling a client they collapse in two quarters says nothing

"Your sales collapse in two quarters" is useless to a decision-maker; they'll just say "that's too bad." What they need to know is what to do now to avoid that future — that is the causal mechanism, and it is the dividing line between simulation and prediction. The highest form of simulation takes not a question as input but a goal: like in Foundation, "compress the chaos to a thousand years," and then output every step. And those steps are often counterintuitive — the first step turns out to be exiling the warning scientist to Terminus. In business terms: an automaker wants to know how to market EVs to push up its stock price, and the simulation may return something like "marketing this way will change consumers' perception of your non-EV models and actually lower total sales" — a conclusion you could never reach by staring only at absolute EV sales.

— Joon Sung Park
26:02

The denominator of 85% is the person reproducing themselves

The method of "Generative Agent Simulations of 1000 People": recruit 1,000 people from a representative US sample into a virtual lab, collect data for two hours (the interview script taken from the American Voices Project, plus as much behavioral data as possible), send them away for two weeks, and during that time build digital twins from this data; two weeks later call the real people back for a full battery of behavioral-economics games, the Big Five, the General Social Survey, and RCTs previously published in PNAS, and have the twins predict what the person would do. The result is 85% reproduction accuracy against "the person reproducing themselves." The denominator is the person's own test-retest consistency, not absolute accuracy — that is the only correct way to read this number.

— Joon Sung Park
28:24

The more rational a frontier model, the worse it simulates a real person

Frontier models are heading toward "hyper-rational, objective machines": pulling data from Mercor and Scale, hiring professional programmers and scientists to annotate, building reasoning ability. Simile's training objective is the opposite — to build a model that makes the same mistakes he makes; whatever mistakes he makes, the model has to make. That is a completely different dataset and training objective, and it explains the performance gap: on the niche populations and topics clients actually care about, frontier models drop to 20–30% behavioral prediction accuracy, and roughly 50–60% on the gen pop — not enough to decide on. Another post-training route is to scrape preregistered studies from the Open Science Framework — tens of thousands of high-quality RCTs with hypotheses and p-hacking safeguards — and use them to significantly improve behavioral prediction; that model is part of open science and is not commercialized.

— Joon Sung Park
37:30

Combining prompts is just retrieving knowledge the model already has

Facing Tencent's billion-persona paper (taking the Cartesian product of occupation and background as a prompt), he first acknowledges the scale, then gives the core rebuttal: that approach leans heavily on statistics already baked into the model's parameters — you are just retrieving knowledge the model already has. If that route worked, simulation would already be solved — and that is not what the market shows. Real people carry a large amount of fine-grained, niche knowledge; a single item looks unremarkable, but assembled it is extremely rich, and that part can only come from bespoke data collection. The same logic determines that the social data they most want is Facebook rather than LinkedIn or Twitter: on LinkedIn people have their professional guard up, on Twitter people have a persona, and Facebook is closer to a person's base state.

— Joon Sung Park
40:04

Scale is not for significance, it's for filtering subpopulations

He says Simile has already seen early signs of a scaling law in simulation: the more data about people and compute you feed in, the more predictable the gain in simulating people. But the meaning of scale needs to be seen clearly — today they collect data on tens of thousands of people per week, their panel partnerships cover tens of millions worldwide, and actual deployments use only tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands, because what clients want is not stronger statistical significance but the ability to filter out the subpopulation they care about. And the same person can be reused for all subsequent studies, because the model is domain-agnostic — what it learns is that person's social physics, and traits like risk preference don't change over time anyway. Looking forward, he judges that within the next several years there will be simulations costing as much as training a foundation model; if one simulation could solve climate change, he would go raise that money today.

Glossary

social physics
The underlying regularities governing how populations behave; if a model lacks it, you must train; if it has it, you only need to prompt.
attitudinal vs. behavioral
The dividing line is whether the decision has a real cost — only something you buy that actually gets shipped to your home counts as behavior.
concept testing
A marketing-side practice: taking different messages, products or ideas and testing how a population reacts.
synthetic panel
A virtual respondent pool generated by a model that can be queried repeatedly, replacing a panel of real people.
agent-based modeling
A method dating to Schelling in the 1970s: give each agent one simple rule and watch the group-level emergence.
Open Science Framework preregistration
Publishing hypotheses before an experiment to prevent post-hoc hypothesis changes; it has accumulated tens of thousands of high-quality RCTs.
