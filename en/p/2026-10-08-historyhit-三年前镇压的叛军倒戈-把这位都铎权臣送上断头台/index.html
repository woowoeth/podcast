Dan Snow's History Hit 2026-10-08

The rebels he crushed three years ago defected—and sent this Tudor powerbroker to the block

John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland, controlled England for four years through decisive action, but when he mobilized to prevent Mary from becoming queen, he encountered the very people he had massacred in East Anglia four years earlier—who switched sides to support Mary.

4:07

When Henry VIII died, his authority died instantly

Henry VIII had ruled for decades, his word absolute. But the moment he died, everyone scrambled for their own interests, and his dying wishes were shelved almost immediately. Dudley's friend William Paget happened to hold a document endorsed by Henry listing ‘unclaimed rewards’ that should be distributed. He seized the moment to elevate their ally Edward Seymour to Duke of Somerset. Susanna offered an analogy: it was like relatives claiming after someone's death, ‘Your mother said before she died that teapot was supposed to be mine.’

— Susanna Lipscomb
10:13

His father's death cast a lifelong shadow on all he did

Dudley's father Edmund had been Henry VII's money-squeezer, extorting nobles. When Henry VIII took the throne, to show his clemency he had Edmund executed as atonement—despite Edmund merely following orders. John Dudley was eight when his father died; his family bore the stain of treason and had to make their own way entirely. He rose through the sword, not the pen; Susanna says he ‘wrote terribly’ but was a reliable man in battle.

— Susanna Lipscomb
13:20

He rose to power twice by exploiting men in crisis

How Dudley acquired Dudley Castle: he lent money to a distressed distant cousin on harsh terms, then seized the collateral and threw the cousin out. More egregious was 1540: when Sir Flammock, steward of Kenilworth Castle, caught plague and seemed to be dying, Dudley immediately wrote to Cromwell asking if he could have the post—then Flammock recovered, making the scene quite awkward.

— Susanna Lipscomb
15:25

After crushing Norfolk's rebels, he turned and betrayed his ally

Seymour had become deeply unpopular through his Protestant reforms and enclosure policies. Dudley was sent to Norfolk to crush the rebellion, massacring roughly 300 in two weeks—Susanna reminds us: that number equals the total number of Protestants executed during the entire reign of ‘Bloody Mary’ later. But the situation kept deteriorating. Dudley realized that if he didn't eliminate Seymour, he'd be dragged down too, so he turned and accused his old friend of plotting to murder him.

— Susanna Lipscomb
24:07

At his peak, he pushed religious reform further than his allies dared

As Duke of Northumberland, foreign envoys said Dudley was effectively governing England. He ended the costly war with France and pushed a more radical Protestant reformation than Seymour had—the 1551 edition of the Book of Common Prayer entirely eliminated Catholic remnants, more thorough than Elizabeth's later compromise. He grasped every power available and pushed every achievement as far as it would go.

— Susanna Lipscomb
28:15

A dying boy-king manually rewrote succession to place Jane Grey on the throne

At age 15, Edward VI wrote his ‘Device for the Succession’ by hand during his final illness, the phrase ‘male heir’ appearing 12 times—he believed the successor must be male. Only as he neared death, confirming that Jane Grey (newly married to Dudley's son Guildford) had no sons, did he cross out the s after ‘Jane's’ and write ‘Jane and her (heirs)’, putting Jane herself in line for the throne.

— Susanna Lipscomb
36:40

He led the army to victory but lost to ghosts of his own massacres

When Mary learned of her brother's death, she fled to East Anglia to raise troops and claim the crown herself. Dudley led the army to suppress her—by military strength he should have won easily. But four years earlier, he had massacred the Norfolk rebels on that same ground; the people hated him to the bone, while Mary had spent years building relationships and a support network there. The result: the navy mutinied and joined Mary, the army deserted in waves, and the Privy Council, seeing which way the wind was blowing, directly proclaimed Mary queen.

— Susanna Lipscomb
40:52

His trial defense echoed his father's fate—a curse he had foreseen

At trial, Dudley argued: everything he did was under the Privy Council's orders, bearing the Great Seal of England. If following those orders made him a traitor, then the orders themselves made no sense. This mirrored his father's fate exactly—Dudley had once written expressing a premonition: he was doing what he was ordered to do, just as his father had, and the moment politics shifted, he'd be cast aside like garbage. In the end, that premonition came exactly true on himself.

— Susanna Lipscomb

Glossary

Lord Protector
High-ranking regent during a child-king's reign, usually held by a close relative, wielding the highest power
Device for the Succession
Edward VI's handwritten document specifying who would inherit the throne after his death
Queen Regnant
A female monarch reigning in her own right, not as a king's consort
attainted for treason
Legal stripping of titles, property, and inheritance rights as punishment for treason conviction
