The 15,000 in the Cobalt Mine: Your Phone Battery Is Stained with Blood

The lithium-ion batteries in smartphones and EVs depend on Congolese cobalt; from Leopold II's rubber terror to Lumumba's assassination to today's few dollars a day, the same history of plunder continues.

The environmental bill for EVs is paid by Congolese miners

When Siddharth Kara first entered the cobalt mines of Katanga in 2018, he saw a vast chasm of pure cobalt stone, with nearly 15,000 workers crammed together, barely able to move; the dull thud of hammers and iron bars striking rock, toxic dust, and shouts merged into one. Most of these workers were teenage boys, without safety equipment, earning only a few dollars a day, while the cobalt ended up in the batteries of smartphones, laptops, and EVs. He described the scene as akin to the forced labor of building the pyramids 2,000 years ago, and pointed to the core contradiction: the EV transition promoted by the developed world in the name of "environmental protection" is destroying the environment of Africa's interior.

— Siddharth Kara
12:28

Trade without goods to trade can only be slavery

In the late 1890s, E.D. Morel, a young clerk at a British shipping company, checked cargo manifests from Congo at the Antwerp docks and found that ships arrived from Africa loaded with ivory and wild rubber, but the ships sailing to Africa carried only soldiers, guns, and ammunition—no trade goods at all. He realized this was not trade but evidence of a slave-labor system 4,000 miles away: weapons and soldiers were sent to force extraction, and what came back were resources obtained without capital. The company tried to bribe him and transfer him away; ultimately he resigned and became the investigative journalist who exposed the Congo atrocities.

19:53

The lie of the civilizing mission was destroyed by a missionary's camera

Leopold II packaged himself as a humanitarian, welcoming Catholic and Protestant missionaries into the Congo Free State. Among them, the African-American missionary William Henry Shepard used an early box camera to photograph the civilization of the Bakuba people: wide streets, fine weaving and woodcarving—but he also witnessed forced labor: women held as hostages, men driven into the rainforest to collect rubber. His photographs and writings became evidence for the later Congo Reform Association, letting the outside world see the truth of the "civilizing mission" for the first time.

— Ramona Austin
27:27

Bullets had to be accounted for with severed hands, halving the population

Leopold's private army entered villages, seized women as hostages, and forced men to deliver a monthly quota of rubber. Each time soldiers drew ammunition, they had to prove the bullets had killed someone, by bringing back the severed hands of the dead; if they missed or used bullets for hunting, they cut hands off living people to make up the quota. Missionaries like Shepard photographed many mutilated bodies. The result: the population of the Congo Free State fell from about 20 million to about 10 million, a decline of about 50%. This is not rhetoric; it is a death toll that was recorded.

— Adam Hochschild
35:00

Saying resources should belong to Congo made you a communist

On June 30, 1960, Patrice Lumumba, the first Prime Minister of Congo, spoke publicly at the independence ceremony, directly attacking colonialism and uttering the episode's key line: Congo's land should benefit Congo's children first. He was not a traditional elite—expelled from Catholic and church schools, self-taught, at one point a beer salesman, and it was his network of customers that later became the organizational base for his political party. This claim of resource sovereignty quickly earned him the label "communist" from Western intelligence agencies.

— Georges Nzongola-Ntalaja
46:33

A prime minister who wouldn't cooperate was replaced by one who would

Just 11 days after Lumumba took office, Belgium helped Katanga secede, cutting off the economic engine of Congo's most mineral-rich province. After appeals to the UN failed, he turned to the Soviet Union; the US first tried to assassinate him with poisoned toothpaste, and when that failed, the task was handed to Belgium: he was taken to Katanga, tortured and executed, his body dismembered and dissolved in acid, leaving only a tooth kept by an assassin as a souvenir. Siddharth's summary is direct and cold: you either cooperate, or you get chopped up and replaced by someone willing to cooperate.

— Siddharth Kara
51:23

The resource for the second automotive revolution is still in Congo

In lithium-ion batteries, cobalt increases energy density and maintains thermal stability, letting devices "store more and not catch fire." The smartphone boom sent cobalt demand soaring geometrically around 2012, and EVs have taken over since. The first automotive revolution was built on rubber; the second is built on cobalt, and both resources are primarily located in Congo. Siddharth's judgment: we are living through the second automotive revolution, and cobalt's source is still in Congo, just like rubber, and life there has consequently "descended into disaster."

— Siddharth Kara
52:29

Cobalt is cheap because miners have no other choice

Large mining companies buy land and evict villages, pushing an already impoverished population into a choice of "either do dangerous work today and survive, or have no food today." After workers are injured, get cancer, or die in pit collapses, thousands more are waiting to replace them. This effectively captive modern slave labor force puts continuous downward pressure on cobalt prices. Siddharth still holds hope: historically, after Leopold's atrocities were exposed, the reform movement toppled the regime, and he believes the truth today will likewise give rise to a new champion.

— Siddharth Kara

Glossary
