Google Paid $10 Million for a Bankrupt Airline's Data: Data Is the Moat in the AI Era

In the AI era, a company's most valuable asset is its data. Google paid $10 million for a bankrupt airline's data; AI agents with legitimate access are the new biggest threat, and data infrastructure must be rebuilt.

3:13

Models and compute have zero switching costs; only data is uniquely yours

The Eon founders point out that models and compute have near-zero switching costs, leaving data as the only truly unique asset a company owns. Two days ago, Google bought data from the bankrupt Spirit Airlines for $10 million, with another bidder reportedly being Mercor. This is no longer an isolated case: CEOs are often asked 'would you sell your data?', and AI labs even go to Wall Street to buy data from hedge funds. Real enterprise data has become scarce fuel for training agents, and even a bankrupt company's data can become an auction item.

6:43

Real enterprise data is scarcer than you think; much public data is synthetic

The host questions whether 'data is the new oil' held in the slogan era, but post-training and reinforcement learning have turned it into real demand. Companies like Applied Comp offer fine-tuning services on datasets, but Eon believes truly usable real enterprise datasets are extremely scarce, and much public data is synthetic. Spirit Airlines' data can train airline customer service agents, but it also serves as a panoramic sample of how a large enterprise operates—the hierarchy, middle management, and worker collaboration—which is exactly the material agent training lacks most.

9:49

Data silos are not a tool problem but a misalignment of incentives

Data tools are actually plentiful; the problem is that data is locked within business units. In the past, data teams chose their own projects and tools (Fivetran, DBT, Monte Carlo). Now CEOs, educated by ChatGPT, demand data activation, but data is scattered across 20-year-old systems, even on servers no one dares to shut down. Extracting data requires engineers, at the cost of security, compliance, and production disruption; the incentives of business owners and platform teams are fundamentally misaligned. Eon's approach is to first automatically map, classify, and build a semantic layer, then continuously supply data without impacting production.

15:00

Security models were built for humans; the intruders are now agents

Existing security models target human threats. At AWS, Eon experienced a customer whose environment was 60% exposed to ransomware because resources were not properly mapped, classified, or tagged. Now the same threat comes from non-human actors—AI agents with legitimate access to databases can drop a table in an instant. Detection and recovery methodologies are similar, but the speed is extreme. A few months ago, nearly every enterprise customer either feared agent attacks or had already experienced one; organizations must design defenses assuming they are already compromised.

18:49

More agents mean dashboards become more essential, not less

The enterprise data stack used to revolve around humans asking questions via dashboards. Agents will dynamically reason over larger datasets, access SaaS and historical data, and take direct action. The guests believe dashboards will not disappear but become more common—humans need them to understand what agents are doing in the environment. Coding agents will write most code, agents will activate other agents, and tracking non-human identities (NHIs) becomes a core challenge, leading to a surge of NHI security companies. When non-technical employees build agents with Lovable, they don't even know the data compliance boundaries, creating new actors in the organization that are not bound by rules.

22:50

Legacy pipelines aren't connected because full context wasn't needed before

Take buying coffee with a card: the transaction is written to a database, extracted, and sent elsewhere for separate processing. The pipelines are not connected because full context wasn't needed before—you only handled predefined questions. Today, if you can intelligently collect, efficiently store, and activate data, you can enable teams to generate combinatorial insights: for example, merging a list of 'people in New York who like burgers' with 'people in New York who like pizza' yields a new audience. Data ingestion is growing wildly, old ETL tools can't handle multi-party noise, and vendors like Databricks are reinventing themselves.

28:13

AI transformation is faster than cloud migration, yet customers have less control

The two Eon founders did large-scale cloud migrations at AWS, with customers running from thousands to hundreds of thousands of servers; transformation required both technical and human investment. The pace of AI-era transformation is an intensified version of the cloud era, but customers are losing control—fearing system crashes, data leaks, and IP exfiltration—and control has become a inhibiting factor. Cloud is abstract, but AI is understandable to everyone (the ChatGPT moment), so boards push companies to adopt AI from both 'opportunity' and 'fear' angles. Traditional enterprises that need to deploy quickly can only shorten sales cycles by bringing in Silicon Valley engineers with mature solutions and playbooks.

31:21

The fastest AI transformation path for big companies is to buy a startup

The way enterprises consume software is changing. Large traditional enterprises want AI but internal processes take one to two years, so they bring in top engineers and ready-made solutions to accelerate; product-led growth (PLG) is particularly effective in AI infrastructure, with Cognition as a case of PLG first, then moving into large customers. Another aggressive approach is to directly acquire a startup and turn it into an AI company, thereby improving gross margin and efficiency faster. The guests believe 'the revolution has just begun'—most companies are still at the starting line of their AI journey, but eventually they will all be pushed forward.
