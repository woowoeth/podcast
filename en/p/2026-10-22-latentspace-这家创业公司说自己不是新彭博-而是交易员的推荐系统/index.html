This startup says it is not a new Bloomberg, but a recommendation system for traders

The founder positions the company as a combination of Bloomberg, Claude, and Netflix: value lies not in giving traders more data, but in proactively surfacing second-order signals they wouldn't think to look for, like Netflix does.

In four seconds, Moshi learns 300 new data points

The founder uses an analogy to illustrate the data density Moshi handles: this four-second sentence, she says, coincides with Moshi learning 300 new data points. Moshi is not another chatbot, but a real-time signal provider for commodity, energy, and macro traders—its goal is to deliver in 30 seconds a judgment that would otherwise take days to assemble, shifting the question of ‘which information matters’ from traders' heads into the system.

9:52

Not a new Bloomberg—a Netflix for traders

When asked if it is ‘a new Bloomberg,’ the founder clearly denies it, saying if Bloomberg, Claude, and Netflix had a child, that would be Moshi. The key difference lies in a homegrown recommendation system: when a trader is focused on one thing, the system knows they should care about second- and third-order relationships—for instance, how an energy company's moves indirectly affect oil prices. These signals won't appear in a news feed; they require an interconnected relationship graph to actively surface them.

— Anika
11:54

Recommendation systems go ignored, yet are indispensable

The host notes that he hosts a dedicated recommendation-systems track at AI conferences each year, staffed by engineers who built YouTube, Netflix, Instagram, and TikTok's recommendation algorithms—yet the track is regularly empty. His explanation: recommendation systems are made to ‘look easy.’ Both agree further: anyone can now build their own Claude dashboard, but getting others to actually use it is a UI problem. Solve the recommendation system well, and the UI problem dissolves.

14:08

Twitter's Firehose costs $300,000 monthly and remains all noise

The host recalls that during his time at hedge fund Balyasny, it was among the earliest institutions to purchase X (Twitter) Firehose data, at roughly $300,000 monthly, yet data quality was poor—many post nonsense, and others deliberately manipulate markets. This exposes buyers' core concern: after paying for alternative data, they still may not dare trade on it directly.

16:12

Disagreement between Bloomberg, Reuters, and the Journal is itself a signal

Moshi's approach to noise is not to filter out uncertain information, but to display it with a confidence score. The founder illustrates: if Reuters, Bloomberg, and the Wall Street Journal disagree on how many ships or planes passed a location, that disagreement itself is a valuable data point—because nobody knows the truth, which precisely signals a potential trading opportunity.

— Anika
17:16

Customer data stays in-house; self-built models follow

On whether to build a custom vision-language model to parse PDFs, the founder names two criteria: first, customer data doesn't go to third parties unless you can verify how they'll use it; second, if the team has engineering capacity—most Moshi staff have NASA backgrounds—building in-house is the better choice. But she acknowledges not every company has that option.

— Anika
19:28

‘You are the team’: engineers forced to do ten jobs solo

Among those who found the founder at the conference, beyond traders were many AI engineers, all describing pressure from management: ‘We don't need a team—you are the team.’ A system meant for ten people now falls to one. Worse, some build it only to find colleagues say they need it but never actually open it. The problem becomes non-adoption, not non-delivery.

— Anika
24:54

One open role, four days, 2500 applications

On hiring versus sales priorities, the founder says finding customers is now the bottleneck, not hiring—one job posting draws 2500 applications in four days, forcing it to close. As a first-time founder, she admits not knowing at first how much noise saturates the pipeline: applicants using unauthorized work eligibility, for instance.

— Anika
