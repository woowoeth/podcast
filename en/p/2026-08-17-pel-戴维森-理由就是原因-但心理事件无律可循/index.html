Mental Events Are Anomalous, So Free Will and Determinism Can Coexist

Davidson answers the mind-body problem with token identity: mental and physical events are the same event, causation requires covering laws, but mental descriptions have no strict laws, so determinism and freedom can coexist.

3:24

Mind-body identity holds at the token level, not the type level

Davidson's starting point is everyday causation: I see physical things in the world, they cause sensations, which give rise to thoughts and decisions, and decisions make the body act. So he rejects a Cartesian mind independent of the causal order. He holds that every mental event is identical with some brain event, but this is token-level, not type-level, identity — the same event can have both a mental and a physical description.

7:52

Acting on a reason is itself a causal relation

In the 1963 paper, Davidson sets out to refute the view then influenced by Wittgenstein and Anscombe: that a reason merely places an action in a "logical space" of self-justification, and is not a cause. He argues that rationalization — acting on a reason — is itself a causal relation. The key is that the same thing can have different descriptions: describing it by a reason and describing it physically do not conflict, and the former does not exclude the latter.

10:12

Reasons don't look like causes only because we stand in the first person

The host uses 5+5=10 to explain why people feel reasons are not like causes: from the first person, I know 5 plus 5 equals 10 because I can see that it equals 10; saying "one brain state causes another brain state, and then 10 pops out" sounds bizarre. But from the third person, the action can indeed be described as a causal chain. The two domains are usually kept strictly apart, and Davidson wants to show they can hold at once.

24:15

Mental events enter into causation yet obey no laws

The paper opens with three principles: mental and physical events causally interact; causation requires strict covering laws; and the mental is "anomalous" — no strict deterministic law can predict and explain mental events. If the first two hold, the third seems false: mental events sit inside this causal web, so they should have nomological character. Yet we have no intuitive laws describing desire and action, because an entire network of beliefs and desires lies in between. Davidson's aim is to prove the three can be consistent.

36:05

Mind and body are the same token, yet there are no bridge laws

Two dimensions yield four positions: whether there are psychophysical correlation laws, and whether mind and body are one or two. Nomological monism is type identity theory; nomological dualism is parallelism or epiphenomenalism; anomalous dualism is Cartesian dualism; Davidson chooses anomalous monism — mental and physical events are the same token, but there are no bridge laws. This position preserves supervenience while avoiding reductionism.

41:49

Supervenience is not reducibility, and the predicate true is the example

Davidson draws an analogy with two languages L and L': L is purely physical sentences, and L' adds the predicate "is true in L." Saying "my foot moved two feet" is a physical sentence; but saying that sentence is true introduces truth — and truth belongs to the mental/semantic level. Tarski's theorem shows that in a consistent language there is no syntactic predicate that applies to all and only true sentences, so supervenience does not imply reducibility.

50:00

Stimulating the amygdala causes fear, and still builds no psychological law

The host confronts the scientific objection head-on: stimulating the amygdala can cause fear — is that not a law? Davidson's reply is that at the token level a physical concept can refer to a given mental content, but at the type level no scientific law can be established; all you get are probabilistic generalizations. Because beliefs and desires are explained through further beliefs and desires, this holism means mental descriptions can never be replaced item by item by neuroscience.

Glossary

token-token identity
Each mental event is identical with some physical event, but mental types are not identical with physical types.
type-type identity
Mental types correspond one-to-one with physical types, and therefore there are psychophysical laws.
anomalous monism
Mind and body are identical at the same token, but there are no strict psychophysical laws.
nomological
Relating to natural laws; Davidson holds that causation requires covering laws.
supervenience
Mental properties are determined by physical properties, but are not thereby reducible to physical laws.
