Talent lived in the villages; the exam window came too late

Without the restored college entrance exam, Liu Zhenyun (刘震云) would still be farming; his cousin who solved calculus between harvests probably should have won the Nobel Prize in Physics.

4:03

Jokes amplified through the internet become cruel

Liu Zhenyun (刘震云) distinguishes between individual joking and jokes life itself tells—sometimes the latter are crueler than seriousness, and unstoppable. Once circulated on the internet, this ‘net woven by humanity’, jokes transform truth into emotion at blinding speed. By the time truth finishes putting on its shoes, lies have circled the globe. This frames the title “闲的玩笑”: the verbs ‘play’ and ‘laugh’ together mean laughter can't survive the play—the punchline ends in tears.

— Liu Zhenyun
9:04

Testing whether a title will be misread, using AI as thought partner

When Luo Yonghuo (罗永浩) received the advance copy, he worried that “闲的玩笑” risked being misread as suggestive, salacious, or internet-comedy material. He fed the title to an AI's deep reasoning engine and got back: roughly 50% probability the title will be understood negatively—in directions like ‘suggestive’, even ‘erotic’, or ‘meme content’. But the AI also caught the bittersweet, tear-streaked undercurrent the title carries. This prompted the publisher to add the phrase ‘There are many jokes in this world, and they're destined to end in tears’ to the cover, preempting reader expectation and blocking ambiguity when the title appears alone.

— Luo Yonghao
17:07

A small town's intellectual is undone by his own learning

The protagonist, Du Taibai (杜太白), is a middle-school teacher in a small county, making his living from knowledge, cultured and versed in classical verse and allusions. Yet that exact learning was his trap: at a dinner, he and another man compete in Li Shangyin (李商隐) couplets, brawl over whether the wife in the poem is alive or dead, the video goes viral, and he loses his job. Later, hosting a wedding, another incident costs him that job too. Liu's diagnosis: Du Taibai is ‘not really an intellectual’—he has knowledge but lacks ‘discernment’. That distinction explains why his learning becomes a punchline at the crucial moment instead of a weapon.

— Liu Zhenyun
33:11

Paris, New York, and London actually live on county streets

In the novel, Du Taibai names his children and nieces Paris, New York, London. Liu emphasizes this is not metaphor but fact—his actual nephew has three children with these three names, and they ‘run around the village streets’ in real life. He says the book's function partly is to unearth the ‘brilliance’ buried under the weight of rural life: an illiterate tailor obsessed with China's Terracotta Army, a meditation-hall owner who calls himself Diogenes—brilliance isn't the rich man's monopoly, ordinary people carry it too, just nobody writes it down.

— Liu Zhenyun
43:14

A cousin who solved calculus between harvests

Liu's cousin went by the nickname ‘Newton’—because he'd aged out of the restored college-exam window, he was bound to farm for life, but he self-taught calculus to university level, deriving equations squatting in the fields in spare time. Liu argues: if he and his cousin had both taken the entrance exam normally back then, both might now be researching physics and mathematics; his cousin might have won the Nobel Prize in Physics decades ago. His conclusion: objective conditions can erase subjective effort—even if you're born with Newton's or Einstein's brain, fate will undo you anyway.

— Liu Zhenyun
1:01:24

Living knots and dead knots—the world needs mercy

Liu recounts a mediation by Uncle Yungen (云根叔): a young couple fights, the uncle tells him, ‘You're mostly to blame’—a sentence that carries a teaching: nobody is flawless; you tolerate your own faults, so why not another's? Late in the book, Du Taibai's pig jumps a cliff and is reborn, echoing the same theme: everything in life becomes either a dead knot—pulled tight, irreversible—or a living knot—leaving room to untie it. He declares: living knots are mercy. The world needs living knots, not locked doors.

— Liu Zhenyun
2:08:59

Grandmother sold her silver pins to pay five yuan in tuition

At five, Liu saw other kids going to school and insisted he go too. Tuition was five yuan—a fortune then. His illiterate grandmother took off the silver hair pins she'd worn for years, sold them in town, and got five yuan for his tuition. Years later, when Liu came back to visit her after moving to the county for school, the silver pins were gone—she'd replaced them with a date-tree branch. She explained: a branch lasts longer, more durable. Liu says that detail shook him—an illiterate woman with such discernment, teaching by example, shaped how he sees the world.

— Liu Zhenyun
3:05:41

Hold on until you're down to the last pane of glass

On the years his writing went unrecognized, Liu says real persistence isn't teeth-gritting in the usual sense—it's the choice to keep going after you've seen your own flaws clearly. At that point, 99% quit. He uses a metaphor: armies don't retreat because they hit the city wall; they retreat when only the last pane of glass remains between them and breakthrough. Push once more and the pane shatters. He eventually broke through by placing rice grains on page three of his manuscripts to tell whether editors actually read them, submitting for years until Anhui Literature (安徽文学) accepted him.

— Liu Zhenyun
