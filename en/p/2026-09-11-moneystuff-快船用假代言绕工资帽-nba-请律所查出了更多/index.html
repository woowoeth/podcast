The Clippers used fake endorsements to get around the salary cap

The Clippers first paid millions of dollars to a company called Aspiration Partners, nominally for vague environmental services; Aspiration then paid that money to Kawhi Leonard in the form of endorsement fees, while Leonard did almost no endorsing. When Pablo Torre first reported it, the claim was still disputed, and the Clippers kept denying it, but the investigation the NBA had Wachtell Lipton do basically confirmed it — and found more similar arrangements.

Even the scoreboard company was pulled in to move the money

What the Wachtell report found was not just the one Aspiration payment. The Clippers also paid "consulting fees" to companies that provided no consulting services at all, including the company that made the scoreboard for their arena. The method: the Clippers would pay a consulting fee, then call and ask for a higher purchase price on the scoreboard, with the clear expectation that the other side would pass that money on to Kawhi Leonard. The money went in a circle, and the ultimate purpose was still to give the star a raise.

An independent law firm investigation is not there to clear you

Max had assumed an outside law firm's independent investigation was meant to produce a clean bill of health after a scandal, but the Wachtell report made things look worse. Matt offers a more cynical explanation: the classic use of a law firm investigation, under the shadow of a DOJ or regulatory probe, is to hand over some "bad apple" as a scapegoat so the company gets credit for cooperating. But this time there was no DOJ pressure to shut down the NBA, the NBA seems to have given Wachtell a lot of room, and Wachtell clearly had a great time investigating.

— Matt Levine
10:14

Once the DAT premium vanished, the arbitrage ran in reverse

Empery Digital was originally an electric motorcycle company, and in July 2025 it transformed into a digital asset treasury company (DAT), stockpiling about $500 million of bitcoin, with its stock at one point trading at a premium to its holdings. The logic: put $1 of bitcoin into a DAT and it becomes $2 of stock. But the premium quickly disappeared and DATs began trading at a discount, so the arbitrage flipped — use $0.90 of stock to buy $1 of bitcoin, buy the DAT, break it up, sell the bitcoin. Hence DAT activism.

— Matt Levine
17:20

Is a shareholder who shorts bitcoin disloyal

One of the core disputes in the Empery case: an activist buys discounted DAT stock while shorting an equal amount of bitcoin, becoming delta neutral and earning only the discount convergence. The company argues this must be disclosed, because the other shareholders are all theoretically long bitcoin, while these people are bitcoin neutral, their interests are not aligned, and they should not be eligible to run for the board. Matt thinks the argument is "wrong, but only accidentally wrong": if the DAT is a vehicle for believers to hold bitcoin (like Strategy), it makes sense; but most DAT shareholders are the same hedge funds, all of whom came for the premium and are unhappy about the discount, so their interests are actually aligned.

— Matt Levine
23:26

Cede & Co holds most American stocks

In his column Matt mentions a fact most people do not know: most shares of most American companies are registered in the name of Cede & Co, the nominee of DTC. A 1998 report said Cede & Co held 83% of all US stocks; DTC now custodies about $74 trillion of stocks. When you hold shares through Robinhood, what actually happens is that Robinhood has a record on DTC's ledger, and Cede & Co is on the company's shareholder register. The system grew out of the efficiency revolution of the 1960s and 70s, when paper stock certificates were centralised in one place and only name changes were recorded.

— Matt Levine
27:31

Robinhood's tokenisation is not an on-chain shareholder register

Tokenised stocks can mean many things, and one ideal version is this: every company maintains a public blockchain shareholder register, no longer needing intermediaries like DTC or Robinhood, and the company can build a relationship directly with its shareholders. But that is not what Robinhood did — it bought AMC stock itself, then issued derivative securities on-chain representing those shares and sold them to overseas investors, effectively "making its own copy of AMC stock." AMC's Adam Aaron was unhappy, and Vlad Tenev asked "what's the problem?"

— Matt Levine
31:35

Apollo pays 100 basis points extra for its "aggressive" reputation

A paper measures private equity sponsors' reputations in three ways: having ChatGPT and Claude rank the most aggressive PE firms; looking at who has done liability management exercises (quasi-forced debt swaps); and putting Apollo in one bucket by itself and the other 24 firms in another. The conclusion: the most aggressive sponsors' portfolio companies borrow at noticeably higher yields, Apollo's loans are about 100 basis points more expensive than other sponsors', and the sample's average yield is 717 basis points. Matt calls it the "jerk premium" or the "aggression premium" — creditors expect to be squeezed, so they charge upfront.

Glossary

DAT (Digital Asset Treasury)
A company that puts crypto assets such as bitcoin onto a public company's balance sheet and arbitrages the stock price premium.
liability management exercise
A quasi-forced debt swap led by a private equity sponsor that transfers value away from some creditors.
Cede & Co
The nominee of DTC, in whose name most US listed company shares are registered.
DTC (Depository Trust Company)
The central US securities custody and clearing institution; Cede & Co is its nominee.
delta neutral
Holding related long and short positions at the same time so that a change in the underlying asset's price does not affect the overall position.
