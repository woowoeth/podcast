Strategic Reserves Are Nearly Drained — the Oil Price Crisis Is Only Now Beginning

Oil hasn't spiraled over the past six months because of three buffers: strategic reserves, sanctioned oil, and China buying less. All three are now being drained, and the real shock is only just about to reach diesel and prices.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:03

Close Hormuz and a fifth of the world's oil is gone

The war began with Iran closing the Strait of Hormuz, cutting off roughly 20% of global oil supply outright. This was the move everyone treated as the "nuclear option" — nobody wanted to see it, because it's too bad for every side. Once the strait closed, oil shot from around $70 a barrel to well over $100. But the market didn't spiral from there; it found several buffers that held prices down. The real question isn't "why did it rise" but "why didn't it rise more."

3:09

Three buffers, each less usable than the last

The first is the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve — backup crude stored in salt caverns along the Gulf Coast after the 1970s Arab oil embargo — plus the commercial inventories held by refiners, which were at high levels before the conflict. The second is sanctioned oil: a large volume of "dark fleet" crude from Iran and Russia drifting at sea, which in March the US temporarily allowed other countries to buy. The third is China — the world's largest oil importer, normally buying about 12 million barrels a day, roughly 10% of global supply, which earlier this year bought about 3 million barrels a day less, effectively leaving that oil to others. Together, these three held oil below $100 and the national average gasoline price just under $5.

6:12

Ceasefire talks made analysts look foolish en masse

In June the US and Iran began talks and reached a ceasefire, and market sentiment flipped 180 degrees instantly: from "extreme crude shortage" to "crude surplus," with analysts predicting oil would fall to $70 or even $60 a barrel. But the peace deal collapsed within weeks, Iran closed the strait again, and Trump declared the ceasefire over. So it was back to the state at the start of the conflict. This episode shows one thing: this round of prices contains a large bet on "the war ends soon," and that bet has repeatedly failed.

8:15

Six months of draining, and the buffers are nearly dry

Commercial inventories have been drawn down, strategic reserves have been released — and reserves can't be released in unlimited quantity; the government only authorized a specific amount. The US has taken more than 130 million barrels out of the strategic reserve, leaving about 280 million, which sounds like a lot but is approaching the authorized ceiling. Worse, this time there's an added refinery crisis: Middle Eastern refineries were struck in the war, and Ukraine, in self-defense, knocked out a large chunk of Russia's refining capacity — and Russia is the world's second-largest diesel exporter. Once a refinery is destroyed, it takes months to restore. So the problem now isn't whether there's crude; it's that there isn't enough refining capacity to produce the fuel the world needs.

9:16

Venezuela can't put out a fire this close

The Trump administration has pitched Venezuela as the solution, and its reserves may be the world's largest. But reserves being there doesn't mean production capacity is there: to actually raise output you need to invest money, move in rigs, hire people, drill, and then ship the barrels out — that's a matter of years and billions of dollars of investment. As for other "miracle solutions," Benoit says there basically aren't any; you can only hope for "small miracles" — like China deciding to raise diesel output, or refinery repairs finishing early. But none of these take effect tomorrow.

10:17

Gas stations haven't blown up yet — diesel blew up first

US consumers haven't seen prices spike at the pump yet; what's actually rising is diesel — the fuel that drives the heavy trucks moving consumer goods from one end of the country to the other. So the question becomes: will companies cut back on deliveries? When do they pass costs on to consumers? Once diesel rises, every kind of business has to factor it into its pricing. This is the real channel through which this shock transmits from energy markets into the real economy, and it's why "gas prices are fine" is a misleading comfort.

12:23

Two paths: absorb it, or pass it on

Businesses have only two choices. One is to absorb it without raising prices: Jim Barber, a third-generation farmer in Pennsylvania, has raised his beef price only once in five years — even though the cost of hauling in hay is up 15% to 20%, he eats it himself, because he values his relationship with customers and believes the conflict will end soon and that holding on will be rewarded. A farmer's fuel exposure is triple-layered — fertilizer, hauling in hay, and shipping his own goods out — so energy costs can be paid three times over or more. The other is to pass it on: Ambix, which makes plastic parts, uses resin, and resin comes from oil; some costs rose 30%, 40%, 50%. At first it resisted raising prices, but as the war dragged on it began passing the 60 cents its suppliers added straight to customers.

15:36

Six-dollar diesel in August, and the shock starts embedding in the economy

When energy prices eased this summer, many businesses had a spell of optimism, thinking maybe they were coming out of the woods. Then in August tensions flared again, fuel prices rose again, and recently diesel hit $6 a gallon. Owen thinks this is the moment when some businesses and economists began to realize "the price shock is truly embedding in the economy." Meanwhile, JPMorgan's commodities analysts said in their latest forecast that they no longer know how to model it — there's no baseline, because they can't see around the corner. On paper many economic indicators are healthy, but consumers feel terrible about prices, and nobody — not oil executives, not politicians, not CEOs — can say clearly how long this lasts.

Glossary

strategic petroleum reserves
Backup crude the US has stored in Gulf Coast salt caverns since the 1970s oil embargo.
tank bottoms
The point at which crude in a tank is too low to be pumped out — inventories completely bottomed out.
dark fleet
A fleet of tankers carrying sanctioned oil while evading regulation.
resin
A petrochemical feedstock made from oil and the core ingredient in plastic products.
