Don't tell children they'll see each other again: goodbye's pain is part of being alive

Susan Cain says the most effective way to comfort children is not to deny the pain of separation, but to tell them ‘this is part of life’—suppressing this pain is actually resisting the cost of love itself.

Honesty about separation calms children better than false promises they'll meet again

Cain and her children grew attached to two donkeys (Lucky and Norman) during a country stay. When saying goodbye, the children cried themselves to sleep. She found that the most effective comfort was not saying ‘don't worry, we'll see them again’ (which might be a lie), nor ‘it's not so bad,’ but honestly telling them: saying goodbye is part of life, it hurts, but it will get better soon, and you'll have happy memories. This honesty calms children better than reassurance.

— Susan Cain
7:21

Resisting goodbye is refusing to pay the cost of love

Cain explains that both children and adults have an illusion that ‘goodbye shouldn't be part of life,’ and it's this resistance itself that creates extra suffering. She believes that the bonds of attachment and love may be the best part of being alive, even the whole point of existence, so breaking these bonds naturally hurts—but if you can understand this pain beforehand as ‘part of being alive itself,’ you can reduce unnecessary struggle.

— Susan Cain
12:48

Your children are not yours: they're on loan to you for a time

Since her children were very young, Cain has read Kahlil Gibran's poem ‘On Children’ to remind herself: children are not yours; they belong to ‘the house of tomorrow’ not ‘the house of yesterday.’ She says this understanding made her a better mother, not just for dealing with empty nest, but throughout the entire parenting process—your love for your children is meant to be passed forward, not to be repaid by them.

— Susan Cain
18:04

Every child arrives bearing an innate power—your job is recognizing which one

Cain uses mythology as a metaphor for her parenting philosophy: each child is born with some form of power. The parent's task is to help the child recognize which power they have—some get a wand, some get a lightsaber; Luke Skywalker doesn't get a wand, and Harry Potter doesn't get a lightsaber. What parents should do is not impose some standard power on the child, but help them see what they were born with and learn to use it.

— Susan Cain
20:07

Quiet children don't need to become loud: they need mastery and survival skills

Responding to the common question ‘how do you turn quiet power into loud power,’ Cain's answer is that the goal is not to change the child's nature, but a two-track approach: help the child gain mastery in fields they truly love (mastery brings confidence, not the reverse), while teaching them the survival skills needed to function in a world that favors extroversion—just as adults must do both these things.

— Susan Cain
22:43

Three words calm frightened quiet children: ‘it's no big deal’—with understanding, no rescue

When a child cries about going to a noisy soccer practice, Cain suggests parents respond with three words: ‘it's no big deal.’ The key difference is that parents must first acknowledge and understand that the child's discomfort and fear are real and normal (you feel it too sometimes), while still insisting the child go—this is neither ignoring the child's feelings nor rescuing them from discomfort out of fear of hurting them, but rather ‘I understand, and I believe you can do this.’

— Susan Cain
24:25

Introverted parents project their childhood pain onto their quiet children

Cain points out that each type of parent has its own trap: introverted parents, when they see their child hesitating at the edge of a team, easily recall all their own painful childhood moments and magnify them, assuming the child is experiencing the same suffering; extroverted parents, having never had that experience, completely fail to understand what their child is going through. She quotes musician Ben Folds' song lyrics where he tells his child ‘you're too much like me, I'm sorry’ to illustrate this complex moment of empathy for introverted parents.

— Susan Cain
35:50

Quiet is not lonely: ask your child instead of imagining her suffering

Parents often see a quiet child shooting hoops alone or eating pizza alone and imagine ‘she has no friends’ or ‘she has social anxiety.’ Cain uses two real examples to show why the key is to ask the child how they really feel rather than imagining for them: one girl just needed alone time to recharge after a full day of socializing; Cain's own four-year-old daughter simply wanted to savor each bite of pizza slowly. Distinguishing between ‘the child yearns for connection but doesn't know how to fit in’ and ‘the child is just self-regulating and deliberately withdrawing’ determines whether parents should intervene.

— Susan Cain
