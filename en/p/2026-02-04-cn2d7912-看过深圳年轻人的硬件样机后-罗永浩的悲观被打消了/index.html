The source is abstract—the film must make the man real

Shuang Xuetao explains the radical rewrite: in the novel, Li Mingqi is assembled by others' searches—less a doer than a fantasist sustained by delusion, with few real events and much of the story unwritten. But film requires a protagonist constantly present, visibly acting. So they rewrote him as an engineering-trained man who actually builds a rocket that flies, making the character concrete.

19:22

What audiences need to believe is harder than mastering a dialect

On set, Jiang Qiming and Shuang Xuetao debated late into the night: how does Li Mingqi's mad ambition connect to his lived life? A novel answers this with a metaphor; film must build it through Jiang Qiming's behavior and speech, moment by moment. The goal: make audiences believe he is unhinged yet understand him, follow him willingly. Shuang Xuetao calls this the role's central difficulty.

— Shuang Xuetao

Sixteen hours of shooting to perfect one gesture of climbing

The Soviet advisors scene: foreigners, translator, friend, Jiang Qiming, and an actual aircraft prop—a micro-theater in cold steel, filmed continuously for over a dozen hours. Mid-shoot, Shuang Xuetao called an engineer to verify height details. Jiang Qiming kept feeling they needed one more push. Working it out on set, they found the gesture of gradual ascent.

54:50

The young hardware makers in Shenzhen rebuilt his faith in the next generation

Luo Yonghao says the past two years made him often pessimistic about the environment. But visiting Shenzhen and seeing hardware startups founded by people in their early twenties changed everything. Their ambitions are grand, their prototypes already built—things he never dared imagine in his youth in 1972. He now believes in the next generation again: a cohort this spirited cannot fail by their thirties and forties.

— Luo Yonghao
1:39:15

His frustration stems from standards set impossibly high

Shuang Xuetao says his frustration is not a matter of talent but of standards set far higher than competence—if he were content to be a second-rate writer, or a dutiful product manager, life would be smooth. But he demands more, so he stumbles, the same as the man building the aircraft in the story.

— Shuang Xuetao
1:45:16

Exactly five years was how long he could bear the banking work

When Shuang Xuetao quit his bank credit job in 2012, he had exactly five years of tenure. Not because writing income exceeded his salary—it was far below—and he endured two more years of meager income after resigning. He quit not for literary dreams but because the job had become impossible. Writing was a small hole carved through stifling days, a place to breathe.

— Shuang Xuetao
2:09:35

A debut author brings risk to a publisher, not opportunity

Shuang Xuetao analyzes literary publishing's contraction: a debut writer is risk to a publisher, not opportunity, while publishing a classic is stable business. So as the market shrinks, publishers take fewer risks. He recalls when literary magazines thrived twenty years ago and publishers bet on new voices. Today, debuts have fewer publishing outlets despite no decline in writing quality.

— Shuang Xuetao
2:17:49

Fame did not make him handsome—it made people see his face

Luo Yonghao says when he first met Jiang Qiming and watched his series, he did not think him handsome, only vividly distinctive. Jiang Qiming himself says he did not hear the word 'handsome' applied to him until after the series aired, when someone told him directly—before that, never. Luo Yonghao emphasizes this cannot be read as mere flattery from fame: many accomplished, award-winning actors never hear the word. What happened to Jiang Qiming is rare.

— Luo Yonghao
