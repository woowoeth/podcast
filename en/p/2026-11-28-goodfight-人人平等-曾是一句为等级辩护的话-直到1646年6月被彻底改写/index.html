The Good Fight 2026-11-28

‘All are equal’ once defended hierarchy, until June 1646 rewrote it

What ancient Rome and early Christianity meant by ‘all are equal’ was people equally subject to law or God—a doctrine used to justify hierarchy. In 1646, John Lilburne misread it as ‘everyone is each other's peer’, and modern egalitarian politics was born.

First-name informality at Oxford conceals rather than reveals hierarchy

Bejan discovered that Oxford's practice of calling everyone by first name appears egalitarian but operates oppositely: senior male professors can casually say ‘call me John’, while younger teachers, female teachers, and teachers of color are automatically called by first name anyway, with one student even correcting Bejan after her introduction as ‘Professor Bejan’, saying ‘we don't do that here’. She argues that uniform first-naming masks real power differences, freeing those with power from reflecting on their responsibility to students.

Demanding academic titles in democratic politics violates democratic norms

Munck cites Jill Biden's insistence on being called Dr. Jill Biden as an example; Bejan argues this indeed crosses an important democratic norm: titles are relevant and informative in an academic context, but appropriating such authority in democratic public life is inappropriate. At the same time, she defends Biden: women and minority-race scholars often lack the authority that male and white colleagues enjoy by default, which may explain why they're more inclined to insist on titles. She cites The Authority Gap and the example of Margaret Thatcher working with a voice coach to lower her pitch to illustrate this authority deficit.

23:20

Education requires hierarchy; politics demands its rejection

Bejan cites Hannah Arendt's ‘The Crisis in Education’: educational activity is inherently founded on the teacher's hierarchical authority over students, who are ‘newcomers’ to the world; the teacher's duty is to introduce them into existing civilizational order, and educators themselves must recognize this hierarchy as justified and conservative. But politics is the domain where adults interact with one another as adults. From this, Bejan draws her book's core claim: the problem with modern egalitarianism is that it insists hierarchy itself violates our status as equals, missing the fact that our equal status often depends on well-functioning, authoritative hierarchies.

— Teresa Bejan
34:30

When Romans called people equal, they never meant identical

Ulpian wrote in the Digest that ‘all people are equal under natural law’; Bejan points out that ‘equal’ here means ‘indifferent’: differences between people don't matter under natural law, everyone is equally bound by this legal system, but this in no way means specific rights or outcomes are identical. Latin distinguishes aequalis (equal/equally bound) and pares (peer, equal in social or evaluative sense); modern English has lost this distinction, which is partly why ‘equality’ arguments today talk past each other.

— Teresa Bejan
48:10

Rome's Lady Justice kept her eyes open because hierarchy and equality coexisted

Bejan notes that the blindfold on Lady Justice is a modern artifact from the 15th and 16th centuries; in Roman times, Justice kept her eyes open because she had to distinguish based on status—Roman citizenship status mattered; rights and political voice varied accordingly, yet everyone still fell under the same law and was aequalis. This detail vividly shows the enormous gap between ancient ‘equality before law’ and modern ‘equality as sameness before law’.

— Teresa Bejan
55:40

A prisoner who couldn't read Latin fundamentally changed what equality means

In June 1646, Leveller leader John Lilburne, imprisoned in his cell, was reading a Magna Carta commentary when he found Chapter 29—‘free men should be judged by their peers’(judicium parium suorum)—translated as ‘by his peers or equals’. Lilburne seized on this translation coincidence and declared that since God created people equal, everyone is therefore each other's peer, so the House of Lords that judged him weren't really his ‘peers’ and had no authority to judge him—and conversely, he had the right to judge those nobles. Bejan calls him an ‘angry entrepreneur’: his logic isn't rigorous, but it's enormously persuasive and actionable.

— Teresa Bejan
59:00

One mistranslation of Magna Carta unlocked universal suffrage, petition rights, religious freedom

In the pamphlet ‘The Free-man's Freedom Vindicated’ that Lilburne smuggled out of prison, he wrote that every man and woman created by God are ‘equal and alike in power, dignity, authority and majesty’, and therefore can only be governed by their own consent. Bejan points out that this seemingly tangential claim was quickly adopted by his followers (the ‘Levellers’) to argue for universal suffrage, petition rights, freedom of publication and speech, and religious freedom; Colonel Rainborough's famous words at the Putney Debates—‘the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he’—extend the same logic. Bejan argues that the 1640s Levellers achieved the key conceptual and practical breakthrough of modern egalitarian politics earlier than the 1776 American Declaration of Independence.

— Teresa Bejan
1:07:30

Modern equality disputes pit two ancient concepts of equality against each other

Bejan points out that in contemporary disputes over racial or gender equality, one side argues that law should treat everyone identically without regard to difference (corresponding to ancient ‘indifferent’ equality), while the other argues that we must recognize and incorporate difference to give marginalized groups parity with the privileged (corresponding to Lilburne's peer-based equality). The ‘equity versus equality’ dispute is essentially these two ancient concepts—conflated in English—pulling at each other. They create tension but are not necessarily contradictory: clarifying this point is key to escaping the futile argument about ‘who supports equality and who opposes it’.

— Teresa Bejan
