Good products aren't persuaded into existence; they're discovered by finding what customers are already stuck on

Founders often think they need to convince customers to buy, but Rob's core thesis is: the pull already exists in customers' to-do lists; your job is to find the person who will grab your product from your hands, not create desire.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:01

Pull describes a person, not a company

Rob defines ‘pull’ as: if the prospect didn't buy your product, that would be weird. Founders' most common misunderstanding is thinking they need to create that desire and convince customers the product has value; but according to Rob, the desire already exists in the customer, and your job isn't to persuade but to find the person who would snatch the product from your hands.

— Rob Snyder
10:02

The same product with a different customer: pull vanishes

Jump's AI note-taking product initially sold to financial advisors who must record client meetings in their CRM for compliance or face legal risk—a ‘must-do’ task where existing tools fell short. When the team tried selling the same product to insurance agents, who also have frequent client meetings, they discovered their prospects had no CRM recording requirement. One of the four PULL elements disappeared. Pull vanished. It took Jump 18 months to find this point.

— Rob Snyder
16:03

You're not selling to a company, you're selling to one person

Many founders say ‘we sell to mid-market private equity firms’, but companies don't buy anything—a specific person makes the decision. Even when selling to an enterprise customer like SpaceX, you're actually talking to the person in the legal department whose to-do list has a stuck project they can't solve themselves. Without finding that specific person, the pull framework falls apart.

— Rob Snyder
23:04

Two slides before code: test the problem, not the solution

Before writing any code, Rob recommends preparing just two slides. Slide one: recap what they're doing, why they're stuck, and why existing options don't work—get them to say ‘yes, exactly that’. Slide two: a rough solution sketch, asking if it matches their need. If their reaction is ‘when can we use it?’ or they volunteer to pay now, that's a real signal.

— Rob Snyder
26:04

True customers ask ‘when do we start?’, enthusiasts say ‘what a great idea’

Rob calls genuine purchase intent ‘getting on the truck’: they ask directly ‘how do we begin?’, ‘does this exist yet?’, ‘we're joining now’. Without pull, even enthusiastic prospects say ‘this is interesting’, ‘it'll be the future of the industry’, ‘it'll save us so much time’, but never ‘I need this now’. Both sound friendly; the difference is whether they're moving forward.

— Rob Snyder
40:08

Cold-start metrics have benchmarks; miss them and it's your pitch

Rob's specific tactic: add 100–150 people weekly on LinkedIn (20/day), then message after they accept. Acceptance rate should be 30%–40%; reply rate 30%–50%, mostly ‘sure, why not’. If your numbers are lower, your profile or opening line is the problem, not your script. It's a signal that something in your positioning is off.

— Rob Snyder
50:14

You don't need 10x better, just solve one person's real stuck point

When asked ‘does the product need to be 10x better?’, Rob cites Linear's win over Jira: experience alone doesn't explain victory; the key is whether someone was genuinely blocked by a specific friction and couldn't do what they wanted. He admits he once applied this logic to HR software and found that no matter how good the UX, if the buyer isn't stuck, they won't buy—experience quality was never the deciding factor; the stuck point is all that matters.

— Rob Snyder
1:09:20

Churn reveals your real customer if you read it as data

A horizontal AI workflow company watched most customers leave, but freight forwarding companies stayed. Rather than call it failure, they asked ‘is this our problem or theirs?’ and found that forwarders were using the product for one very specific workflow. The team pivoted from ‘low-price, broad, high-churn’ horizontal product to ‘high-price, vertical, freight-focused’ specialist product.

— Rob Snyder

Glossary

PULL framework
A four-element test (Project, Urgency, List, Limitation) to identify whether a prospect will actually grab your product off the shelf.
Hail Mary question
Direct question about what problem they'd pay for right now, forcing out genuine needs beneath polite enthusiasm.
Goblin mode
Unashamed direct outreach when you can't find pull signals anywhere else, discarding all social polish for raw contact attempts.
Design partner
A prospect who gives feedback but never pays; Rob sees this as a common trap that masks the absence of pull.
