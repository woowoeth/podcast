School in two hours: 50% of students prefer it to vacation

Founders use cognitive science plus LLM to compress traditional curriculum learning to two hours, freeing time entirely for teaching teamwork, entrepreneurship, and interpersonal skills—things schools never taught before.

2:00

Learning engine compresses a day of lessons into two hours

Joe Liemandt says teaching methods haven't changed in a hundred years—a teacher stands in front of the classroom and lectures. But cognitive science, learning science, plus LLM means a ‘learning engine’ can accelerate traditional curriculum learning by 10x. Once children learn in two hours what used to take six hours plus homework, restructuring the entire school day becomes a completely new problem—this is exactly what Alpha School has been working on for years.

— Joe Liemandt
8:08

Group projects in regular schools teach the opposite of teamwork

Joe says group projects in traditional schools typically have one person doing the work while others coast, teaching children the opposite of teamwork. Wharton Business School has a Mount Everest simulation test for MBA teamwork where 50% of MBA students fail because they never actually practiced teamwork in school. Starting in third grade, Alpha has students wear microphones during escape room challenges and uses AI to analyze the conversation records. They discovered the percentage of language aimed at lifting teammates was under 20%; students must reach 85% to pass, and team coordination makes escape time three times faster.

— Joe Liemandt
12:14

The key to sustained learning engagement is 80-85% correctness

Joe presents a specific engineering parameter: to keep children engaged, problem accuracy must stay between 80 and 85 percent. Near 100% accuracy means the problems are too easy—children are just repeating what they already know. Game designers know that once accuracy drops below two-thirds, people quit. A teacher with 30 students can't customize practice difficulty for each child to hit this band, but an adaptive learning engine can—provide foundational material for hard problems, accelerate for easy ones. He calls this the core value of the entire engine.

— Joe Liemandt
20:35

What universities formally provide is what students most want to skip

Gagan observes that the only thing universities formally provide is academic curriculum; everything else—clubs, small group seminars, networking—these ‘bundled’ benefits students have to assemble themselves. Joe adds that Alpha's first graduating class, three years later, reported that large lecture classes at university are a complete waste of time. Some even wrote articles in the school paper complaining that classmates spend the whole lecture on their phones. The irony: large lectures occupy less than half the time students are on campus, yet are seen as least useful.

— Gagan Biyani
24:37

The case to parents: 1910 America's tractor moment

Joe draws a parallel to 1910 America: back then 90% of people had only an eighth-grade education—no high school existed. Then the tractor arrived and more than 30% of farm jobs vanished overnight. 130,000 small towns voted to tax themselves to build high schools they themselves had never attended—creating the education system that supported America for the next hundred years. He argues parents today face the same position: they haven't experienced this new kind of education, but they already know their children face a completely different world and must make the same leap.

— Joe Liemandt
28:45

Finding the first ten families is hardest; finding them triggers exponential growth

Joe says every time they open a new campus, thousands attend the information session, yet they can't fill the first 25 signing families. When they opened 12 campuses last year, the New York campus started with just 10-12 students, but is now at full capacity of 150. The early adopters bear the most risk, but once the first cohort's results are out, demand explodes. His advice: if the child genuinely wants to go, let them try for a year—it's one of life's best learning experiences even if it doesn't work out, and it's lower risk than staying on the old path.

— Joe Liemandt
45:00

Don't ask which major is safe; ask what you're actually interested in

Faced with anxiety about whether AI will make certain majors obsolete, Gagan inverts the question: the problem isn't ‘which major guarantees I'll still be relevant’ but ‘what am I actually interested in’. He notes that old-school mathematicians still teaching with blackboard and paper will genuinely be displaced, but the AI-native generation of mathematicians will do things nobody has imagined. Defensive risk-avoidance is a losing stance; they want students to take the offensive and figure out what they actually want to do.

— Gagan Biyani
49:01

Founder school guarantees $1M profit by high school graduation, or full refund

Alpha's dedicated high school entrepreneurship school, Founder School, opened this August. Its core promise: students will build a business that earns $1 million in profit by graduation, or tuition is fully refunded. The curriculum builds skills in stages at $100, $1,000, $10,000, $100,000, and $1,000,000 price points. Students are advancing faster than the curriculum was designed. Joe sees entrepreneurship as a teachable, replicable skill set, not talent or luck.

— Joe Liemandt
