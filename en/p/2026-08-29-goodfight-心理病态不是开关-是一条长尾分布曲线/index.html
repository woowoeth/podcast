Psychopathy isn't a switch, it's a long-tailed distribution curve

Psychopathy isn't an on-or-off switch but a long tail on a bell curve; people who feel no empathy at all are extremely rare, and most are just suppressed very low.

Psychopathy is three traits, not a switch

Marsh breaks psychopathy into three groups of traits: a bold, fearless, socially dominant personality; meanness or callousness, meaning a lack of remorse, guilt and sympathy; and disinhibition, difficulty sticking to plans, difficulty keeping commitments, impulsivity. She stresses this is not an on-or-off switch but a bell curve, with most people on the side with ample empathy and people who feel no empathy at all on a very long tail, small in number. She also notes that the word psychopathy does not appear in the DSM; the closest diagnosis is antisocial personality disorder, but that diagnosis does not capture the full combination of these three trait groups.

Nearly half of prison populations have psychopathic traits

Marsh says roughly one to two percent of the general population has clinically significant levels of psychopathy, but in prison populations the proportion is close to half. She cites the 80-20 rule: a small share of people is responsible for most antisocial behaviour, and some estimates hold that about one percent of the population is responsible for two-thirds of serious violent crime. A large population study from her team, soon to be published, found that the most common antisocial behaviours among highly psychopathic people are dangerous driving, drink-driving, speeding and breaking traffic rules — the vast majority of highly psychopathic people have done these; about forty percent have committed an assault, and a smaller proportion have been arrested.

— Abigail Marsh
0:00

They don't fear punishment, so punishment doesn't work on them

Marsh points out that a bold, dominant personality is linked to fearlessness, meaning no fear of being punished. This explains why threatening them with prison or harsh punishment rarely works: it isn't that they like prison, it's that they don't fear punishment in advance, so they won't change their behaviour to avoid it. She likens it to different kinds of accelerator and brake: some people are antisocial because their disinhibition is too strong, some because they control anger poorly, some because of status goals they choose not to call on empathy. She also notes that many psychopathic people maintain normal family lives and long friendships, but those relationships tend to be instrumental, and once the other person realises they are being used, the relationship doesn't last.

— Abigail Marsh

Ages two to three are the window for observing a fearless temperament

Marsh says ages two to three are statistically the most violent period of a person's life, so the judgment is hard. What parents notice first is often a fearless temperament: the child isn't afraid of big dogs, the dark, being alone, heights, or punishment. But that alone isn't enough to worry about, because fear and passion are two separate axes — Alex Honnold isn't fearless, he just loves climbing so much that the love outweighs normal fear. What is genuinely worth watching is fearlessness plus indifference to others' suffering: a normal two- or three-year-old asks ‘Mama, are you okay?’ when their mother cries, while a psychopathic child doesn't, because others' welfare has no intrinsic value to them.

— Abigail Marsh
0:00

The more parents escalate punishment, the more they push the child toward antisocial behaviour

Marsh describes a developmental loop: mild punishment that works on ordinary children doesn't work on a fearless child, parents don't understand why it works for other children but not this one, so they keep escalating — from sending the child to their room to yelling to physical punishment. But if the child doesn't fear punishment in advance, none of it works, and the result is a fearless child who feels everyone around them is against them and treating them unfairly. She recommends ‘one, two, three magic’: count to three and then enforce a timeout, quickly, certainly, without needing to be harsh, but making it annoying for the child. For a fearless child you also need extra warmth and physical contact, not less because the child seems not to like it.

— Abigail Marsh

About four percent of executives are clinically psychopathic

Marsh cites a study: among business executives, an estimated four percent have clinically significant psychopathy, two to four times the one to two percent in the general population, but ninety-six percent still don't. She notes that high-risk, high-reward professions — surgery, politics, business — attract more men, and men score slightly higher than women on psychopathy on average, with more men at the extreme high end and more women at the extreme low end, so part of the elevated psychopathy rate in these professions is due to gender composition. Even after correcting for that, there may still be a small effect, because boldness and social dominance are adaptive in these professions.

— Abigail Marsh
0:00

Spotting psychopathy in ordinary conversation is nearly impossible

Marsh says psychopathic people are extremely hard to identify in everyday interaction; they are shapeshifters who put on a mask in most interactions, presenting what the other person wants to see, so they often come across as charming, likeable and friendly, and don't seem fake. She believes there is no reliable signal in any casual conversation for judging whether someone is psychopathic, and everyone who studies psychopathy has been fooled at least once. What is genuinely useful is biographical information: whether they have real friendships and long-term relationships based on mutual affection, whether they had hard-to-control behaviour in childhood, whether there was juvenile delinquency or poor academic performance, and whether dishonest behaviour recurs across different domains.

— Abigail Marsh
0:00

Treating political opponents as psychopaths is a common misjudgment

Marsh says people tend to treat those whose politics are opposite to their own as psychopaths, and she always reminds them: not liking a politician or a voter doesn't mean they are psychopathic — they may sincerely believe they are doing the right thing. She notes that one of the original aims of the Milgram experiment was to refute the idea that ‘Germany is a psychopathic nation’. She also says people who behave in extremely antisocial ways online are more likely to have psychopathic personality traits, and that online behaviour reflects real personality rather than anonymity turning someone into a different person.

— Abigail Marsh

Glossary

psychopathy
A personality disorder characterised by three trait groups: bold/fearless, meanness, and disinhibition.
TriPM
Triarchic Psychopathy Measure, a scale assessing the three trait groups of boldness, meanness and disinhibition.
PCIT
Parent-Child Interaction Therapy, which trains parents to improve children's behaviour through warmth and structure.
floor time
Five to ten minutes a day in which a parent imitates, narrates and positively comments on the child's play to express approval and affection.
euphemism treadmill
The process by which a neutral term gradually becomes pejorative because of the negative associations of what it refers to, so people keep coining new words.
