Strategically stepping back from emotion can be more effective than confronting it

Psychology has long treated emotional avoidance as taboo. But Kross argues that strategically taking temporary distance from a difficult emotion, allowing time to work its effect, is itself a valid and effective emotion regulation strategy.

Emotion management in childhood predicts lifelong health and career success

Kross cites a longitudinal New Zealand study tracking subjects from birth to midlife, showing that childhood and adolescent emotion management capacity strongly predicts adult career achievement and health. He calls this regulatory capacity ‘shifting’—the ability to turn the emotional volume up or down, lengthen or shorten how long an emotion lasts, even switch states entirely. He emphasizes this is not just important; it is a game changer.

— Ethan Kross
10:33

Emotional dysregulation first destroys attention, then everything else follows

When emotions spiral, the first casualty is attention—leaving no capacity for the necessary tasks: listening in class, to a partner, to a child. Kross shares a personal example: when his child tells him about their day, his mind wanders to other worries, and minutes pass before he realizes he was not actually listening. The child, noticing his absence, looks defeated. Poor management of his own emotions directly damaged their connection.

— Ethan Kross
17:01

Most adolescents underestimate their capacity to manage emotions

A survey asking teens whether they believe they can control emotions found approximately 40% answered no. Kross calls this frustrating: if you do not believe you can do something, you will not try—like not believing weight loss is possible, so you skip the gym. But he makes an important distinction: the moment a feeling is triggered, you have no control. The trajectory after, though—the unfolding of the emotion—is controllable terrain.

— Ethan Kross
20:14

Connect emotionally first; explain new perspective only after

When facing a child struggling with low self-esteem, start with sensory intervention—a hug, their favorite music. Humans are tactile creatures, and emotional touch and music automatically shift emotional state. But order is crucial. The first step must always be social and emotional connection: conveying unconditional support, love, and empathy. Only after that emotional bridge is genuinely established should you move to step two: helping the child broaden their perspective and reframe their situation.

— Ethan Kross
23:18

Strategic emotional withdrawal, done right, is a performance enhancement

Kross was taught as a child to never avoid and always face fears—a belief reinforced in graduate school until it felt like universal truth. He now sees that as oversimplified. Consider Dennis Rodman: in the high-pressure NBA Finals, he would deliberately work intensely, then completely detach—moving his attention elsewhere—before returning with his composure restored. Strategically stepping back from a difficult situation temporarily, letting time dilute emotional intensity, can be remarkably effective emotion regulation.

— Ethan Kross
31:41

Projecting forward in time automatically reduces emotional intensity

For a parent spiraling over their child's repeated emotional episodes, Kross suggests ‘mental time travel’: imagine how you or your child will view this moment in a day, a week, a month, or ten years. Across a lifetime, a person lives through millions of emotional episodes, each triggered and then gradually fading. But caught in the moment, we fixate on the current awfulness and forget this larger pattern. Stepping mentally into the future automatically awakens the understanding that ‘this will improve,’ which lowers the emotional volume. The same works in reverse: recalling a past hardship you survived.

— Ethan Kross
38:01

Family culture shapes children's beliefs about managing emotion more than biology

Kross introduces the concept of the ‘cultural regulator’: culture is like air, everywhere and invisible, teaching us how to view and manage emotions. Each family is a micro-culture where parents have the opportunity to shape children's core beliefs and values about emotion. Rather than formal instruction, Kross places these tools ‘informally in the air’—a casual remark at dinner or while driving about how a particular strategy worked, nothing staged or preachy.

— Ethan Kross
40:04

Mental time travel works in crisis but loses power with overuse

Lisa Damour, the interviewer, shares decades of clinical and parenting experience using ‘mental time travel’: asking children how they will view the current moment in several years. The effect feels almost magical. It works because it honors the child's feelings without negating or trying to persuade them away, while relocating the emotion into a different time dimension. She notes it cannot be deployed daily—perhaps twice a year is enough—but during genuine ‘code red’ moments, it has carried her through times she did not know what to say.

— Lisa Damour

Glossary

shifting
The capacity to lower or raise emotional intensity, shorten or lengthen its duration, or switch emotional states; involves internal strategies or external tools.
mental time travel
Imagining how oneself or another will view a present difficulty when looking back from the future (or forward from the past), used to reduce emotional intensity.
psychological immune system
The mind's internal mechanisms for processing emotional shocks and recovering over time, analogous to the body's immune system.
