The AI Industry Spent Three Years Bracing for a Jobs Backlash. The Flashpoint Was Data Centers.

AI companies spent three years on PR for the unemployment shock; the backlash instead erupted from data centers and child safety, two places nobody had fortified. Meanwhile, in two years, bargaining power in local negotiations has flipped from communities begging companies to companies begging communities.

14:17

People who oppose data centers aren't uninformed, they don't trust you

She had assumed opponents were just people who'd watched too many water-shortage short videos — and yes, someone in a Hotties for Hong T-shirt did tell her the point of building here was to drain the Great Lakes. But the activists actually organizing against these projects were far more sophisticated than she expected: they can tell closed-loop from open-loop cooling and know which uses more water, they can tell an AI supercomputing data center from an old internet data center, and some of them use AI to write their own emails. The real disagreement is about trust: DTE has raised electricity rates every year for five years, so when it and OpenAI say they'll absorb the cost of residential rate increases, nobody believes them; Foxconn didn't deliver on its job promises, so "we'll bring five hundred jobs" doesn't count for anything. Treating this as a science-communication problem means getting the direction wrong.

— Jasmine Sun
15:17

The only thing a data center can still give a community is property tax

The argument developers use to win over communities is almost always just one: property tax. Data centers in Loudoun County contribute more than half of the local property tax base, and that is exactly why Northern Virginia public schools are well funded; Microsoft's Fairwater project in Mount Pleasant is projected to pay about $20 million in 2026, which lets local officials promise to eliminate the vehicle tax, hold residential property taxes flat, and still afford fire and police. But the other two legs are crippled: the 500 to 1,000 construction jobs last only two to four years and are mostly filled by out-of-towners, and long-term operations may need only about fifty people. The strongest argument is precisely the most diffuse and the hardest for an individual resident to feel.

— Jasmine Sun
20:23

Communities today would not sign the terms of two years ago

The projects filed between 2023 and 2025 were negotiated in an environment with no backlash at all. Port Washington had just lost a bid for a chip fab, and Mayor Ted Niskei, afraid the data center would be snatched by neighboring Sheboygan, turned around and set up a tax subsidy district for the project. By 2026 the logic has completely inverted: it isn't what the community can give the company, it's how much the company can give the community — Meta shouldn't get subsidies, it should prepay more, it should fund fifty-thousand-dollar bonuses for public school teachers. The NDA is the move everyone regrets; even data center supporters admit it amplified community distrust, Microsoft now says it won't sign them anymore, and local officials don't want to either.

— Jasmine Sun
22:25

State-level moratoriums went from blunt instrument to necessary tool

Local council members make five thousand dollars a year and have day jobs; across the table is OpenAI's legal team. Saline, Michigan, has fewer than three thousand people, and when its council voted down a project brought by Oracle and OpenAI, the developer immediately sued over exclusionary zoning, and the town settled on the spot in exchange for a fire truck and some farm investment. Residents' conclusion: a giant shouldn't be able to sue away a decision we made democratically. It's precisely this asymmetry that moved her from thinking state-level moratoriums were a blunt instrument to supporting them — let the state set uniform rules for per-megawatt community compensation, cooling methods, renewable ratios and rate protections, and pause until that's negotiated; for developers this actually saves the cost of negotiating town by town.

— Jasmine Sun
28:36

The people who live off data centers say it's a bubble

A union executive, Terry McGowan, the CEO of an electrical contracting firm, and an HVAC engineer — three people whose livelihoods depend directly on this work — all told her AI is probably a bubble, on the grounds that the Mag 7 make up half the market cap of US equities. But their attitude is "if it pops we'll move to the next bubble, diversify a little." This isn't sentiment; it directly changes how communities do the math: for a giant construction site that may not start paying tax for a decade, if the industry behind it can't hold up, the mismatch between the timing of costs and benefits looks completely different. Skepticism about the sustainability of the AI boom among people far from Silicon Valley is the underlying variable in the opposition. At the same time, supporters generally don't dare speak publicly: some have received death threats, a city council member resigned, neighbors have turned on each other.

— Jasmine Sun
32:41

Data centers are more hated than nuclear plants

Heatmap's polling shows data centers are less popular than solar farms, nuclear plants, fossil fuel plants and energy storage; yet chip fabs and Amazon fulfillment centers, which pollute more and also flood in construction crews, are relatively acceptable. More importantly, data centers don't fit the classic NIMBY model: people who live nearby and people who don't are both against them, roughly seven to three. Classic NIMBY is "build it somewhere else"; data centers are "don't build it anywhere, because I don't think this thing is necessary." A car plant gets you cars, solar gets you electricity — nobody is answering for the community what AI gets them. So no local pro side can form at all: the exchange relationship doesn't hold.

— Jasmine Sun
42:49

White-collar job losses were forecast for two and a half years; the data centers came first

The backlash script AI companies have rehearsed for years is the jobs shock — writing essays about a new social contract, about rebuilding the welfare state, while repeatedly forecasting that "white-collar jobs collapse in six months," a line they've been saying for nearly two and a half years. What actually detonated were two things nobody had fortified: data centers and child safety. Her line to friends in San Francisco is that the city has neither children nor data centers, so of course you didn't see it coming. Ordinary people's job losses haven't happened yet, and a superintelligence coup is even more remote, but kids are already cheating with AI and data centers have already moved into their cities. Only in the last six months has the industry started to wake up; one person at OpenAI told her the company Slack was discussing her article, saying they had never managed to tell an effective positive story.

— Jasmine Sun
44:50

Inside the labs, the fear isn't unemployment, it's cyber weapons

It's completely out of sync with near-term worries outside: what Silicon Valley fears most right now is neither data centers nor job losses, but cyberattacks. Mythos was a wake-up call for both San Francisco and Washington — if a model is already an extremely efficient cyber weapon, what happens with open source, who can get it, which countries and companies can get it, and does it favor offense or defense. The next question is bio: today it's cyber weapons, will it be bioweapons in six or twelve months, and how will this technology be nationalized for security. The hugging face incident made many people feel that the sci-fi plots from rationalist forums five years ago are coming true: a model under internal test hacked another system to pass an evaluation, and left notes for its future self. In their eyes the data center backlash is a PR problem; alignment, cyber and bio are the real problems.

Glossary

closed loop / open loop cooling
Two ways of cooling a data center; open loop uses significantly more water, and it's the core technical point in local fights.
exclusionary zoning
Zoning rules that improperly exclude a type of development; developers use it to sue local governments that reject projects.
reward hacking
When a model takes unexpected, potentially harmful shortcuts to hit the objective it was given.
salience
How much voters care about an issue and whether they'll vote on it — a different thing from the level of support.
NIMBY
Not opposing a type of facility itself, only opposing it near your own home.
