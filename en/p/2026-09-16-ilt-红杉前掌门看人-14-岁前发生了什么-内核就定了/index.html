The grandfather who waited for the situation to pass vanished

Moritz's two grandfathers were two reactions to the same catastrophe. His maternal grandfather was a lower-court judge in Bavaria, had fought at Verdun in World War I, believed in the durability of the state, and had a pension he was unwilling to give up, so he delayed leaving, and in the end he and his wife were sent east and disappeared from history. The other grandfather was nearly blind, supported his family alone by trading cattle, had no pension to wait for, and left with his wife 72 hours before the war broke out — three days later and they too would have died. Moritz says the lesson he draws is not a moral judgment but that you must always have a bolt hole to hide in, and that you can never have too many passports.

His most effective question makes people cry on the spot

When he interviews people he always asks one fixed question: looking back, if there were one thing, one action you could have done differently, what would it be. He remembers a man interviewing at Sequoia bursting into tears when asked this — the man was the son of Latino immigrants, and at around age 12 he and a friend stole candy from a corner grocery store; the owner told his father, who dragged him back to the store by the ear and made him apologize and empty his pockets. Years later, asked this question, the first thing he still thought of was the shame he had brought on his family. Moritz says many people conducting interviews are not really listening to the answers.

— Michael Moritz
26:03

On Zoom he sorted everyone he knew into categories

Inspired by a series about a French village in World War II, he divides people into three categories: resisters, the silent, and collaborators. During the pandemic, in those Zoom meetings with a dozen little windows, he would stare at each person he knew and think: if these people had lived in a French village in the 1940s, who would stay silent, who would resist, who would collaborate. He says resisters are always a tiny minority, because bad things happen in a country precisely because most people stay silent. Asked how many of the people he has invested in would be resisters, he says he would like to think so, but he is not sure.

38:04

The painter gave himself only one day off a year

Frank Auerbach was a refugee from Nazi Germany, and Moritz calls him a complete monomaniac: apart from one day off each Christmas to rest at Brighton Pier, he painted every single day. His models were a small fixed group of people, and for decades he painted the same few faces; one model who lived in Venice flew to London once a week to sit for him. His wife's house was right on the English coast, and Moritz asked whether he went there; he answered: I have never been to that house. From this Moritz discusses the cost of monomania — the cost to relationships is almost inevitable, but sometimes great things come out of exactly there.

— Michael Moritz
43:04

He bet on something beyond the résumé

Around 1985, Moritz was still a journalist and wrote to five small funds looking for a job. Everyone except Don Valentine told him he had no technical background, no engineering degree, no experience at a tech company, and suggested he go into PR or marketing. Don was willing to take the risk, for a direct reason: he had seen several people with operating backgrounds at large tech companies move into investing and fail, and he had also seen several who succeeded — an actuary, and the one he cites most, Arthur Rock: an investment banker by background, with nothing in his résumé that predicted he would succeed. Don did not believe in teaching people; he believed in throwing them into the deep end and seeing whether they drowned.

— Michael Moritz
46:15

Venture capital's advantage was imperfect information

Moritz says doing venture capital in the 1980s and 90s had several advantages that have now disappeared, the first being imperfect information: before the internet, unless you were close to something you did not know what was happening, whereas now the whole world basically gets the same information. The second is that the early market has far too many participants — individuals, two- or three-person funds, giant mutual funds — too many middlemen, and it is no longer possible for investors to build the kind of real, close relationship with founders they once had. He therefore says that a moderately intelligent, quick-minded 14- to 16-year-old could have done venture capital in the mid-80s; he is not sure he could still do it well today.

— Michael Moritz
52:57

He believes in the great man theory without apology

Asked whether it is great individuals or enduring institutions that shape the course of history, Moritz's answer is blunt: he is a firm believer in the great man theory. From Lee Iacocca to Steve Jobs, from politics to the battlefield, what matters is great leaders; and of course there are also tyrants who cast a shadow over tens of millions of people for decades. In his work at Sequoia and in writing Ferguson, he cares about the same thing — how an organization stays alive across decades. And speaking of Elon, he says he is completely staggered by the scale of his achievements across so many different industries, and thinks he may be the greatest industrialist in American history.

— Michael Moritz
58:40

The more AI writes, the more valuable a human voice becomes

Moritz says that because AI can easily produce vast amounts of text, excellent writing that cuts through the garbage is actually appreciating in value; his definition of excellent writing is ‘a voice you can hear that cannot be mistaken for anyone else's’. The hardest thing for a writer is to get close enough to your own inner feeling and voice that it outweighs the weight of others' expectations — he quotes Joseph Campbell: the privilege of a lifetime is to become who you truly are, and that is also the hardest thing. He does not consider himself a technologist, and says AI is rarely discussed at home; on employment he is optimistic: people predicted doomsday when the atomic bomb exploded, and predicted a genetic revolution creating humans in the 1970s, and neither happened, and this time he thinks more jobs will be created than destroyed.

— Michael Moritz
