When Your Kids Fight, Be a Witness, Not a Judge

Fairness is not equality: give each child what they need, not the identical thing. A parent's job is not to rule on who is right, but to describe what happened and let them find the next step.

Deal with your own sibling baggage first

The episode opens by saying: before you intervene, sort out your own emotions. Everyone carries sibling baggage — even people without siblings carry a longing for that relationship, or relief at not having it. The fairness trap is what most easily sets parents off: a piece of cake, a looser rule, a new dress from childhood all leave behind a memory of "I got the short end." Those feelings get summoned when you step into your kids' conflict. So the core instruction is: you are a witness, not a judge. Once you start ruling, the judgment comes from your own history, not from the two children in front of you.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
5:07

When you must step in: danger, power imbalance, deadlock

There are three criteria: if a child is in danger, step in; if there is a power imbalance, step in; if they are stuck in a deadlock, step in. If the two are evenly matched and both safe, you can step back and occasionally ask, "Is everyone okay?" What deserves special vigilance is when power dynamics turn into toxic bullying — this is rare, but when it happens, an adult must cut it off. The episode stresses that children who are bullied by peers or siblings have far worse outcomes as adults, and the damage is much more serious than we realize. But bullying has a strict definition: a power difference, repetition, malice, intentional abuse. Merely being "annoying" does not count.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
6:07

Intervene in sportscaster mode, don't ask who started it

If you must step in, use "sportscaster mode": like a commentator, describe only the facts you saw — "I noticed this happened over here, did I get that right?" This validates both children at once, takes no side, and produces no winner. Questions to avoid include "Who started it," "Why are you always like this," and "There's just something wrong with you." The episode draws a key distinction: telling a child "what you did was inconsiderate" and telling a child "you're stupid" are two different things. The latter attacks a person's worth and gets a child to bind their anger and emotional outbursts to "I'm not worth anything as a person." The moment you hear dehumanizing language, even mid-screaming-match, pause on the spot and require a different way of saying it.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
9:13

Under two, protect the little one without making the big one hate him

Under two, you only need to protect the younger one, while trying not to alienate the older one. The older child is already processing "the baby stole my attention," and then gets told "this is your best friend, don't you love him?" — what he's thinking is "No, I don't love him," though sometimes he does. So tell him plainly: you can be angry at your sibling and love him deeply at the same time, you can dislike him today and still love him very much — this is called mixed feelings, and it's completely normal. Also explain: protecting the little one's body is the adult's job, because he can't get out of the way himself, so sometimes I will pick him up and carry him off. This kind of intervention doesn't heighten tension, and it isn't saying who is better or worse — only who needs protection from real danger.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
14:28

Fair is not equal — it's giving each child what they need

The most useful item in prevention mode: fair is not equal — it's not equality, it's equity. You give each child what he needs, not the identical thing. The episode gives a concrete example: giving two children peanut butter cupcakes the same size, same recipe, same flavor looks perfectly equal, but one of them is allergic to peanuts — so it isn't fair. You can give him vanilla, ensuring both children have cake, but not the same cake. This framework applies directly to challenges like "Why does my twelve-year-old sister get a phone and I don't": you are different people, I learned more later, so I made a different decision.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
16:31

Don't label your kids — labels follow them for life

Labels shared by all the children can promote good behavior — telling the kids "you are helpers" produces more helping behavior. But if you give the "helper" label to only one child in the house, it creates tension and a problematic dynamic. The episode says labeling builds a dynamic that, to put it dramatically, "clings to them." Teachers, friends, other people's parents, relatives all label them, so the parent's job is to offset that damage, because labels bring resentment, insecurity, lack of motivation, and tension between siblings that need not be so distant.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
18:41

Forced apology is not repair, it's fake repair

Repair doesn't require a formal apology. Kids who are fighting suddenly giggle together, share an inside joke, make faces at each other — that is repair, that is reconnecting, and you are watching it happen. Every reconnection strengthens the relationship, telling them this foundation is permanent: it will get messy, but we always come back. But if you feel you must force an apology, the episode says plainly: an apology is not repair unless it truly comes from the heart, from genuinely feeling you did wrong and wanting to make it up to the other person. Saying "I'm sorry" and saying "Apologize to your brother" are neither of them what the scientific literature calls repair. Fake repair can't build a strong foundation. What you can ask is: "How do you think that made your sibling feel? What could you do to make him feel better?" If the child says nothing, you don't just drop it — you come back and ask again a little later.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
23:55

Be curious about the instigator, don't just punish

When one child really is repeatedly instigating, first let the other children know you see it: "I've noticed your sister has started it the last few times, and I also see how hard you're trying not to fight — that's hard, let's keep practicing." With the instigator, be genuinely curious first: why is he instigating this often? Does he need more one-on-one time? Is there some longing, jealousy or need you haven't noticed? Try to meet it without taking away from the other children. If you just yell "you always start it" and punish, you don't teach him the underlying skill, and you don't teach him self-reflection — "Why do I keep starting this? What do I actually want?" Teaching a child to figure out what he wants and get it in a productive way is one of the most important things. Going after it by instigating loses most of the time.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
29:17

Forced family time is an underrated glue

The episode says her favorite thing, and one that isn't popular today, is "forced family time." She often hears parents say, we used to have dinner together on Friday nights, but then the kids didn't want to come, or had other friends to see. Her position: find one time in the week that is the family's sacred, non-negotiable time. Kids can invite friends, but you can't give the thing up. Parents often fear their kids will be annoyed at being forced, but it's exactly these recurring shared traditions that build connection. Even if both children find the tradition annoying, it's something they can complain about together — it's connection time. Once it becomes "that's just how it is," it stops being a battle. You can call it "fun family time," but it must be consistent and non-negotiable, and it can evolve as the kids grow, but it can't be each child doing whatever he wants.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman

Glossary

sportscaster mode
Describing only the facts you saw, like a commentator, without judging who was right or wrong, so both sides feel acknowledged.
emotion coaching
Helping children identify and express emotions, rather than solving the conflict for them or suppressing the feelings.
glass child
A child who is "seen through" and overlooked by parents because another child's needs are higher.
