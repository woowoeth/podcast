Your Parenting Mojo 2026-09-28

Downgrade 'I'm a bad mother' from fact to thought, and interrupt the yelling that passes down generations

Change 'I'm a bad mother' to 'I'm having the thought that I'm a bad mother'—and emotion becomes just a thought instead of the truth. Enough to break the yelling pattern you inherited from your own mother, and let your child apologize first and tell you what matters.

4:16

A book on the table drew scolding; she apologized for existing

Terese remembers her teenage years: her mother blamed her for almost everything, never her brother. She did homework at the kitchen table and got scolded for hogging it; she moved to the living room and got scolded there too. Finally she started studying at evening study hall—just to have somewhere safe to do her work. She describes the feeling as ‘sorry for existing’—sorry for taking up space, sorry for being a person at all.

— Terese
6:14

Her job was not being loved—it was keeping her mother's emotions under control

Jen points out: Terese's real childhood job was keeping the peace—making sure her stressed, single mother in a small house wouldn't lose control. Terese confirms it: she was always ‘walking on eggshells’, finding ways to keep her mother from spiraling. It wasn't a childhood of being cared for; it was a child managing an adult's emotions.

— Terese
9:21

Fighting over a toy, she would have just taken it away before

Her two daughters fight over a pink unicorn—the one-year-old pushes and pulls, older daughter Freya won't share. Terese used to get angry and frustrated, so she'd just take the toy away and nobody plays. This time she tried something different: both kids want to play—can they play together? She suggests Freya find a game that includes her sister. Minutes later they're both feeding the unicorn and taking it on adventures.

— Terese
11:16

After a fight, she goes back and apologizes—not silent treatment

When asked ‘what do you do when you don't handle it well?’, Terese says she goes back to her kids quickly, admits it was rough, and says she's sorry. She explains where her feelings came from, and sometimes figures out with them how to avoid it next time, and sometimes just talks about what happened. She's careful to say this also tells her kids: Mom is not perfect.

— Terese
13:02

Her son apologized without being asked—unusual even in Norwegian culture

When his friend refuses him a hug, he threatens: ‘if you don't hug me, I won't come play tomorrow.’ His friend's mother responds: threats usually don't work, they just make people sadder. Without being asked, he says he's sorry—and says she doesn't have to hug him—then rides home. Terese says volunteering an apology is rare even in Norwegian culture, and she was surprised.

— Terese
15:04

He dares to report mistakes because he's never been yelled at for them

When asked how her son went from never talking about feelings to opening up, Terese says the core is that he trusts he can be himself without judgment. She gives an example: he accidentally hits another boy with a stick, comes home and tells her on his own, and she says ‘thank you for telling me’—even though she was actually angry. It's this repeated response that lets him know it's safe to confess a mistake—something she could never do in her own home.

— Terese
19:20

Reframe 'I'm a bad mother' from verdict to just a thought

Jen explains the tool Terese mentioned: when the voice says ‘I'm a bad mother,’ reframe it as ‘I'm having the thought that I'm a bad mother.’ This small shift moves it from a truth about who she is to a thought she can step back from and examine. She can see: I only slept two hours, of course I have no patience—instead of being sentenced by that voice.

— Jen
