GTA6 Burned Billions, but Rockstar Says That's an Outlier, Not the Industry Blueprint

Rockstar can spend billions because GTA's track record makes success certain, while other studios are cutting budgets because sales haven't kept pace with rising costs—GTA6's formula can't be replicated.

Cosmetics in a Game Outearns the Game Itself

GTA Online became a money machine after GTA V's release; microtransactions on clothing, weapons, and similar items generate roughly $500 million yearly. For Take-Two, in-game spending accounts for about 80% of company revenue—far more than game sales. This explains why Rockstar wasn't rushing a sequel: as long as GTA Online kept printing cash, the opportunity cost of delay shrank.

— Jason Schreier
11:19

Making This Game Doubled the Studio's Headcount

Red Dead Redemption 2 ended with 2,400 Rockstar employees; GTA6 required 4,700. This isn't just GTA6's story—it's an industry trend: games have evolved from 2-3 years and $20 million budgets to 7-8 years and costs in the hundreds of millions, possibly exceeding $1 billion.

— Jason Schreier
16:26

Two Characters Playing Together Stretched Development

When the player controls Jason or Lucia, the other character performs believable responses automatically—walking to the car door and sitting down when entering a vehicle, moving to a stationary bike in the next room to exercise. This system—where every character the player doesn't control must behave like a real person—required programming, art, and QA working in lockstep. Jason Schreier emphasizes it's far less predictable than ordinary software, and was a concrete reason development stretched.

— Jason Schreier
21:30

Total Realism Became Tedium, So Rockstar Abandoned It

In Red Dead Redemption 2, hunting deer required shooting, skinning, and tying the carcass to your horse—a grueling sequence many players loved for its immersion. For GTA6, the team ultimately chose a faster, more arcade-like pace instead of piling on tedious realistic mechanics, trading immersive systems for snappy GTA-style gameplay.

— Jason Schreier
31:48

The Pandemic Closed the Motion Capture Stage, and Killed the Schedule

Extensive cutscenes and dialogue relied on actors in motion-capture studios; all such studios closed during the pandemic, halting production—a documented source of delay in the 8-year development cycle. Whether the expensive production pipeline and team chemistry now built will actually speed up the next game is uncertain, Schreier says; the industry has never been here before.

— Jason Schreier
36:58

GTA6's Gameplay Trailer Outdraws a De Niro Film

Rockstar premiered a half-hour GTA6 gameplay trailer on Netflix this summer; that week, the most-watched content on the platform wasn't a film or series—it was the game trailer, outperforming Robert De Niro's new release. PlayStation and Xbox both reported that GTA6 shattered their respective preorder records; estimates put preorders above 10 million.

— Jason Schreier
42:06

AI Drained GPU Stocks, and Console Prices Rose for the First Time

AI chip demand pushed GPU prices higher; PC gamers paid double or triple for high-end cards and still couldn't find them in stock. Consoles, though not customizable, also faced their first-ever price increases due to supply constraints: PS5 launched at $500 and the base model now costs $650. Industry insiders worry next-generation consoles could exceed $1,000.

— Jason Schreier
43:09

Take-Two's CEO: Only Rockstar Earns Unlimited Budget

Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick joked about Rockstar's spending with a wedding metaphor: he told his son the wedding budget had no limit, and his son actually overran it. The joke conceals a serious judgment—GTA's brand power makes Rockstar nearly the only studio that can guarantee money spent will return profit. Most other game companies are figuring out how to cut budgets, because sales haven't kept pace with rising costs.

— Jason Schreier
