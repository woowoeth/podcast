Planet Formation Happens When Dust Collapses on Its Own, Not Through Gas Turbulence

Purely hydrodynamic instabilities like VSI and COS stir dust back into the atmosphere and cannot make planetesimals; only when dust actively feeds back, or even when gas accretes, can dust be compressed to collapse density.

Planet FormationProtoplanetary DisksHydrodynamic InstabilitiesMHDDust Dynamics
This is a technical group meeting talk of moderate information density; the first half is a review, and the second half on accretion flows and Hall MHD promoting collapse is more worth hearing.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

10:27

1% dust gets stirred back into the atmosphere by VSI

With the standard interstellar medium dust-to-gas ratio of 1%, dust particles that settle to the midplane are quickly mixed back into the atmosphere by VSI, with no hope of forming planets. But if metallicity is increased by a few times, dust feedback on the gas brings extra inertia, producing dust-induced buoyancy that allows a dust layer to form. The key is that you do not need to reach a dust-to-gas ratio of 1, because the vertical shear itself is weak, and only a small increase in metallicity makes feedback important.

— Min-Kai Lin
12:29

Vortices can catch dust, but not hard enough

Vortices commonly appear in 3D VSI simulations, and they do collect dust, but the concentration falls far short of what is needed for direct gravitational collapse. In 2D axisymmetric simulations, as dust content increases from 1% to 10%, the morphology shifts from non-axisymmetric vortices to a more axisymmetric ring-like disk. The author thinks this is worth exploring: linking the large-scale morphology seen in the sky to underlying properties like the disk's dust-to-gas mass ratio.

— Min-Kai Lin
14:29

The dust layer itself generates new vertical shear

If dust successfully settles to the midplane, the story is not over: the dense dust layer orbits at Keplerian speed, while the pure gas above and below orbits at sub-Keplerian speed, creating strong vertical shear between the dust layer and the gas layers. Using Dedalus for global eigenvalue analysis, a family of extremely unstable modes was found, with growth rates on the orbital timescale, but scales of only 10 to the minus 3 H, requiring very high resolution to capture. The author notes that a decade ago spectral simulations already found that this dust version of the vertical shear instability would disrupt the dust layer, but those were early simulations and deserve revisiting with modern codes.

— Min-Kai Lin
26:38

Too much dust collected, and the zonal flow disappears

Simulations coupling Boussinesq gas with pressureless dust show that after the instability saturates, a pressure ring slowly forms and dust collects in the ring. But interestingly, when dust particles become quite concentrated, the pressure ring disappears instead. Raising the background dust-to-gas ratio from 1% to 10% results in nothing happening, no zonal flow forming at all. The author does not strictly explain this, but the best guess is: the dust fluid's angular momentum flux is always more positive than the gas, making the total angular momentum flux less negative than in the pure gas case, thereby weakening the toy model of zonal flow formation.

— Min-Kai Lin
37:45

Vortices formed, but pressure bumps did not appear

In the "local global" approach, the author stuffs a global disk into a box, precisely controls the N² profile, and makes N² negative only near X=0. For a 2×6 cube, 3D simulations each run for about 2000 orbits, and it is found that the disk must be unstable over a sufficiently large region to form vortices; below an empirical line, it only saturates into an axisymmetric state, and doing 3D is meaningless. But the most critical finding is: even when vortices form, no vortex signature can be identified in the pressure field at all, no pressure bump as a dust trap. More worryingly, merely changing the vertical boundary condition from periodic to fixed top and bottom lids makes the vortices disappear.

— Min-Kai Lin
47:56

Gas accretion flows can push weak clumps into strong clumps

Protoplanetary disks are essentially accretion disks, so accretion should be in the model. In Fargo simulations, a torque is applied to the gas to make it accrete toward the star, with no pressure gradient applied; the system itself is unstable, forming dense filamentary structures with dust-to-gas ratios approaching or even exceeding 100, enough to expect gravitational collapse. At a parameter point with Stokes number 0.1 and extremely low metallicity, strong clumping was not expected, but adding the accretion flow lifts the system into the strong clumping regime. The author thinks the reason is that the accretion flow reduces the turbulence level, allowing dust to settle. Moreover, this effect is not monotonic: if the accretion parameter is tuned too high, it returns to the non-strong-clumping state.

— Min-Kai Lin
53:01

Ideal MHD directly wipes out the traditional SI

Extending the standard linear theory of magnetohydrodynamic flow instabilities from pure hydrodynamics to ideal MHD, adding the Lorentz force and induction equation, the picture changes completely. The appearance of MRI is not surprising, nor is a new branch where Alfvén waves resonate with dust-gas drift, but what is truly surprising is that the original flow instability completely disappears. If SI existed independently, it should remain even if not dominant, but it is actively suppressed by the Lorentz force (very likely magnetic tension). However, protoplanetary disks are far from ideal MHD, so the author turns to non-ideal MHD with kinetic dust.

— Min-Kai Lin
56:02

Hall MHD lets even dust-poor disks strongly clump

In the anti-parallel Hall effect case, linear calculations reveal, besides the usual flow instability and MHD waves, a new mode that can be reproduced with the standard Hall MHD equations plus an extra term representing the B field drifting through the dust-gas mixture. New postdoc Remis Actor is running simulations with thermal diffusion: without MHD almost nothing happens; under fully developed MRI turbulence, highly concentrated dust is obtained even without feedback; under Hall MHD, quite strong dust concentration is also obtained. The key is that the starting dust-to-gas ratio is only 0.2, very poor, but dust feedback plus Hall MHD can still produce strong clumping.

— Min-Kai Lin

Glossary

VSI (vertical shear instability)
A hydrodynamic instability driven by vertical shear from radial temperature gradients, essentially inertial waves becoming unstable.
COS (convective overstability)
A hydrodynamic instability where fluid elements exchange heat with their surroundings during radial oscillations, amplifying the oscillations through buoyancy.
streaming instability
An instability from two-way coupling between dust and gas, the leading theory for planetesimal formation.
zonal flow
Alternating super-Keplerian and sub-Keplerian angular velocity bands in the disk, corresponding to pressure variations, which can act as dust traps.
Stokes number
A dimensionless number describing the degree of coupling between dust particles and gas, characterizing particle size.
resonant drag instability
A modern framework interpreting dust instabilities as resonance between wave speeds and dust drift speeds.
