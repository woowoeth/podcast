Apple Pay built on NFC and security chip technology already inside the company

When Jennifer Bailey asked Tim Cook for a new challenge, he offered two paths: a major operations role or a startup-like project. She chose the latter, inheriting existing work from engineers on NFC, wireless capabilities, and secure elements—when the company had just decided to build mobile payments. Her decade leading online stores gave her foundational payments knowledge.

— Jennifer Bailey
8:14

Apple built payments to solve real friction, not for revenue

Among many potential projects (the ‘gold bricks’), Apple chose payments based on a simple observation: plastic cards use outdated technology. The team believed better tech could make payments more secure, simpler, and more private—and that everyone would adopt this approach within years. This was a user experience judgment, not a business one.

— Jennifer Bailey
8:50

Only 3% U.S. contactless acceptance, yet overseas markets validated the model first

When Apple Pay launched in the U.S., contactless acceptance sat at just 3%. Yet in the UK, Australia, and other markets where regulators had mandated contactless payments earlier, acceptance was high. It was through these overseas markets that Apple saw consumers actually adopting Apple Pay at scale, validating the value proposition. The EMV chip migration helped too, as merchants upgraded contactless hardware alongside chip readers.

— Jennifer Bailey
10:44

Apple tackled offline payments first—the harder problem—not the easier online route

Though online payments seem easier to build, Apple went after offline first—where about 80% of transactions still occur. The NFC and secure element architecture was designed for in-person use. Overseas markets had already proven consumer willingness to adopt contactless payments. Offline was where the solution could have the most impact.

— Jennifer Bailey
14:31

Saudi Arabia skipped physical contactless cards and went straight to phone payments

When expanding globally, Apple evaluated three factors: iPhone user scale, local contactless infrastructure maturity, and ecosystem willingness to integrate. Saudi Arabia proved an outlier. Regulators mandated all merchants switch to contactless payments at once. The entire country skipped the contactless card stage and moved directly to phone payments—a stark contrast to America's uncoordinated market.

— Jennifer Bailey
16:35

Apple designed the system from day one to never see transaction data

Apple and Visa, MasterCard, and Amex together designed a network-based tokenization approach: tokens encrypted on the phone, released only through biometrics, with networks and banks handling verification and processing. The team's explicit design goal was stark: don't want this data, can't see it, can't aggregate it. Offline transactions flow entirely through traditional payment infrastructure, invisible to Apple—a deliberate choice to avoid becoming a honeypot for sensitive transaction data.

— Jennifer Bailey
22:47

No credit card marketplace in Wallet, because Apple never holds the data

When asked whether Wallet could host a Credit Karma-style credit card recommendation marketplace, Bailey was direct: Credit Karma's precision comes from having massive personal data on its servers. Apple has no such data, so it can't. She envisions a future where open banking data stays on-device under user control—like account balance displays already in the UK—enabling better matching. But she's clear: that doesn't exist in real form today.

— Jennifer Bailey
25:57

Daily Cash on Apple Card solves the opacity problem of loyalty points

The Apple Card's design goal was to help users ‘use credit more healthily’: no late fees, no hidden forex charges, with an interest wheel showing exactly what they'll owe. For rewards, Apple rejected traditional points—which card companies adjust at will with minimal disclosure, undermining transparency. Daily Cash solved this: users get real cash back every night, spendable as Apple Cash or shared directly with family and friends.

— Jennifer Bailey

Glossary

tokenization
Replacing real card details with encrypted, one-time tokens for secure transaction transmission.
open loop
Using standard debit or credit cards directly to pay transit fares, replacing dedicated transit cards.
open banking
Regulatory framework permitting third-party apps to access bank account data with explicit user consent.
