GPU scarcity's real problem: compute sold only in three-year rectangular blocks

The real GPU shortage isn't a supply problem—it's that nearly all capacity gets bundled into three-year contracts with three-to-six-month delivery, which completely mismatches the lumpy, day-to-day demand curve most customers actually have.

A single customer propped up 90% of early revenue

Modal's true inflection came between November 2022 and January 2023: monthly revenue jumped from $100 to $2,000 to $30,000. But almost all this growth came from one customer—Mage, a Stable Diffusion company in New York—which accounted for 80 to 90 percent of company revenue in Q1 2023. Erik said the feeling wasn't celebration but the relief of finally being able to put anxiety down.

— Erik Bernhardsson
13:48

Compute market shifted from spot to three-year prepayment

Modal started by buying spot and on-demand compute from AWS, GCP, and Azure—wherever was cheapest. But over the past year the market completely shifted: to get capacity now requires three-year-plus contracts with roughly 25 cloud providers, with large upfront payments. The company created a dedicated capacity-buying team—four people on the phone all day negotiating with vendors and verifying hardware—plus a roughly ten-person engineering team handling bare metal servers, networking, and failed hardware.

— Erik Bernhardsson
19:25

He fears collapse more than price increases

Many people think compute companies fear GPU price increases. Erik says the opposite: price increases are actually good for Modal because the company has locked in a large volume of cheap three-year contracts—effectively "going long" on GPU prices. What really worries him is price collapse, which would leave everyone who bought long-term contracts at peak seriously underwater. He also explicitly said he doesn't plan to hoard the entire market's supply, even if he theoretically could.

— Erik Bernhardsson
28:35

Neoclouds snowball by signing contracts first, then buying cards

He broke down how emerging cloud vendors like nScale go from nearly zero capacity to several hundred billion dollars in order backlog: first, sign large buyers like Anthropic and OpenAI into five-year compute contracts, then take those contracts to Wall Street to raise capital, then use that money to buy hardware from Supermicro, Dell, and Nvidia for delivery—essentially pre-selling cards you haven't bought yet. Modal is taking a different path: product-driven growth and dispersed customers, which makes it hard to deploy the same leverage-based playbook to rapidly expand capacity.

— Erik Bernhardsson
34:27

Sandbox from near shutdown to one-third of revenue

Modal Sandboxes came from a 2023 lunch conversation—"LLMs are outputting code, we need somewhere to run it"—prototyped and shipped in a day or two, but saw almost no use for a long time, and the team once discussed killing it. The turnaround came in the second half of that year: models suddenly got good at generating full applications, and Lovable (and other vibe-coding shops), Ramp's backend agents, and new reinforcement-learning labs started pouring in. Today Sandbox accounts for one-third of Modal's revenue.

— Erik Bernhardsson
44:13

Sandboxes should boot-to-close in milliseconds, not be cloud dev machines

He thinks most people currently treat sandboxes as "moving a dev machine to the cloud," which is wrong mental model. The right way is: agents running 24/7, when they need to execute a command, spin up a sandbox in ~100 milliseconds, run it, snapshot, and close. Modal sandboxes now have a median lifetime of 8 seconds. This lets CI testing genuinely achieve one sandbox per test running in parallel, instead of the current approach where each shard redeploys its dependencies.

— Erik Bernhardsson
47:34

Hugging Face incident: the application was compromised, not the platform

The compromise of the Hugging Face agent exploited a vulnerability in an application deployed on Modal itself—not a breach of Modal's platform—he compared it to "someone's app on AWS getting hacked doesn't mean AWS got hacked." The team is reviewing whether security defaults could be tightened further, taking AWS's later tightening of S3 public-bucket defaults as a reference. They've also started using agents to specifically try "jailbreaking" their own sandboxes to find vulnerabilities, and haven't found any so far.

— Erik Bernhardsson
1:18:56

Real GPU scarcity is a rectangle problem, not a quantity problem

Erik quoted one of his own tweets: the real GPU shortage problem isn't not being able to get cards, but that nearly all capacity gets bundled into "three-year rectangles delivering three to six months out," which completely mismatches most users' actual hourly and daily demand curves. Modal's core value is exactly this: aggregate demand from thousands of customers, smooth out the jagged fluctuations into a flat curve, then turn around and slice compute finely to each person.

— Erik Bernhardsson
