Products with legs show in dilated pupils, not in DAU metrics

Google Labs director says early products are measured by whether people's eyes light up—a signal that comes sooner and more reliably than any retention or DAU dashboard.

5:43

Good ideas don't come from brainstorms

Josh Woodward says good ideas almost never come from scheduled brainstorms or innovation sprints, but from people tinkering relentlessly on weekends or in hallways. The team maintains an ‘almost possible’ list—whenever an experience crosses the threshold from impossible to possible, they immediately assemble a team to pursue it. The Labs internal docs contain 82 predictions starting with ‘We believe the future is’—Josh admits most of them will be wrong, but maintaining a concrete vision of the future is what matters.

— Josh Woodward
14:47

Test products by watching dilated pupils, not retention charts

When judging whether an early prototype has potential, Josh looks not at DAU, not at daily-to-monthly active ratio, not at 7-day retention, but at whether someone's eyes light up during a demo and whether their body leans forward. He believes teams should ‘fall in love with the problem, not with any specific solution’—products worth shipping usually need three to five pivots, and those who fall in love with their first solution tend to crash hard.

— Josh Woodward
17:57

Teams know when to kill a project before leadership does

Whether to kill a project, Josh says, the team usually knows first—ebbing enthusiasm is the signal. He gave an example: a Gemini feature about to ship, but after internal testing and eye-contact feedback, the PM sent a company-wide email saying ‘I think we shouldn't ship this,’ and Josh publicly thanked her. Leadership's real job, he thinks, is not to decide unilaterally but to build an environment where teams can say ‘this isn't working."

— Josh Woodward
27:18

Model benchmarks are overrated; values conversations are underrated

Josh argues that the industry overestimates model benchmarks—most people don't care about Elo ratings and don't even know how to pronounce them. What's underrated is having principled debates about ‘what kind of product do we want to build and what future do we want?’ Products express the company's values; this conversation deserves more airtime than chasing speed and proxy metrics.

— Josh Woodward
31:36

Hiring depends on unlearning speed and explosive endurance

One signal Josh now watches when hiring is ‘unlearning rate’—how fast someone can learn a conclusion and then unlearn it, pivoting to a new method. Another comes from a book on Federer: ‘explosive endurance’—sprinting all-out while also taking care of yourself and your teammates for the long haul. He also stresses the ability to build trust collaborating with AI and with people; as execution costs drop, these human skills grow more valuable.

— Josh Woodward
34:23

Everyone becoming a builder is a misdiagnosis

Some think all roles will merge into one ‘builder’ type. Josh disagrees: PMs adapt fastest to new roles because their work is more horizontal, but if everyone becomes builder, specialized depth gets diluted—he compares it to majors and minors; vibe coding is the minor, don't drop the PM major. Meanwhile, Labs projects now run on 2–3 people, sometimes 4, down from the old 5–7. The team has optimized for speed over headcount.

— Josh Woodward
46:41

Labs lifecycle splits into three stages to protect new experiments

As projects like Notebook LM and Flow graduated from Labs into large-scale products, Josh spotted a risk: mature projects crowd out the effort to run zero-to-one experiments. The team now divides Labs lifecycle into ‘zero to one,’ ‘one to ten,’ and ‘ten to 100,’ each phase tracking completely different metrics—eye-contact feedback for zero to one, conversion funnels and CAC for ten to 100. This carved-out space keeps new experiments alive.

— Josh Woodward
52:58

Doubling team size is a vanity metric, not success

Scaling the experimental org, Josh points out, often trips up on hiring too fast: doubling or tripling headcount is intoxicating and often a vanity metric. He recalls an early project he led that reached 30 engineers and made him feel great—but the product-market fit was zero, buoyed only by an inspiring vision. That story predicts its own ending.

— Josh Woodward
