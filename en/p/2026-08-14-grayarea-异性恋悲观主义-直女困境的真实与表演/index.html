Straight women's dating pessimism isn't melodrama — it's political regression and economic dependence

Sarah Jones argues that straight women's awkwardness and pessimism about dating men is both a real feeling and a performance, and behind it lie political regression, economic dependence, and a complex reality reinforced by social media.

1:52

Straight women have fewer rights than they were born with

Asked whether straight women are okay, Sarah Jones says flatly: "No." She points out that although women have made progress over decades, in post-Dobbs America that progress looks fragile and is even reversing. This political reality has knock-on effects for all women, straight women included. She stresses that women now have fewer rights than they were born with, and that the Trump administration and the right are actively embracing misogyny and racism, explicitly aiming to strip women and people of color of their civil rights.

— Sarah Jones
4:18

Heteropessimism is a real feeling, not an internet abstraction

Sarah Jones explains why her column focuses on straight women rather than all women. She is bisexual herself but married to a man, so she has firsthand observation of heterosexual relationships. She wrote the column to join a broader conversation about "heteropessimism" or "heterofatalism," discussions happening both among friends and online. She wanted to know whether these feelings are real or an abstraction, and if they are real, why — and what to do about them.

— Sarah Jones
9:40

Enduring isn't weakness — it's being economically unable to leave

Responding to the question of why women endure, Sarah Jones says the reasons are complicated. Economically, women may not have the means to leave; emotionally, leaving is never easy either. She notes that women still bear most of the child-rearing burden, husbands may not share housework, and these grievances get simplified online, but reality is far more complex than the surface suggests. She admits she has wondered about it herself, but understands how hard it is.

— Sarah Jones
15:00

Straight women's self-mockery hides a kind of exceptionalism

Sarah Jones responds to the possibility that LGBTQ people roll their eyes at straight women's self-deprecation. She argues that straight women's self-mocking pessimism may carry a kind of "exceptionalism" — ignoring their own relative privilege. She reminds listeners that the forces driving men to abuse women are also anti-trans and anti-queer, so self-mockery needs to be aware of the bigger picture and avoid severing one's own predicament from broader oppression.

— Sarah Jones
18:42

Hope isn't optimism — it's believing things could get better

Sarah Jones proposes "hopeful pessimism" as a coping strategy. She distinguishes optimism from hope: optimism is believing things will get better, while hope is believing things could get better. She sees this stance as closer to reality and carrying a sense of responsibility — not only acknowledging reality but thinking about what one can do. She calls for building a society that is "easier to love in," with fewer economic and institutional obstacles.

— Sarah Jones
31:53

This isn't the first gender backlash, and history doesn't move in a straight line

Sarah Jones sees the current predicament of heterosexual relationships as a manifestation of an era of gender backlash. She notes this is not the first time America has experienced a gender backlash, nor will it be the last. Romantic relationships are hard to begin with, and now they carry added burdens like economic inequality and political regression. She reminds listeners that history is not linear progress, and people are struggling to make sense of this reality.

— Sarah Jones
34:15

Economic dependence makes "just move out" no option at all

Sarah Jones details how economic factors block women from leaving unhealthy relationships. She gives examples: moving in early because you can't afford rent, marrying for health insurance, or becoming disabled after marriage and needing a caregiver. These structural factors make the decision to "move out" hard, including practical questions like whether you can find a job or take the pet. She stresses that economic inequality is the key reason women struggle to get out.

— Sarah Jones
40:59

Men and women aren't opposites — the opposition narrative is what's dangerous

Sarah Jones warns that reducing heterosexual troubles to a men-versus-women opposition is dangerous. She points out that men and women are not truly in conflict; they face adverse cultural, social, and economic realities together. This opposition narrative fuels the zero-sum thinking of the "manosphere," which holds that women's progress necessarily harms men. She worries that heteropessimism may inadvertently reinforce this mistaken idea.

— Sarah Jones
