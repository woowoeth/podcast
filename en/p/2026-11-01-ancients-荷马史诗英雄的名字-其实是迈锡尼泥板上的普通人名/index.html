Homer's epic heroes were everyday names for ordinary Mycenaeans

On the accounting tablets from Mycenaean palaces, Eteocles and Achilles were merely the names of commoners who owed goods to the crown—the "heroic" names from Homer's epics were actually everyday names anyone bore then.

"Mycenaean" is a modern label, not what they called themselves

The term "Mycenaean" was coined by modern scholars using the name of the Mycenae site. The people themselves are called Achaeans or Danaans in Homer's epics. How they actually referred to themselves remains unknown; Linear B tablets reveal only how certain place names were written then.

— Steve Kershaw
13:18

A federation of independent states, not one unified Mycenaean empire

Mycenaean civilization was not a unified empire but a collection of independent kingdoms: Pylos, Mycenae, Argos, Thebes, and others. They shared language and religious beliefs, but remained politically separate. While Mycenae sat strategically between Argos and Tiryns and Egypt's artifacts have been found there, suggesting regional dominance, there is no evidence for a "king of kings" like Agamemnon.

— Steve Kershaw
17:28

Crete's shift to Linear B may have been cultural adoption, not conquest

Around 1450 BCE, Linear A on Crete was replaced by Linear B (the Mycenaean script), and burial styles shifted to mainland "warrior graves" with swords and helmets. The standard assumption is military invasion, but Crete's elite may simply have adopted continental customs and weaponry. No written records document an actual conquest.

— Steve Kershaw
29:59

Tablet records show "heroes" were simply common people's names

Mycenaean palace tablets primarily recorded mundane transactions: how many sheep or goats villagers owed the crown. Yet these mundane records contain the names Eteocles and Achilles—the very heroes of Homer's epics—proving these "heroic" names were ordinary labels anyone could bear. The tablets even mention one real "Smith" who made bronze tools professionally.

— Steve Kershaw
43:45

Cyclopean walls were oversized by design: built to intimidate, not defend

The "Cyclopean" fortifications at Mycenae and Tiryns were built with massive stones, seven to eight meters thick—far exceeding what actual defense required. Later Greek writers like Pausanias refused to believe mortals could build them, attributing them to mythical giants. The walls were meant to awe and intimidate. The passage before the Lion Gate was enclosed on three sides to create a killing zone where archers could fire down on attackers.

— Steve Kershaw
56:58

Agamemnon was likely a composite memory, not a historical person

Asked directly whether Agamemnon was real, Kershaw gave a blunt answer: probably not. He likely represents a vague folk memory of some powerful ruler, projected backward in time—similar to how the mythological Eurystheus may have a historical kernel. Myth preserves not what actually happened, but a version of the past crafted to justify the present.

— Steve Kershaw
1:03:23

When Mycenaean palaces fell, the people remained—only the system collapsed

Around 1200 BCE, Mycenaean palaces throughout the region were destroyed in succession. Yet this did not mean the population vanished. People continued to live at Mycenae, Tiryns, and elsewhere; they even contributed soldiers when Persia invaded Greece five centuries later. What truly disappeared was the palace-centered administration and the use of writing. The language itself—early Greek—never broke the chain of transmission.

— Tristan Hughes
1:05:27

Bronze Age collapse was systemic: the whole structure fell when one piece broke

Scholars have proposed many causes for Mycenaean collapse: earthquakes, climate change, plague, invasions by the Sea Peoples. None is definitively proven by archaeology. A more widely accepted explanation is systemic collapse—the civilization was built like a tower of blocks so interdependent that removing any single crucial support brought the entire structure down.

Glossary

Linear B
The syllabic script of early Greek used by Mycenaeans, inscribed on clay tablets and recording mostly administrative transactions.
Wanax
The title of the highest Mycenaean ruler, a precursor to the later Greek word for king.
Tholos tomb
A large beehive-shaped stone burial chamber used by Mycenaean elites.
Cyclopean walls
Massive stone fortifications that later Greeks believed could only have been built by mythical giants.
Corbelling
An architectural technique of building arches or vaults by progressively offsetting stones inward, used in Mycenaean construction.
Sea Peoples
A mysterious maritime confederation that raided the eastern Mediterranean in the late Bronze Age, their origin uncertain.
