From Our Neurons to Yours 2026-11-26

The brain's forebrain and hindbrain originate from two distinct cell types, not one

A Stanford team discovered that forebrain and hindbrain are determined not by a single type of early progenitor cell, but by two fate-restricted progenitor cell types that exist simultaneously in the embryo. This overturns the classical model, in place since 1952, that a single progenitor cell generates the entire brain.

The 1952 classical model claimed the brain has a single origin

The renowned embryologist Pieter Nieuwkoop proposed in 1952 that the entire brain originates from a single class of progenitor cells called the neural ectoderm—a claim that has since become textbook consensus. When Kyle Loh's team went back to find this cell type, however, they discovered not one, but two progenitor cell types existing simultaneously, which became the starting point for their research.

— Kyle Loh
5:40

The key difference lies in cell fate commitment, not anatomical location

During embryonic development, there's a stage where a single cell can still generate all organs throughout the body. At some point thereafter, this cell loses the ability to form skin and intestines, becoming capable only of forming the brain—this is called the first-generation brain progenitor. The real question is: at that moment, can this initial brain progenitor still generate the entire brain, or has it already been split into two separate paths—one leading to forebrain and one to hindbrain? The team set out to verify whether this fate commitment had already occurred.

— Kyle Loh
6:58

A headless chicken that lived 1.5 years shows the hindbrain runs vital functions

In 1945, a farmer severed a chicken's head but cut incompletely, removing the forebrain and midbrain while leaving the hindbrain intact. The chicken, named Mike, survived for 1.5 years. Kyle Loh uses this story to illustrate that it is the hindbrain/brainstem, not the forebrain (which we typically associate with cognition and memory), that keeps us alert, breathing, and maintains our heartbeat.

— Kyle Loh
8:56

Behind this research is a patient: a child with type 1 SMA

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is the leading genetic cause of death in infants under one year old. One of Kyle Loh's colleagues has a child diagnosed with type 1 SMA and receiving treatment at Stanford Hospital. The hindbrain motor neurons control facial, jaw, eye, tongue, and neck muscles; when these neurons are damaged, the child cannot eat or swallow, and food entering the airway can be fatal. This direct motivation drove the team's persistence in generating hindbrain neurons in culture.

— Kyle Loh
16:07

The dual-origin pattern traces back to acorn worms from over 500 million years ago

In collaboration with Stanford evolutionary biologist Chris Lowe, the team discovered that the same dual brain progenitor cell structure exists across humans, mice, chickens, zebrafish, and acorn worms—organisms that diverged from humans over 500 million years ago. This demonstrates that the dual-origin pattern is a conserved evolutionary feature across species, not a human-specific coincidence.

— Kyle Loh
17:23

Jellyfish's paired nerve rings suggest the brain is fundamentally an assembled organ

This material was too speculative to include in the paper but appears only in the podcast: cnidarians like jellyfish have no single brain; instead, they have a physically separate nerve ring at each end of their body. Kyle Loh speculates that the notion of 'the brain as an organ' may be a historical bias stemming from scientists first studying humans and mice, which have a single brain enclosed in a skull. Had they first studied jellyfish, the conclusion might have been entirely different.

— Kyle Loh
21:38

No animal exists with only a hindbrain and no forebrain, undermining textbook claims

Textbooks claim the hindbrain is 'evolutionarily the oldest component' because animals had to learn to move before becoming intelligent. But Kyle Loh points out that if the hindbrain were truly older, we should find existing animals with only a hindbrain and no forebrain. To his knowledge, no such animal exists, making the claim that 'the hindbrain is more primitive' questionable.

— Kyle Loh
26:02

Ozempic suppresses appetite through the hindbrain, not the hypothalamus, a 2024 study showed

Textbooks hold that the hypothalamus in the forebrain controls hunger and body weight. However, a 2024 Nature paper found that in mice given Ozempic, destroying neurons expressing the GLP-1 receptor in the hypothalamus had minimal effect on the drug's efficacy; destroying hindbrain neurons expressing this receptor, by contrast, nearly eliminated weight loss from Ozempic. This suggests the true site of action for Ozempic is an evolutionarily ancient hunger circuit in the hindbrain, explaining why years of drug development targeting the hypothalamus has yielded limited results.

— Kyle Loh

Glossary

neural ectoderm
A layer of embryonic cells capable of differentiating into future brain cells.
brain progenitor
An early cell whose developmental fate is restricted to forming only the brain.
GLP-1 receptor
A receptor that Ozempic and similar weight-loss drugs bind to and activate, concentrated in the hypothalamus and hindbrain.
