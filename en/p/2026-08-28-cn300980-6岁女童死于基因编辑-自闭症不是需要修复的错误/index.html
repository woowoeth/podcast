6-Year-Old Dies from Gene Editing: Autism Is Not a Defect to Fix

Autism is not a war to be won but a way of being. If parents and scientists don't accept that, they will put non-lethal children on high-risk operating tables.

5:09

The girl didn't have to die

Xu Yue's first reaction to the news headline was to wonder if the girl was so severely affected that she couldn't walk or care for herself. But it turned out that just before dying, she said a full sentence: 'I want to go home.' She wasn't severely affected—just delayed in speech development, and could have received behavioral intervention and speech therapy. But her parents chose high-risk gene editing. Xu Yue believes this reflects society's ableism—the default assumption that a life with disability isn't worth living, leading people to gamble with life to 'fix' a child who isn't 'standard.'

7:08

Scientists ran human trial after monkeys were harmed

A month before the human trial, four monkeys developed kidney damage and one developed severe liver damage. Even more shocking, the researchers' Nature paper only reported the successful parts of the monkey experiments, completely omitting the clinical data from Xiaomei's death. Xu Yue notes that the U.S. also had cases of gene-editing-related child deaths in the early 2000s, but Chinese biological research now has a 'Great Leap Forward' atmosphere, with scientists thinking, 'We want to create something America can't do.'

12:16

Shift from 'combating' to 'caring'

A U.S. congressional bill was once called the Combating Autism Act—'combating autism'—and that era saw dangerous therapies like gluten-free diets, bleach enemas, and heavy metal removal. These were later suppressed by the scientific community, partly because more and more autistic self-advocates began to speak up: 'Why do you have to turn us into what you want?' Around 2010 was a turning point; since then, no biological treatment has been claimed to 'cure' autism.

20:20

Autism 'increases' because society changed

Over the past 30 years, diagnoses of intellectual disability have declined while autism diagnoses have risen. Xu Yue argues this isn't due to a change in gene mutation rates but to cultural stereotypes: autism can be imagined as genius, while intellectual disability cannot. Meanwhile, industrialization has eliminated the manual labor that once suited autistic people—the Michelangelo-like repetitive movements and attention to detail that once earned a decent living are now seen as problems.

29:25

The golden intervention window is 18 months to 6 years

Evidence-based intervention is currently the most reliable path—if between 18 months and 2 years a child doesn't look at people, doesn't point, or only stares at spinning wheels, intervention should begin. But intervention isn't medication or gene editing; it's parents putting down their phones and sitting with their child to play: guiding joint attention, taking turns building blocks, and naturally learning language through interaction. There's no magic pill, but it might help the child enter preschool by age five.

36:28

U.S. support system: from school to housing

New York and California offer about ten hours per week of behavioral intervention, and children aged 3–5 can attend half-day inclusive special education. The IDEA Act guarantees every disabled child a free public education in the least restrictive environment. Starting at age 14.5, students get planning for post-high-school life, including community college, vocational training, or direct employment. Severely affected adults can use Medicaid for care funding, and there are options like shared housing. A California boy who didn't speak until age 11 now drives himself, has a girlfriend, and works as a janitor in a public school.

40:27

'The nail that sticks out gets hammered down' hurts Chinese families

Chinese parents are generally very involved, but language barriers prevent them from understanding special education plans; schools won't proactively offer translation because it costs money. More tricky is the cultural mindset—Chinese families believe 'the nail that sticks out gets hammered down' and are reluctant to engage in policy advocacy. Xu Yue observes that among minority groups, Black families advocate best: they understand the system, dare to fight, and know how to use the law to protect themselves and their children.

42:35

Suffering depends on whose suffering it is

When Xu Yue was younger, she might have said 'suffering needs to be eliminated,' but now she first asks: Who is suffering? If it's the autistic person's own pain, intervention is warranted; if it's suffering caused by social stereotypes—like thinking a disabled life is over—then society is what needs to change. 'Why can Hawking be considered worth living and a scientist with only his eyes left, yet this little girl's life is deemed not worth living?' A thorough solution requires a social support system to underpin it, not parents bearing all the risk alone.

Glossary

ableism
The bias that treats 'able-bodiedness' as the default standard and value, deeming lives with disability not worth living.
IDEA
The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, a U.S. federal law guaranteeing students with disabilities aged 3–21 a free and least-restrictive public education.
evidence-based intervention
Interventions proven effective by substantial scientific research; in autism, primarily behavioral intervention.
ASD
Autism spectrum disorder, a neurodevelopmental condition with onset in childhood, involving social communication and sensory features; a clinical behavioral diagnosis.
ABA
Applied behavior analysis, the behavioral approach with the most evidence in autism clinical intervention, commonly used in schools and treatment centers.
Medicaid
U.S. health coverage for low-income individuals, sometimes paying for caregiving costs for those with severe conditions.
