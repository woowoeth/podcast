Screenwriters should fear humans becoming machines, not machines becoming human

Song Fangjin says AI can now write story outlines better than 60% of screenwriters. The real crisis isn't technology replacing creation—it's that creators have already turned themselves into machines of formula.

16:11

Screenwriters can never shake off the tool-maker's curse

Song Fangjin says that since cinema's birth in 1895, no screenwriter has possessed complete expressive agency—scripts must pass through directors, cinematographers, actors, and producers before reaching audiences, inevitably transformed. The credit is asymmetrical: when a film fails, screenwriters bear the pain; when it succeeds, they feel no pride because the achievement belongs to the director, actors, and producers. He sees one path to relief: director and screenwriter as the same person.

— Song Fangjin
42:19

In micro-dramas, every minute must score a goal

Song Fangjin explains mechanically why micro-dramas can't sustain weighty stories: in a two-hour film, major character-relationship shifts happen only three times; a fourth would send the story into meaningless loops. But micro-dramas must complete a full character-relationship arc every minute—no ten-minute setup like film allows. He compares it to soccer: a feature film is watching the full match; a micro-drama is every kick must go in and score.

— Song Fangjin
56:28

AI outlines now exceed 60 percent of screenwriters

Song Fangjin says he felt anxiety about AI for the first time this year—not whether it will replace screenwriters, but that testing major models showed they write story outlines better than 60% of screenwriters. He traces the deeper crisis to Chinese screenwriting itself: whether from clients demanding data-driven metrics or screenwriters imitating tired formulas, the output ships as standardized, prefab—which he sees as more serious than AI itself.

— Song Fangjin
1:14:36

Major IP franchises turn screenwriters into commodities via copyright

Song Fangjin marks 2015 as a watershed in Chinese film and TV: capital discovered that screenwriters, directors, and actors wouldn't obey, so it invented the big-IP model. Invest tens of millions or hundreds of millions to buy an IP, own all rights, and reduce the screenwriter to a de-credited tool. This killed original screenplays—platforms fund only data-backed IP. The healthy ratio of adaptation to original to remake—6:3:1—became 8:1:1 domestically.

— Song Fangjin
1:56:55

The screenwriter is the household registrar of the film

Why do nearly all directors claim the screenwriting credit, yet Zhang Yimou rarely does? Song Fangjin explains: the screenwriter is the only person holding original copyright in a film or series. He compares credits to a household registration—whether the screenwriter's name appears there decides if they're one of the proprietors. Zhang Yimou's custom to credit himself as story rather than screenwriter, in Song Fangjin's view, is rare decency.

— Song Fangjin
2:13:09

Zhang Yimou told a UFO story as a joke

Song Fangjin recounts Zhang Yimou's anecdote: in 1984, while filming a parade, his consciousness lifted away for three to five minutes watching a hovering UFO, then returned—afterward, he stopped tiring or getting hungry easily. Song Fangjin stresses Zhang told it as a joke and relayed it the same way. Years later, he found the story in Zhou Xiaofen's official biography and felt free to repeat it publicly.

— Song Fangjin
2:56:32

Deny the audience closure, and lose millions at the box office

Song Fangjin calculates the price of leaving viewers without resolution using two recent releases: Three Teams added a final shot of the protagonist lost in thought—Song estimates it cost the film 50 million yuan in box office. Chang'an: The Lychees ends with the hero weeping over lychees; if recut to show him living quietly with family, Song believes it could have earned 200 million yuan more. Creators must decide upfront who the story serves, then choose whether to fight the audience's need for closure.

— Song Fangjin
3:10:43

Hollywood screenwriter credits require a minimum contribution threshold

Hollywood has strict rules: a film can carry at most three screenwriter names (or three teams of three), and each must prove they contributed over 33% to the script; for adaptations, the bar is higher. Below that threshold, you get "additional screenwriter," off the credits entirely. China has no equivalent arbitration, spawning categories like "associate screenwriter"—an invention of Guo Jingming—and gray-market practices where established screenwriters hire ghostwriters, then pay them to surrender credit.

— Song Fangjin
