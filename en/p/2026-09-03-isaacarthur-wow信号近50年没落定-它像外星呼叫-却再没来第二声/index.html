The Wow! Signal Hasn't Been Settled in Nearly 50 Years: It Looks Like an Alien Call, but No Second One Ever Came

That 72-second narrowband hydrogen-line signal from 1977 has never repeated, and it falls short of the threshold for evidence of an alien civilization; its legacy isn't an answer but a reminder: in a noisy universe, the distance between detection and proof is an entire galaxy.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:09

The 72-second length comes from Earth's rotation

Big Ear was a transit telescope: it didn't turn to track a target, it let Earth's rotation sweep the whole sky past overhead. A point source fixed on the celestial sphere crossing the beam naturally takes about 72 seconds — exactly 1/1200 of a day. So the duration doesn't mean the sender deliberately spoke for only 72 seconds; what really matters is the gain curve: a real source enters the beam, strengthens, leaves the beam, weakens. The Wow! signal matched that whole profile, and any later terrestrial-interference explanation has to clear that bar first.

5:10

6EQUJ5 is an intensity curve, not a cipher

6EQUJ5 is the signal-strength reading under Big Ear's early print format: each symbol represents an observation window of about 12 seconds, and digits and letters indicate the multiple above background noise. In time order they are 6, 14, 26, 30, 19, 5 — strong in the middle, weak at both ends, like a brightness curve written sideways. Renormalized to 1–100, that's 17, 40, 74, 86, 54, 14. The host's quip is worth remembering: if the aliens' first sentence looks like a failed software activation code, that's a reasonable opening too.

— Isaac Arthur
7:11

It hugs the hydrogen line, yet sits 50 kHz off

1420 MHz is the radio spectral line of neutral hydrogen, the natural landmark of the universe's most abundant element, and the frequency SETI most often assumes a call would use. Because Big Ear had its master oscillator set 0.1 MHz high, the Wow! signal's frequency has two corrected values: 1420.356 MHz or 1420.456 MHz; the true hydrogen line is at about 1420.406 MHz. Both are only about 50 kHz off, as if deliberately avoiding hydrogen noise while using the hydrogen line as a coordinate. But that spot is also full of natural hydrogen-cloud signal sources, and frequency alone can't distinguish cleverness from coincidence.

— Isaac Arthur
11:13

One horn received it, the other didn't

Big Ear had two feed horns side by side. A source fixed on the celestial sphere, swept past by Earth's rotation, should enter the two horns in sequence and be recorded twice. The Wow! signal appeared in only one horn, and the records don't say which, so the sky retains two candidate positions. Possible reasons include: the source itself lit up only briefly, a narrow beam happened to graze past, or the interference came from nearby. Every later search — including Breakthrough Listen's 2022 hunt for Sun-like stars in the candidate region with Green Bank and the Allen array — heard no repeat. The missing second detection is the core reason it can't make the confirmed list.

— Isaac Arthur
15:17

Terrestrial interference and satellites are both hard to mimic

Earth is noisy, but a genuine candidate signal has to pass the 72-second profile test: an ordinary aircraft crossing the beam wouldn't last so conveniently long; a low-Earth-orbit satellite usually sweeps past in just seconds. A satellite would need a chain of coincidences to do it from high orbit, via a side lobe, or on a special orbit. Terrestrial transmissions are banned in the internationally protected 1420 MHz band, but illegal equipment or faulty electronics could produce out-of-band signals that leak in through an antenna side lobe. There's also an effect called interstellar scintillation that can briefly brighten a distant signal, but that requires an amplified source to exist in the first place. None of these explanations is perfect, which is exactly why the puzzle survives.

— Isaac Arthur
19:18

A magnetar could pump a hydrogen cloud into a maser

Research drawing on Arecibo archives has produced a natural explanation: some high-energy transient — a magnetar flare, say — pumps atoms in a cold neutral hydrogen cloud into a population inversion, briefly turning the cloud into a natural microwave maser, much as a laser amplifies microwaves. That would explain several things at once: the signal came from space, sat near the hydrogen line, was strong enough, yet appeared only once; the hydrogen cloud was glowing as the first horn swept past and had gone dark by the time the second horn arrived. The host says this is his personal favorite, because it solves multiple problems within one mechanism, but it remains a speculative path with no conclusive evidence.

— Isaac Arthur
27:27

Becoming a discovery means passing four gates

If a signal like this arrives again, calling it a true technosignature would require at least four conditions: one, it repeats at the same position, so we know it's still there; two, telescopes at two different locations receive it simultaneously, ruling out Earth-based interference; three, the signal carries modulation, encoding or mathematical structure, not just a bare carrier; four, known natural processes are ruled out one by one. Historically, when pulsars were first discovered they were nicknamed "little green men," showing that nature can perfectly impersonate intelligence. The right posture isn't to pick a side between belief and mockery, but to allow the excitement and then do everything possible to prove you're fooling yourself.

— Isaac Arthur
31:29

Its legacy is a window that was far too narrow

The Wow! signal appeared for 72 seconds in exactly the form early SETI hoped to recognize — narrowband, near the hydrogen line, strong enough, shaped like a real source in the sky — and then nothing more. It showed what a great discovery might look like before the evidence is sufficient. The host's landing point: the galaxy is 100,000 light-years wide and billions of years old, and even the largest civilization is just one flickering spark within it. If civilizations don't spread, don't transmit often, and don't often point a beam at us, the Fermi paradox may not exist at all. With that 1/1200 of a day, the Wow! signal briefly put that possibility in front of humanity.

Glossary

transit telescope
A radio telescope with a fixed orientation that doesn't track celestial objects; Earth's rotation sweeps the sky through its beam.
hydrogen line
The spectral line emitted by neutral hydrogen near 1420 MHz, the most common radio landmark in the universe.
water hole
The quiet radio band between the hydrogen line and the hydroxyl lines, where SETI often assumes civilizations would call.
maser
A microwave-band stimulated-emission amplification mechanism that can produce narrowband strong signals like a laser.
side lobe
A secondary reception direction outside the main beam; strong interference can leak into a telescope through a side lobe.
technosignature
An observable signal or trace indicating a non-natural technological origin, the search target of SETI.
