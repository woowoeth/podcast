Lawrence wrote letters exposing British lies about Iraq, then faced a coordinated smear campaign from former colleagues

His scathing critique of the Iraq occupation presaged the war's reality a century later, but those very criticisms triggered a full-scale smear campaign from the India Office and Foreign Office—charging he didn't speak Arabic and had no right to speak.

6:17

Seven Pillars of Wisdom is fundamentally a book about guilt

Rory Stewart argues that contemporary scholars are eager to ‘discover’ Lawrence's betrayal, but Lawrence himself was writing about betrayal and guilt from the opening pages—he wanted simultaneously to serve Britain, the Arab cause, and himself, and quickly realized these were mutually incompatible. After retiring with the rank of colonel, he later enlisted under a pseudonym as a private, spending years folding blankets, a man entirely broken by guilt.

— Rory Stewart
12:31

Lawrence's letters to the Times read as prophecies of the Iraq War

In the 1920s, Lawrence wrote as a journalist to denounce British rule in Iraq as more brutal and closer to catastrophe than the public had been told. When Rory Stewart found himself in the same area of Iraq in 2003, he was startled by how exactly these letters matched the American and British occupation that followed. More tellingly, when Lawrence began criticizing British policy, the Foreign Office and India Office systematically spread claims that he ‘didn't speak Arabic’ and ‘had no right to speak’—the identical tactic that Rory himself would later encounter when critiquing Afghan policy.

— Rory Stewart
16:42

American military treated Lawrence as counterinsurgency theorist but forgot he was a terrorist

Rory Stewart speaks plainly: by modern standards, Lawrence blowing up railways and spreading fear was terrorism—just terrorism from the Ottoman perspective. American generals around Petraeus later drew deeply on Lawrence's counterinsurgency theory, yet ironically these Americans who admired him were precisely the type of men Lawrence himself would have found alien: gym-hardened tough guys rather than the slight, bookish figure Lawrence was.

— Rory Stewart
27:06

Rory Stewart says he was a modern-day colonial administrator

He acknowledges serving as deputy governor of two Iraqi provinces—which was essentially an imperial scenario replayed. The Coalition Provisional Authority announced it held complete governmental power over all of Iraq, and he found himself unelected but responsible for police, elections, and tribal disputes. He sees two ways to judge men like Lawrence and Alexander Burnes: as complicit in colonial violence, or by the standards of their own time, as markedly different from colleagues interested only in hunting and utterly indifferent to the place.

— Rory Stewart
34:32

At Versailles, Faisal's delegation arrived nearly empty-handed

William Dalrymple notes that when Faisal arrived at the Paris Peace Conference accompanied by Lawrence, they lacked even complete maps and McMahon letters, while Weizmann's Zionist delegation arrived six weeks early with river maps, agricultural surveys, and immigration projections already in hand, having already cultivated Lloyd George and Balfour. Rory counters that this is an unfair comparison: Weizmann had been a British citizen since 1904, teaching chemistry in Manchester, whereas Faisal was not a British subject, had no team or resources, and faced structural disadvantages that were already determined.

— William Dalrymple
37:35

Many smears on Lawrence originated in old grievances of the India Office

McMahon, who drafted the McMahon letters promising Sharif Hussein Arab lands, claimed superior Arabic, Urdu, and Persian to Lawrence and came from an old-line Anglo-Indian political family. The discussion notes that Indian circles had long harbored contempt for young officers like Lawrence self-appointing themselves as tribal advisers—an internal hierarchy of disdain older than any anti-colonial stance.

— Rory Stewart
41:45

Lawrence's military philosophy: never give orders to Arabs

In the Arab Bulletin, Lawrence's advice to young officers was: don't command, but guide sheikhs to decide and err for themselves like children learning, even if imperfectly. Dalrymple describes this as ‘behaving like an Edwardian wife’—precisely the kind of ‘soft leadership’ that strongman officers who admire him today would most scorn.

— William Dalrymple
45:53

Gaza's responsibility cannot be retroactively pinned on Lawrence a century ago

When asked whether the mandate system was the trigger for Gaza's crisis, Rory Stewart counters sharply: attributing deaths in Gaza in 2024 and 2025 to a British officer in his twenties who spent only eighteen months on the ground is absurd. He grants that Lawrence was part of the chain of Zionism, but insists responsibility lies with the Israeli government and what he calls Netanyahu's ‘murder bureaucracy’; any attempt to relativize present crimes through history is ‘insane’.

— Rory Stewart
