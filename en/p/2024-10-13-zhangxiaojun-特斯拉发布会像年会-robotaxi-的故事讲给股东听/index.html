Tesla's Launch Felt Like an Annual Meeting: The Robotaxi Story Is Told to Shareholders

This launch event didn't discuss any technology; it felt more like an annual meeting for longtime shareholders. What's truly valuable is its two paths to monetize idle vehicle time: as a Robotaxi, or as distributed compute.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

8:40

Supervised is a cleverly evasive word

Meng Xing believes Musk replaced the L2/L3/L4 framework with supervised and unsupervised, which is a clever evasion. In the past, L4 meant two things: the car has the capability, and it bears responsibility in an accident. But FSD is 'I have this function, but I don't bear this responsibility'—under Chinese regulations it might still count as an L2 device. So unsupervised is more like: after the takeover rate is low enough, you can randomly pick a period to sleep and with high probability won't encounter a scenario requiring takeover. It's essentially an improvement in L2 assisted driving capability, not a true Robotaxi.

— Meng Xing
14:28

A car used 10 hours out of 168 is not a reason for driverless

Musk gave two reasons: an average passenger car is used only about 10 hours out of 168 hours a week, and after autonomy usage frequency would increase 5 to 10 times; and autonomous driving is 10 times safer than human driving. Meng Xing says both are theoretically reasonable, but the first is not a reason for driverless—it's more a reason for the sharing economy. Cars are underutilized, which is exactly what Didi, Uber, and car-sharing have been solving; it's an old story told for over a decade in the ride-hailing era. The second, surpassing human safety, is a potential possibility, but today all autonomous driving companies are still chasing humans, and Tesla itself is far below actual human driving conditions.

— Meng Xing
25:20

Cost dropping from $1 to $0.2 means humans are 80%

Musk said Cybercab operating cost drops to $0.2 per mile, with total cost including taxes and fees at $0.3 to $0.4. Meng Xing did the math: from $1 with a driver to $0.2, a drop of 80%, implies human cost accounts for 80%, which is an overestimate. In the ride-hailing system, human cost is the highest proportion, but generally estimated around 50%. And after removing the human, you still have to add back compute chips, sensors, insurance, operations and maintenance, etc. His more rational estimate is a net reduction of 30% to 40%, from $1 possibly to 60-70 cents.

— Meng Xing
28:19

The shepherd model ignores that individual capital is most expensive

Musk talks about how in the past a ride-hailing driver could only drive one car, but now you can manage a flock of Cybercabs like a shepherd, being a car partner holding assets to earn money. Meng Xing sees this as financial leverage, so Tesla doesn't have to buy cars itself. But he tends to think it's wishful thinking: the ride-hailing system already has CPs (car partners), asset-holding companies; in China there may be thousands, large ones with thousands of cars, small ones with dozens. The reason the industry hasn't become individuals holding a batch of cars is that the cost of individual capital is not cheaper than a company's; many CPs have special resources or low capital costs, e.g., automakers setting up their own CPs with existing financing lease businesses. Individuals borrowing is definitely the most expensive.

— Meng Xing
34:22

Driverless turns hidden costs into explicit costs

Meng Xing says managing a car takes a lot of thought: where to wait for jobs, parking, fines, car washing, maintenance, repairs, charging and refueling. When a driver brings their own car, these costs aren't counted because they'd maintain, wash, and fuel the car anyway. But without a driver, these become very explicit costs, and they're not easy to solve. For example, charging: the system needs to know it can't take more orders when low on battery, know where charging stations are, drive there—many are in underground garages, which may not have maps, and you have to back in, but most Robotaxis today don't have automatic reversing capability, generally only under remote safety driver monitoring. Then you have to take the charging gun, open the cover, return it after charging, and pay. Waymo's choice is to build large depots with centralized staff, but that brings a series of complex issues like deadhead miles, real estate site selection, depot density, and automation level.

— Meng Xing
38:54

Stock fell, Uber rose instead

On the night of the launch, Tesla fell 4% to 5% in after-hours trading, while Uber rose 8%, and about 10% after the open. Meng Xing says this is the result of secondary market investors voting with their money, believing Tesla didn't deliver what it said—at least not expressed at the launch. Conversely, Uber, as the most threatened competitor, may now be safer.

— Meng Xing
41:30

When the car isn't driving, there are two ways to monetize

Musk said every Tesla has 1000 watts of inference compute capability, used 50 to 60 hours a week, and the rest of the time it can become distributed inference compute resource, adding up to over 100 watts of compute. Meng Xing says this could mean the world's largest compute capacity, many times larger than the largest supercomputing center; Li Bin has said similar things. He calls it option value—a potential, undeveloped but readily monetizable upside. So after the car is sold, it shows value when driving, and when not driving there are two additional ways to monetize: as a Robotaxi, or as a compute center. Both possibilities are huge opportunities.

— Meng Xing
50:38

The robot is too smooth, 80% likely teleoperated

The only thing exceeding expectations was Optimus, but Meng Xing says it's a bit too good to be true. He observed: when the robot played rock-paper-scissors, its right hand played, and the left hand had an unnecessary small movement at the end—very human-like. Unless it's truly end-to-end imitation learning that learned these small movements, these actions shouldn't be there. He talked to many robotics people yesterday, and about 80% confidence says it was teleoperated. Teleoperation is generally used in training robots to imitate and collect data, which also explains why the robot's legs didn't move—walking while moving would make teleoperation very difficult.

— Meng Xing

Glossary

BOM
Bill of Materials, the cost of a car's component materials.
CP / car partner
In the ride-hailing system, an asset-holding company that buys cars and rents them to drivers.
Remote Assist
In the L4 framework, a cloud-based safety operator provides auxiliary decision-making to the car in complex scenarios.
option value
An undeveloped but readily monetizable potential upside value.
teleoperation
A person uses a robotic arm or device to control a robot's movements in real time, often used to collect imitation learning data.
