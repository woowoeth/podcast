Tesla's Launch Felt Like an Annual Shareholder Meeting: The Robotaxi Story Was Told to Investors

This event contained no technology at all — it was more like an annual meeting for longtime shareholders. What's actually valuable is the two ways it monetises a car's idle hours: as a Robotaxi, or as distributed compute.

8:40

supervised is a convenient piece of wordplay

Meng Xing argues Musk swapped the L2/L3/L4 framework for supervised and unsupervised, which is a convenient dodge. L4 used to mean two things: the car has the capability, and it bears liability when something goes wrong. FSD-type systems are ‘I have this capability, but I don't bear this liability’ — under Chinese regulation they might still count as L2 equipment. So unsupervised is more like: once the takeover rate is low enough, you can pick a stretch of time at random, take a nap, and probably won't hit a scenario requiring takeover. In essence it's still an improvement in L2 assisted-driving capability, not a true Robotaxi.

— Meng Xing
14:28

168 hours a week, driven 10, is not an argument for driverlessness

Musk gave two reasons: an ordinary passenger car averages only about 10 hours of use out of 168 hours a week, and autonomy would raise utilisation 5 to 10 times; and autonomous driving is 10 times safer than human driving. Meng Xing says both are theoretically reasonable, but the first is not an argument for driverlessness — it's more an argument for the sharing economy. A car being underutilised is exactly the problem Didi, Uber and car-sharing have been solving; it's the same old story ride-hailing has told for over a decade. The second — surpassing humans on safety — is a potential possibility, but today every autonomous driving company is still chasing humans, and Tesla itself is far below actual human driving performance.

— Meng Xing
25:20

Cost falling from $1 to $0.2 means humans are 80% of it

Musk said Cybercab operating cost falls to $0.2 per mile, with total cost including taxes and fees at $0.3 to $0.4. Meng Xing did the math: falling from $1 with a human driver to $0.2 is an 80% drop, which implies human cost accounts for 80% — an overestimate. In the ride-hailing system human cost is the single largest share, but the usual estimate is around 50%. And once you remove the human you have to add back compute chips, sensors, insurance, operations and maintenance. His more rational estimate is a net reduction of 30% to 40%, with $1 possibly falling to 60 or 70 cents.

— Meng Xing
28:19

The shepherd model ignores that individual capital is the most expensive

Musk's pitch: a ride-hailing driver used to be able to run only one car, but now you can manage a flock of Cybercabs like a shepherd, becoming a car partner who holds the asset and earns from it. Meng Xing sees this as financial leverage — Tesla doesn't have to buy the cars itself. But he leans toward calling it wishful thinking: the ride-hailing system already has CPs (car partners), asset-holding companies, possibly thousands of them in China, the big ones with thousands of cars and the small ones with a few dozen. The reason the industry hasn't become individuals holding a fleet of cars is that capital accumulated by individuals costs more than a company's capital; many CPs have special resources or low capital costs — for example, automakers setting up their own CPs, or already having leasing businesses. Borrowing as an individual is definitely the most expensive.

— Meng Xing
34:22

Driverlessness turns hidden costs into visible ones

Meng Xing says managing a car takes a lot of thought: where to park while waiting for a job, fines, car washes, maintenance, repairs, charging and refuelling. When a driver brings their own car in, these costs aren't counted, because you'd maintain, wash and fuel the car whether or not you drove ride-hailing. But with no driver in an autonomous car, these become very visible costs, and they aren't easy to solve. Take charging: the system has to know it can't take more orders when the battery is low, know where the chargers are, and drive there — many chargers are in basements, basements don't necessarily have maps, and you have to reverse in, yet most Robotaxis today can't reverse autonomously and generally only do so under remote safety-operator supervision. Then you have to take the charging gun, open the port, put it back when done, and pay. Waymo's choice is to build big depots and staff them centrally, but that brings a whole chain of complex problems: deadhead miles, real-estate site selection, depot density and degree of automation.

— Meng Xing
38:54

The stock fell, and Uber rose instead

On the night of the event Tesla fell 4% to 5% in after-hours trading while Uber rose 8%, and roughly 10% after the open. Meng Xing says this is secondary-market investors voting with their money, judging that Tesla didn't deliver what it said — at least not at the event. Conversely, Uber, as the most threatened competitor, may now actually be safe.

— Meng Xing
41:30

When the car isn't driving, there are two ways to monetise it

Musk said every Tesla has 1,000 watts of inference compute, used 50 to 60 hours a week, and the rest of the time it can become distributed inference compute — adding up to over 100 watts of compute capacity. Meng Xing says that implies possibly the largest compute capacity in the world, many times larger than the biggest supercomputing centre, and Li Bin has said something similar. He calls it option value — a potential, undeveloped upside that can be monetised at any time. So after a car is sold, it delivers value while being driven, and when it isn't driving there are two extra ways to monetise it: as a Robotaxi, or as a compute centre. Both possibilities are enormous opportunities.

— Meng Xing
50:38

The robot is too smooth — 80% likely it's teleoperated

The only thing that beat expectations all evening was Optimus, but Meng Xing says it exceeded expectations a little too good to be true. He noticed that when the robot played rock-paper-scissors, it used its right hand, and the left hand still made an unnecessary little gesture at the end — very much what a human would do. Unless end-to-end imitation learning really did pick up those little motions, they shouldn't be there. He talked to many robotics people yesterday, and about 80% confidence says it was teleoperated. Teleoperation is normally used in training robots and collecting imitation-learning data, which also explains why the robot's legs don't move — walking and moving at the same time makes teleoperation very difficult.

— Meng Xing

Glossary

BOM
Bill of Materials — the parts and materials cost of a car.
CP / car partner
In the ride-hailing system, an asset-holding company that buys cars and rents them to drivers.
Remote Assist
Within the L4 framework, a cloud-based safety operator providing auxiliary decision-making to the car in complex scenarios.
option value
A potential upside that has not yet been developed but can be monetised at any time.
Teleoperation
A human using a robotic arm or device to control a robot's movements in real time, often used to collect imitation-learning data.
