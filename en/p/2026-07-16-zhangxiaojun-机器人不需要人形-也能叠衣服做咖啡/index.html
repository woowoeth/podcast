Robots Don't Need to Be Humanoid to Fold Laundry and Make Coffee

Pi used three main papers to push the robot brain from capability to generalization to performance. Ke Liyiming (柯丽一鸣) argues humanoid form is not the problem to solve right now, real-robot data is irreplaceable, and the experience data robots generate themselves is the next step.

1:54:43

Pi's three main papers each answer one question

Ke Liyiming breaks Pi's main publications into three keywords: π0 is capability, π0.5 is generalization, π*0.6 is performance. π0 did three tasks—folding laundry, folding boxes, and tabletop pick-and-place. The November 2024 laundry folding was at a level no one had seen at the time. π0.5 had to answer the in-domain and out-of-domain question—if the model only performs well in houses it was trained on, its impact is limited. So they went to a hundred Airbnbs to collect data and found that after data reaches a certain size, performance in new houses plateaus; you don't need to collect every home in the world. π*0.6 returns to the skepticism of "can do everything but does nothing well," emphasizing simplicity of method and letting the agent collect its own experience data in the real world and put it back into the training pool.

— Ke Liyiming
2:00:12

Data robots generate themselves is cheaper than human teleoperation

Real-robot data is expensive. Ke Liyiming breaks it down: you need a real robot platform, you need to put it in people's homes, you need to maintain it, you need to find someone to do tasks, and you need to communicate with that person to plan. The most complicated part is managing that person. But if you replace the data manipulation source with an already-trained large model and let the model roll out on its own, the data cost can drop a lot. He is quite bullish that robots will eventually be cheap and convenient. Many people's current worries about data are actually unimportant in the long river of time, because the data collected by so many robots during deployment can all be used by me. This is also the meaning of the asterisk in π*0.6: put finished data back into the model.

— Ke Liyiming
2:03:15

Bad data is good data, but you can also let the robot run itself

Imitation learning has a serious problem called compounding errors: every tiny mistake gets amplified, and the robot reaches a terrible situation it never imagined and that a human data collector would never encounter. Once it enters, it doesn't know how to correct its path. So when collecting data, you not only need perfect human data, but also correction data on how the robot recovers after entering a bad situation and continues to complete the task. But Ke Liyiming again emphasizes that correction data can also come from the robot running itself—this is the more essential thing about reinforcement learning. On the real-robot versus simulation data debate, he is "black cat or white cat, it's a good cat if it catches mice," but fundamentally believes in real-robot data, because for tasks like laundry folding with flexible materials, friction, and stickiness, a simulator may simply not be able to produce it.

— Ke Liyiming
2:09:17

Evaluation is why the robot frontier is hardest to define

In NLP, generating a sentence and having someone score it was already not easy. Robots are worse: you have to run it on a robot to know what it did and to score it, and you're constrained by mechanical physics—some variations you don't even know about will affect model performance. Take the task of picking up a cup: the cup placed anywhere on the table, different lighting, different backgrounds, table height, even a pillar instead of a table, the cup's own angle—infinite initial conditions all affect final performance. Difficulty in evaluation directly means the robotics field doesn't have a leaderboard like other fields where you release a huge dataset, every model runs it, and you see who's first. So the frontier is very hard to define now, everyone's directions are scattered, all moving in different directions, and no one knows which line is the main road.

— Ke Liyiming
2:32:30

If Pi did humanoids, he wouldn't have joined

Ke Liyiming talked with Pi within a week of its founding in 2024, asking whether they would do humanoids first and what commercialization they would do. The atmosphere he felt was that they indeed would not do humanoids. He said if they had said humanoids, he wouldn't have come, because he worried that people from different backgrounds doing humanoids would shift the research focus to how to make a better humanoid robot, while his research focus is making better tasks. He strongly believes you can make very good tasks without humanoids, and you can make them faster without humanoids—folding laundry, folding boxes, making coffee were all done first, and can be done without humanoids. He thinks this is a matter of taste, and there are endless tasks waiting to be done.

— Ke Liyiming
2:46:33

Reinforcement learning is essentially Pavlov's dog

Ke Liyiming says reinforcement learning is explained as Pavlov's dog: the dog does something, you give it a reward, and from then on it knows this thing is good and should be done more. Through reward and punishment, the agent learns better actions. It has a lot to do with human self-improvement; pursuing excellence is a very strong factor in reinforcement learning—just keep practicing. Another important part is exploration, which can be big or small—maybe moving a muscle slightly to the left improves tennis performance, or maybe switching research directions. There's also attribution: a pet does a series of things and you finally give a reward, and it needs to know which thing was the decisive factor for getting the good reward. π*0.6 includes exploration: collecting so much deployment data, which step in which data point was done well, making the whole data point get the key reward.

— Ke Liyiming
3:16:50

China's hardware dominance leaves the US unsure how to catch up

Ke Liyiming says a year or two ago, wanting to see a demo at the level of the Spring Festival Gala was just a thought; seeing it actually done is very exciting, backed by algorithmic improvements and hardware strength. Looking at the China-US industry comparison at this stage, the most direct feeling is that China has leadership and dominance in hardware. It's hard to imagine a robot company assembling a robot with no Chinese parts in it. China's advantage in supply chain and manufacturing is simply too large; he doesn't even know how Americans would catch up. At the same time, hardware iteration gives existing algorithms some advantages toward commercial deployment. But he also admits that lacking the supply chain and manufacturing link will slow down US companies' iteration speed.

— Ke Liyiming
3:17:50

Premature commercialization cost Covariant general-purpose generalization

Ke Liyiming explains why Pi is temporarily not considering commercialization at all, saying there's a historical reason. Peter Abbeel was Sergey Levine's advisor and has had two startup experiences: one making a system to grade student homework, now basically used by all US universities; another founded in 2015 or 2016 called Covariant, a robotics company aiming to do general machine learning solutions for robots. But at some point the company started focusing deeply on logistics and warehouse robots. Looking back from a large-model development perspective, this was actually a distraction from developing large models—because of premature commercialization, it lost general-purpose generalization, put energy into many commercialization-related things, and didn't really trace back to fundamental problems. So Pi, influenced by this experience, emphasizes not thinking too much about commercialization, and everyone purely says they want to do research and make its performance the best.

— Ke Liyiming

Glossary

VLA / Vision-Language-Action Model
A robot architecture that unifies vision, language, and action into one model.
in domain / out of domain
The difference in model performance within and outside the coverage of training data.
Diffusion Policy
A method that uses diffusion models to generate robot actions.
sim2real
Transferring policies trained in a simulator to real robots.
throughput
An evaluation metric proposed by π*0.6, measuring the amount of successful task completion per unit time.
FAST / action representation space
A Pi paper studying how to learn a better action representation for large models to predict.
