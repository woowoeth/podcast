Volkswagen spent £430 million to buy Rolls-Royce but couldn't buy the name

Since the 1971 split, Rolls-Royce's trademark has not belonged to the automaker; Volkswagen spent £430 million buying the factory and Bentley but couldn't acquire the Rolls-Royce name itself, and had to cede it to BMW.

8:54

Bentley's turbo strategy turned it from 5% to 50% of Crewe's production

Bentley was originally just Rolls-Royce's second-tier badge variant; in 1980, fewer than 5% of Crewe's output carried the Bentley name. David Plastow, who led at the time, spotted Bentley's racing heritage and its distinctly sporty positioning, so he commissioned independent turbocharged models like the Mulsanne Turbo. Within six years by 1989, Bentley's share of Crewe's production had rocketed from 5% to 50%, becoming the company's true profit driver.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
15:38

BMW wanted to buy the Rolls-Royce badge to charge luxury prices

BMW group CEO Pischetsrieder was blunt about it: BMW had the capacity to build a car that matched Rolls-Royce in performance—technology wasn't the issue. The problem was that a BMW badge on the grille couldn't command that price. Swap it for Rolls-Royce and the price could climb to the heavens. BMW had already maxed out the luxury segment; what it needed was an ultra-luxury brand to fill the pricing gap—the core motivation driving its persistence more than Toyota or Ford would have shown.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
18:09

Eight days after signing with BMW, Volkswagen outbid it at the airport lounge

Vickers had accepted BMW's £340 million first and final bid on April 29, 1998—the deal seemed done. Just eight days later, Volkswagen CEO Piëch flew into London's Northolt Airport on a company jet, met Vickers' chief commercial officer face-to-face in the Churchill lounge, and handed over an offer of £430 million—nearly 25% higher. A month later, Vickers' shareholders voted for Volkswagen.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
21:05

The Rolls-Royce name and logo stayed with the engine maker, not the automaker

The crucial clause was buried in the 1971 split: the Rolls-Royce name and the RR logo were permanently kept by Rolls-Royce plc (the aero engine business), merely ‘permanently licensed free of charge’ to the car company—with two veto rights. If the car business sold to a foreign company, or if quality fell below standard, Rolls-Royce plc could revoke the license. Volkswagen got the factory, drawings, the Bentley brand name, and the Spirit of Ecstasy hood ornament, but not the Rolls-Royce name itself.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
26:08

The licensing risk was public from the start; Volkswagen did its homework anyway

Many post-mortems paint Volkswagen as a cash-rich bumbler caught off guard, but the episode notes this risk was always public: when the 1971 agreement was made, the UK government reported it to the EU Commission; in 1991 when Crewe laid off staff, parliament members publicly questioned the trademark clause; and in early 1998, Vickers itself petitioned the EU to abolish both restrictive conditions. VW's executives had also quietly sounded out Rolls-Royce plc's intentions.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
31:11

Volkswagen bet on fait accompli, but BMW fought back and won the name

Piëch's strategy fit his reputation: control the factory, technology, and people first, put the chips on the table, then force Rolls-Royce plc to comply—skip the direct negotiations. But BMW moved fast. It immediately paid £40 million to buy the license to the Rolls-Royce trademark from Rolls-Royce plc, and on July 9 issued notice that one year later it would stop supplying engines to Rolls-Royce cars—cutting off the oxygen.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
32:11

Two state premiers settled the split on a golf course in mid-July

The stalemate lasted until the morning of July 28, when Volkswagen, BMW, and Vickers suddenly signed a memorandum of understanding at a golf course in Germany: Volkswagen kept Crewe and the Bentley brand; BMW took the Rolls-Royce trademark and pledged to build a new factory in the UK; cars built at Crewe during the transition could still wear the Rolls-Royce nameplate until the end of 2002. Present besides the three companies were the premiers of Saxony and Bavaria—the highest officials of the states where VW and BMW are headquartered.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
37:14

BMW recruited shipyard and restaurant workers to hand-craft Rolls-Royce cars

BMW's new factory was sited in Goodwood, which had traditions in shipbuilding, furniture, and saddlery craftsmanship. It hired skilled woodworkers and leather craftspeople from the area; even the paint shop workers were recruited from a restaurant—because restaurant staff were accustomed to rigorous cleaning protocols. Meanwhile Volkswagen stabilized Crewe's original workforce by elevating veteran engineer Gott to oversee operations, upgrading facilities, and immediately promising no layoffs—preventing BMW from poaching talent wholesale.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
38:34

Both sides invested hard enough to win because they couldn't afford to lose

By 2004 Bentley sales reached 7,000 units; by 2006 they crossed 10,000—it had never sold more than 2,000 in a year before. BMW's Rolls-Royce sales also far exceeded the past, with the brand position secure. The episode argues this positive result came because both BMW and Volkswagen ‘couldn't afford to lose’—they were forced to invest enormous resources to ensure both brands succeeded, not because the acquisition was brilliantly structured. From the moment Piëch and Vickers' executives met in Churchill's lounge, a clean victory was nearly impossible.

— Xiao Wenjie/Chen Rui
