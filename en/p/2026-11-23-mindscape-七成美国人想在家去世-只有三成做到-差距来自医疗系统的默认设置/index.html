70% of Americans want to die at home, only 30% do: the gap comes from healthcare system defaults

Hospitals and the medical system default to continuous treatment rather than stopping at the right moment; hospice referrals come on average only 18 days before the end, meaning most people have no time to use it.

70% want to die at home, 30% actually do

Surveys Spencer cites show that when Americans are asked where they'd prefer to die, 70% choose dying at home, but only about 30% actually manage it. She attributes part of this gap to the healthcare system itself—most people are referred to hospice too late to have a chance to realize their wish of dying at home.

— KS
13:50

Median hospice stay is only 18 days

Medicare covers hospice for up to six months, but the actual median stay is only about 18 days—a figure that hasn't improved in years—with about one-quarter of patients staying a week or less. By the time most people get the referral, they're already bedridden and unable to eat or speak, with no time left to complete bucket-list items like ‘taking the grandkids fishing’.

— KS
14:11

Hospice is not a place, it's a type of care

Spencer clarifies a common misconception: hospice is not a facility you move into, but a service under the broader umbrella of palliative care, most often delivered by healthcare workers visiting you at home. The price of this Medicare benefit is renouncing all curative medical insurance coverage—what's known in medical circles as ‘the terrible choice’.

— KS
32:35

It's not that doctors are cold, the system only knows how to treat

Spencer pushes back against blaming doctors personally: they themselves say that delivering bad news is what they most hate doing, fearing patients will feel abandoned. But doctors are embedded in the same system—since Nixon's war on cancer, the entire American medical apparatus has been built around incentives, training, and insurance reimbursement for ‘cure’. A 30-minute ‘goals of care’ conversation generates far less reimbursement than surgery or an MRI.

— KS
40:08

Make end-of-life conversations as routine as colonoscopies

Spencer proposes a concrete policy idea: if end-of-life conversations were not treated as a special signal for a particular patient at a particular visit, but instead became a default system item in the electronic medical record—like how doctors routinely recommend colonoscopies after 50—then patients wouldn't misinterpret the conversation as ‘does my doctor think I'm dying?’

— KS
44:28

Even the expert on the system couldn't escape the medical treadmill

Spencer admits that despite years of researching healthcare systems and having a mother who was a nurse for 45 years, when her mother developed a brain tumor, her family still waited for a doctor to tell them ‘time for hospice’—and the hospital was still giving chemotherapy days before her mother died, with no one proactively suggesting a change in direction.

— KS
45:49

Asking for palliative care proactively changed everything

A decade later, facing her mother-in-law's heart condition, Spencer this time proactively asked the family ‘would she be open to a palliative care referral?’ A palliative care team member came to the hospital room and talked with her about what actually mattered: ‘I'm tired of being hospitalized, I just want to spend more time at home’. The mother-in-law eventually fired the team, but when her condition worsened she called them back herself and eventually transitioned to hospice care.

Glossary

hospice
Comfort-focused care service requiring renunciation of curative medical insurance coverage, usually provided in the patient's home.
palliative care
The broader umbrella of comfort care, can be delivered alongside curative treatment, with no specific prognosis requirement.
advance directive
A document signed in advance that specifies what medical interventions you want if you're unable to express your wishes.
medical aid in dying
The legal pathway where patients can seek medical means to end their life, not deeply explored in this episode.
Palliative Performance Scale
A 0-to-100 scale used to assess a patient's capacity for independent living and mobility.
