He Tongxue's Overseas Videos Hit 10 Million Views: Six Months of YouTube Income, Just $20K

Industry rumors claimed he made $1–10 million annually from YouTube. He Tongxue set the record straight: six months in, total income was just $20,000—about $2–3K per long-form video.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

27:16

TNT's direction was right; resources and timing were the problem

Luo Yonghao reflects on Hammer's TNT (phone connecting to monitor for desktop-level operation): he was correct that compute would become commodity, and using a phone for desktop productivity made vastly more sense than building phone and computer separately. Apple, he notes, is still just reinventing the wheel with iPad–Mac integration. He acknowledges the error wasn't direction—it was insufficient company resources and the lack of AI-assisted speech recognition at the time. He even suggests 2018's shutdown might have been mercy: had the company limped along until AI's 2023 breakthrough, the five and a half intervening years of barely-alive operation might have killed the team before they got there.

— Luo Yonghao
31:23

Three buyers wanted Tianti, not Hammer's hardware

When Hammer was failing, four potential acquirers circled; three of them—ByteDance and Lenovo among them—were after the TNT and Tianti (天梯) human-computer interaction system. Lenovo even disguised the acquisition as a way to poach core engineers, since their own team matched Samsung's mediocrity. Final price: 180 million yuan to ByteDance. Luo argues the company had zero cash value without Tianti. This distinction crystallizes his view: established tech brands like Huawei, OPPO, and Xiaomi are fundamentally followers; they thrive through process and management. Innovation-driven firms depend on individual heroism, and the odds of success are structurally lower.

— Luo Yonghao
44:29

Topping out in an ordinary class hurts less than bottoming in the best

He Tongxue recalls entering the top track at the best middle school—and becoming depressed. With grades as the sole measure, he collapsed from elementary-school #1 to 53rd place; his nearsightedness spiked to 500 degrees, but shame kept him silent with his parents. A regular high school track freed him. Luo Yonghao adds a parallel: talented students from ordinary U.S. states who barely scrape into Harvard or Yale at the bottom of their class often suffer more disappointment than elite resources justify.

— He Tongxue / Luo Yonghao
1:03:39

After fame, every video faces triple scrutiny before it even posts

He Tongxue says the hardest part of fame isn't mockery—it's the exploding cost of armor. He describes a coworker with a broken leg who volunteered to film a wheelchair-drifting video. Before posting: review and rewording. After posting: simultaneously facing three kinds of attack—claims the injury was fake (the crutch supposedly supported a healthy leg, not an injured one), backlash that they're working injured, and the platform flagging it as dangerous. Luo offered concrete countermeasures on set: post the hospital receipt, add a line about taking leave, and the whole mess evaporates. This playbook only comes from getting roasted enough times to extract the pattern.

— He Tongxue
1:17:48

Never taking funding didn't protect the studio from a major loss this year

The studio has never raised venture capital—He Tongxue's logic: "Good content has nothing to do with money; the only thing that matters is finding quiet to create." Yet this year the studio is in the red; last year it was profitable. The cause: one controversial tweet cost them several ad deals. The flagship origami-pigeon pipeline—from pitch to publish took eight-plus months—meant shipping 88,000 paper pigeons to viewers with logistics alone running approximately 800,000 yuan. Total project cost was over 3 million yuan, with behind-the-scenes footage failing to recoup its share.

— He Tongxue
1:44:02

No public apology—he feared it would just reignite the controversy

The year's biggest crisis: He Tongxue posted about refusing false praise, choosing to tell a driver the truth to his face—and triggered massive backlash. He apologized to the driver by phone, to his team, to clients. But he issued no public apology, fearing the apology letter itself would be analyzed from new angles and spark a second wave. In hindsight, he sees the failure: lack of empathy. To the driver, that "honest truth-telling" cut far deeper than politeness would have.

— He Tongxue
2:33:22

Ideas are everywhere; finding topics that hit both criteria is the real constraint

He Tongxue distilled topic selection into two criteria—rooted in real-life need, and able to deliver visual spectacle. Only ideas meeting both become true hits; finding one a month feels like grace. He admits the bottleneck isn't talent or equipment. It's the screenplay-quality premise. His team spends most hours keeping the lights on—shooting content that holds the upload schedule but has zero viral potential—pushing back his dream of DIY-project monthlies indefinitely.

— He Tongxue
3:17:42

The short-video creator built anti-addiction tools to fight what he designed

He Tongxue revealed the studio is rebooting an app designed to cut users' phone-scrolling time. An earlier version stalled for lack of maintenance, but system API access has improved. He tested it on himself: video and feed time dropped two-thirds. This sparked Luo's riff on short video: "We deploy enormous human brainpower engineering algorithms and hooks to make you addicted." His countermeasure: repeatedly install and uninstall addictive apps, keeping most of them off his device most of the time as a defense against state-of-the-art attention hijacking.

— He Tongxue / Luo Yonghao
