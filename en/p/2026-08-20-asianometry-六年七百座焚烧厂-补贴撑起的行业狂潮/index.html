The Incinerator Boom Was Not an Environmental Win. It Was a Business Calculated From an Electricity Subsidy

A 2012 electricity price subsidy turned waste-to-energy from a money-losing business into a profitable industry. China built nearly 700 incinerators in six years, and now faces overcapacity, with average utilisation below 60%.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:07

700 plants in six years, until there wasn't enough trash to burn

In 2019 China had roughly 400 municipal solid waste incineration power plants, handling about 50% of waste by volume. By 2025 the number of incinerators exceeded 1,137, the share handled by incineration passed 80%, and national daily incineration capacity reached 1.2 million tonnes — while actual daily waste collection capacity was only 700,000 tonnes. There is already surplus capacity, some plants have shut down, and some localities have gone back to digging up old landfills. This is a buildout of incinerators unprecedented in human history: roughly 700 added in six years.

6:19

China's waste output passed the US back in 2004

In 1981 China collected only about 26 million tonnes of municipal solid waste; at the time US generation was six times China's, on a quarter of the population. After reform and opening, urbanisation accelerated and waste output grew 8-10% a year. In 2004 China's total municipal solid waste generation surpassed the United States to become the world's largest, and by 2014 it reached 178 million tonnes — nearly seven times the 1981 figure. The World Bank projected in 2018 that by 2030 China's waste generation would be twice America's.

9:24

Incineration has always cost 5 to 10 times more than landfill

Building an incineration power plant costs $66,000 to $120,000 per tonne of daily capacity, and can run as high as $365,000. A landfill, by contrast, costs only $5,000 to $20,000 per tonne of daily capacity over its full life cycle — a 5- to 10-fold gap — and illegal landfills are cheaper still. Which means that from a purely economic standpoint, the reason incineration spread at scale after the 2000s is not that it saves money versus landfill.

12:28

Subsidies are over half of revenue: burning trash is really selling electricity

The new electricity pricing policy that took effect in April 2012 converts each tonne of waste into 280 kWh, for which the government pays a subsidy of RMB 0.65 per kWh; only the excess is settled at the local coal benchmark price (about RMB 0.25-0.45 per kWh). That means an incinerator can lock in roughly RMB 182 per tonne in revenue from the subsidised tariff alone. Add a municipal waste handling fee of RMB 60-160 per tonne, and each tonne of waste brings in RMB 250-350 in total revenue, against a post-depreciation cost of about RMB 200 per tonne. Subsidies account for more than half of total revenue; without them the business basically doesn't exist.

20:48

The siting was fine. The opaque process is what set off the conflict

In April 2014 the Hangzhou municipal government announced a new incinerator in Yuhang with daily capacity of 3,000 tonnes, more than a third of the city's waste output. The siting was technically sound — tucked into a former quarry, so residents' roads wouldn't be used — but the decision process was completely opaque, and 20,000 residents signed a petition against it. In May the situation spun out of control: tens of thousands clashed with police, and the official report said 39 were injured and no one died. Academics later named this failure mode — decide first, announce, then defend — Decide-Announce-Defend.

22:56

Hangzhou didn't back down; it swapped operators and redid the process

Hangzhou didn't abandon the project. It replaced the inexperienced, low-trust state-owned operator with the more seasoned Everbright Environmental Protection, and restarted a full environmental impact assessment with public participation. The government mobilised staff to visit more than 25,000 residents, collected 500 comments and suggestions, earmarked the disposal fee specifically for environmental spending, hired local residents as monitors, and organised 5,000 residents to visit Everbright's facilities elsewhere and inspect the automatic monitoring equipment. This process later became the standard playbook for handling NIMBY movements.

24:01

The boom was squeezed out by an administrative deadline, not the market

The central government set a target of 591,000 tonnes of national daily incineration capacity by 2020 and worked backwards from it: provinces were required to complete siting by 2018, because building an incinerator takes 15 to 24 months. From 2013 to 2017 China added only 41 incinerators a year, reaching 360 nationwide by the end of 2017. But by mid-2019 official media reported more than 400 projects under construction nationwide. The real boom was squeezed out directly by that administrative deadline.

26:05

China overbuilt incinerators: average utilisation is only about 60%

By the end of 2024 the national total reached 1,137 incinerators, but 60% of capacity is concentrated in five provinces — Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Guangdong, Shandong and Fujian — a clear regional imbalance. E20 Research Institute calculates that average capacity utilisation for domestic incineration projects from 2023 to 2025 was about 60%. Some projects run above 80%, but others are as low as 24%, and about 16% of projects run below 50%. Fixed costs are high, so low utilisation means losing money — one reason quite a few localities have started digging up old landfills. After 2021 new projects no longer enjoy the RMB 0.65 per kWh subsidy, forcing companies toward new revenue sources such as service fees, overseas plants and metal smelting.

Glossary

Moving Grate
The most common incinerator design, in which waste dries and burns as it travels slowly along a moving grate
Circulating Fluidized Bed
An alternative process that burns waste in a high-temperature fluidised sand bed, usually with higher efficiency
BOT
Build-Operate-Transfer: a company funds and operates the plant for a 20-30 year concession, then hands it to the government
Decide-Announce-Defend
The NIMBY decision-making failure mode identified by academics: decide first, announce, then defend it by force
