The Pizza Tracker: We Were Trained to Pay for Fake Progress Bars

Domino's pizza tracker started as an internal management tool and became a global app standard. It works because it turns waiting into visible work; and once companies discovered we love watching progress bars, they started faking them.

The 30-minute guarantee sold control, not speed

Domino's once promised delivery in 30 minutes or it was free. The guarantee made drivers speed, caused deaths and lawsuits, and was eventually dropped. But David Halbenstricker has a theory: the guarantee was popular not just because it was fast, but because it gave customers a sense of control. In the early 2000s Domino's commissioned a study and found that the customer's psychology after ordering a pizza is a roller coaster: the peak is at the moment of ordering, then it slides all the way down — "Where's my pizza? Did they lose my order?" However long the actual wait, what customers hate is the waiting itself.

The internal tool had to work before the customer side

Executives did not initially want to build a pizza tracker, because tracking every pizza at every store was too time-consuming and looked like a gimmick. What they were really obsessed with was "out-the-door time." A UK franchisee put the out-the-door times of five to ten nearby stores on one display so they would compete with each other, and paid cash bonuses to the top-ranked stores. After David's boss visited the UK, he had David replicate the system in the US. David connected every store's out-the-door time to a central system and turned it into a real-time dashboard. The CEO said it was the thing that drove performance most in his tenure.

— Alex Mayasi
11:25

The tracker was a byproduct of the internal system

David and a colleague realised they already had a system tracking every pizza, so they decided to "hitch a ride" and build a customer-facing tracker. In 2006 most people didn't have smartphones; FedEx, UPS and Amazon could track packages, but on a scale of days, not minutes. Real-time tracking of pizza progress was entirely new. The designer's interface looked like a horizontal thermometer: order, prep, bake, box, delivery — each step completed, the next segment turned red. When David saw the mockup he said: "That's a hot dog. We're a pizza company, why is there a hot dog?" But executives liked it, and decided to launch before the Super Bowl.

— Alex Mayasi
15:36

Designers use the pizza tracker as a meeting prop

Shuya Gong was a design director at IDEO and later taught design at Harvard. Consulting for many companies in the mid-2010s, she found one of the most effective ways to open a meeting was to order a pizza on the spot, project the tracker onto a big screen, and ask the client: "How do you feel right now?" She says you can feel the anxiety rise or fall in your body very directly, feel the reassurance and groundedness. Then you can ask: which of those parts do you want your users to feel when they use your product? The pizza tracker became shorthand in her design circle: whenever a client involved waiting, they'd say "they need a pizza tracker."

— Alex Mayasi
17:39

The tracker manufactures a "labor illusion"

Shuya says one reason the pizza tracker is popular is that as shopping experiences become more digital, the tracker replicates something important from the physical world — watching other people work. Online experiences lack that visibility, so people get impatient more easily. Researchers found that reminding customers there is labor behind a product makes them value it more, and they call this the "labor illusion." Shuya also describes a stranger mechanism: the tracker changes our perception of time. Like walking somewhere, the return trip feels shorter because it's familiar and has landmarks. The pizza tracker gives us a familiar route and landmarks — pizza being made, on the road, out for delivery — making the wait feel shorter.

— Alex Mayasi
19:47

Banks deliberately slow transfers to fake a wait

Working with banks and fintech companies in the mid-2010s, Shuya found their problem was the opposite of Domino's: customers weren't used to transfers being so fast. Two computers talk, confirm the amount, and the money is there — no road to cross, no cheese to melt. But for people used to money taking a while to arrive, instant arrival "feels suspicious." So companies deliberately slow down the "money tracker," giving you no real-time feedback but adding a buffer, making you wait. Shuya says this is intentionally slowing down time, adding meaningful friction so that in the wait you feel the seriousness and weight of the transfer. The host pressed: isn't that advising lying to customers? Shuya said she prefers to understand it as "curating the experience," adding back the physicality that existed in the traditional experience, to make people comfortable.

— Alex Mayasi
21:48

TurboTax and Uber progress bars may be fake

TurboTax pops up an animation during tax filing saying "we're checking your deductions and credits." But a few years ago someone looked at the code and found the animation is identical for everyone, in every situation, and takes the same amount of time. The question is: does TurboTax want to reassure us during the terrifying tax process, or use the labor illusion to make us feel that paying a big bill is worth it? Uber is similar: the host repeatedly received updated pickup times, but the car almost always arrived later than promised. It felt like the tracker was part of a deliberate deception — to make you choose Uber over Lyft, to keep you from cancelling, to get you standing on the street early. The show contacted TurboTax and Uber; TurboTax did not respond, Uber did not arrange an interview but shared some data suggesting their pickup times are fairly accurate.

— Alex Mayasi
25:52

Field test: the pizza tracker is mostly real

Nick biked to a local Domino's to stake it out, while Alex ordered a half-Hawaiian, half-pepperoni pizza at home and monitored the tracker in real time. Nick ordered a pepperoni pizza in the store as cover. The tracker showed "Ivan" making the pizza, but when Nick asked the staff their name, they said "Alex." The tracker also showed a "quality check," but Nick saw no quality inspector. David had explained, though, that because Domino's uses conveyor ovens, the bake time is nearly fixed, so the tracker automatically jumps to the next step after a set time. In the end the pizza came out, was boxed, and was driven off by Alex (aka Ivan), with Nick chasing on his bike and nearly being taken for a stalker. Alex hit "delivered" at Nick's door and the tracker updated quickly. Conclusion: the tracker is mostly accurate, but "Ivan made it" and the "quality check" were fake.
