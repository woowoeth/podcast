The Pizza Tracker: We've Been Trained to Pay for Fake Progress Bars

Domino's pizza tracker started as an internal management tool, then became a global app standard. It works because it turns waiting into visible work; and once companies discovered we love watching progress bars, they started faking them.

The 30-minute guarantee sold control, not speed

Domino's once promised delivery in 30 minutes or it's free. That guarantee made drivers speed, leading to deaths and lawsuits, and it was eventually dropped. But David Halbenstricker has a theory: the guarantee was popular not just because it was fast, but because it gave customers a sense of control. In the early 2000s, Domino's commissioned a study and found that customers' psychology after ordering pizza is a rollercoaster: the peak is at order time, then it slides downhill—'Where's my pizza? Did they lose my order?' No matter the actual wait, what customers hate is the waiting process.

8:16

Internal tool had to work first, before customer-facing

Executives initially didn't want to build a pizza tracker because tracking every pizza at every store was too time-consuming and seemed like a gimmick. What they were truly obsessed with was 'out-the-door time.' A UK franchisee used a monitor to display out-the-door times from five to ten nearby stores, letting them compete, and gave cash rewards to top-ranked stores. David's boss visited the UK and had David replicate the system in the US. David connected each store's out-the-door time to a central system, creating a real-time dashboard. The CEO said it was the thing that most drove performance during his tenure.

— Alex Mayasi
11:25

The tracker was a byproduct of the internal system

David and a colleague realized they already had a system tracking every pizza, so they decided to 'hitch a ride' and build a customer-facing tracker. In 2006, most people didn't have smartphones; FedEx, UPS, and Amazon could track packages, but on a timescale of days, not minutes. Real-time pizza progress tracking was entirely new. The designer's interface looked like a horizontal thermometer, from order, prep, bake, box, to delivery; each completed step turned the next segment red. When David saw the design, he said: 'This is a hot dog. We're a pizza company, why is there a hot dog?' But executives liked it and decided to launch before the Super Bowl.

— Alex Mayasi
15:36

Designers used the pizza tracker as a meeting prop

Shuya Gong was a design director at IDEO and later taught design at Harvard. In the mid-2010s, consulting for many companies, she found one of the most effective meeting openers was to order a pizza on the spot, project the tracker on a big screen, and ask the client: 'How do you feel right now?' She said you can feel anxiety rising or falling in your body, feel reassurance and groundedness. Then you can ask: which parts do you want your users to feel when using your product? The pizza tracker became a shorthand in her design circle: whenever a client involved waiting, they'd say 'they need a pizza tracker.'

— Alex Mayasi
17:39

The tracker creates a 'labor illusion'

Shuya said one reason the pizza tracker is popular is that as shopping experiences become more digital, the tracker replicates an important part of the physical world—watching others work. Online experiences lack that visibility, so people get impatient more easily. Researchers found that reminding customers of the labor behind a product makes them value it more, calling it the 'labor illusion.' Shuya also mentioned a more mysterious mechanism: the tracker changes our perception of time. Like walking somewhere, the return feels shorter because it's familiar and has landmarks. The pizza tracker gives us familiar routes and landmarks—pizza being made, on the road, being delivered—making the wait feel shorter.

— Alex Mayasi
19:47

Banks deliberately slow transfers to fake waiting

In the mid-2010s, Shuya worked with banks and fintech companies and found their problem was the opposite of Domino's: customers weren't used to transfers being too fast. Two computers talk, confirm the amount, and the money moves—no road to cross, no cheese to melt. But for people used to money taking a while to arrive, instant arrival 'feels suspicious.' So companies deliberately slow down the 'money tracker,' not giving real-time feedback but adding a buffer to make you wait. Shuya said this is intentionally slowing down time, adding meaningful friction, so you feel the seriousness and weight of the transfer while waiting. The host pressed: isn't this advising lying to customers? Shuya said she prefers to see it as 'curating the experience,' adding back the physicality of traditional experiences to make people comfortable.

— Alex Mayasi
21:48

TurboTax and Uber progress bars may be fake

TurboTax shows an animation during tax filing saying 'We're checking your deductions and credits.' But a few years ago someone checked the code and found the animation is the same for everyone, all situations, and takes the same time. The question is: is TurboTax trying to reassure us during the terrifying tax process, or using the labor illusion to make us feel a large payment is worth it? Uber is similar: the host repeatedly received updated pickup times, but the car almost always arrived later than promised. It felt like the tracker was part of a deliberate deception to make you choose Uber over Lyft, to keep you from canceling, to make you stand on the street early. The show contacted TurboTax and Uber; TurboTax didn't respond, Uber didn't arrange an interview but shared some data suggesting their pickup times are fairly accurate.

— Alex Mayasi
25:52

Field test: the pizza tracker is mostly real

Nick biked to a local Domino's to stake out, while Alex ordered a half-Hawaiian half-pepperoni pizza at home and monitored the tracker in real time. Nick ordered another pepperoni pizza in the store as cover. The tracker showed 'Ivan' making the pizza, but when Nick asked the staff their name, they said 'Alex.' The tracker also showed 'quality check,' but Nick saw no quality inspector. However, David explained that because Domino's uses conveyor ovens, bake time is nearly fixed, so the tracker automatically jumps to the next step after a set time. Eventually, the pizza came out, was boxed, and driven off by Alex (aka Ivan), with Nick biking after, nearly mistaken for a stalker. Alex pressed 'delivered' at Nick's door, and the tracker updated quickly. Conclusion: the tracker is mostly accurate, but 'Ivan making' and 'quality check' are fake.
