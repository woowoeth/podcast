A community's sound change always outruns the individual, and innovation mostly happens in adolescence

Your accent barely changes over a lifetime, yet an entire city's speech keeps moving: new changes are mostly added once in adolescence, then each person freezes with them, and the next generation adds a little more.

17:46

Perfectly identical accents would actually be harder to understand

Daniel asks: if the point of language is to be understood, the easiest route is for everyone to speak the same way, so why isn't that so? Lauren says it's anatomically impossible — every person's vocal tract is different, so variation is built into speech. And once variation exists, it gets recruited for other work: little boys and little girls have the same vocal tract anatomy, yet deliberately amplify gender differences, because that matters to their social world. She offers a counterintuitive corollary: if someone whose body differs a lot produced exactly the same sound, that would actually impede understanding, because we are used to normalizing what we hear against the body we see. The other side of the same mechanism is what they call the "Social McGurk Effect": show a listener only a photograph and tell them this is the speaker, and they will "hear" an accent that isn't there.

35:48

The more intuitive the story, the more you should doubt it

Daniel floats a hypothesis: Australian Aboriginal languages generally lack fricatives, which might be tied to a high rate of hearing loss in the population — in 2018-2019, more than two-fifths of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people aged seven and over were assessed as having some degree of hearing loss. Jennifer doesn't accept the explanation: she says she wasn't aware of that characteristic in Aboriginal populations, questions the premise, and presses that such an account could explain losing certain subtle contrasts but not why an entire series of fricatives would disappear. She offers a methodology: the more intuitive and plausible a story is, the more she wants to doubt it, especially when the generalization is about a group underrepresented in academia. Daniel himself concedes up front that it sounds like a Just-So Story.

— Jennifer Nycz
36:50

Individuals freeze, the community keeps moving

The book poses a paradox: sound change in a community is faster than in any individual in it. Lauren says this is almost a logical necessity — if an individual changed more than the community, he would no longer belong to it, and the community already contains the individuals' changes. The actual mechanism is called the adolescent peak: innovations are usually introduced into the grammar by adolescents, who then keep using them for the rest of their lives but never use them more. Take /u/-fronting: one generation pushes it to a certain point and stops, the next generation pushes a little further from there, and the community thus travels a road no individual could walk in a lifetime. Hedvig follows up by asking "why do people always think the music they loved at 13 to 17 was the best," and Lauren says that is exactly the period when you are shaping "who I am," and speech is just one of the many markers that set along with you.

— Lauren Hall-Lew
41:42

Media changes listening, not pronunciation

The baseline judgment in sociolinguistics is this: put a child in front of Peppa Pig or Bluey and he won't permanently change his accent — what people want to learn is the person next door. Jennifer says media is more likely to have an indirect effect: if a show presents an accent, or people from a certain community, positively and as cool, the social payoff of adopting that feature in real life goes up, and change may be accelerated. She notes that past research mostly looked at asymmetric broadcast systems like radio and television, whereas podcasts and social media are more invested and interactive — listeners feel the host is like a friend in their ear, and it might influence vowels and consonants at a finer grain. Lauren adds: media's influence is probably more in perception than production; what it changes is your ability to understand various accents.

— Jennifer Nycz
44:10

You are unaware of 95% of what you say

Daniel asks how much of our own accent we are conscious of — fifty-fifty? Lauren's answer is "95% not aware," and for linguists about 85%. She stresses this doesn't mean we only receive 5% of the information: we are receiving 100% all the time, but only 5% of it enters awareness. Her example: the moment you open your mouth you have to pick a language, which immediately tells others something about you, and you weren't thinking about it at all. Jennifer gives the reason from the other end: in one second we produce roughly ten to twelve phonemes, on top of stress, neighboring segments and morphological categories, so it's impossible to "choose" how to pronounce each one. So accusations like "someone is deliberately using an accent to be annoying or insincere" don't survive the arithmetic.

— Lauren Hall-Lew
50:59

A rare male-led sound change

Lauren says what she is focused on now is East Asia and West Africa. East Asia has a large body of research on various feminine voice qualities: the "cute" voice popular in Korea, Japan, Taiwan and parts of China, and what happens when male bodies start using it. She gives Seoul Korean as an example — vowels after nasals used to be nasalized and are now denasalizing, an ongoing sound change. What's special is that it is rarely male-led: nasalization and hyperarticulate speech are already bound up with a certain hyperfeminine "cute" style, so men are leading the change in the opposite direction. She then responds to a listener's claim that "American women are more nasal," arguing that, like creaky voice, it is simply more noticeable because it sits inside a higher fundamental frequency, and probably doesn't hold up when you actually measure it.

— Lauren Hall-Lew
56:23

Teach people the game, and also point out the game

Hedvig asks: since the ideal that all accents should be treated equally won't be realized any time soon, should schools teach the prestigious variant so people suffer less discrimination? Jennifer uses an analogy: it's like "capitalism is terrible, but I also have a 401k" — an individual has to survive inside the system, and that doesn't mean giving up your judgment about the system. She separates two things: knowing that high rising terminals and creaky voice are linguistically no worse than anything else is one thing; telling a woman who feels she has to change her voice "don't change, you're fine" is another, because in her own situation she probably isn't fine. Lauren aims at the stigma around style shifting: every few months a politician gets slammed in the media for being insincere about switching registers, yet all of us switch all the time.

— Jennifer Nycz
1:03:03

Deaf schools create dialects in sign language

Sign languages have no sound, but they have phonology and social variation just the same. Lauren says the most distinctive thing about sign-language sociophonetics is this: most deaf people are born to hearing families, so there is no intergenerational transmission the way there is in spoken language, which makes deaf schools play a far bigger role than spoken-language schools in "what counts as input," and people will say certain signs belong to this school or that school. Hedvig mentions a Catholic deaf school that separated students by gender and ended up producing gender dialects — Lauren corrects the location to Ireland; in the US, racial segregation produced Black ASL as distinct from white American Sign Language. At the articulatory level, sign languages have two hands, non-manual features like the face and body, and a range of arm movement far larger than the mouth, so signing height, the size of the signing space and the speed of movement can all become individual variation.

— Lauren Hall-Lew

Glossary

sociophonetics
The subfield studying how phonetic variation is bound to a speaker's social identity, context and listener expectations.
actuation problem
Why a given phonetic variant gets assigned social meaning at a particular moment, thereby setting off a sound change.
adolescent peak
Sound-change innovations mostly enter an individual's grammar in adolescence, and are only used, never deepened, afterward.
chronolect
A coinage Hedvig wants to promote, meaning the shared way of speaking of the people of a given era.
style shifting
The same person adjusting their speech between classroom, family and friends; everyone does it, yet it is often taken as insincerity.
denasalization
Vowels after nasals losing their nasalization — an ongoing, male-led sound change in Seoul Korean.
