What the Fed fears most isn't tariffs—it's unanchored inflation expectations

Chicago Fed President Goolsbee points to inflation as the biggest risk: after five and a half years above target, the AI narrative could push rates higher, not lower in the short term. His key advice for rate-sensitive industries: watch the data, not the stock market.

4:15

19 chairs aren't for consensus—they're for argument

He explains why the FOMC keeps 12 regional Fed presidents plus 7 Board governors, 19 chairs in total: not for consensus, but to let different worldviews collide under high uncertainty. Goolsbee says colleagues can shout at him and change his interpretation of the data. This structure insulates rate decisions from the political cycle as much as possible, because staggered 14-year terms mean no one can replace the whole table at once. The direct lesson for founders: surround yourself with people who will push back on major judgments, not an echo chamber.

— Austan Goolsbee
9:25

The Fed's real fear isn't price hikes—it's unanchored expectations

The real danger of tariff and oil shocks isn't a one-time price increase, but convincing people that high inflation will persist. Once that expectation forms, workers demand 6% wages, firms price at 7%, and the Fed must engineer a deep recession to break the expectation. Goolsbee calls this 'unanchoring of inflation expectations' and says it's the self-fulfilling mechanism he watches most. For founders, the logic means: whether a cost increase is one-off matters more than how much costs rise.

— Austan Goolsbee
10:31

Five and a half years above target—the Fed hasn't done well

Goolsbee admits the Fed's recent performance hasn't been pretty: nearly six years, at least five and a half above the 2% target, with a reversal in the last year. He cites on-the-ground impressions from the Seventh District (the Midwest): businesses say input costs are up sharply, farmers say prices aren't rising but costs are crushing, and everyone talks about affordability. His conclusion: with the public highly sensitive to prices, any new supply shock—tariffs, Middle East oil, chip shortages, AI data centers—is more easily read as long-term inflation.

— Austan Goolsbee
14:44

The new chair is pulling back the Fed's forward guidance

New Chair Kevin Walsh is changing how the Fed communicates: he dislikes 'forward guidance'—explicit promises like 'if X happens, we'll hike or cut.' Goolsbee observes that FOMC statements have become noticeably shorter, and Walsh has set up five working groups covering inflation, AI and productivity, the balance sheet, and more, staffed with well-known people he trusts. Reports aren't out yet, but they could become Walsh's signature reform. Markets should adapt: the Fed will promise less, and future expectation volatility may be higher.

— Austan Goolsbee
20:34

Replacing people to influence rates crosses the independence line

Goolsbee divides today's pressure into two types: personal attacks on Powell, Lisa Cook, and others, including criminal investigations and attempts to remove them; and outright demands to 'cut rates.' He says Fed independence has traditionally been narrowly defined—setting rates without political interference. Once a sitting government tries to influence rates by replacing people, 'any economist would see that as a problem.' He doesn't go all the way, but it's his clearest statement to the White House.

— Austan Goolsbee
25:00

AI is a reason to hike in the short term, not cut

He first cools the AI narrative with district facts: his district has the highest manufacturing share of any Fed district, but firms are still looking for use cases and complaining that tokens are too expensive; productivity growth, after a two-and-a-half-year pickup, has shown about six months of weakness. Then he offers a counterintuitive judgment: if everyone simultaneously pours money into data centers, betting with IPOs or expected future earnings, the short-term economy could overheat. The central bank's daily job is to prevent overheating, not to judge whether AI will revolutionize things in 20 years, so AI's short-term policy implication may be to push rates up, not down.

— Austan Goolsbee
28:26

AI taking all jobs is the lump-of-labor fallacy

He categorizes the 'AI will make everyone unemployed' narrative as the lump-of-labor fallacy: treating the total amount of labor as a fixed stone that, once AI surpasses, leaves no new jobs for people. He says this claim has been proven wrong every time in the past; he admits 'maybe this time is different,' but he's willing to bet against it: in 20 years, there won't be 95% unemployment, nor will only six people own all AI companies. He positions himself as a 'grim optimist'—not avoiding short-term pain, but optimistic about long-term income growth.

— Austan Goolsbee
35:00

The terminal rate is roughly 3%, if inflation turns

Goolsbee offers a rare quantitative anchor: his personal loose view is that if inflation returns to a 2% path, rates will converge to the neutral rate (what he calls star), roughly 3% nominal = 2% inflation + 1% real. But the precondition is that inflation must turn down; the past five and a half years above target, with the last year going the wrong way. For rate-sensitive industries, his advice is to watch data and inflation, not the stock market—the people around the FOMC table discuss the real economy; the stock market is a secondary reference.

— Austan Goolsbee

Glossary

Forward guidance
A central bank's explicit commitment about the future path of rates; new Chair Walsh dislikes it and favors fewer promises.
Core inflation
Inflation excluding food and energy; the Fed sees it as better reflecting underlying true inflation.
Unanchoring of inflation expectations
When inflation expectations lose their anchor, wages and prices chase each other in a self-fulfilling spiral.
Stagflation
A situation where inflation and employment both deteriorate, putting the two goals in conflict.
Lump of labor fallacy
The mistaken assumption that the total number of jobs is fixed, so technological substitution won't create new jobs.
FOMC
The Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed's rate-setting body, with 19 people around the table voting.
