9:07

Seeing millionaires around you is better than any business school

Zhu Jiangming grew up in Yiwu in the 1980s when farmers in the small-goods market were getting rich overnight. The wealth erupting around him wasn't demoralizing—it was proof of concept. He realized later that entrepreneurship isn't a decision people make in the abstract; it's something people do because the environment proves it works. In places where nobody starts companies, 1-in-10,000 will try. In boom towns, 1-in-10 will jump. He attributes his entire founder career to proximity and observation, not genius.

— Zhu Jiangming
32:25

The invisible market gap became a billion-dollar product

In the early 1990s, Zhu was working in remote surveillance. At a trade show in Beijing, he noticed the market had no real solution—vendors were just stitching together modules. Banks used cassette recorders for surveillance, producing eight hours of grainy footage. He saw the gap: someone needed to design a purpose-built digital recorder with its own compression chip and embedded CPU. He built it. Dahua's first DVR didn't open a market segment; it pulled the entire surveillance industry from millions per year in custom work into tens of billions in device sales.

— Zhu Jiangming
1:01:41

Ignorance was essential—knowing the true cost would have stopped the venture

Before founding Lepton, Zhu's team had almost no contact with automotive industry veterans. They hired pure engineers and designed the car from first principles. He admits now: if he'd understood upfront that serious car manufacturing requires 200 billion RMB or more, Lepton never would have launched. He calls it ‘the luck of ignorance’—both curse and blessing. If entrepreneurs actually calculated the failure rate (under 1%) and capital required, almost nobody would start. Lepton succeeded partly because Zhu didn't know enough to be afraid.

— Zhu Jiangming
1:26:01

A coupe designed in a villa weekend nearly bankrupted the company

Lepton's first car, the S01, was a two-door sports coupe. The decision came from a young investor who loved the styling. A few tech founders—none of whom knew consumer car markets—met at a villa in Qiandao Lake and decided on impulse to build it. Zhu reflects that a startup with zero brand recognition has no room for cars that live on styling alone. An EV without marketing overhead needs no explanation to sell. The S01 at 110,000 RMB was positioned wrong; if Lepton had discounted to 70,000 RMB like rivals did, the company wouldn't have survived. The lesson stuck: every car after S01 was designed with ruthless discipline.

— Zhu Jiangming
1:36:14

Nearly killing the bestseller-to-be saved the company

In spring 2020, Lepton was stretched for cash and nearly shelved the C11 project (then called the 411). Zhu decided to bet everything: design it to GLC-class Mercedes specs, target 200,000 RMB, and use pre-orders with tiered discounts to create scarcity. C11 launch exceeded expectations by orders of magnitude and became Lepton's true inflection point. S01 had been a learning disaster; T03 was just in-between. C11 proved that Lepton's design instinct was real.

— Zhu Jiangming
2:01:40

Vehicles break even; all profit hides in self-made parts

Vertical integration lets Lepton own the 10% margin that suppliers would normally capture. Instead of pricing high to keep that spread, Lepton prices aggressively and passes savings to buyers. Result: vehicles operate at near-zero profit, and the company survives on margins baked into its own semiconductors and components. Zhu compares Lepton to Uniqlo: thirty-yuan shirts that don't feel cheap even to people like him. The brand doesn't earn from pricing power; it earns from being trustworthy enough that quality-obsessed customers don't hesitate. All the money is in the parts.

— Zhu Jiangming
2:27:15

Western OEMs now reverse-engineer the partnership, not the other way around

In 2023, Stellantis invested 1.5 billion EUR and formed Lepton International, a 49%-51% joint venture to sell Lepton tech into markets outside China. This inverts decades of automotive playbook: Western companies exported technology to China in exchange for market access. Here, Lepton exports technology; Stellantis brings overseas channels, brand credit, and financial infrastructure. Zhu sees this as inevitable: after 40 years of industrial accumulation, China owns the final frontier—new-energy vehicles. It happened in appliances and phones; now it's happening in cars. The tech is Chinese-led; partnerships just become distribution.

— Zhu Jiangming
3:02:59

Refusing to retaliate against attacks makes personal attacks pointless

Lepton publicly committed to never deploying fake accounts, badmouthing competitors, or spreading dirt. Zhu's logic: mudslinging is like two kids fighting—once you hit back, the conflict spirals. But if you get hit and don't retaliate, the other side has no sport and quits. He also believes illegal smear campaigns should trigger legal action, not silence—a hybrid stance: don't engage in mudslinging, but don't absorb legal violations either. So far, the commitment has held, freeing Lepton from spending energy on reputation defense.

— Zhu Jiangming
