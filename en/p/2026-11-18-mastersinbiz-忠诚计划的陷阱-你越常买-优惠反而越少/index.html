The Trap of Loyalty Programs: The More You Buy, the Fewer the Discounts

Once algorithms identify you as a stable customer, companies stop offering discounts—Starbucks and McDonald's data reports prove it happens systematically.

Fixed pricing's 150-year era ends as algorithms take over product pricing

American fixed pricing was born in the late nineteenth century with Quaker merchants who believed haggling was unjust. John Wanamaker first put price tags on goods at his Philadelphia department store. This system lasted nearly 150 years, but now electronic shelf labels and algorithmic pricing are disrupting it, making prices less stable and less transparent. The same item can be charged at different prices to different people—a new mechanism for corporate profit.

3:10

Consumers can reverse corporate experiments by simultaneously checking prices

Companies experiment on consumers to discover how much more they can charge. The author suggests consumers flip the experiment: when shopping for Christmas flights, you and a spouse can open browsers side-by-side and compare prices, then check out at whichever is lower. When riding Uber home with friends, everyone opens the app at once and books from whoever gets the cheapest quote. This is something ordinary people can do immediately while waiting for legislative protections.

— Lindsay Owens
5:13

Loyalty programs backfire: discounts vanish once you're confirmed as loyal

Companies offer loyalty program discounts initially to harvest your data, but once they confirm you're a ‘stable customer’, they stop sending promotions. A Washington Post journalist obtained their own Starbucks data report—55 pages long—and found that as Starbucks confirmed their loyalty, discounts and complimentary items progressively disappeared. A Wired magazine journalist obtained McDonald's app data spanning 500 pages, showing the system calculated their ‘lifetime churn score’ as zero, meaning they were flagged as permanently locked in.

— Lindsay Owens
6:15

Data sales now rival retail operations in profitability for grocers

Loyalty programs cut both ways: companies use your data to decide whether to offer you discounts, then repackage and sell that data. Kroger locks 95% of transactions to its loyalty card, and its ‘alternative profit business’ accounts for 30% of net operating income, with a large share from data sales.

— Lindsay Owens
8:19

Two industries have already outlawed the drip-pricing trick

Drip pricing attracts you with a low opening price, then layers on additional charges at checkout, raising the final bill far above the initial offer. Airline baggage fees and seat selection fees are textbook examples. Late in the Biden administration, Federal Trade Commission rules banned this across two sectors: short-term rentals (Airbnb and VRBO must now display the full weekly rate including cleaning fees) and event ticketing (you must see the total price including fees when you select seats).

— Lindsay Owens
13:23

Cancellation ease must match signup ease by federal requirement

The author's organization published a ‘Shopper's Bill of Rights’ model legislation containing 12 rights, including two key ones: refunds must return to your original payment method without three hours of chatbot negotiation, and unsubscribe methods must be as simple as signup—if signup auto-fills your address and credit card info, so must cancellation. New York City enacted a ‘one-click cancellation’ rule just one week ago, requiring local merchants to provide cancellation methods matching their signup flow.

— Lindsay Owens
14:26

Repair monopolies create a chain of markup: product, parts, labor

The author sees repair restrictions themselves as a form of price-gouging—chained price-gouging: begin with a steep initial product price, then charge for proprietary parts, then charge for proprietary repair labor. If you can't repair it at all, you buy a new one. John Deere prevents farmers from fixing their own harvesters; during harvest season farmers sit idle waiting for dealer technicians. McDonald's soft-serve machines perpetually malfunction because equipment owner Taylor Commercial Food Services forbids franchisees from self-servicing them.

— Lindsay Owens
23:44

AI agents more often return higher prices despite lower-price instructions

Two large meta-analyses of AI shopping assistants reached a troubling finding: when instructed to find lower prices, the AI agents returned higher quotes more often. The author worries that giving wallet control to AI agents is equivalent to releasing ‘the world's most sophisticated con artist’, and consumers must know whether AI agents serve them or the company—as with Walmart's Sparky.

— Lindsay Owens

Glossary

surveillance pricing
Companies use collected personal data to set different prices for different customers.
drip pricing
A low opening price attracts customers, then additional fees are charged progressively at checkout, raising the total far above the initial quote.
dark patterns
User interfaces deliberately designed to obscure additional fees or make service cancellation difficult.
loyalty penalty
Once a company confirms a customer is a stable repeat buyer, discounts and special offers are withdrawn.
right to repair
The right of consumers or third parties to repair purchased products without manufacturer restrictions.
