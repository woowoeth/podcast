Chinese brands go global not by shipping goods, but by embedding factories and R&D locally

From retail shelf battles in Berlin to a Polish factory where one Chinese face leads 50 workers, TCL embeds distribution, manufacturing, and R&D inch by inch into local markets, rather than simply exporting goods.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:07

Entering European retail means displacing an incumbent brand

Berlin's Media Markt flagship occupies 8,000 square meters across four floors with daily foot traffic exceeding 20,000, yet stocks only 6 TV brands. TCL gained shelf space last May. New entrants must first replace an existing brand—in-store shelf space itself is scarce inventory, an entirely different challenge than launching at a trade show.

8:03

German manufacturing began as a discriminatory label, not a quality mark

Xu Mingwei recounted overlooked history: post-war, "Made in Germany" was initially a requirement other countries imposed on German exporters to identify their goods to local consumers—not the quality symbol we understand today. Germans invested in R&D for roughly 20 years before converting this stigmatized label into today's default assumption of excellence. His conclusion: "Made in China" currently signals low cost and shoddy work, but traversing the same path might take only 10 to 20 years.

— Xu Mingwei
11:06

Twenty-two years at the Polish factory, and only one Chinese person

Thomas is a born-and-raised Pole who joined TCL in 2004 when it acquired Thomson's TV division—fresh from graduation, he recalls feeling "curious and a bit afraid" because the TV industry sat at a technology inflection point, shifting from CRT tubes to flat screens. Today he manages a quality department of roughly 50 people across three shifts. Asked how many Chinese staff and how many locals: besides him, everyone is Polish. The factory doesn't make whole units; it performs final assembly of semi-finished goods from China (SKD mode). Last year's output: 4.9 million units sold across Europe.

— Thomas
18:21

The Polish R&D center doesn't develop products for Poland

TCL's European R&D center in Poland has 24 people, part of TCL's global network of roughly 50 R&D centers. It focuses on AI-driven picture quality and voice research—but deliverables go to China headquarters' product clusters: phones, TVs, and all global product lines draw on work from here. Location in Poland doesn't mean serving the Polish market; rather, it integrates local engineering talent into a global collaboration network. The biggest cross-cultural friction: China's fixed interpretation of requirements often misaligns with local understanding.

26:25

Establishing roots means getting into consumers' minds, not just opening stores

Zhang Guangquan, the Polish subsidiary head, describes his role as a "translator"—relaying market signals to headquarters, translating corporate strategy into local execution. His definition of rooting: letting locals move from knowing you, to being familiar with you, to understanding you, to letting you into their lives, to becoming indispensable in those lives—landing you on their mental shelf. Even if TCL stops selling a product someday, when they think of that category, TCL should be the first name that comes to mind.

— Zhang Guangquan
28:25

TCL's German-speaking market ranking climbed from ninth to fifth in 19 months

Ma Jianxin moved TCL from ninth to fifth place in German-speaking markets in 19 months, replicating methods from his Spain track record. At the time, the team had no fixed office and worked one day a week; he spent three months recruiting a local sales director, mandated five-day office presence, and shifted the product mix from 75%-plus revenue relying on cheap 32-inch and 40-inch TVs to the group's flagship large-format mini-LED premium sets. The logic: if TCL ranks second or third in France, there's no reason Spain and Germany can't reach the same tier.

— Ma Jianxin
31:27

German consumers believe only in expensive-for-a-reason

Ma Jianxin calls Germany one of Europe's toughest markets. Local consumers are rigidly rational—they won't buy a TV simply because it costs 200 euros less than competitors. Instead they ask: why is yours 200 euros more? What does that premium buy? Success isn't about value-for-money; it's about clearly articulating why you cost more and whether they'll pay it. This is why TCL positioned itself as a mid-to-premium brand in German markets, not a high-value-for-money player.

— Ma Jianxin
36:31

Only above ten thousand nits does the image have that searing intensity

TCL's flagship X11 TV uses mini-LED—the backlight itself doesn't emit; over 20,000 local dimming zones control light, plus a custom chip for contrast. The team stresses a brightness threshold: in the hundreds-to-thousands nit range, sunlight glints and sparks look flat; only past 10,000 nits do you get true "searing" realism. The same tradeoff extends to AI glasses: Leiniao (雷鸟) glasses hit 34 grams as the industry's lightest, but sacrificed a camera to sidestep European users' social anxiety about cameras. Weight, battery, and compute form an inescapable "deadly triangle," the team concludes.
