Income inequality, not building shortages, is driving up housing costs

Max Buchholz argues the core of America's housing crisis is not insufficient construction but income inequality—incomes among college-educated workers have soared while those with only high school education have seen stagnant wages for forty years.

Evidence for housing supply shortages is weaker than believed

Buchholz questions three types of commonly cited evidence: elasticity studies show no clear differences across cities, deregulation impact studies fail to demonstrate substantial increases in supply, and housing shortage metrics themselves likely reflect income inequality rather than actual shortages, conflated with demand-side factors. His conclusion: the evidence that housing markets are failing or suffering from supply-side problems is far weaker than widely assumed.

— Max Buchholz
5:10

Forty years of wage growth has sharply diverged across income levels

The top 40% of American earners saw strong income growth over four decades, but those with only high school education have seen virtually no real wage growth since 1970. The two income curves run in opposite directions. High earners have bid up housing prices far beyond what standard models predict, squeezing everyone with lower incomes. Private equity's role in housing has been overstated; capital flows into real estate are an endogenous response to its emergence as a profitable asset class.

— Max Buchholz
9:16

New housing raises prices in transit-rich areas, lowers them elsewhere

Building new units in high-income, transit-rich areas often raises rather than lowers prices because demand is elastic at the neighborhood level—residents are drawn by new amenities (cafes, restaurants) and positive signals, bidding up local rents, a phenomenon called a correlated demand shock. Building in high-income, low-density single-family areas is more likely to lower prices, since existing residents genuinely dislike the externalities of shadows and congestion. Buchholz criticizes California's current policy for failing to distinguish between these two cases.

— Max Buchholz
16:31

Rezoning redirects construction but doesn't increase total housing supply

Research on deregulation shows that rezoning a lot does raise the probability it will be developed within about ten years. But these studies cannot prove that total housing construction in the metro area actually increases—development likely just relocates to the rezoned sites from elsewhere. This unresolved question is routinely mistaken for settled.

— Max Buchholz
24:42

Closing affordability gaps would take decades even with massive supply surges

Buchholz runs a thought experiment: if the least affordable cities (San Francisco Bay Area, New York, Los Angeles, Washington, Boston) magically gained housing equal to 1.5% of their existing stock annually—far exceeding normal demand-driven construction—how long until high school graduates could rent a one-bedroom for 30% of income? Using elasticity and depreciation rates from the literature, optimistic scenarios require roughly ten to twenty years; pessimistic ones approach a century in many places. The conclusion: supply increases from deregulation alone cannot close the gap.

— Max Buchholz
32:53

Europe has higher housing costs but fewer people crushed by them

Data show residents of wealthy European countries spend a slightly higher share of income on housing than Americans, yet far fewer are truly crushed—spending an extreme fraction of income. Buchholz attributes this to Europe's more robust public housing investment, rental subsidies, and well-designed rent controls. But he flags an overlooked tension: scaling up public housing suppresses prices and can discourage private developers from building at all.

— Max Buchholz
41:00

Bay Area actually added more housing per capita than Houston

Between 2000 and 2020, the Bay Area added more housing per capita than Houston—contradicting the stereotype that the Bay Area simply doesn't build. Buchholz attributes mid-twentieth-century faster construction relative to population growth to much larger household sizes then—averaging about 3.5 people per household versus 2.5 today—whereas today income gains translate more into demand for higher-quality housing—larger per-capita square footage—than simply more units.

— Max Buchholz
47:03

Housing supply problems are mostly demand problems rooted in inequality

Buchholz offers two conclusions. First, many housing phenomena blamed on the supply side are likely demand-side problems rooted in income inequality. Second, much supposed supply-side consensus—whether elasticity differs by city, whether rezoning truly increases total supply, whether shortages actually exist—deserves more skepticism than it receives.

— Max Buchholz

Glossary

filtering
The mechanism by which new luxury housing frees up existing stock, theoretically cascading down to lower overall prices.
land value tax
A tax on land value only, exempt on structures, intended to encourage development of vacant land and parking lots.
correlated demand shock
When new development brings neighborhood amenities upgrades, raising demand and prices in the area.
