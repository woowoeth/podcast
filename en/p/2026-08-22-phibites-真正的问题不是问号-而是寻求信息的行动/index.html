A question isn't defined by its question mark, but by the act of seeking information

A real question is defined by function, not form; asking is a power you can practise, and most of the answers AI gives today haven't yet answered a question anyone seriously asked.

A question mark doesn't define a question, and neither does intonation

Nigel starts out assuming a question is something with a question mark at the end. Lani pushes back: in spoken language, questions have no question mark at all, and we ask them every day; intonation isn't reliable either, since different languages and cultures have completely different intonation systems. In her 15-year online survey, the "how are you" case split participants almost 50/50, with the disagreement turning on whether the speaker really wanted to know the answer. So the marker of a question isn't in the form; the form is just the surface.

— Lani Watson
2:31

A question is an action, not a sentence

Lani's formal definition is: a question is an information seeking act, and whether it holds up depends on function, not form. Many participants counted looking both ways before crossing the street, checking a dictionary, even raising an eyebrow as questions, because these are all non-verbal acts of seeking information. A rhetorical question therefore isn't a real question, because she holds that it isn't aimed at getting information; what's genuinely fuzzy is how we draw the line around what counts as rhetorical.

— Lani Watson
5:15

An insincere question is still a question

Nigel presses: must the asker sincerely want an answer? Lani says no. She uses a hammer as an analogy: a hammer is still a hammer even if it's hung on a wall as art, because hammering nails is its function; an insincere question is still a question as long as the act carries the information-seeking function. This contrasts with the earlier 50/50 split on the "how are you" test: sincerity isn't a defining term, just one dimension along which a question can be used legitimately.

— Lani Watson
7:30

Philosophy can sit comfortably inside questions with no answers

Nigel asks why other disciplines just applaud after a talk, while philosophy insists on asking questions and finding holes. Lani says philosophy has a tradition of thousands of years of pursuing questions without answers, so it can sit comfortably inside a question, and a 15-minute Q&A is nothing. She also accepts another view of philosophy: testing ideas to destruction, probing their robustness with the harshest criticism. But she cautions that this can easily read as rudeness, and the key is how and with what intent you ask — if you're genuinely seeking truth, you shouldn't just be trying to provoke the other person.

— Lani Watson
10:30

Don't first ask whether ChatGPT is good, ask what it's answering

She worries about the flood of answers in the tech world, where everyone races to answer instead of asking the question first. Her favourite question right now is: what question is ChatGPT the answer to? If it's a solution, an answer, then what was the question it originally answered? This way of asking pulls the evaluation of AI away from "is it answering well" toward "is the question itself worth answering." Once you haven't thought the question through first, no amount of answers is anything but solving an object that was never defined.

— Lani Watson
13:00

The questioning cliff is taught, not the price of growing up

Children's questioning drops off quickly once they enter school, and by secondary school it almost falls off a cliff; a classroom going silent at "any questions?" is the everyday form of that cliff. But Lani says the neurochemistry driving questioning at ages 3-5 is still operating in the adult brain, and exploration and questioning still bring dopamine rewards. So we shouldn't "grow up and stop asking," but embrace this natural impulse and resist the suppression of questioning by the answer-oriented model.

— Lani Watson
16:13

The best question can be the simplest one

At the end Nigel asks her, "what's the best question anyone has ever asked you," and she answers with a laugh, "the one you just asked." It's both the show's closing joke and a live demonstration of the episode's whole argument: the best question isn't necessarily complex or profound, it can come in the simplest form — as long as it's genuinely seeking something.

— Lani Watson

Glossary

information seeking act
An act whose function is to obtain information, not necessarily in linguistic form.
rhetorical question
A question that takes the form of a question but isn't aimed at obtaining information.
private questions and social questions
The distinction between asking yourself in your head and asking in interaction with others.
answer oriented model
A mode that only pursues systematic answers and no longer goes back to check whether the question is worth answering.
