A question is defined by the act of seeking information, not the question mark

A real question is defined by its function, not its form; asking is a power you can practise — and most of the answers AI hands out today have not answered any question that was seriously posed.

Neither the question mark nor intonation marks what a question is

Nigel begins by assuming a question is whatever ends in a question mark. Lani counters that spoken questions carry no question marks at all, and we ask them all day long; intonation is no more reliable, since intonation systems differ completely across languages and cultures. In the online survey she has been running for 15 years she includes a "how are you" case, and participants split almost 50/50 — the disagreement turning on whether the speaker actually wants to know the answer. So the mark of a question does not lie in its form; form is only the surface.

— Lani Watson
2:31

A question is an act, not a sentence

Lani's formal definition is that a question is an information seeking act: whether something counts is settled by function, not by form. Many participants counted looking both ways before crossing the road, consulting a dictionary, even raising an eyebrow as questions, because all of these are non-verbal acts of seeking information. A rhetorical question is therefore not a real question, since on her view it is not aimed at acquiring information; what is genuinely blurry is where we draw the line on what counts as rhetorical.

— Lani Watson
5:15

An insincere question is still a question

Nigel presses: must the asker sincerely want the answer? Lani says no. Her analogy is a hammer — a hammer hung on a wall as a piece of art is still a hammer, because driving nails is its function; an insincere question, so long as the information-seeking function attaches to the act, remains a question. That sits in contrast with the 50/50 split on the "how are you" test earlier: sincerity is not part of the definition, only one dimension of whether a question is being used properly.

— Lani Watson
7:30

Philosophy can sit comfortably inside questions that have no answers

Nigel asks why other disciplines simply applaud after a talk while philosophy insists on questioning and hunting for holes. Lani says philosophy has a tradition thousands of years old of pursuing questions that have no answers, so it is comfortable sitting inside a question; 15 minutes of Q&A is nothing. She also accepts the other view of philosophy: test an idea to destruction, check its robustness with the harshest criticism available. But she cautions that this is easily read as rude, and that what matters is the manner and the intent of the asking — if you are genuinely seeking the truth, you should not simply antagonise the other person.

— Lani Watson
10:30

Before asking whether ChatGPT is good, ask what it is answering

She worries that in the technology world answers are everywhere and everyone is racing to answer rather than to ask first. Her favourite question at the moment is: what question is ChatGPT the answer to? If it is a solution, an answer, then what was the question it originally answered? The framing pulls the assessment of AI away from ‘how good is the answer’ and towards ‘is the question itself worth answering’. Once the question has not been thought through, no quantity of answers amounts to more than solving an object nobody has defined.

— Lani Watson
13:00

The collapse in children's questioning is taught, not the price of growing up

Once children enter school the volume of their questions drops quickly, and by secondary school it has all but fallen off a cliff; the silence that follows a teacher's ‘any questions?’ is the everyday face of that collapse. But Lani says the neurochemistry that drives questioning at ages 3 to 5 is still running in the adult brain — exploring and asking still deliver a dopamine reward. So we should not accept ‘you grow up and stop asking’; we should embrace that natural impulse and resist the way an answer-oriented culture suppresses questioning.

— Lani Watson
16:13

The best question can be the simplest one

Nigel closes by asking her what the best question anyone has ever asked her is, and she laughs and answers: the one you have just asked. It is both the show's closing joke and a live demonstration of the whole episode's argument — the best question need not be complex or profound; it can arrive in the simplest possible form, so long as it really is seeking something.

— Lani Watson

Glossary

information seeking act
An act whose function is to obtain information, not necessarily in verbal form.
rhetorical question
A question in form, but not aimed at obtaining information.
private questions and social questions
The split between questions you ask inside your own head and questions asked in interaction with other people.
answer oriented model
Chasing answers from the system without ever going back to check whether the question was worth answering.
