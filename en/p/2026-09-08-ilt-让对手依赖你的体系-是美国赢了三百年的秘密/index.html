Making Your Rivals Depend on Your System Is How America Has Won for Three Centuries

The Dutch, the British and the Americans took turns as hegemon not by blockading rivals but by getting them to stake their survival on someone else's maritime order — once they can't do without the sea lanes and the markets, cutting them off becomes the most effective weapon short of war.

4:47

Unpredictability is a form of power

‘Unpredictability’ is not madness; it is a modern strategy of power. Mead says post-Cold War American diplomacy held itself up as a pillar of stability by ‘letting the whole world know where America's red lines are’, while Trump profits by doing the opposite: crossing lines, threatening allies, turning commitments into bargaining chips. He saw through a secret of the information age — the marginal cost of lying: the first lie is expensive, the thousandth is nearly free. When Clinton was engulfed by the Lewinsky scandal, he discovered that shame only lives in other people's minds; as long as you keep walking, the dogs bark but the caravan moves on. Trump took that insight to industrial scale. The cost is borne by the American diplomatic system: when the world's number one power turns itself into the unpredictable card, every alliance and rule built on predictability after World War II is coming apart faster.

— Walter Russell Mead
10:00

The last step of global hegemony is cutting the cord

The Anglo-American maritime order did not win through any single battle but through a five-step playbook: first, an open society on an island — the 17th-century Dutch tolerated heretics and Jews, and ideas and capital poured in; then global trade, with Dutch merchant ships sailing the world, so rich that their paintings are full of Turkish carpets and Brazilian parrots; third, while continental powers fought over inland Silesia, you seized India and Canada across the globe. The fourth step is the riskiest: instead of copying Spain and sealing off your colonies, you invite the whole world in to do business, the British navy suppresses pirates, and every merchant ship benefits; Germany and Japan were likewise invited in after the war. Only the last step is the hole card: once the other side cannot do without your grain, your rubber and your shipping lanes, you cut it off — Germany discovered in World War I that Argentine grain and Malayan rubber had all been cut off, and its economy promptly collapsed.

— Walter Russell Mead
16:38

Everything China does is to avoid having the cord cut

Look at China through the same framework and you find Beijing is well aware of its own vulnerable point. Mead says: suppose the mainland blockades Taiwan, and the US counter-blockades from a hundred miles off Taiwan's coast: oil cannot get in, manufactured goods cannot get out, soybean contracts with Brazil and Argentina are voided, and China's economy is strangled alive. He has discussed these things with officials and scholars at Chinese universities, and this worry is always present. So China's energy policy has to be read side by side: on one hand building oil reserves so large they surprise outsiders; on the other, pushing hard on solar and hydropower while digging as much coal as possible. It is not schizophrenia, it is the same thing: pulling out, one by one, the soft spots of ‘dependence on the outside world’. He also warns the US not to retreat into isolationism — agriculture and AI both need overseas markets to keep themselves alive.

— Walter Russell Mead
25:50

Every round of technology dividends needs new institutions to match

Mead starts from the ownership society: when America was founded it was full of independent farmers with land, and while a bridge is a good thing, it raises my taxes — that trade-off is itself a political education. The railroad era was matched by the Homestead Act, with the government giving land to railroad companies and farmers, and a national market grew out of it; in the 20th century what replaced the family farm was the single-family suburban house, paired with tax-exempt municipal bonds and highways, becoming a new wealth-distribution machine. Now that machine has hit a wall on house prices: young people cannot afford to live near where they work. His remedy is remote work and autonomous driving. When the commuting radius expands from 40 miles to 75 miles, usable land for housing rises with the square of the radius, and the physical space opened by technology is enough to carry two more generations. But the precondition is filling in the institutional ‘infrastructure’ — he coined the word info-structure, meaning the institutions and regulatory rules that let AI actually land in hospitals and courts.

— Walter Russell Mead
40:22

Silicon Valley is falling in behind the Tech Hamiltonians

American foreign policy debate has for centuries been four vocabularies: Hamiltonians want a strong state clearing the way for capital, Wilsonians want to spread liberal democracy, Jeffersonians fear a bigger government, Jacksonians stay isolationist in peacetime and fight to the death when hit. The post-Cold War establishment was a marriage of Hamilton and Wilson, but Trump brought Jefferson and Jackson into the game. Mead says the new species really worth watching is the Tech Hamiltonian. In the 1990s tech companies loved to say ‘we are a global company, Google has no borders, Washington has no say’; AI competition has turned tech into a contest of national destiny. Economic positions changed too: the old multinational car companies wanted borderless labor and markets, while Silicon Valley wants supply-chain and IP security, and even wants its employees to be patriotic, considers selling code to China shameful, and expects colleagues to report you outright. The core of business has shifted from the old multinationals to tech nationalists, and this is the underwater current of America's political realignment.

— Walter Russell Mead
50:00

The power with more equipment fears technological turnover most

Mead has been to Ukraine twice. The first time he visited a drone factory and saw them converting children's toy trucks into ground drones carrying mines to blow up tanks; months later he went again, and the progress was unimaginably fast. The battlefield is now a gray kill zone, usually only two or three soldiers move together, and one of the hardest problems is ‘how to get the wounded out’ — you need to build a ground drone with a metal shield to drag people, with a squad of drones alongside hunting the enemy drones coming to attack. He heard of one wounded soldier who took 63 days to be evacuated from the front. Things flip every six months. The bitter irony for the US: the power with the most tanks and aircraft carriers is precisely the one most afraid of this kind of parameter revolution — existing equipment turns from an advantage into a relocation cost. Russia's Black Sea Fleet has no great battle to fight, yet it is bottled up at home by Ukrainian small boats and drones.

— Walter Russell Mead
54:16

The new variable most worth watching in the Taiwan Strait is Japan

On ‘will the mainland attack Taiwan’, Mead's judgment is that Beijing needs a very high probability of success before it is willing to cross the threshold — once it fails, it is catastrophic for the initiator. But between ‘attack’ and ‘do nothing’ there is a long gray ladder: for example requiring all merchant ships bound for Taiwan to first call at mainland ports for customs clearance, or imposing a soft blockade, doing things that change facts enough without triggering an armed US response. The real new variable on the Taiwan board is not America's attitude but Japan. The Japanese have already publicly concluded that if China takes Taiwan, Japan's independence is finished too; they are raising defense budgets, rewriting security doctrine, reshaping the national psyche. Historically Japan turns very fast when faced with a real threat. How steep Japan's learning curve is, and whether Beijing has to factor Japan into its calculations, is the key to the Taiwan question in the coming years.

— Walter Russell Mead
1:10:00

AI actually makes deterrence more credible

Moving Cold War deterrence logic into the algorithmic age, Mead points to a counterintuitive corollary: deterrence succeeds or fails on making the opponent one hundred percent believe that ‘if I do X, you will inevitably do Y, and you can do it and are willing to’. As long as there is a human veto point in the decision chain — ‘I might go soft when the time comes’ — that commitment has a crack in it. Automating part of foreign-policy decision-making with AI amounts to turning retaliation into an executable mechanism that will not hesitate — deterrence becomes more credible. But this is the doomsday machine problem from science fiction: decisions of civilizational scale no longer require a human to bear the consequences. His own assessment of the question is ‘very interesting and a bit puzzling’, and he does not think the answer has appeared yet.

— Walter Russell Mead

Glossary

Tech Hamiltonian
A new force in American foreign policy that wants state power to clear the way for tech companies, replacing the old free-market multinational capital.
info-structure
The institutions, laws and regulatory frameworks needed for AI to land safely in hospitals, courts and similar settings, as distinct from physical infrastructure.
Jacksonian
The American hawkish tradition of not meddling in the outside world in peacetime and, when attacked, destroying the enemy with full force.
soft blockade
A way of pressuring a rival without open war, slowly strangling its trade through customs, insurance and port rules.
