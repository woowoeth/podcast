Making rivals depend on your system is the secret to America's three centuries of winning

The Dutch, British, and Americans took turns as top dog not by blockading but by getting rivals to stake everything on their maritime system—once they can't live without the shipping lanes and markets, cutting them off becomes the most effective killer short of war.

4:47

Unpredictability is a form of power

‘Unpredictability’ is not madness but a modern power strategy. Mead says post-Cold War US diplomacy relied on ‘letting the whole world know where America's red lines are’ as a pillar of stability, but Trump profits from the opposite: crossing lines, threatening allies, turning commitments into bargaining chips. He saw through a secret of the information age—the marginal cost of lying: the first lie is expensive, the thousandth is nearly free. Clinton, besieged by the Lewinsky scandal, discovered that shame lives only in other people's minds; as long as you keep moving forward, the dogs bark but the caravan passes. Trump scaled that insight to industrial proportions. The cost is borne by the US diplomatic system: when the world's top power itself becomes the unpredictable card, the alliances and rules built on predictability since World War II are rapidly unraveling.

— Walter Russell Mead
10:00

The final step of global hegemony is cutting off

The Anglo-American maritime order won not through a single battle but a five-step playbook: first, open societies on islands—17th-century Holland tolerated heretics and Jews, and ideas and capital flowed in; then global trade, Dutch merchant ships traversing the world, wealth so abundant that paintings showed Turkish carpets and Brazilian parrots. Third, while continental powers fought over Silesia inland, you grabbed India and Canada globally. The fourth step is the riskiest: instead of imitating Spain's colonial blockades, invite everyone in to do business—the British navy fought pirates, and all merchant ships benefited; Germany and Japan were likewise invited in after the war. The final step is the trump card: once the other side can't live without your grain, rubber, and shipping lanes, cut them off—Germany in World War I suddenly found Argentine grain and Malayan rubber cut off, and its economy collapsed.

— Walter Russell Mead
16:38

All of China's efforts are about preventing the umbilical cord from being cut

Viewing China through the same framework, you see Beijing knows its own vulnerability well. Mead says, suppose the mainland blockades Taiwan, and the US counter-blockades from a hundred miles off Taiwan's coast: oil can't come in, manufactured goods can't go out, soybean contracts with Brazil and Argentina are voided—China's economy would be strangled. He has discussed these scenarios with officials and scholars at Chinese universities, and this concern has always been present. So China's energy policy should be read in parallel: on one hand, building oil reserves on a scale that surprises outsiders; on the other, ramping up solar and hydro, while mining as much coal as possible. It's not schizophrenia; it's the same thing: pulling out, one by one, the weak points of ‘dependence on the outside world.’ He also warns America not to retreat into isolationism—agriculture and AI both need overseas markets to sustain themselves.

— Walter Russell Mead
25:50

Each technological dividend needs new institutions to match

Mead starts with the ownership society: at America's founding, the land was full of independent farmers who owned their plots; building a bridge might be good, but it would raise my taxes—that trade-off itself was political education. The railroad era paired with the Homestead Act, as the government gave land to railroad companies and farmers, letting a national market grow. In the 20th century, the suburban single-family home replaced the family farm, paired with tax-exempt municipal bonds and highways, becoming a new wealth distribution machine. Today that machine hits housing prices: young people can't afford to live near work. His remedy is remote work and autonomous driving. If the commuting radius expands from 40 miles to 75 miles, the usable land for housing grows with the square of the radius—the physical space opened by technology can support another two generations. But the prerequisite is institutional ‘infrastructure’—he coined the term info-structure, meaning the institutional and regulatory rules that let AI actually land in hospitals and courts.

— Walter Russell Mead
40:22

Silicon Valley tilts toward the Tech Hamiltonian camp

American foreign policy debate for centuries has been four rhetorics: Hamiltonians want a strong state to pave the way for capital, Wilsonians want to spread liberal democracy, Jeffersonians fear big government, and Jacksonians are isolationist in peacetime but hit back hard when attacked. The post-Cold War establishment was a marriage of Hamiltonian and Wilsonian, but Trump brought Jefferson and Jackson into the tent. Mead says the truly notable new species is the Tech Hamiltonian. In the 1990s, tech companies liked to say ‘we're a global company, Google has no borders, Washington can't touch us’; AI competition turned tech into a matter of national destiny. Economic stances have shifted too: old multinational car companies wanted borderless labor and markets, while Silicon Valley wants supply chain and IP security, even hoping employees are patriotic—selling code to China is shameful, and colleagues will report you. The commercial core shifting from old multinationals to tech nationalists is the underwater current of America's political realignment.

— Walter Russell Mead
50:00

The more equipment a great power has, the more it fears technological generational shifts

Mead has been to Ukraine twice. The first time at a drone factory, he saw them converting children's toy trucks into ground drones carrying mines to blow up tanks; a few months later, progress was unimaginably fast. The battlefield is now a gray kill zone, usually with only two or three soldiers operating together, and one of the hardest problems is ‘how to evacuate the wounded’—you need a ground drone with a metal shield to drag people out, accompanied by a swarm of hunter drones to kill the enemy's attack drones. He heard of one wounded soldier who took 63 days to be evacuated from the front line. The changes flip every six months. The irony for America: the great power with the most tanks and aircraft carriers is precisely the one most afraid of this kind of parameter revolution—existing equipment goes from advantage to sunk cost. The Russian Black Sea Fleet has no major battles to fight, yet it's trapped at home by Ukrainian small boats and drones.

— Walter Russell Mead
54:16

The most important new variable in the Taiwan Strait is Japan

On whether the mainland will attack Taiwan, Mead's judgment: Beijing needs a very high probability of success to cross the threshold—if it fails, it's catastrophic for the initiator. But between ‘attack’ and ‘do nothing’ lies a long gray ladder: for example, requiring all ships bound for Taiwan to first stop at mainland ports for customs clearance, or imposing a soft blockade—actions that change the facts on the ground without triggering an armed US response. The truly new variable on the Taiwan chessboard is not the US attitude but Japan. The Japanese have publicly stated that if China takes Taiwan, Japan's independence is over; they are raising defense budgets, rewriting security doctrines, and reshaping national psychology. Historically, Japan has turned quickly when facing a real threat. How steep Japan's learning curve is, and whether Beijing's calculations must factor in Japan, will be the key to the Taiwan issue in the coming years.

— Walter Russell Mead
1:10:00

AI actually makes deterrence more credible

Applying Cold War deterrence logic to the algorithmic age, Mead points to a counterintuitive conclusion: deterrence succeeds when the opponent is 100% convinced that ‘if I do X, you will inevitably do Y, and you can and will do it.’ As long as there's a human veto point in the decision chain—‘I might soften at the last moment’—that commitment has a crack. Automating part of diplomatic decision-making and handing it to AI turns retaliation into a mechanism that is executable and won't hesitate—deterrence becomes more credible. But that's the doomsday machine problem from science fiction: decisions of annihilation no longer require humans to bear the consequences. His own assessment of the issue is ‘very interesting and somewhat puzzling,’ and he doesn't think an answer has emerged.

— Walter Russell Mead

Glossary

Tech Hamiltonian
A new force in US foreign policy that advocates state power to clear the way for tech companies, replacing old free-market multinational capital.
info-structure
The institutional, legal, and regulatory framework needed for AI to safely land in settings like hospitals and courts, as distinct from physical infrastructure.
Jacksonian
The American hawkish tradition of staying out of foreign affairs in peacetime but, once attacked, using overwhelming force to destroy the enemy.
soft blockade
A method of strangling an opponent's trade without direct warfare, using customs, insurance, and port rules to gradually choke it off.
