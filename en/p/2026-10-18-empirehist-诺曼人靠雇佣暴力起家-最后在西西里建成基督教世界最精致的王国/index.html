Normans built their empire on mercenary violence, then created Christendom's most refined kingdom in Sicily

Normans conquered Sicily before the Battle of Hastings: they started as violent contractors-for-hire, then transformed military power into political rule, building Christendom's wealthiest and most politically sophisticated state.

Normans built their fortune on mercenary contracts

They arrived in southern Italy as pilgrims in the early 11th century but were really hired killers for local nobles—the more violence, the better the business. By the 1030s–40s, they stopped working for others and transformed military force into political power, becoming parasites consuming their former employers. Frankopan compares this to organized crime or the East India Company's playbook.

— Peter
7:17

Norman cavalry were peacetime showpieces, not battlefield essentials

Though famous for heavy armor dominance, Frankopan argues the kit was more like a tank—devastating in a charge, but a money sink otherwise. Horses required vast pasture, training, and feed. Peacetime, they became status symbols—like modified supercars on London or Dubai streets today—pure displays of dominance rather than practical advantage.

— Peter
8:24

Brutality: Robert's path from debt collector to duke

‘Robert the Weasel’ earned his nickname and power through brutal debt collection—pulling fingernails when debtors couldn't pay. After consolidating Apulia and Calabria, he leveraged Byzantine and papal court politics so effectively that in 1059 Byzantine recognized him as Duke of Apulia—easier to grant a title than wage war.

— Peter
11:30

Roger rejected the Crusade because wars destroy business

When the First Crusade sought his forces, Roger raised one leg and released a loud fart as his response—because war disrupted trade. Frankopan frames Roger as ruthlessly pragmatic: war was good business when it meant seizing assets and power, but once it tanked commerce and spiked prices, it became pure loss. This logic defines Norman Sicily's entire ruling philosophy.

— Peter
12:30

Roger II kidnapped weavers to build his textile monopoly

During the Second Crusade, while Louis VII and the German emperor mobilized east, Roger attacked Byzantine territory in Corfu and Epirus, then sent raiders to Thebes and Corinth to abduct textile workers. He brought them back to Sicily to expand his factories—the entire operation was business logic from start to finish.

— Peter
25:00

Religious pluralism was Roger's principle—and Western Christendom's scandal

Ibn Jubayr's account portrays Roger's court as luxurious and religiously open—Muslim physicians and astrologers at his side. But Frankopan argues the travelogue was composed as a brief for Andalusian rulers in Granada and Córdoba—a model to emulate. Simultaneously, Bernard of Clairvaux denounced Roger as a ‘baptized sultan,’ treating Muslim freedom of movement and dress as existential threats, revealing that this tolerance narrative was contested and never universal.

— Peter
31:10

In Roger's court, competence trumped birth and faith

Roger II's inner circle recruited on pure merit. George was a Greek Christian from Antioch in Syria, had served Muslim rulers in Tunisia, then ran Sicily's fleet and treasury under the title Amiratus Amiratorum (Commander of Commanders)—the etymological root of the English word ‘admiral.’ Frankopan calls the Normans shrewd headhunters: loyalty and competence were the only requirements.

— Peter
40:55

Hindu-Arabic numerals debuted on Norman coins, not Italian merchant ledgers

The numbers we use today first appeared not in Italian trading cities but on coins minted in Palermo in 1138 by Roger's court, displacing Roman numerals and Greek letters. After Roger's death, his English treasurer Thomas Brown brought the system to England and attempted introducing it to Henry II's exchequer—but it never took hold. When the old system worked, nobody switched.

Glossary

Tari
The Islamic-standard gold coin used by Norman Sicily, stamped with Arabic script on one side and Greek or Norman symbols on the other.
Cappella Palatina
The chapel Roger II built inside his Palermo palace, fusing Islamic domes, Byzantine mosaics, and Norman arches.
Amiratus Amiratorum
The title of George in Roger's court; the term is the etymological root of the English word ‘admiral.’
Tabula Rogeriana
A world map and comprehensive geography compiled by Arab geographer Al-Idrisi for Roger II, presenting both cartography and detailed regional descriptions.
