Kent State Shootings: Why the National Guard Fired on Students

In 1970 at Kent State University, the National Guard opened fire on student protesters, killing four. Confused orders, outdated tactics, and armed confrontation turned a peaceful protest into bloodshed.

American historyKent StateNational Guardanti-war movementshooting
This episode reconstructs the full course of the Kent State shootings, revealing the chaos and misjudgment behind the events, and directly helps in understanding the social fractures of 1960s-70s America.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:05

National Guard armed and deployed on campus

On May 3, 1970, 850 National Guardsmen with M1 rifles, bayonets, and tear gas arrived at Kent State University, bivouacking on the football field and guarding the ROTC building. The deployment enraged students; even those not originally part of the protest felt the war had come to their doorstep. But students were not particularly afraid, because previously the Guard had handled demonstrations with tear gas or scuffles, never firing live rounds. This time, however, the troops carried live ammunition.

3:08

Confused orders, governor stoking the fire

Governor Rhodes's speech was inflammatory, comparing protesters to Nazi Brownshirts and the KKK, calling them 'the worst people in America.' But in fact, his order to deploy the Guard was not official until May 5, even though troops had arrived on May 3. There were serious disagreements among the university president, local government, and governor about the Guard's mission: the president believed the assembly had been banned, but protest leaders and professors pointed out the ban had not yet taken effect. This confused communication set the stage for the later conflict.

5:09

Last-minute rule change sparks standoff

On the evening of May 3, students gathered on the Commons to protest, but local authorities moved the curfew up from 1 a.m. to 9 p.m. and sent police to disperse them. Students saw this as 'changing the rules mid-game' and split into two groups: one went to the president's house to demand First Amendment rights and was driven back with tear gas; the other sat down in the middle of a main road, blocking traffic and singing 'Give Peace a Chance.' Police and Guardsmen surrounded them, with helicopters overhead. Eventually, the Guard used tear gas and bayonets to push students back to the dorms, resulting in at least two students stabbed and 68 arrested.

9:11

May 4: peaceful rally resumes

At 11 a.m. on May 4, about 2,000 students again gathered on the Commons. Although there was no official state of emergency, university officials distributed flyers declaring the assembly illegal. General Canterbury, the Guard commander, insisted the demonstration was illegal, but students still spread word of the rally via loudspeakers. By noon, the rally was largely peaceful, aside from some vulgar chants. Campus police used a loudspeaker to declare the assembly illegal and order dispersal, but the voice was drowned out, and a few rocks were thrown at vehicles. Canterbury then ordered two volleys of tear gas, but they missed, and the wind blew the gas away.

12:13

13 seconds of fire, 67 bullets

The Guard advanced to clear the area, successfully dispersing most protesters, but a group of 52 advanced too far and were trapped against a fenced-in practice field, pelted with rocks. After 10 minutes, they received orders to withdraw and moved toward Taylor Hall. When they reached the top of the hill, 72 Guardsmen turned, raised their weapons, and fired a 13-second volley, totaling 67 bullets. Four students were killed: Jeffrey Miller (20, 265 feet away), Allison Krause (19, 340 feet away), Sandra Scheuer (19, 390 feet away), and William Schroeder (19, 390 feet away). Nine others were wounded, from as close as 60 feet to as far as 750 feet.

14:16

A photograph changes America

After the shooting, there was dead silence; students stood frozen. Fourteen-year-old Mary Ann Vecchio knelt over Jeffrey Miller's body and screamed, and 21-year-old photography student John Filo captured the image, which became a nationally shocking icon. But the conflict did not end; protests spread across the country, followed by the hard-hat riot and the Jackson State shootings. The program emphasizes that this history has striking parallels to the present and is worth reflecting on.
