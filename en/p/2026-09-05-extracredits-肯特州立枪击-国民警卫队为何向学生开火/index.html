Kent State Shootings: Why the National Guard Opened Fire on Students

In 1970 at Kent State University, the National Guard opened fire on protesting students, killing 4. Confused orders, outdated tactics, and an armed standoff turned a peaceful protest into a bloodbath.

U.S. HistoryKent StateNational GuardAnti-War MovementShooting
This episode reconstructs the entire Kent State shooting, revealing the chaos and misjudgments behind the event, and offers direct help in understanding the social fractures in America during the 1960s and 70s.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:05

The National Guard occupies campus with live ammunition

On May 3, 1970, 850 National Guardsmen carrying M1 rifles, bayonets, and tear gas entered Kent State University, stationed at the football field and guarding the ROTC building. This deployment enraged students, even those who had not originally participated in the protests felt the war had come to their doorstep. But students were not particularly afraid, because previously the Guard had usually only used tear gas or engaged in brawls when handling demonstrations, and had never shot anyone. However, this time was unusual: the troops carried live ammunition.

3:08

Confused orders, and the governor adds fuel to the fire

Governor Rhodes's speech was full of inflammatory rhetoric, comparing protesters to Nazi Brownshirts and the Ku Klux Klan, calling them "the worst people in America." But in fact, his order to formally deploy the Guard did not come until May 5, while the troops had already arrived on May 3. There were serious disagreements among the university president, local government, and the governor about the Guard's mission: the president believed the assembly had been banned, but protest leaders and professors pointed out that the ban had not yet taken effect. This chaotic transmission of information laid the groundwork for the subsequent conflict.

5:09

A last-minute rule change triggers a standoff

On the evening of May 3, students gathered in the square to protest, but local government suddenly moved the curfew from 1 a.m. to 9 p.m. and sent police to disperse them. Students saw this as "changing the rules mid-game," so they split into two groups: one went to the president's house to demand First Amendment rights and was driven back by tear gas; the other sat in on the main road blocking traffic, singing "Give Peace a Chance." Police and the Guard surrounded them, with helicopters circling overhead. Eventually, the Guard used tear gas and bayonets to drive the students back to their dorms, leaving at least two students stabbed and 68 arrested.

9:11

May 4: A peaceful assembly forms again

At 11 a.m. on May 4, about 2,000 students gathered in the square again. Although there was no official state of emergency, university officials distributed leaflets saying the assembly was illegal. Guard commander General Canterbury insisted the demonstration was illegal, but students still spread word of the gathering through loudspeakers. By noon, the assembly was largely peaceful, with only some crude slogans. University police announced through loudspeakers that the assembly was illegal and demanded dispersal, but their voices were drowned out, and a few rocks were thrown at vehicles. Then Canterbury ordered two rounds of tear gas, but they missed, and the wind blew the gas away.

12:13

13 seconds of shooting, 67 bullets

The Guard advanced to clear the area, successfully dispersing most of the protesters, but a group of 52 pushed too far and were trapped in the fenced stadium, pelted with rocks. Ten minutes later, they received orders to retreat and moved toward Taylor Hall. When they reached the top of the hill, 72 Guardsmen turned, raised their weapons, and fired a 13-second volley totaling 67 bullets. Four students died: Jeffrey Miller (20, 265 feet away), Allison Krause (19, 340 feet away), Sandra Scheuer (19, 390 feet away), and William Schroeder (19, 390 feet away). Another 9 were injured, the closest at 60 feet, the farthest at 750 feet.

14:16

One photograph changes America

After the shooting there was dead silence, students stood frozen. 14-year-old Mary Ann Vecchio knelt screaming beside Jeffrey Miller's body, and 21-year-old photography student John Filo took the photo that became a national icon. But the conflict did not end; protests spread across the country, followed by the Hard Hat Riot, the Jackson State shootings, and other events. The episode emphasizes that this history has striking parallels to the present and is worth reflecting on.
