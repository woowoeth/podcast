Congress can't stop AI with votes alone—the next major crisis might do it

Democrats dominated the midterms, but overriding a presidential veto of AI legislation still requires 67 Senate votes—what might actually move that threshold is probably the next warning shot, more serious than the Hugging Face hack.

AI safety concerns were probably the fourth force behind the Democratic midterm surge

Democrats captured at least 250 House seats and 56 Senate seats, even winning Mississippi, South Carolina, Kansas, and Texas. While AI wasn't the top three issue, it was probably the fourth or fifth driver of voter backlash—reflecting a massive gap between government policy and public demand. Two-thirds of Americans think AI is advancing too fast, just 2% think too slow, and this ratio barely shifts between parties.

1:29

Breaking the filibuster needs 60 votes, but overriding a veto needs 67 votes

Getting 60 votes to break filibuster is actually achievable thanks to cross-party support—four-fifths of Americans back AI safety rules even if they slow development, and Republican senators including Hawley, Kennedy, and Blackburn are already pushing legislation. But overriding a presidential veto requires 67 Senate and 290 House votes, which at current concern levels is nearly impossible without gutting the rules' meaning. Even so, Congress has four immediate workarounds: common-sense measures, must-pass appropriations bills, personnel confirmations, and confirmation hearings.

— Rob Wiblin
4:36

Rushing AI without safety pauses will likely produce deceptively aligned superintelligence

Rob argues we need at least to slow the race when models approach human level, giving companies time to test personality, review work, and refine alignment. Without this time, we get superficially aligned but deeply deceptive superintelligence that could cause total disaster. Companies themselves want this breathing room, but Congress hasn't yet gathered enough votes to override a veto. A recent METR report shows AI R&D could be fully automated by 2027, dramatically accelerating progress.

— Rob Wiblin
5:48

Only a major AI crisis will likely shift Congress enough to override a veto

Rob speculates that changing Trump's mind or gathering enough override votes probably needs another warning shot at least as shocking as the Hugging Face hack or Jacob Coxon's resignation—at minimum, another AI accident showing power-seeking tendencies or service disruption; at worst, AI paralyzing critical infrastructure that can't be shut down, or pandemic-causing AI. The days and weeks immediately after the warning shot will be the critical policy window, so someone should be prepping legislation now to be ready for a 100-hour work week.

— Rob Wiblin
7:42

Post-crisis politics will almost certainly unite around banning superintelligence

The public overwhelmingly opposes training superintelligence that crushes human experts in all domains; both chambers have already proposed bills on this. Rob proposes a concrete mechanism: don't directly cap training, but require AI companies to dedicate some compute to external inference services—set it to 100% to pause research without killing products, then gradually lower to around 70%, raising it whenever warning signs appear. This works like the Fed adjusting rates: flexible AI-speed control that also cuts inference costs for ordinary users.

— Rob Wiblin
9:17

A six-month freeze on internal AI model access could significantly slow development

Rob's most promising specific mechanism: ban AI companies from using their newest models for internal R&D—they can only use models trained and released at least six months prior. Companies can't argue this isn't feasible; they worked this way six months ago. This rule won't halt research entirely but substantially slows it, preventing capability gains from immediately feeding back into recursive self-improvement loops, and gives alignment testing at least six more months. If six isn't enough, this lag can extend to 12, 18, or even 24 months, gradually approaching a full recursive self-improvement ban.

— Rob Wiblin
13:49

America's AI safety agency is starved compared to Britain's massive investment

CAISSI is the core US AI regulation agency with an annual budget of approximately $15 million and only dozens of staff, while the UK's AI Safety Institute has more than 100 technical staff and 8 times CAISSI's budget, plus 300 million pounds over four years added, plus hundreds of millions more for biosecurity, cyber, and rogue AI incidents. Adjusted for GDP, Britain invests about 50 times more in national AI capacity-building than the US. The consequence: if serious crisis hits, the US government might depend on Britain's risk assessment capability because it never built its own.

— Rob Wiblin
16:38

Emergency AI legislation must preserve state regulatory authority to avoid backsliding

Rob flags a trap: the expectation was that Congressional legislation would broadly suppress state AI authority. But if legislation emerges hastily after a warning shot, states will likely have functioning rules and regulators already in place. Repealing state law at that moment would actually loosen overall oversight, defeating the legislation's purpose. Emergency bills should default to preserving state authority, leaving federal-state coordination to post-crisis negotiation.

— Rob Wiblin
