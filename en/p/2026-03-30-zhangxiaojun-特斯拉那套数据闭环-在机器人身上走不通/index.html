Tesla's data flywheel doesn't work on robots

Tesla collects the most data because it has deployed the most of its own cars — it relies on the body. But embodied AI has no millions of robots, so the most data must come from simulation and human first-person footage. A body maker cannot become the best brain maker.

Data has been called oil for a long time. This episode gives you a mechanism you can make judgments with: who produces data, who evaluates it, who prices it, and why the large-model camp and the real-robot camp are diverging.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

21:18

Data is not a dataset, it is an education system

Xie Chen defines data not as a dataset but as ‘the signals that help you learn, and the corresponding transmission of that experience’. He compares the three stages of the data industry to education: ImageNet is a static dataset, equivalent to one-off force-feeding — buying a batch of textbooks and handing them to students. Scale AI turned data into a factory-style process, equivalent to mass-market education. In the large-model era, pretraining has consumed the internet, and the focus shifts to post-training and evaluation, requiring highly capable engineers, physicists, math gold medalists, lawyers, and doctors to write questions, set evaluation criteria, and then teach according to aptitude based on those evaluations — this is where ‘the teacher transmits the way, imparts skills, and resolves doubts’ comes in. So data is slowly turning from a static dataset into an education system.

— Xie Chen
33:26

The most effective data is failure followed by success

The earliest request from clients to Guanglun Intelligence was to provide perfectly correct, flawless simulated long-horizon task data — for example, a robot making a pizza: take the dough out of the fridge, add toppings, put it in the oven, press the button, every action must be correct. But after iterating with clients, they found that the most effective data is failure-then-success data — the mushroom wasn't gripped firmly and fell on the table, then it's picked up and put back on the pizza. These samples, called negative samples or correction data, are actually more expensive and more valuable. Xie Chen's explanation is that once a model's generalization ability improves, it can better learn cognition back from mistakes, which is closer to human learning: the experience of succeeding after failing is often the most precious.

— Xie Chen
44:46

Using robot arms precisely because they don't want to touch hardware

When large-model teams build VLAs, they choose the simplest robot arm rather than humanoid or wheeled bases, precisely because they don't want to do hardware-related work — humanoid maintenance and debugging each body is far more complex. What they want is zero-shot generalization: trained on ten or a hundred tasks, and able to do five other unseen tasks. At the same time, the infrastructure on the two sides differs by an order of magnitude: a robotics company with a few thousand GPUs is already a lot, while large-model teams typically have tens of thousands. Large-scale parallel reinforcement learning infrastructure is hard to build in-house for embodied models, but large-model teams already have it — they just migrate from the large-language-model setting to fine-tune VLAs.

— Xie Chen
51:55

Tesla's data flywheel doesn't hold for embodied AI

Tesla's data engine is essentially body-dependent logic: it deploys the most cars worldwide, collects the most data, trains the best brain, so the OEM itself is the biggest brain maker. Xie Chen believes this logic will be overturned in embodied AI — the world does not have millions of robots deployed at the edge executing tasks, and relying on human teleoperation is too costly and cannot scale. So the embodied data architecture must follow the data pyramid: the most expensive real-robot data is the smallest in volume and hardest to scale, simulation is in the middle, and at the bottom is internet and human first-person data. This means the most embodied data will definitely not be provided by the body maker; there will not be a body maker that is both the most widely used hardware and the world's best brain — Optimus's brain is provided by xAI.

— Xie Chen
1:18:13

Top real-robot camp collectively pivoted in the past three months

Guanglun Intelligence's earliest clients were all strong simulation believers, while the top frontier labs and top large-model teams used to be of the real-robot school, absolutely unwilling to try any simulation. But in the past three months, these teams have basically all become clients. The problem they all encountered was that they couldn't scale their own evaluations: real-robot data or academic benchmarks couldn't produce true industry meaning because they were too simple and not scalable enough. Brain teams working on home scenarios can already fold clothes and do chores well, but they need a thousand different home scenarios and tens of thousands of tasks to evaluate themselves at any time, and that can only be obtained through simulation.

— Xie Chen
1:50:45

Real-robot data is overrated, simulation evaluation is underrated

Xie Chen's judgment: real robot data is definitely overrated. In the past few months, even companies and large-model teams from the original real-robot camp have been purchasing simulation data, simulation evaluation, and human data at scale. Simulation as a whole is still underrated, and the most underrated part is simulation evaluation — large-model teams have fully seen this, because without simulation you can't do large-scale evaluation; many robotics companies, because their scale isn't that big yet, will feel this pain more and more as task numbers and open scenarios grow, and they can't avoid simulation. Human data is also underrated; it supplements and enhances the simulation-centric loop rather than replacing it.

— Xie Chen
2:12:55

Bickering solves nothing; only symbiosis works

The classic bickering between data companies and model companies — the data side says your model isn't trained well, the model side says your data collection is bad — Xie Chen acknowledges it objectively exists and compares it to Scale AI and OpenAI's situation during the GPT stage: everyone is actually jointly searching for the data recipe. The general direction is clear, the differences are in details, such as from initially wanting perfect data, to later wanting negative samples and correction data, to requiring broader distribution — the way you hold the bottle can't be the same every time. The solution is symbiotic iteration with the most leading clients. He says there are very few teams in the world that can truly develop cognition at the scale of large-scale pretraining data — maybe only about five — and Guanglun basically works with all of them.

— Xie Chen
2:31:05

If you could solve only one data problem, it would be evaluation

If solving only one critical data problem could achieve a major leap, Xie Chen's answer is evaluation. The reason is that the pretraining pathway and scaling law for body-agnostic data have already emerged, and evaluation has become the real bottleneck — without solving it, it's hard for anyone to measure their own intelligence improvement. For embodied AI, truly scalable simulation evaluation must be built; it's a capability everyone will need. The large-model side is similarly stuck on evaluation and post-training, and it's an arms race: as model capabilities improve, you need even more brilliant people to provide better feedback, design harder questions, and create more effective evaluation metrics.

— Xie Chen

Glossary

Sim-to-Real
The performance gap when capabilities trained in simulation are transferred to real robots; it is the core issue for which the simulation camp is criticized.
Shadow Mode
The algorithm only outputs signals on the vehicle side without executing actions, comparing with human operation; the difference is a free evaluation signal.
Data Engine
An evaluation-feedback-driven data production loop centered on systems rather than manpower, distinct from a pipeline-style Data Factory.
Behavior
An embodied evaluation suite built on simulation by Li Feifei's team, all difficult long-horizon tasks; later there is also Enact, which uses the same system to evaluate world models.
VLA
A robot action model based on a foundation large model, taking vision and language as input and outputting actions.
Zero-shot
Being able to do unseen tasks; Xie Chen believes a model without this generalization ability is not a model on the path to general intelligence.
