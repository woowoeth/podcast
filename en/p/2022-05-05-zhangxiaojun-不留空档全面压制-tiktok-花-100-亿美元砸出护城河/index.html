No Gaps, Total Suppression: TikTok Spent $10 Billion to Build a Moat

TikTok's first global push was money and a code word: over several years it spent roughly $10 billion on user acquisition, content subsidies and servers. Any rival that wants to block it needs at least $20–30 billion.

4:05

Cutting the Facebook API took only a week

Between March and April 2020, TikTok added 110 million DAU in a single two-month period. During that window, Zhu Jun pulled a group of key owners into a Feishu group and asked, "What risk do we run by removing the Facebook SDK login?" He demanded the discussion be finished by 1 p.m. that day, with feedback by end of day at the latest. Every department's assessment concluded there would be losses: without the API, they couldn't get precise profiles during acquisition, hurting growth and monetization efficiency. Zhu Jun pressed on how much user experience would suffer, and the team jointly concluded: no more than 20%. He immediately ordered it cut. In under a week, TikTok pulled every last Facebook login API, a move the author describes as them "resolutely cutting off their own pacifier."

6:06

No gaps, total suppression is the highest directive

In early 2020, Zhang Yiming handed off China operations and became global CEO. His two-month OKR contained the phrase "no gaps." People close to power say this was actually a code word, the full version being "no gaps, total suppression"—these eight characters were the highest directive this taciturn, outwardly non-aggressive boss issued to TikTok. Under the eight-character guideline were two unwritten rules: wherever a competitor went, TikTok had to advance and crush them; and whenever a rival appeared ahead on any ranking, it had to be destroyed within a week, at any price. Even at a cost of a thousand enemies for eight hundred of your own, as long as you stand undefeated, no price is too high. The directive was carried out by the user growth middle platform, led by Zhao Qi, a Peking University computer science PhD and former co-CEO of the startup Che Laile, whom colleagues call the person who has spent the most money in Chinese internet history.

— Zhang Xiaojun
9:09

Iraq: zero spend, 6 million DAU

TikTok divided countries into four tiers, SABC, with strategic priority decreasing in order: S-tier was the US, Japan, the UK, India; A-tier was Germany, Brazil; B-tier had 15 to 20 countries; C-tier had 20 to 30; the remaining 160 to 170 countries got almost no paid acquisition. In the Middle East, TikTok only spent in Saudi Arabia and the UAE; surrounding countries grew by natural spillover. Iraq never spent a single cent, yet during the pandemic it surged to 6 million DAU—with Iraq's mobile internet population at about 25 million, that's a 24% DAU penetration rate and a monthly active penetration rate of 40%. Employees summed up the biggest difference between TikTok and Kuaishou's internationalization: TikTok took developed countries first, with US content supplying the globe and Douyin content supplying East and Southeast Asia, giving it enormous power. By contrast, Kuaishou used a horse-racing mechanism, adopting bizarre names in each region, with small teams taking root independently and fighting alone, leaders changing like a revolving door, and now only maintaining a presence in Brazil.

— Zhang Xiaojun
13:11

A $10 billion moat

People close to strategy say that from 2018 to 2023, TikTok had piles of cash and spent it mainly in four directions: marketing, growth and brand took about $5 billion; content building and subsidies took about $2 billion; servers and bandwidth took about $1.5–2 billion; and finally personnel costs. All told, the investment was $10 billion—equivalent to the valuation of 10 unicorns. This person believes that any platform wanting to build a rival product would need at least two or three times that in user acquisition costs, i.e. $20–30 billion. For any giant, that's a huge sum, enough to make them frown and worry for a while. Zhang Yiming dared to spend, and the money came from Zhang Lidong sitting downstairs: this "money-printing machine" monetized through Jinri Toutiao and Douyin, filling the company's coffers.

— Zhang Xiaojun
17:17

Four waves of localization, servers given only 15 days

Before the political storm knocked, ByteDance began assessing isolation risks and making contingency plans. Localization formally launched in Q3 2019 and took over a year, in four waves. The first wave was moderators: a 300-plus moderation team was cut. The second wave targeted operations staff; in many regions power shifted overnight from China to overseas, the European operations team was nearly dissolved or reassigned, and two former Music.ly members were told to hand over their work to the European team within a week. The third wave was servers. At a meeting, Zhu Wenjia, who oversaw algorithm technology, said splitting the algorithm code would take two months. Zhang Yiming couldn't wait and asked if it could be done in 15 days. The answer was yes. The fourth wave was product and technical staff, including Zhu Wenjia, as TikTok's top brass transferred to Singapore. The principle of data isolation: Chinese data stays in China, overseas data stays overseas; US data would not cross the US border even if other data centers were geographically better placed.

— Zhang Xiaojun
33:44

GMs don't know their own country's tier

Under Shou Zi Chew, reporting was dual-line: the Europe growth lead reported solid-line to Zhao Qi, head of the user growth middle platform, and dotted-line to Europe GM Rich, which gave Rich a sense of security. An analyst who studied TikTok said the essence of ByteDance's global management is "control what you can, let go of what you can't." On the other side is an information-layering mechanism: a GM who appears to hold sole authority has no right to see which SABC tier their country belongs to—especially when the tier is bottom-tier B or C, the company is even less likely to let them know how much budget was allocated to other countries, for fear of breeding resentment and creating management headaches. The author describes it as ByteDance using one hand to sweep away barriers and ensure smooth operation, while the other hand buries barriers, placing power in the hands of a very few. In the past two years, PMO has also swept ByteDance; after Zhu Wenjia arrived, he set up a PMO department and over 20 project teams. In April 2021, when Kuaishou was burning cash on overseas viral growth, TikTok used it to set up a special task force to block Kuaishou.

— Zhang Xiaojun
35:45

Shou Zi Chew changed out of his suit in month two

After May 2021, TikTok's top leader was neither the Chinese Zhang Nan or Zhu Jun, nor the Americans Mayer or Pappas, but Shou Zi Chew, a Singaporean national with a UK and US education. Business people initially worried he only understood finance, strategy and investment, and lacked business understanding, but after a while found he exceeded expectations. The most visible change: from the suited CFO style of his Xiaomi days, he immediately downgraded to a plaid shirt. One employee pinpointed the change to his second month on the job—because in the first month the company expected him to take it to the capital markets like he did at Xiaomi, and in the second month ByteDance urgently announced the IPO was cancelled and indefinitely postponed. He also quietly cleared his Twitter and Instagram, rarely commented on business details, but proposed one key focus: depoliticization, turning it into a pure entertainment product.

— Zhang Xiaojun
39:48

The moment TikTok's DAU overtook Douyin

India's ban knocked TikTok's DAU down by over 100 million, and it took more than a year to catch up. According to multiple sources, by early 2022 TikTok's DAU had exceeded 700 million and was still growing very fast, while Douyin had barely grown in the past year at about 670 million DAU. TikTok had fully overtaken it as desired, and combined they broke 1.3 billion DAU globally; at the same time its downloads surpassed the dominant Facebook to rank first among global social products. Organizationally, Zhao Qi handed all growth for mature domestic businesses to the various business units, keeping only overseas growth for himself, while also taking over the commercialization product Chuan Shan Jia, with UG becoming a second-level department. Commercialization still has a lot of room: foreign media reported TikTok's 2022 revenue could reach $11 billion, and internally there's a more aggressive target between $11 billion and $20 billion, while the biggest risk remains geopolitics.

Glossary

ByteDance's middle platform responsible for spending on user acquisition and scaling products

A role serving specific projects, managing planning and scheduling, and acting as firefighter in emergencies
