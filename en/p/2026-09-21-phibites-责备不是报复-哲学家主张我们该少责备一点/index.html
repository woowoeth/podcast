Blame Is Not Revenge: A Philosopher Argues We Should Blame Less

Miranda Fricker argues that retributive blame makes no sense, and that the most valuable blame is "communicative blame" — aimed at getting the other person to genuinely understand the moral significance of what they did, and reaching shared moral understanding.

There is no single definition of blame

Fricker points out that the philosophical literature holds a great many different views of blame: some say it is a hostile emotion, some a reactive attitude, some merely a judgment, some a rupture in a relationship, and some a form of protest. She suspects these views are not talking about the same thing at all. She holds that the object of blame can be any agent — an individual, an institution, or even a collective as large as a nation — as long as it has done something wrong, it is a fitting object of blame.

— Miranda Fricker
2:05

Retributive blame has no logic to it

Fricker defines retributive blame as: you wronged me, I am at a disadvantage, so I am entitled to even the score by making you hurt a little too — "getting even" is its most apt metaphor. She says she does not believe this logic in interpersonal morality, and does not find it useful. She admits she does not want to say anything specific about criminal punishment, but confesses she is strongly inclined to think the retributive idea there is rather distasteful. She believes genuinely useful alternatives exist.

— Miranda Fricker
3:08

Communicative blame wants shared understanding

Fricker holds that the morally most valuable model of blame is the one aimed at getting the other person to fully grasp the moral significance of their conduct. If you blame me for wronging you, perhaps I have not fully understood the moral weight of what I did; you put it into the conversation, and I either come to understand what I did or push back that it was "not that serious," and in the end the two of us reach shared moral understanding of what happened between us. She admits this is an ideal, but one worth pursuing, and calls it "communicative blame."

— Miranda Fricker
5:11

Blame is at bottom a judgment of moral wrongdoing

After the functional analysis, Fricker gives her own definition of blame: blame is a judgment of moral wrongdoing, and that judgment is in some sense activated or motivated by a disposition suited to holding someone responsible. She stresses that this is the context of moral blame. The definition is minimalist, and she thinks its advantage is that it transplants across domains — when she studies epistemic blame, for instance, she need not assume the blamer harbours hostile feelings toward the blamed, only that there is an epistemic fault and that the judgment issues from a responsibility-holding stance rather than passive observation.

— Miranda Fricker
6:11

An apology must be made to the right person

Fricker points to a key difference between apology and blame: communicative blame can be broadcast anywhere, and conveying the meaning of blame to a third party can be derived from conveying it to the person concerned — both promote shared moral understanding; but apologising to someone you did not wrong is not an apology at all. Apology is essentially second-personal moral address, and must be done to the right person. She also notes that apology's reparative power is tied to an ongoing relationship — if a stranger wrongs you at random, there is no need to chase an apology, and we all have to learn to let things go.

— Miranda Fricker
9:12

Sincerity is not all-or-nothing

Fricker rejects treating sincerity as all-or-nothing. She holds that in most apology practices a person need only be "sincere enough" to count as apologising, and sincere enough in the right way to be accepted by the particular person being apologised to. She cites the philosopher Jeffrey Helmich's notion of the "apology stance": an apology is a speech act that not only expresses but "enacts" the appropriate sorry inner state. She adds that an overly florid apology is merely irritating — apology is a social performance, and must fall within the bandwidth of aptness to be accepted.

— Miranda Fricker
12:15

Responsibility is a social construction, not metaphysics

Faced with the minority view that genes and environment determine everything and no one is ever responsible, Fricker argues that this line of thought locates the threshold of responsibility in a metaphysical rather than a natural place. She understands responsibility as something that grows out of the practice of human beings living together, for the sake of social coordination and harmony, constructing a shared moral consciousness. She borrows Peter Strawson's approach: whatever the metaphysics, in everyday life we simply cannot help praising and blaming. She holds that separating metaphysics from the practice of responsibility is itself a way of leading people astray.

— Miranda Fricker
19:20

Blaming less is a trainable choice

Fricker says that if blame is a judgment of fault, and the judgment issues from a responsibility-holding moral stance, then we can choose how much human behaviour to bring into this "fault-finding" construction. She gives the example of lateness: some people are highly moralistic about others being late, others do not feel offended at all. She confesses she prefers a world in which everyone is a bit more relaxed about lateness of up to 15 minutes, where it need not be constructed as a matter of respect — it is just "you were a little late," and that is all.

— Miranda Fricker
21:21

Abandoning blame breeds something more toxic

Fricker says she cares about both blame and forgiveness, and that her real focus is moral address, with apology the middle term between the two. She thinks we blame too much, and that the wrong ways of blaming really are toxic; but she disagrees with those who think blame is necessarily toxic and should be abolished altogether. She thinks it matters for everyone's moral development to be able to ask plainly, "What did you do? You treated me badly that day." She adds that the fear of blame being toxic leads to something more toxic — passive aggression, dishonesty, gaslighting blame.

— Miranda Fricker

Glossary

communicative blame
Fricker's proposed model of blame, aimed at getting the other person to grasp the moral significance of their conduct and reach shared moral understanding.
reactive attitude
Strawson's concept for the attitudes such as praise and blame that we naturally have in interpersonal interaction.
apology stance
Jeffrey Helmich's concept: an apology not only expresses but "enacts" the appropriate sorry inner state.
epistemic blame
Blame that holds someone responsible in the epistemic rather than the moral domain, such as blaming someone for sloppy calculation.
