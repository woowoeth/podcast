AI Is Not a Bubble, It's a War Where Giants Would Burn Their Last Dollar Rather Than Leave the Table

The so-called AI bubble is an accounting exercise based on commercial returns; the giants treat it as a new kind of national defense or nuclear weapon, and what they're calculating is the risk of being disrupted — so they'd rather burn their last dollar than leave the table.

5:04

Not a bubble, but an AI war you can't afford to lose

The trigger was Sam's $1.4 trillion compute commitment in October: 30 gigawatts, GPUs depreciated over six years, which means OpenAI would have to burn an average of over $200 billion a year, while its public forecast for 2030 is only over $200 billion in annual revenue and a cumulative $500-600 billion over the next five years — from a cash flow perspective, the math doesn't work in the short term. But Guangmi says OpenAI's cash flow will be fine for the next two to three years, and it recently raised another $100 billion at a valuation of over $800 billion. He argues this isn't a valuation game but an arms race: developer choices have shifted from "AWS or Azure" to "which model," and cloud providers are being white-labeled; once agents become the entry point, Super Apps and information intermediaries like Uber and Ctrip will be short-circuited — take an Uber in the Bay Area, and the platform can take 50%. Those who can't afford to lose won't leave the table.

— Guang Mi
8:06

You can see $200 billion clearly, but not ten trillion

What's clear are three pieces: subscriptions — assuming 4 billion monthly active users, 2.5 billion weekly, and 1.5 billion daily by 2030, a 10% paid conversion rate means 400 million users, each paying $200 a year, about $80 billion; advertising and e-commerce — over 2 billion weekly active users, $20 to $50 per user, about $50 to $100 billion; API — a rough guess of $50 to $100 billion. That adds up to two to three hundred billion dollars a year, and Guangmi's assessment is "just another internet platform, nothing that interesting." The unclear part is the key: if agents replace labor end-to-end, with 1 billion white-collar workers globally and each agent white-collar worker costing $1,000 to $10,000, that's $1 to $10 trillion in revenue. The reference point is the software development market at $150 billion, while AI coding this year is only over $10 billion.

— Guang Mi
15:10

Nvidia is Android, Google is Apple

This war is split into two camps: Nvidia's GPUs and Google's TPUs. For the first time, Google has integrated models, chips, cloud, and products end-to-end, and Guangmi says it's more like the Apple of the AI era; Nvidia stands in the position of Android in the AI era, with OpenAI and Anthropic below it, and those two are somewhat better in talent density. He thinks the Nvidia-OpenAI line is currently undervalued: GPUs are overall better than TPUs, the downside is cost, but TSMC's capacity is limited, and the Ruby generation of cards should be a generation ahead of TPU V8, and Nvidia has already secured TSMC's 1.6-nanometer capacity in advance. Another trend judgment: the stronger Google gets, the more an anti-Google alliance will form; the stronger OpenAI gets, the more an anti-OpenAI alliance will form — OpenAI was founded precisely to challenge Google.

— Guang Mi
25:17

Do you want to be vertical e-commerce, or Xiaohongshu?

Guangmi's stolen analogy is: foundation model companies are very much like the general e-commerce companies of a decade ago, scaling SKUs equals scaling data — add data for whatever capability you want to do well, and it's more efficient. Like Amazon in its early days, which sold books to pull together logistics, warehousing, and users, then expanded horizontally into categories, today's model companies are doing PPT, Excel, data analysis, and investment research. The conclusion is that foundation models still capture most of the value in the value chain. Applied to startups, it's his question: are you doing vertical e-commerce, or Xiaohongshu? Vertical e-commerce can also make money, but you need to retreat flexibly; historically, vertical e-commerce has never produced a very large platform company; Xiaohongshu built a layer of content assets on top of a general e-commerce platform. Whether Perplexity and Cursor belong to one or the other, there's no answer yet.

— Guang Mi
40:29

Robots and world models may all be fake problems

This is a judgment Guangmi himself labels as a hot take: robots, world models, and even multimodality may largely be fake problems, and Online Learning may be the only truly important real problem — only when it breaks through will other problems be fundamentally solved, because the core is generalization; without generalization, you're stuck on the old autonomous driving path, where the amount of intelligence equals the amount of manual labor. The implication is heavy: future breakthroughs in robots and world models may not necessarily come from the people working on them today, and today's crowd might end up like the previous generation of NLP. The metaphor for the three paradigms is: pre-training data is like oil, fossil fuels, huge in quantity but limited, already 70-80% used; RL's expert data is like new energy, useful but small in total, hence the emergence of outsourcing companies that specifically recruit human experts; Online Learning is like nuclear fusion, not yet broken through, but if it is, it's invincible.

— Guang Mi
50:39

The agentic web is the real web3

What he most wants to see in 2026 is the next nuclear-level paradigm breakthrough, such as Online Learning, because that will bring more proactive agents and change product forms. Another inspiration comes from the Doubao phone, which he calls the agentic web: this is the real web3, the first time giving startups and new companies a chance to flip the table, which was very difficult in previous years. The logic is traffic short-circuiting — once agents become the traffic entry point, today's Super Apps, Google Search, phone manufacturers, and information intermediaries like Uber and Ctrip will all be affected; the essence is grabbing the power to allocate traffic. He also sees 2026 as a big year for multimodality, with the biggest opportunities for knowledge workers, especially in coding, and phone manufacturers will be an important theme next year, because there may be more than one entry point.

— Guang Mi
1:04:55

The more top-tier the company, the cheaper it is, the less bubble

Counting ARR from top to bottom: OpenAI $20-21 billion, Anthropic $9-10 billion (of which Claude Code has publicly exceeded $1 billion), Cursor over $1 billion, the two data labeling companies Surge AI and Mercor both over $1 billion, Midjourney $700-800 million, serving company Together AI $300 million; after that, basically all are less than $300 million — enterprise search Glean, web coding Replit $200-300 million, voice Eleven Labs nearly $300 million, front-end Lovable over $200 million, medical ChatGPT Open Evidence $150 million, Sierra and Harvey between $150-200 million. His conclusion is that the more top-tier the company, the cheaper it is, the more top-tier the company, the less bubble; if there is a bubble, it may still be in some of the companies further down.

— Guang Mi
1:12:06

Don't package yourself as an American company

Three pieces of advice for Chinese entrepreneurs: First, firmly target the global market, especially the high-paying US market — the US can contribute 60% of revenue and 70% of profit; Second, make good use of China's talent and engineer dividend, don't give up China's industrial advantages, and don't awkwardly package yourself as an American or Singaporean company; the team is 100% Chinese, and making a good product is the king's way. Third, getting money from top-tier Silicon Valley VCs is actually not easy, trust chains are hard to build, so take whatever money you can get early on. He also mentions a shortcoming: in the past three or four years, there have been over a hundred AI application companies that grew into unicorns globally, but he can only think of three to five Chinese teams, a ratio far lower than in the internet era.

— Guang Mi

Glossary

Online Learning
A model continuously learns and updates itself during inference and interaction, no longer relying solely on static pre-training.
Agentic Web
The network form after agents become the traffic entry point, which Guangmi calls the real web3.
Neo Labs
A new batch of small frontier labs in Silicon Valley founded by top researchers who left big companies, such as SSI and Thinking Machines.
Credit Assignment
In reinforcement learning, determining how much each action contributes to the final outcome.
