He Unlocked the Chains of the Mentally Ill, Then Forged Psychological Ones

Pinel cut Bicêtre's annual mortality rate from 50% to under 13% in a year, mostly through cleanliness; but he denied organic brain causes, and Foucault accused him of replacing iron chains with psychological ones.

No license is what forced the case-file system into existence

The medical degree Pinel earned in Toulouse only allowed him to practice in Toulouse, so once he reached Montpellier and then Paris he could not practice directly. During his four years in Montpellier he instead visited hospitals repeatedly, talked with patients at length, and took copious notes, organizing the information into detailed records. Because he followed the same group of patients over a long period, he could observe how illness evolved and thereby identify patterns in its course. This ‘observe—record—follow’ method was later carried to Bicêtre, where it became the prototype for building a file on every patient, recording age, temperature, diagnosis, and even comparing patient temperatures against the building's temperature.

8:19

Classifying the mentally ill with plant taxonomy

The seventh ward at Bicêtre held 200 men judged to be mentally deranged, many chained in extremely small rooms, the chain long enough only to let them shuffle around inside. Pinel communicated closely with the director, Jean-Baptiste Poussin, gathered as much patient information as possible, organized it into tables, and transferred his knowledge of plant classification over, trying to build a finer diagnostic taxonomy rather than simply sorting people into ‘mad.’ He did not call patients ‘mad’ or ‘mentally ill’ but said they were in a state of ‘mental alienation’— estranged from their own reason.

9:21

Mortality fell from 50% to under 13%

Pinel put a concrete set of practices in place at Bicêtre: moving patients out of prison-style confinement into rooms with as much natural light as possible; no longer using chaining as a routine measure; establishing hygiene standards for the institution; having patients take hot baths regularly; and providing what today would be called occupational therapy to help patients overcome the obstacles of daily life. Within a year, the institution's annual patient mortality rate fell from 50% to under 13%. The show states plainly that this was largely just a matter of keeping patients and their environment clean—where disease spreads, without cleanliness there is no controlling it. In addition, a considerable share of patients were able to return to life outside the institution, whereas at the time the expectation for most people entering an asylum was to die inside it.

— Holly Frey
19:43

He measured skulls to prove phrenology was garbage

Pinel recorded age, temperature and diagnosis for every patient, and also measured skulls. This was not because he believed in phrenology—quite the opposite: phrenology was just taking shape during his main working period, built on the craniology that originated in Austria in the 1790s, which held that the shape of a head revealed a person's tendency to violence or madness. Pinel considered the whole thing useless, and he collected skull measurements precisely to falsify it. He later wrote that the data seemed to confirm his earlier view: strong mental emotions are the most common cause of mental derangement, and the skulls of the insane have no special formation that cannot be found in any other skull picked at random.

— Tracy V. Wilson
21:46

He did not unlock everyone's chains

The image of ‘Pinel breaking all his patients' chains and turning prisoners into patients’ needs discounting. A famous late-19th-century painting by Tony Robert-Fleury depicts him removing chains, and it shows the women's institution. But he did not release every patient bound in irons: where he judged that loosening them would create danger, patients remained confined and tethered. In the paper he read in 1794 he gave several cases of men he called ‘agitated and violent,’ one of whom had been chained for more than forty years. He described their behavior in detail to explain why irons were needed, and wrote that one of the basic principles for dealing with the mentally deranged is ‘a judicious mixture of affability and firmness,’ avoiding unnecessary coercion and using only as much force as suffices to restrain them.

— Holly Frey
27:53

He denied brain pathology, and that is a real flaw

One long-standing criticism of Pinel's work is that he ignored the physiological causes of mental health problems. His writing discusses at length how life events worsen mental conditions, yet never considers bodily causes—which is strange, because writing on the physiological causes of mental illness goes back to Hippocrates and Galen, and in Pinel's own time Lavoisier, along with his university colleague Michel-Augustin Theret, were already discussing brain chemistry. In 1801 Pinel explicitly argued against bodily causes of madness, writing that ‘the derangement of the understanding is generally considered as the result of an organic lesion of the brain, consequently as incurable; a supposition that is, in a great number of instances, contrary to anatomical fact.’ The show says directly: he was completely wrong on this point.

— Tracy V. Wilson
30:56

The ‘Citizen, I am convinced’ story may be invented

In 1849 Chambers' Edinburgh Journal published a story about Pinel, sourced to Pinel's son Scipion Pinel—who also became a psychiatrist. The story goes that during the Terror, a Paris Commune official, Georges Coudon, came to Bicêtre hunting ‘treasonous men feigning madness,’ and after seeing the chains, the filth and the mad howling with his own eyes, asked Pinel whether it was he who had gone mad to want to unchain these people. Pinel is said to have replied: ‘Citizen, I am convinced that these madmen became so unmanageable only because they were deprived of air and liberty.’ The show says this sounds very cool and clever, but whether the story is true we do not know—it may be family legend, or an editor's invention. It certainly added bricks to Pinel's 19th-century legend.

— Holly Frey
32:59

Foucault: the iron chains were swapped for psychological ones

Pinel's writing and work have been argued over almost continuously for nearly two hundred years. Supporters point out how progressive his work was and how many of his ideas still matter today. One particularly forceful critic was the 20th-century philosopher Michel Foucault. Foucault argued that Pinel may have removed the physical shackles from patients, but only replaced them with psychological shackles, describing his work as a form of ‘moral imprisonment’ in which patients were manipulated in more subtle ways during treatment. By Foucault's logic, in becoming obedient to the doctor, patients became estranged from themselves. The show also notes that this line of argument in Foucault has problems of its own.

Glossary

mental alienation
Pinel's term for the state in which a patient is estranged from their own reason, avoiding the word ‘mad.’
moral treatment
Pinel's therapeutic system; a literal translation, but it actually refers to care at the psychological level, with no moral judgment implied.
nosography
The study of classifying diseases and symptoms, which Pinel used to write his textbook.
phrenology
The pseudoscience holding that character and mental tendencies can be read from skull shape; Pinel opposed it.
Thermidorian Reaction
The events of summer 1794 that ended Robespierre's Terror and Jacobin rule.
