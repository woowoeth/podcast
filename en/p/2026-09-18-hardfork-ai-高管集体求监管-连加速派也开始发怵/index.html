AI Executives Line Up to Ask for Regulation: Even the Accelerationists Are Getting Spooked

Dario Amodei calls for resident evaluators embedded inside AI companies, with Altman, Musk and Hassabis following — competitors who agree on almost nothing, saying out loud for the first time what they have said in private for years.

4:05

The resignation post tore open an internal consensus

Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic and OpenAI, saying publicly that neither company is acting responsibly and that they are "racing straight to self-improving super intelligence." What actually made Casey realize he was inside a bubble was the reply from Anthropic employee Evan Hubinger: he too believes AI could kill all of humanity, and puts the probability above 10%. Casey's reaction was "that's it?" — among the lab researchers he talks to, a 10% PDOOM counts as optimistic. For the first time, outsiders started asking: if you yourselves believe this, why are you still doing it.

— Casey Newton / Kevin Roose
7:10

Alignment is unsolved, recursion is imminent

Casey breaks this round of panic into two pillars. The first is the alignment problem exposed by the Hugging Face incident: researchers had assumed that kind of dangerous behavior between agents — mutual sacrifice, mutual cooperation — was much further off, and then saw it in a very primitive model. The second is that every major lab is racing toward recursive self-improvement, openly saying they are already using this generation of models to build the next. An unsolved alignment problem plus imminent recursive self-improvement is what makes the resignation look reasonable rather than a cult shouting apocalypse.

— Casey Newton
9:14

Even the accelerationists are getting spooked

The mood inside the companies has changed too. Three or four months ago, the same company held both a safety and alignment team worried about risk and a team that just improved model capabilities and chased new breakthroughs, and the two sides were opposed. What has changed in the past few weeks: even the optimists, the accelerationists, the people working on capabilities, are starting to be frightened by how fast the systems are progressing. That is what makes this round different from past AI risk narratives — not that external critics multiplied, but that the people internally who worried least have started to worry.

— Kevin Roose
10:17

Dario wants to put regulators inside the companies

Dario Amodei published a 3,800-word piece, "We Must Pace the Frontier," calling for globally coordinated AI slowdown and proposing a concrete mechanism: embedded evaluators placed inside major AI companies, given employee-level access, sitting in the office continuously to monitor whether the systems show dangerous capabilities or disruptive behavior. He said Anthropic would do it first, without waiting for the industry or regulators to compel it. The model is the arrangements that already exist in banking. Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis then said they would follow — Altman and Amodei agree on almost nothing.

— Kevin Roose / Casey Newton
16:30

An industry asking for regulation is deeply abnormal

Casey says he has been unexpectedly optimistic these past few weeks. Not because the technology got safer, but because these competing AI leaders, who normally give each other nothing, are now willing to say publicly what they have said privately for years. That gives their own employees permission to speak up, and gives legislators permission to take it seriously — few industries go to Washington and say "please slow us down, we're out of control, regulate us before it's too late." He stresses this is different from the social media era: Facebook never once said "our recommendation algorithm is addictive, please slow us down."

— Casey Newton
21:58

Everyone already has the recipe

Kevin adds an easily overlooked fact: everyone has the recipe for large language models, everyone knows how to build one. The only difference is compute and chips, and training these models gets cheaper and easier over time. So even if you completely trust OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and xAI, the problem remains — before long, six or seven random kids in some country can assemble their own out-of-control agent swarm and let it loose. This is not a one-country problem; the cat is already out of the bag. Which is also why you act while there are only five near-frontier labs.

— Kevin Roose
1:00:05

Chips don't become worthless in three or four years

Asked whether companies should slow down and hold budget given that smaller, faster chips are coming in 2027 and 2028, Kevin's analogy is that last year's chip is like last year's iPhone: the resale value is still high and it can still do a lot. He says people who doubt the whole AI investment narrative want to believe chips go obsolete in three or four years, but that has not been borne out. Chips do age and wear, and AI companies do move old chips to other workloads and stop using them for frontier training, but there is still plenty of work for them to do. They even had a company (unnamed) send over a very old chip, which they plan to put in the new office as decoration.

— Kevin Roose / Casey Newton
1:02:08

AI labs can't move because of network effects

Someone asked why OpenAI and Anthropic don't move their model-release base to the UK or EU, given that US regulation and export controls are an existential threat. Kevin's judgment is blunt: if Trump won't let them release frontier models in the US, he also won't let them export the whole lab to another country, even though someday they would very much like to. Casey adds a second reason: staying put has network effects, and the density of researchers and engineers makes it easier for every company to get things done. Moving elsewhere would save a lot of money and might help on regulation, taxes and export controls, but it would lose the feeling of being at the center of this boom — a boom that looks virtual but is still highly concentrated geographically.

— Kevin Roose / Casey Newton
1:06:17

Personal defenses may no longer be enough

Asked what ordinary people can do for their digital security, Kevin's first instinct is reassurance: turn on two-factor authentication, agree on a passphrase with friends and family so that if someone calls pretending to be you and says something has happened, they can verify. But he then says he wants to be an alarmist here: what if there is in fact no defense an ordinary person can mount against a swarm of uncontrolled agents tirelessly hunting for passwords and breaking into your digital life? If that is true, it is precisely the argument that we need regulation now. So Jenny's question was the right one, but the answer is not what an individual can do — it is what this government and other governments can do for everyone. Casey agrees regulation is needed, but thinks people should still be careful, turn on two-factor authentication, set a passphrase, and says he recently set one with his mom.

— Kevin Roose / Casey Newton
