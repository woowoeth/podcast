New Jersey Governor: Republicans Trapped in a Shrinking X Bubble

X's users and revenue are both declining, yet they still create an illusion among Republican politicians that ‘everyone thinks this way’—an illusion shattered by reality in the midterm elections.

4:03

Campaigns ask voters first, then set positions

Sherrill said her campaign started the day Trump won in November 2024. New Jersey elections depend heavily on retail, face-to-face voter contact—meeting thousands of voters per day. Through this frequent contact, her team confirmed that ‘affordability’and ‘accountability’are what actually drive voters, not positions the team had decided in advance. She emphasizes that in 2024, this wasn't the obvious answer; they determined it through extensive frontline feedback.

— Sherrill
16:45

X's shrinking bubble convinced Republicans they had mainstream support

Neil argues Elon's Twitter acquisition failed on all dimensions—users declining, revenue down, and political influence shrinking. Sherrill echoes using a new term: ‘astroturfed’social media noise is often artificial turf, not real grassroots. If candidates judge opinion purely through X, they easily misread where real voters actually stand. She notes that reporters shortcut to X too, causing serious distortion of reality.

— Sherrill
22:58

State port authority let the governor block Russian energy

The Trump administration planned to allow Russian diesel into US ports. Sherrill sent notice to all New Jersey ports and businesses citing the law Congress had passed, noting the five-year lookback period means ‘fail to enforce now, can't escape later.’Ships already started leaving. She frames this as state power reactivating after Trump concentrated federal authority.

— Sherrill
36:23

Data centers must prove they won't harm communities first

Sherrill says New Jersey set multiple requirements for data centers: water transparency, self-powered, don't raise electricity rates, and community benefits agreements where part of revenue must truly stay local and employ local workers. She admits that moratoriums partly work by forcing data center companies to develop a ‘business model you can sell to communities’rather than simply extract all returns to Silicon Valley.

— Sherrill
50:05

Markets punish companies that voluntarily restrain themselves

Sherrill predicts YouTube will reject Meta's settlement terms and need litigation, because nobody volunteers self-restraint. Example: Facebook tried algorithm improvements and immediately lost market share to competitors. Therefore Dario Amodei's public calls for AI regulation without unilateral self-restraint probably reflects that unilateral concessions just shift market share to others without solving the problem. This requires government regulation, not corporate self-discipline.

— Sherrill
54:18

Markets reward engagement over safety in social networks

Neil asks: if Zuckerberg says markets reward safe AI products, why haven't safer social media versions succeeded in the market? Sherrill's assessment: the real traffic driver is ‘eye-on-screen time,’not safety. Kids placed on kid-friendly apps quickly escape to other platforms anyway. So you can't rely on ‘be a good parent’to fight this structural problem.

— Sherrill
58:26

New Jersey's research center is built to win future tech litigation

Sherrill explains that New Jersey established the country's first relevant research center partly to advise parents and teachers, but crucially because social media companies shut down their own internal research once the data looks bad, leaving state government without evidence for lawsuits—they've lost many cases. She positions this research center as the data foundation to survive ‘strict scrutiny’review under First Amendment analysis in future suits.

— Sherrill
1:07:46

AI turned license plate readers into real-time mass surveillance

Neil notes that license plate readers themselves are not new, but once all plate-reader data feed into a unified AI system for analysis, you can track any person's movements in real-time—past surveillance couldn't do this. Sherrill admits this is what unsettles her: before, the legal principle ‘you have no privacy expectation in public places’was acceptable, but once AI connects scattered records into a permanently-available tracking network, this principle needs re-examination.

— Sherrill
