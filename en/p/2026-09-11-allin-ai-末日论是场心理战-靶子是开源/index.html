The AI Doom Narrative Is a Psyop, and the Target Is Open Source

Anthropic asks public markets to underwrite it at a trillion-dollar valuation while its safety lead endorses the claim that AI may end civilization — not an internal contradiction, but regulatory groundwork aimed ultimately at open source.

0:00

The resignation letter wasn't spontaneous — it was organized PR

Jacob Coxon quit Anthropic after only six weeks and posted that the people building AI genuinely believe it could kill everyone by the end of this decade. Sachs points out this was not spontaneous: the account had almost zero followers and zero posts before, yet got hundreds of millions of views in a day; within the first 15 minutes three Doomer organizations amplified it, all funded by the same big EA donor, who also co-led Anthropic's Series A. More tellingly, the Wall Street Journal story went out minutes before the tweetstorm — meaning the reporter had been briefed under embargo. Sachs says if Coxon really came on the show, the first question would be: who introduced you to the Wall Street Journal?

6:12

The company didn't distance itself — it amplified him

A normal company facing a whistleblower immediately pushes back and distances itself. Anthropic did the opposite: Evan Hubinger, who runs alignment, publicly replied that Jacob is right, that they genuinely believe AI could kill all of humanity, and that he personally puts the probability above 10% over the next decade. Sachs argues this exposes the contradiction — what he said matches the company's internal Doomer agenda exactly, which is why he got internal support. Chamath adds the knife: the company can't distance itself, because the moment it does, that whole internal bloc blows up.

— David Sachs / Chamath
10:18

Asking for a trillion-dollar valuation while saying the product could end civilization

Sachs lays out Anthropic's IPO dilemma clearly: you are asking public-market investors to underwrite your company at a trillion-dollar valuation while your safety lead says the core product is unsolved and could end civilization. That is at minimum the seedbed of product-liability litigation. Chamath uses the Philip Morris analogy: we know cigarettes kill you, we'll say so, but we won't disclose it, we'll still go public and let you buy the stock, and we'll figure it out later. Sachs calls it "a time for choosing" — either publicly deny that Coxon is a Doomer operation, or admit Bernie Sanders is right and development should be frozen, in which case how do you IPO at a trillion-dollar valuation?

— David Sachs / Chamath
22:45

The real target of regulation is open source

Sachs is blunt: this is a Doomer psyop, and the end goal is open source. Because FDA-style regulation won't say "ban open source" — it will say "the same standards apply to open and closed models" — and Dario has already testified in the Senate that models are dangerous if they can't be centrally monitored and rolled back. Once open-model weights are released they can't be rolled back, so the standard landing is equivalent to banning the release, use and hosting of those weights. Freeberg adds: open source has no corporate structure to file regulatory paperwork, and open source is precisely what cut AI costs by 50x and benefited everyone. Chamath's version: once open source is banned, you get a monopoly in partnership with the federal government.

— David Sachs / Friedberg / Chamath
39:17

AI can't even nail autocomplete yet

The core anti-Doomer argument isn't theoretical speculation but everyday experience: autocomplete still isn't good, and AI can't even handle a chore like "bring the toiletry bag upstairs." If it can't finish one mundane task, why believe it can kill all of humanity? They call this humanity's insurance against AI taking over the world — company decisions still require humans to wake up, drink coffee and sit in the same room, and there is a natural 16-hour window between decisions.

What's truly damning is that retweet endorsement

From a legal standpoint, the key isn't Jacob Coxon's resignation tweet but Evan Hubinger's endorsement retweet — he is still a senior alignment executive at the company, running one of the safety teams, and his co-sign plus the "greater than 10% probability" figure is what made it go viral. If it were only Coxon, the company could call him a disgruntled employee; but an executive endorsement turns "was disclosure adequate" into a real question.

— David Sachs
1:00:12

OpenAI solved the math problem with brute force, not divine inspiration

The Navier-Stokes solution used roughly 130 billion output tokens, with 10,000 agents communicating with each other, equivalent to between 50,000 and 500,000 years of human work (estimated at 40 words per minute of typing). AI had no flash of insight humans couldn't have; it just did a huge amount of brute-force work, and every step of the message chain is recorded and human-readable. The conclusion is that AI is a leverage tool, not a math god above humans.

— Chamath
1:15:40

De-identification doesn't remove the method itself

De-identification can strip personal information and company-specific information, but the general method for solving a math problem is itself IP. A user iterates on that method inside the tool, and that conversation can be de-identified and used for training, making the model better at math. This is the network effect of closed models: they can see what the whole world is doing, and this observation system feeds them more data than anyone else, constituting a long-term advantage over customers.

— Sacks / Chamath
1:26:53

It's not that the ads got worse — the shoes fall apart in six weeks

Sacks says all his shoes used to be Nike, then he stopped buying them, and the reason wasn't advertising or the ordering experience but the product itself getting worse — shoes fall apart in about six weeks, thin and not durable. He walked into REI, tried on a pair of Brooks and switched. Chamath says he stuck with Nike a long time, buying a pair every six months, then it became every six weeks, and he finally switched to On because of knee problems, for a straightforward reason: Roger Federer represents mastery and excellence. Brooks is owned by Berkshire Hathaway, has posted nine consecutive years of double-digit growth, and has $1.6 billion in revenue; its CEO says Buffett gives just one instruction a year: make the product better than it was at the end of last year.

Glossary

Doomer
The camp that believes AI will most likely cause human extinction, often overlapping with effective altruism (EA) funding networks.
RSI / recursive self-improvement
The loop in which AI helps improve the next generation of AI; a mild version is already happening, while the extreme version means AI fully autonomously designs and launches the next training run.
ZDR / zero data retention
A clause in which a model provider promises not to retain user data, but it is only a commercial best effort and cannot be guaranteed.
S-1
The prospectus a US company files with the SEC before an IPO, which must accurately present both opportunity and risk.
DTC / direct-to-consumer
A strategy of selling straight to end users while bypassing retailers; Nike pushed it aggressively and alienated retail partners.
