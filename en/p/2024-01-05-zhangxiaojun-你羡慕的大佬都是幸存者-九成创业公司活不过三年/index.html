The Tycoons You Envy Are Survivors: 90% of Startups Don't Live Past Three Years

Xiao Lin says the common trait of big shots is "guts" — the willingness to take on risk. But that's the survivor's view: 90% of startups don't live past three years, and the risks taken by those who made it out are hidden by the spotlight.

5:04

Before data drops, the trading floor goes dead silent

She says that when she was in school she couldn't understand financial news at all, and hearing "GDP is up" she had no idea what it had to do with her; only after starting work did she feel "the real connection between data and your job." The investment bank's trading floor is normally very loud — you have to shout to be heard a few rows away — but before US employment data is released, the whole floor really does go dead silent, everyone staring at screens; the moment the data comes out, client calls start coming in. What she wants to convey through her videos and book is exactly this connection: behind macroeconomic data there are stories related to life.

— Xiao Lin
13:07

You can make VP and then stay VP forever

Promotion at an investment bank follows a pyramid ratio: you come in as an analyst, after about three years you move up to associate, after another three years to VP. VP is basically guaranteed — as long as you're not too bad and work hard, you'll get there. But above that, director is where elimination starts; if you never get promoted, "you might be a VP for the rest of your life." Above that is managing director, a very managerial level. Her own summary of the rules is two things: first, you have to have ability and rely on it; second, on that basis, read the situation clearly and understand current events.

— Xiao Lin
15:11

What made her dare to quit was a fallback

Before quitting to start her own business she agonized for about half a year, working her job while doing a side hustle. The turning point was her mentor — a senior alumna with a BlackRock background who had also quit to start her own business — who told her, "Just go out and do it. Worst case you do it for a year or two, and if it doesn't work out you go back and find a job." She says she's actually quite conservative and risk-averse, and wants a floor plan; only when she knows there's a fallback does she dare to move forward. Asked when she felt she no longer needed a fallback, she answered 2019, after the product was on track in all respects.

— Xiao Lin
16:12

A platform taking 20% commission couldn't survive

She started at the end of 2017 and formally quit in mid-2018. At first she wanted to build a consulting platform: she sent cold emails to many people on LinkedIn looking for mentors, and being rejected and questioned was routine; when someone replied in the middle of the night she'd pick up the phone and pitch like a salesperson. In the end she onboarded one or two hundred teachers, but hit a big obstacle when promoting it — people didn't buy into paying for consulting. The platform took a 20% commission, far from enough to sustain the business, and after about half a year she pivoted to education consulting services. It was only when looking for investors that she understood what they cared about was market size.

— Xiao Lin
26:19

Hot topic plus story plus substance equals a hit

Her hit formula has only three things: "hot topic plus story plus substance equals a hit." First borrow a hot topic, and ideally the hot topic is itself a story — people have been learning lessons through stories since childhood, and accept and remember them more easily than frameworks and logic; then add substance so people feel they've gained something from listening, which makes it easy to spread. The accompanying topic-selection process is to first research heavily, judge whether there's a knowledge framework that can be extracted and whether it would be interesting to tell, then discuss it together into an outline, then refine it into a script, shoot, edit, add music, and publish.

— Xiao Lin
27:19

The big shots you see are all survivors

She sums up the common traits of big shots with two words: "guts" and "substance." "Guts" because being able to reach that position means they took on the corresponding risk, but she stresses this isn't necessarily something everyone should pursue: what we see are the most brilliant, the ones who made it out, and "maybe 90% of startups don't live past three years" — many who took those risks died. "Substance" means being down-to-earth; people who truly make it have a lot of things at the cognitive level from the ground up, and the approach of just raising money definitely won't work.

— Xiao Lin
37:26

College is when the cost of trying is lowest

She says college is a particularly good period for trying things, because after starting work suddenly switching careers wastes a lot of time — three years have already passed, and in another industry you have to start from scratch, whereas in college you don't have that time cost. The threshold for finding an internship is really about being thick-skinned: when she was looking for her first internship in the US she directly said she didn't need pay and asked if she could help with work, and someone actually accepted; once you have that first stepping stone you can snowball, starting with very small companies and then applying a little higher. The hardest part is how to get that initial entry point, so you should do more internships and reach out more.

— Xiao Lin
41:28

To analyze a big economy, start from the timeline

When she gets a big topic like a country, her entry point is the angle of history and development: giving you a Turkey or a US directly is too complex, but from its process from zero to one you can find some points or patterns worth telling, and then find key nodes in the timeline for extended analysis. She says researching companies is the same: look at how it grew step by step, why it pivoted at key nodes, what the market environment was at the time, and gradually you can see clearly why it became what it is now. She also mentions that the hardest part is finding "a line" through a pile of information and building a framework, which is more useful than memorizing details.

— Xiao Lin
