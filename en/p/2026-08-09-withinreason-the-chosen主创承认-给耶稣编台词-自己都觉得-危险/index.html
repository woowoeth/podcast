The Chosen's creator confesses: putting words in Jesus' mouth feels ‘dangerous’ to him

His Jesus says ‘I am the Law of Moses’—Scripture never contains this line. He admits this is theological stance-taking, not storytelling.

5:46

He draws the line: adapting is not extending Scripture

Against accusations of ‘adding to Scripture,’ Dallas defends: the Bible itself hasn't changed a word. Open any Bible in a bookstore—you won't find Rhema or Atticus, or the Jesus dialogue he wrote. His argument: if viewers can tell canonical from imagined, adding scenes isn't corrupting Scripture. Hence ‘we are not Scripture.’

— Dallas Jenkins
26:34

Gospel sources conflict—so he chooses which to film

Jesus cleansing the temple: the synoptics place it at the end of his ministry; John places it at the beginning. Some scholars think it happened twice, but Dallas says staging it twice would dilute the impact, so one version had to be chosen. That choice isn't passive retelling—it's an editor making a judgment inside Scripture's own contradictions.

— Dallas Jenkins
38:06

His Jesus actor rejected a line on theological grounds

Jonathan Rumi, a devout Catholic, plays Jesus. During season 6's crucifixion scenes, he said for the first time ‘I've never been this uncomfortable’—not because the words violated doctrine, but because the script gave suffering Jesus some light, joking moments. Jonathan reasoned that the agony itself is theology; a lighter tone would undermine it. They negotiated for hours. Some lines were cut.

— Dallas Jenkins
42:06

He deliberately leaves the question of Jesus' siblings open

Catholic doctrine holds Mary perpetually virgin—Jesus had no biological siblings. Protestants generally accept that Scripture's ‘Jesus' brothers’ were born to Mary. Dallas personally leans this way, but admits the topic is too sensitive. If he'd filmed Jonathan's Jesus meeting a biological brother, it would likely cross the line for his Catholic actor. So the show names the brothers but doesn't develop the plot, leaving interpretation to the viewer.

— Dallas Jenkins
50:38

One line he admits writing was dangerous—theologically

In season 3, Jesus tells his accusers ‘I am the Law of Moses’—nowhere in Scripture. Dallas admits this isn't casual improvisation but a high-stakes theological claim that would certainly have been recorded if Jesus said it. His defense: Jesus is God and fulfills Mosaic law, so theologically it stands. But he also acknowledges putting words in Jesus' mouth—a theological assertion Jesus never made—which he calls dangerous.

— Dallas Jenkins
58:02

For a fictional character, he invented a theodicy

An actor with serious spinal deformity was cast as ‘Little James.’ This created a Scripture problem: Jesus heals through disciples but refuses to heal him. Jesus asks ‘Do you want healing?’ He says yes; Jesus says ‘No’—then gives a full theodicy using Job to explain suffering's meaning. The gospels never show Jesus articulating why suffering matters.

— Alex O'Connor
1:19:17

Judas wasn't just corrupted by greed, he argues

The gospels give scattered accounts of Judas' motives—money, or demonic possession. Dallas rejects the simple reading: ‘He was always bad, and Satan just corrupted him further.’ Instead, he layers in history: Jews of that era expected a warrior Messiah who'd liberate them from Rome. Judas becomes tragic—hopeful, then disillusioned, gambling that forcing Jesus' hand will prove his messianic identity. If Jesus is truly Messiah, he'll escape; if not, Judas will have exposed a false one. This leans on psychology, not Scripture.

— Dallas Jenkins
2:01:18

Ninety-seven pieces hold up the three that don't fit

Dallas uses a puzzle metaphor: of a hundred pieces, ninety-seven he'd stake his life on. Three don't fit, but instead of doubting the whole picture, he trusts those three fit too—he hasn't figured them out yet. Better to trust ninety-seven pieces than discard the whole over three. Alex proposes a different test: Is this cited in multiple independent gospels? Is it an unflattering detail the author kept anyway? These filters reveal what's more likely historically true.

— Dallas Jenkins

Glossary

Theodicy
An explanation for why an all-good God permits suffering and evil.
Synoptic Gospels
Matthew, Mark, and Luke—the three gospels whose narratives closely parallel each other.
Sicarii
Jewish militants in the first century who opposed Roman rule; the name means dagger-carriers.
Criterion of embarrassment
A historical test: if an ancient source reports something that harms the author's cause but stays recorded anyway, it's probably true.
Holy imagination
In Catholic theology, the practice of reverently filling the gaps in Scripture with imaginative artistic work.
