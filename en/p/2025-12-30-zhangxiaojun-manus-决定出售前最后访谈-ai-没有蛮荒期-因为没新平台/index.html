Manus's Last Interview Before the Sale: AI Has No Wild West Phase, Because There's No New Platform

The mobile internet's wild west phase came from a hardware generational shift: desktop to phone made giants and individual developers equals. AI's technological leap is bigger, yet no wholly new platform appeared, so giants, startups and individual developers all moved at the same speed. The wild west phase doesn't exist.

4:03

AI has no wild west phase, because there's no new platform

Ji Chao attributes the mobile internet's opportunity to a generational change in hardware medium: desktop to phone, where BAT and the big overseas companies were equals with individual developers, everyone was experimenting, so there was a wild west phase. He argues that although AI's technological breakthrough is bigger, no wholly new platform emerged, and as a result giants, startups and individual developers reacted at the same speed and moved just as decisively — the wild west phase doesn't exist. This judgment directly explains why he later didn't build models and only built applications.

— Ji Chao
20:12

GPT-3 made him decide on the spot to sell the company

Maggie's team ran from late 2014 to 2018, self-training two models of roughly 0.3B, starting from pretraining, and solving 16K long context themselves. After getting GPT-3 early access, he wrote a casual prompt and found it was fifty-fifty with the end-to-end model they had trained themselves. He realized: it's expensive now, but it's a general solution. Previously the NLP world was sharply divided between people doing information extraction, machine translation and customer service systems; GPT-3 crushed that line of thinking. His first reaction was to sell the company fast.

— Ji Chao
23:12

Search engines aren't a technical problem, they're a data source problem

Maggie built everything from crawler to index in-house, using no third-party search; he calls it the peak of his engineering ability. But the conclusion was: he's not bullish on building another new search engine, because many data sources have already formed a mutually beneficial, recyclable relationship with Google, and a disruptor cannot repeat what Google spent twenty years accumulating on data sources. From this he sums up the two reasons products fail — technical and non-technical — and the line ‘one step early makes you a pioneer, ten steps early makes you a corpse.’

— Ji Chao
33:19

A browser extension is a perfect observation window

His reason for choosing Monica wasn't the product form but unbiased observation: it doesn't change user habits, users are still using Gmail and watching YouTube, their trajectory isn't forcibly altered. More crucially, an extension distributes features by context — video understanding only appears when watching YouTube, rewriting only appears in Gmail or Google Docs — dissolving the complexity explosion that comes from stacking features. He quotes that line from GitHub: Everything added dilutes everything else. An extension isn't even a product form; it's an empty container, an empty canvas.

— Ji Chao
1:04:30

Shipping an uncool product drags you into a self-justification loop

After abandoning the AI browser, his judgment: if you finish a product and you yourself don't think it's cool, don't ship it — you should be the person who likes it most, and if you don't like it, how can you expect users to. The more practical reason is opportunity cost: a responsible team that ships a product has to keep maintaining it, enters a self-justification loop, and misses new opportunities that are clearly more valuable. But he leaves room for disagreement: if another team has already taken the browser to a level they're satisfied with, entering the self-justification loop is right, and along that path a completely different product form may grow.

— Ji Chao
1:08:30

Cursor exposed that programming is a general capability

The observation that turned the team toward Manus: operations colleagues at the company used Cursor to write blog posts, data analysis colleagues used it for analysis and visualization. Cursor was originally the most professional IDE form, yet a flood of non-target users poured in. They stood behind colleagues and watched: code on the left, a window for chatting with AI on the right, and people who can't write code use conversation to make AI complete non-coding tasks with code as the medium. The conclusion: programming is not a vertical capability, it's a medium for solving general tasks; Cursor's form is not optimal for them — it should run in the cloud, code shouldn't be the main presentation, and it should target prosumers rather than professional engineers.

— Ji Chao
1:11:34

Manus was finished in January, deliberately held to March

Manus started at the end of September 2024 and was basically finished by mid-January 2025, but Ji Chao decided not to ship immediately. The best model available then was Claude 3.5 Sonnet V2, which had preliminary agentic ability but lacked true reasoning. He heard a rumor that a model release would come two months later, so he chose to spend an extra month and a half polishing the product, aligning the launch with the next model iteration, so that the product launch would enjoy the biggest generational improvement in one instant and eat the model overflow.

— Ji Chao
1:12:36

Monica's $12M ARR let them gamble big

Building the browser didn't burn much money, and the headcount invested was only about a dozen people. At the time Monica already had close to 12 million, i.e. $12 million, in ARR, and was a profitable product. He stresses the accounting: ARR is MRR times 12, you can't count annual payments received within a month into that month; internally they only look at Stripe and mobile MRR data, otherwise there are far too many ways to inflate the number, and that's lying to yourself. Precisely because they had a positive-cash-flow product, they could be both bold and rational when building a second curve.

— Ji Chao
1:31:42

An agent's input/output ratio is 100:1 to 1000:1

In the chatbot era, cost estimation generally assumed an input-to-output token ratio of 3:1 — because in a transformer input is prefilling, parallelizable and compute bound, while output is decoding, bandwidth bound, and pricing differs a lot. But in an agent like Manus, the input-to-output ratio is 100:1 to 1000:1, depending on task type. With output length roughly the same, input length differs by tens or even hundreds of times. This explains why in early 2025 nobody predicted token consumption would grow exponentially — the input/output ratio changed.

— Zhang Tao
1:35:45

Context beyond 200k no longer matters

He offers a hot take: more important than longer context is giving the model compaction awareness — an awareness of compression itself. If context length increases indefinitely and monotonically, even with kv cache keeping latency and cost low, it's still a growing process and not worth it. What matters more is letting the model know ‘my context is already very long now’, and whether it can offload some information to the file system, just as a person organizes memories into documents and puts them in Notion, and next time knows when to retrieve them. The model must understand that this stuff didn't vanish into thin air, it was compressed.

— Zhang Tao
1:37:46

Porting a reasoning model to agent scenarios makes results worse

Porting a reasoning model designed for competitive programming or math directly into agent scenarios makes results worse: instruction following degrades, and the probability of hallucinations and hallucinated tool calls rises. The right approach is interleave thinking — following the ReAct framework, after getting an observation, don't immediately predict the next action; instead do a relatively brief intermediate reasoning, thinking about what has been done and what to do next. Not like O-series models solving math problems, where the user gives a very short question and it thinks thousands of tokens all inside its head in a flash.

— Zhang Tao
1:45:51

Manus is SOTA on Remote Labor Index, with a 2.5% completion rate

scale.ai released a new benchmark called RLI (remote labor index), and Manus is SOTA, first place, beating competitors like Claude and Gemini. The criterion: whether the work an AI system completes is something a real client would be willing to pay for, and whether it's impossible to tell if a human or an AI did it. But the completion rate is only 2.5%, far from one hundred percent. He values this benchmark because it fits the metric for a general agent — how much of what a remote worker can do can it complete. Optimistically, maybe by 2026 it could climb to 20 or 30.

— Zhang Tao
1:52:53

Don't hand the model the limits of being human

Many agent companies have an inertial way of thinking, splitting a multi-agent system into roles like designer, programmer, manager. He thinks that's wrong: human society has division of labor because no person is all that versatile, and under an organizational structure a lot of information is lost in communication, adding friction to cooperation. But a model is something more versatile than a person, so you should fully exploit the model's advantages and not mechanically copy the constraints that come with humans. Building a general agent is building a system that can do what people do, but you shouldn't demand the model meet human division of labor or specialization.

— Zhang Tao
2:13:03

Invite codes weren't marketing, cloud vendors said opening up would crash them

After talking to every cloud vendor and inference provider before launch, he was surprised to find that the compute in the world that could be in place immediately the next day was far less than imagined. None of the clouds and model vendors they used could provide that volume; Cloud said you absolutely must not open it up, if you open it up we'll crash. So the only choice was to limit volume, and the way to limit volume was invite codes. He admits there were better approaches, like targeted invitations rather than explicit codes. Less than a month later they removed the invite codes. He says outright: if when we launched in March, if there had been no paid promotion, may my whole family die.

— Zhang Tao
2:56:26

Mass personalization doesn't have to go through parameter updates

He judges that mass personalization doesn't necessarily have to be done via online learning or a parameterized approach — hanging a set of non-reusable parameters on each user (like a multi-lora scheme) actually lowers inference efficiency, because cutting cost and latency depends on economies of scale. What's truly worth distinguishing is ‘continual learning’ versus ‘online learning’: online learning is only valuable when the ideal distribution of the task changes over time (he cites financial markets, where today's correct answer isn't necessarily tomorrow's). Much of what's now called online learning practice is essentially on-policy data collection plus periodic optimization, with no dynamism in the task itself; it will quickly saturate the benchmark and has no room for continued improvement.

2:57:27

His four suggestions for agentic models

First, rather than infinitely expanding the context window, teach the model compaction awareness — to realize its context may be compressed, and to know how to offload to and retrieve from the file system. Second, the optimization target for reasoning shouldn't be a pure ‘brain in a vat’; it should consider combining observation, i.e. TIR (Tool Integrated Reasoning), which is a different path from solving competitive programming and math purely with RLVR. Third, interaction mode: in chatbot scenarios the user and model execute alternately, you wait for me and I wait for you, whereas what was novel when Manus first appeared was that while the agent keeps working the user can cut in at any time — change the goal, add information, even terminate it — and many models haven't mastered this asynchronous interaction. Fourth, error resilience: in real environments errors are the norm, and the best model should always find another path to try, which requires dedicated training.

3:00:29

Self-positioning: from the CBA to the NBA

He describes this startup as his first full participation in global competition, joking internally that it's ‘going from the CBA to the NBA’. Even though Manus may now already have 100 millionaires (his words), looking sideways at the top players in every industry, he feels he's nothing much, maybe just the NBA average. The cost structure is completely different too: in the mobile internet era you could see IGA behavior and marginal cost was very low, whereas now from Manus's first day online it's basically hundreds of thousands of dollars, hundreds of thousands of dollars burning, a fairly asset-heavy investment right from the start.

3:03:32

Technology has veto power over product

Who has more say, product or model? His answer is that technology serves product, but technology has veto power over many things: product may have some very tempting quick-and-dirty approaches, like whether to abandon the pure sandbox idea and switch to a quick fix, and in that case both he and the technology side will step up and stop it outright. He explicitly opposes the concept of voting, believing voting alienates the team — people end up serving their own opinions, and you should look at the goal rather than the voting mechanism; if the goal is aligned, consensus can definitely be reached. In discussions nobody is domineering, but they expect Red to make the final call; the value of discussion isn't producing a result, it's providing more alternatives.

3:14:40

Manus's biggest worry is losing its distinctiveness

From the outside, his biggest worry is Manus losing its distinctiveness; from the inside, what he fears most is Manus becoming complicated. He quotes that GitHub line — every feature added eats away at everything else — so he hopes Manus keeps walking with restraint, yet restraint can't be allowed to affect continued growth. As for whether it dies from competition, he thinks the greater possibility for Manus isn't losing to competition, but users leaving after it loses its unique value; that also counts as competition, so the answer is: Manus could die from competition.

3:18:45

Yann LeCun leaving Meta is a positive signal

He thinks Yann LeCun is a highly respected titan in the industry, but playing such a role inside a commercial organization has its own pain, and finding free space to do research is good, while it also frees Meta of a lot of ideological burden; Meta may invest in some more plain work with quick results. On Tian Yuandong, he particularly champions the latent reasoning direction: RLVR essentially increases the model's stability under pass@1, raising the probability that a single inference reaches the correct answer, but whether the model can solve the problem still depends on base quality — a non-RLVR model sampling multiple times will with high probability also sample a correct trajectory, which shows RLVR is close to solving problems by search, and the sampling step itself brings collapse; latent reasoning doesn't do this sample, and can consider multiple possibilities simultaneously in near-parallel dimensions, making reasoning more efficient and also solving the high latency from the user's perspective.

Glossary

compaction awareness
The model realizing its own context may be compressed, and knowing how to offload to and retrieve from the file system.
Remote Labor Index
A benchmark released by scale.ai measuring the share of remote-worker tasks an AI completes that a client would be willing to pay for.
TIR
Tool Integrated Reasoning — reasoning combined with observation, rather than solving math problems purely with RLVR.
RLVR
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards; essentially increases the model's stability under pass@1.
latent reasoning
Not sampling, but considering multiple possibilities simultaneously in near-parallel dimensions, making reasoning more efficient.
