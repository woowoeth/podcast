‘Invasive species are doing their own work’: Indigenous ecological knowledge doesn't use a good-bad binary

Species commonly labeled ‘invasive’—like European honeybees and Asian ladybugs—are viewed in indigenous ecological knowledge as fulfilling their own ecological roles, not as black-and-white enemies.

Honeybees don't come native or invasive—only working or not

The Oneida Nation's own orchards specifically borrow European honeybees from Florida farms for pollination; these bees are not native to North America, yet they're crucial labor for increasing yields. Cheyenne says indigenous languages often lack the word ‘invasive species’, instead using a framework where every species has work to do, and European honeybees are no exception. This directly contrasts with mainstream conservation discourse's default stance that ‘invasive = must eliminate’.

— Cheyenne Reuben-Thomas
9:11

Endangered bumblebees are rebounding in indigenous-managed reserves

According to data from the Xerxes Society for Invertebrate Conservation, the rusty patched bumblebee has declined roughly 87% in recent decades and was listed as endangered in 2017; threats include introduced plant pests, lack of cultural burning, evergreen trees encroaching on meadow habitat, and pathogens from managed bees. But Cheyenne says this bee is making a strong comeback in the Oneida reserve, with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recently designating critical habitat nearby.

Cultural fire is not a government burn program with a different name

Both are controlled burns, but cultural fire is led by indigenous communities, with lower intensity, clearer intent, and timing determined by environmental cues like star positions; government and institutional burns are mostly about meeting area quotas. Indigenous community burns are motivated by ceremony or food sovereignty—a completely different logic from institutional burning's administrative goals.

— Cheyenne Reuben-Thomas
23:42

Native American doctorate holders are less than one-third of one percent

According to 2024 article data, fewer than 2% of the U.S. population holds a doctorate; of that, only about one-third of one percent are Native Americans. Cheyenne says she rarely encountered scholars who looked like her or spoke like her growing up, which is why she once felt that getting a PhD ‘wasn't for someone like me’.

— Ally Ward
25:48

Dandelions tell the Tuscarora when their fish are spawning

Dandelions are a post-colonial introduction to North America, but their bloom period aligns with the spawning season of sturgeon culturally significant to the Tuscarora Nation and is still used as an important environmental signal. Similarly, Asian ladybugs were introduced for biological pest control and have crowded out native seven-spotted and nine-spotted ladybugs, yet they still eat aphids in the Three Sisters intercropping system and protect crops—so communities still acknowledge and respect the work they do.

— Cheyenne Reuben-Thomas
37:22

The Haudenosaunee mosquito legend is about retaliation, not enmity

A Haudenosaunee story tells of two giant mosquitoes the size of trees that constantly attacked people paddling on the river; the people banded together to kill them, and when the giants fell, they scattered into countless tiny mosquitoes. Cheyenne says these small mosquitoes' existence today represents the mosquitoes retaliating for what the ancestors did—a ‘we struck first’ story, not one casting blood-sucking insects as pests to be eliminated.

— Cheyenne Reuben-Thomas
54:07

The hardest part isn't fieldwork—it's defending why the work matters

Cheyenne says the most difficult part of her work isn't data analysis or writing, but constantly being asked ‘why is this important’ and ‘why should this shape policy’. She responds with one-on-one casual conversations, since a 12-minute academic presentation often can't convey the value of traditional ecological knowledge—the host notes with surprise that academia should be grateful for this knowledge rather than questioning it.

— Cheyenne Reuben-Thomas

Glossary

cultural fire
Controlled burning led by indigenous communities with ceremonial intent, distinct from government-managed prescribed burns.
Three Sisters
Corn, beans, and squash grown together in a traditional system where each plant provides nutrients, support, or shade to the others.
traditional ecological knowledge (TEK)
Knowledge accumulated over generations by indigenous communities about ecosystems and species relationships.
