Military dominance without strategy: why America's Iranian advantage proves hollow

America holds every tactical and technological edge over Iran but faces stalemate on the ground, because nobody thought through how allies would respond, what Iran's leaders actually want, or what victory looks like after the shooting stops—a catastrophic failure of strategy, not military capability.

Weapons win battles; strategy wins wars

Cohen opens with the book's core claim: you can have the world's most advanced weapons, the most professional soldiers, perfect tactics—and still lose. The culprit is almost always strategy, never hardware or tactics. This is why he wrote the book: to rescue the word ‘strategy’ from overuse and restore it to its actual meaning.

— Eliot Cohen
8:30

Trump's lightning-strike playbook failed in Iran

Mounk notes Trump uses a formula of ‘shock strike + zero casualties + fast exit’ that worked against Maduro. Cohen says Trump's team expected Iran to follow the same script—find a successor, destroy facilities, get out clean. But Iran shut down the Strait of Hormuz; the Houthis escalated in Yemen simultaneously. Suddenly Trump faced the exact no-exit-strategy quagmire he'd spent years criticizing Bush for.

— Eliot Cohen
20:30

Churchill's Atlantic memos define what good strategy looks like

Cohen's model throughout the book is Churchill's memos written during his December 1941 Atlantic crossing to meet Roosevelt after Pearl Harbor—nearly the ceiling of strategic thought. It shows three things: empathy for your adversary (Cohen argues Churchill understood Hitler better than Hitler understood himself), intellectual humility about foreseeable limits, and clarity that wars never unfold as planned. But the part everyone misses: strategy is mostly execution. Churchill spent enormous effort designing institutions like the Combined Chiefs of Staff.

— Eliot Cohen
26:00

Intelligence agencies count tanks but misread minds

Cohen uses a scene from Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid—the protagonist asking ‘who are these guys?’ repeatedly—to illustrate a pattern: intelligence excels at inventory (how many tanks, jets) and fails catastrophically at intent (what does the adversary actually want). Putin miscalculated Ukrainian resolve. Israel held Yahya Sinwar for ten years, interrogated him, and still didn't grasp who he was. These ‘who exactly are these people?’ questions are the hardest part of strategy and the easiest to skip.

— Eliot Cohen
32:00

A vivid memory can blind you to how much things actually changed

Cohen introduces anchoring bias from cognitive psychology: once pinned to a strong historical image, even acknowledged small changes don't register as large ones. Hitler's generals used the Soviet Union's disastrous Winter War performance to forecast the full invasion; they drastically underestimated Soviet capacity. The West used 2014's Crimean annexation to forecast 2022's Ukraine; it missed that ‘seize territory’ and ‘invade an entire nation, commit massacres’ are categorically different.

— Eliot Cohen
37:00

The Gulf War's four objectives are forgotten within years

The elder Bush's four Gulf War objectives are textbook strategy teaching—until you ask someone to name them, and nobody can. Close reading finds holes everywhere: objective one (protect Americans in Iraq) expired once hostages came home; objective three (restore Kuwait's ‘legitimate government’) disguises support for an absolute monarchy. Plus unspoken goals (overthrow Saddam) and unexpected post-war ones (defend southern Shias). War remakes its own objectives.

— Eliot Cohen
42:00

One allocation decision echoes for decades across a war

World War II: America budgeted for over 200 army divisions, then cut to 90, funneling those resources to what became the decisive air arm. Cohen sees this as a pivotal trade-off. Deeper problem: path dependence. FDR committed to getting U.S. troops into Europe within a year of Pearl Harbor to sustain domestic political will. That decision locked out a 1943 French landing; D-Day got pushed to 1944.

— Eliot Cohen
45:30

There may be no winning condition in Iran anymore

Asked how an Iran war ends, Cohen answers grimly: only two ‘victory’ scenarios exist—regime collapse or so much social pain the government surrenders. He doubts the second works because rulers rarely care what their people endure. He traces the conflict back to 1979, framing this as the threshold into an age of ‘long wars’ where the real target is breaking the other side's social fabric—as Ukraine now does by bombing data centers and e-commerce giant Wildberries.

— Eliot Cohen

Glossary

theory of victory
A causal explanation of how specific military means will achieve political objectives, not merely an action plan
TACO
Acronym for "Trump Always Chickens Out"; describes Trump's pattern of backing down when facing serious resistance
path dependence
The phenomenon where one strategic choice forecloses other viable options that become impossible afterward
Clausewitz's dictum
The principle that war is an extension of politics by other means; political goals shape military objectives throughout conflict
