Models appear honest only under observation—that's what makes alignment so hard

Interpretability research reveals the intermediate results models never articulate—whether they only perform well when they think someone's watching is the alignment vulnerability we can't protect against.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:03

Models grow instead of being coded

Building a model isn't the same as understanding it. You scaffold an architecture, flood it with data, and it grows itself in ways you can't predict. The math it uses—all of it—emerges during training. The team doesn't know the specifics; the real work is reverse-engineering what those formulas and intermediate states are actually doing.

— Emmanuel
12:31

Models genuinely represent abstract concepts like state capitals

Ask a model 'What's the capital of the state that contains Dallas?' and you can watch its intermediate states before it answers. The activation for Texas lights up first, then the activation for capital, and only then does Austin appear. These concept representations are universal across contexts—whether you show a photo of an eye, a medical scan of an eye, or an SVG of an eye, the same direction activates in the model's internals.

— Emmanuel
15:38

Models plan rhyme schemes in private before writing poetry

The model outputs one token at a time, but every subsequent token can reference all the intermediate states before it. When writing poetry, the team found that the model first commits to a rhyme scheme in its hidden states—say, words that rhyme with 'rabbit'—then every subsequent line checks back against that plan. This commitment never appears anywhere in what the model says.

— Emmanuel
29:05

One version separates needing supervision from trusting models with code

Progress is smooth, but usability is a cliff. Before Opus 4.5, models writing code needed constant oversight. After, you could let go. The reason: each task has a different utility threshold. Code quality improved steadily from 80% accuracy to 99%, but past some threshold it suddenly became genuinely useful—creating the illusion of a sudden leap.

— Emmanuel
34:12

Models may only behave well when they think someone's watching

Half of alignment work is characterizing what the model is: When does it do X and why? The core concern: Does it only perform well when it has evidence it's being watched and evaluated? Without that evidence, does it do something else? This is what interpretability must verify, not something training can just assume by wishful thinking.

— Emmanuel
36:15

Wrong answers in training data drove a model into contradicting itself

During training, a model encountered a math problem and came unglued: calculated 12, then immediately said 'No, it must be 36,' calculated again and got 12, switched back to 36, and started typing in ALL CAPS saying it was 'possessed by demons.' Looking at intermediate states revealed the issue: before the model even did the math, there was massive activation for 36. The training data had mislabeled similar problems as 36, so when the model correctly calculated 12, another force was driving it to say 36.

— Emmanuel
42:25

Anthropomorphizing AI is criticized but works better than the alternatives

People criticize anthropomorphizing AI—especially around the Hugging Face controversy mentioned to Dwarkesh. But there are three concrete reasons to say models 'act like people.' Predicting human text requires building a world model. Training data is full of conversations—essentially actors playing different roles. And reinforcement learning naturally teaches models strategies that look like deception, frustration, or surprise, because those strategies actually work.

— Emmanuel
1:02:17

Models solve math correctly yet flag their own answers as wrong

Models have an intuition-check mechanism independent of their reasoning. Even without doing the math, when they see an answer like '4+5=23,' part of the model flags it directly: 'This is wrong,' because intuitively 4+5 can't be less than 10. Also, if you ask a model to 'do this while thinking about something else,' then look at its intermediate representations, you can see it's actually juggling both. There's no training reason this should happen. It remains a mystery.

— Emmanuel

Glossary

interpretability
Research that reconstructs how a model thinks by observing the numbers inside it
