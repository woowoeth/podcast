Being falsely accused of theft taught him not to trust in reward

Second grade: he saved five dollars to buy a model airplane, but a family member mistook it for something he'd stolen and dragged him back to the shop. The owner confirmed it was paid for—because his grandmother had once overpaid there—but his family never apologized or clarified the misunderstanding. That incident planted an early conviction: effort doesn't guarantee return, and no one will speak up for you.

22:39

Gave up on features; betting on Oscar-bound shorts instead

Tim says he no longer has time to chase his dream of directing feature films, so he started an internal shorts unit called "Scalpel." Three years in, he's spent close to 10 million yuan on high-quality shorts rather than features. Eleven of them have now qualified for Oscar preselection-eligible festivals. He reasons that shorts spread faster on social platforms with quicker iteration cycles; features aren't necessarily the optimal play.

— Tim
34:45

Review media faces an impossible choice: stay objective or get paid

Tim pinpoints the core contradiction: audiences want honest evaluation, but brands want praise. Early on, reviewers can stay neutral. Then a brand offers a real budget—and what they actually want isn't your review; it's your reach and credibility to serve their agenda. This tension is nearly unsolvable unless you're massive enough to turn down bad-faith partnerships.

— Tim
37:46

Merchandise is the business that actually scales

Content's fatal flaw is no economies of scale—every episode is a one-time earn. Tim found the exit: T-shirts and weatherproof jackets. This year Filmstorm shipped hundreds of thousands to millions of units, with individual designs hitting roughly 200,000 units each—already outpacing most garment manufacturers. He's modeling Mr.Beast's chocolate approach: e-commerce is won or lost on three mechanics: customer acquisition, returns, and error correction.

— Tim
1:04:03

Without brand deals, Chinese review creators can't survive

Luo Yonghao asked whether Chinese review creators could live independently if they rejected brand sponsorships. Tim's answer: no. China never developed a paid-subscription culture like print media did. Individual creators can't partner with institutions the way teams can. And online, someone will always produce the same content for free. Reviewers who stay objective are crushed by the competition.

— Tim
1:34:17

Stranded, and lighting a fire was his happiest moment this year

Before a 100-hour desert island livestream, his team confiscated his insect repellent and fire-starting tools. Day one: mosquito fever, no fire. Day two: he focused light through a magnifying glass and ignited kindling. He calls that moment his happiest this year—pure joy at completing something he thought was impossible. It crystallized how hard it is for civilization to reignite once it breaks.

— Tim
2:13:33

AI breaks the belief that effort earns reward

Tim sees AI's real terror: it demolishes the core conviction that hard work pays off. Ten years of accumulated expertise can be worthless overnight. He predicts editing and execution work vanish in two years, but creative roles aren't safe either—everyone's fighting on the same field. The only relative shelter is personal lived experience; AI can't yet wear multimodal memory and live through your whole life.

— Tim
2:38:55

Five-year goal: one billion people watching his work globally

Tim set a five-year target: one billion people worldwide see Filmstorm content—matching Mr.Beast's 800-million-plus YouTube subscriber scale. The company plans to win or land an Oscar nomination for a short by 2028. On a personal level, he harbors a distant dream: reaching Mars. He assumes he won't return, yet considers it worth the risk.

— Tim
