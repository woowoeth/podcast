Yahoo refused to buy Google because it didn't believe search could be big

Yahoo and P&G made the same mistake: they didn't believe search would move to the front end, and they didn't believe Americans would sit on the sidewalk drinking Starbucks. When founders step back too early, the company loses its ability to take a bet.

McKinsey sells half a century of database

Wang Huanan says McKinsey is essentially a ‘shell company’ with no assets, yet consultants in their twenties can sketch out a client's hundred-year history in a month or two, because behind them stands half a century of business accumulation. In 1997 and 1998 he worked on a case for Ping An Insurance in Shenzhen, charging 1 million US dollars for three months — nearly 10 million RMB at the time — with two or three people. Ma Mingzhe's reason was ‘we want to aim at world-class companies, so we use a world-class consulting firm’. This made him realize for the first time that China's best private companies began using world-class consulting services very early.

— Wang Huanan
13:10

When you know nothing, never read the literature

At P&G working on Pringles, Wang Huanan had no understanding of Americans' snacking habits. His methodology was ‘when you know nothing, never read the literature — go find users and listen’. Four out of five days a week he ran focus groups across the US, three sessions a day, seven or eight people each, reaching over two hundred users a month. After three months he could put himself in the shoes of a twenty-year-old American kid. The product judgments that came out were very specific: chips can't shatter all over the back seat when you open the can, and can't leave your hands greasy — hence the tube, less oil, and flavor powder on the surface.

— Wang Huanan
28:19

Yahoo was a Yahoo without a soul

Wang Huanan attributes Yahoo's decline to two things: the founders stepped back too early — and ‘truly believed they lacked management ability, so they stepped back’; and the technical soul was replaced by a commercial soul. His exact words: ‘A tech company without a founder, where professional managers took over too early, is especially prone to decay’, because professional managers run things well but can't grasp the technology wave. By contrast, when Google brought in professional managers, it was clear: ‘You teach me, guide me, and when I'm ready I'll come back’ — so the founders returned.

— Wang Huanan
29:55

Yahoo missed Google, P&G missed Starbucks

Yahoo and Google talked about a merger; the conclusion was no merger, because ‘search will never come to the front end, search is just a steam engine’. At the time Yahoo outsourced search to Google, feeding Google for a long time. Google's two founders couldn't find a business model and said they might as well sell to Yahoo, but Yahoo didn't believe search could be big, nor that users would care about a search brand. Wang Huanan puts this alongside P&G missing Starbucks: P&G assembled a big team to study it and concluded they didn't believe Americans would sit on the sidewalk drinking coffee like Europeans. His summary: ‘America loses, China loses, no entrepreneurial spirit, founders left their core positions too early’.

— Wang Huanan
32:55

Google dares to bet, and doesn't touch you after buying

On acquisitions, Wang Huanan sees two differences: Google dares to gamble on what matters — for example, when YouTube came out Google bought it outright, which seemed unbelievably expensive at the time; and after buying, it doesn't remodel the company first, letting it run freely for several years. He cites Keyhole: Google barely touched it for ten years, and that company is today's Google Maps. Yahoo, by contrast, ‘didn't dare to gamble on what mattered, but gambled in many places’, and after buying wanted to remodel quickly. He treats Tencent's acquisition of Zhang Xiaolong's mailbox as a positive case: no tasks assigned, just don't leave the system, let him live flexibly, and once he finds his way, support him in doing interesting things.

— Wang Huanan
36:59

What Google lacks isn't algorithms, it's closed-loop data

Wang Huanan thinks Google has an inherent flaw: no closed-loop data on its plate. Users search and click, but after clicking through to a delivery site, Google doesn't know whether they ordered, reordered, or were satisfied. ChatGPT, by contrast, is interactive learning: every conversation adds content, and a hundred million users means a hundred million people helping it improve. He judges that Google ‘indeed lost the first round, and I estimate it lost badly’, because this is a game of scale, and once it's open it's hard to catch up. But he also says Google doesn't lack the precursors of algorithms, compute, and data, and may win battle by battle in vertical scenarios.

— Wang Huanan
41:24

China and the US will share half the world

Wang Huanan bets that data carries political and boundary color, that Chinese answers, knowledge, and cultural ways of thinking differ, so a Chinese ChatGPT will certainly be very different — therefore ‘Chinese and American chat and AI will share half the world’. He divides labor by company size: algorithms are basically big-company business, small companies can participate in part; compute is entirely big-company business — Amazon has dozens of AI groups, and a group burning 10 to 20 million US dollars a month is normal; data is a combination of mid-sized and big companies. He cites Babytree: nearly 150 million pieces of maternal and infant knowledge can be made into a vertical-scenario robot, using a closed small loop against the big loop.

— Wang Huanan
1:03:18

This is already an arms race in high tech

Wang Huanan says the ChatGPT team is under 100 people, 80-something, with many Chinese, and many Chinese who left China, so this has become a talent war. He describes how Google plunders talent: no work for you to do, 1 million US dollars a year, just come — the idea being ‘as long as you don't go to a competitor’. He judges that China is okay on compute, not that far behind on algorithms, but may have only a hundred top engineers while others have a thousand. His conclusion: ‘Today is an arms race in high tech’ — the US has its own, between the US and China there's another, and within China there are contradictions and competition.

— Wang Huanan
