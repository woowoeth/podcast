AI Makes Starting Up More Democratic, but Concentrates the Epicenter in the Bay Area

AI distributes technology to the whole world, yet concentrates the fastest change back into the Bay Area; the borderless founder's leverage is not local knowledge but a diaspora network they can mobilize across borders.

In the US everything can be as a service; elsewhere it can't

Angela says that when she was doing fintech she found that in the US every layer has an as a service, but in Latin America there is no fraud as a stack, no KYC as a stack, and founders have to build everything themselves. This leads to a counterintuitive conclusion: the less mature the local market, the deeper the local knowledge required of the founder, but once it's built, the thicker the moat. Her first check went to Adi's CEO Santiago Suarez, and today Adi serves the banking and payments of a quarter of Colombia's population.

— Angela Strange
4:12

Cramming 30 unicorns into one WhatsApp group

When Gabriel was doing Latin American mapping, there were only 30 unicorns in all of Latin America, and founders in Brazil, Colombia and Mexico weren't talking to each other. So they built a WhatsApp group and put Angela, Marc Andreessen, Alex Rampel and these founders into it. The unexpected payoff: these people are themselves prolific angel investors and started exchanging deal flow in the group, so a16z both saw early projects and participated in the community. This was the starting point of the entire global strategy.

— Gabriel Vasquez
6:19

AI democratizes and concentrates at the same time

Gabriel says AI has an interesting dichotomy: the technology is very democratic, distributing capability to anywhere in the world and opening new markets; but it simultaneously concentrates the fastest-changing epicenter into the Bay Area. The result is a reversal of behavior — previously founders in Brazil and Colombia wanted a16z to fly to them to connect, now they all want to come to the Bay Area, and to be integrated faster. This point is the premise for understanding every mechanism that follows.

— Gabriel Vasquez
11:27

They all have a chip on their shoulder

Gabriel says borderless founders often have a chip on their shoulder: coming from an overlooked country or a small place, no one will build the benchmark for you, you have to become the benchmark yourself. He gives his own example — growing up in El Salvador, after coming to the US no one would say ‘I know another impressive person from El Salvador’. Angela adds that this isn't limited to countries; people from small rural towns in the US are the same. The driving force is ‘no matter where I was born, I can achieve greatness’.

— Gabriel Vasquez
21:51

Poaching Capital One people to Bogotá

The most practical use of brand advantage is recruiting. Adi expanded extremely fast and needed credit officers who could handle billions of dollars in GMV, and the best credit talent often comes from Capital One. The problem: how do you get someone at Capital One in Virginia to be willing to go to Bogotá? The answer is to become Colombia's hottest company — rather than joining one of 50 look-alike companies in the US, join the one that is already a clear winner in brand terms. Brand also brings government relations: Jeeves works with Brazil's central bank, using stablecoins to help companies move money across borders.

— Angela Strange
24:01

The AI talent isn't at the university you think

Angela gives the example of an AI insurance company in Brazil: the best AI talent isn't at the most famous university, but at the one you've never heard of. How to find them? Go to the scholarship programs that scour the country for the best students, go to robotics competitions. If you get 10 extremely smart people early, the 11th person in the US holding a hundred offers will see your team and think ‘how did they get such strong people there’, and the talent flywheel starts. The same applies on the customer side: no one wants to be the first bank or the first insurance company, but through a borderless network you can get big banks and big insurers faster in another country.

— Angela Strange
27:08

Every market has a local luminary

Gabriel gives their concrete method for entering a new market: first find the local luminary — someone extremely respected in the local ecosystem who desperately wants their ecosystem to be seen. Company size and degree of success don't matter. In Sweden the example is Boy's founder Frederick Yeom, who from the outside looks like just a scooter company founder, but he has invested in almost every relevant company in Sweden because he connects both young and senior founders. The first step is understanding who these people are and how to help them; the second step is finding the country's rising stars, like Swedish high school dropout Gabriel Peterson, who worked at MidJourney and later became one of its youngest researchers, and now is starting his own company.

— Gabriel Vasquez
33:26

The first wave of founders didn't go the whole way

Gabriel says the first wave of entrepreneurs in these local ecosystems got to the $1 billion to $5 billion scale, but couldn't go the whole way — because the ecosystem itself was too hard, they have many lessons and failed to realize their vision. This group now wants to do a second company, bringing all their knowledge and network to do something bigger. a16z's approach is: track them, help them come to events, help them reactivate their networks (especially connecting to Stanford's smartest engineers), help them ideate, and introduce customers for early validation. When Frederick Guillaume's new company pip.com came to the Bay Area, they arranged meetings with executives from DoorDash and Lyft; after going back the direction was clear, and they ultimately chose a16z.

— Gabriel Vasquez
36:33

The visa is the most anxiety-inducing first hurdle

Asked what it's like for a seed-stage founder entering this ecosystem, Gabriel says the first thing is the visa — it sounds unimportant, but it's the most anxiety-inducing process of moving to the US: which one to apply for, whether I qualify, who will write me a recommendation letter. a16z invested in a visa company, Extraordinary, and works with them to help founders figure out eligibility. Second is the hacker house: they sponsored Palo House, where four students from Germany's CDTM program wanted to open a house in Palo Alto and bring the smartest people to live there, hosting 1200 people over four months through dinners and lodging.

— Gabriel Vasquez
39:39

The next generation of family businesses wants to become AI native

Angela points out an internationally specific opportunity: many countries have a few powerful families controlling a large number of businesses, and the next generation of these families very much wants to become AI native. a16z pulls them into the borderless founder group. If you want to find global operations and finance leaders to ideate with and talk about their problems, they are very willing to talk with the smartest people in the community about what to build. This mechanism has already gotten portfolio companies real contracts.

— Angela Strange
41:41

Stay at least three to six months

For international founders who want to come to the Bay Area, Gabriel's advice is: you must come and stay for a while, because no one who comes says it was a waste of time, everyone goes back more excited, and you can calibrate speed — what the Bay Area still leads other ecosystems in is speed. His personal advice is at least three to six months; a few weeks isn't enough, because you have to go through the depth of the network; a few weeks can sometimes be frustrating instead, because it's hard to connect with people. People who stay longer go back with the right connections, whether they ultimately decide to go back or move here. Angela adds: lean on your diaspora, the Bay Area's diaspora network is much deeper than you think.

— Gabriel Vasquez

Glossary

borderless founder
A founder who starts in their home country but has global ambition, or builds a team across borders.
local luminary
Someone extremely respected in the local ecosystem who desperately pushes for the ecosystem to be seen.
preferential attachment
The flywheel in which, after attracting the best talent and customers early, subsequent connections become easier and easier.
hacker house
A base that provides short-term free lodging and exchange for early founders.
fraud as a stack
Turning anti-fraud capability into a ready-to-call service layer.
