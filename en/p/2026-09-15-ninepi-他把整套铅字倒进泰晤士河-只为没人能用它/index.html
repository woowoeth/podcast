He Dumped an Entire Font into the Thames So No One Else Could Use It

Over 173 nights, Thomas Cobden-Sanderson dumped 2,600 pounds of Doves Type into the Thames, because metal type is a physical object — dump it, and you own it exclusively.

5:20

Friends who live too close fall apart faster

Emery lived at 3 Hammersmith Terrace; in 1897 the Thomas family moved to number 7 on the same street, and Emery even rented commercial space from Thomas. The two took family holidays together and stayed in adjacent cottages. In 1899 they decided to go further and start Doves Press as partners. Helen says they had been very important friends for more than a decade, but the relationship fell apart in only about two years.

— Helen Zaltzman
9:41

Complementary skills couldn't save the partnership

Thomas was the perfectionist who cared only about the art of printing; Emery was the pragmatist who ran the business. On paper it was a perfect match, but things soured almost from the start. Thomas felt he did all the printing and proofreading himself, that Emery was barely there, and that Emery never responded to his long arguments about how a letter should be designed. Emery, meanwhile, had another business to run and money to make, and had no time to spend weeks or months with Thomas fussing over a single letter.

— Helen Zaltzman
13:47

Type is a physical object, so ownership can be fought over

The breakup dragged on for seven years, and the core issue was who owned Doves Type. At the time a typeface was thousands upon thousands of physical objects that couldn't be copied infinitely — without that set of metal type, there was no typeface. Thomas wanted it exclusively and didn't want Emery, or anyone he couldn't control, printing anything with Doves. He offered a small sum for Emery to give up his rights; Emery wouldn't accept, and the tug-of-war went back and forth for years.

— Helen Zaltzman
15:58

Dumping it in the river was a revenge seven years in the planning

Thomas dumping Doves Type into the Thames was not a sudden impulse. He waited seven years and wrote a 116-page apologia — the word doesn't mean an apology, it means a defense. He had meant to publish it in 1913, but realized it might incriminate him, so he kept waiting, until he was about to shut down Doves Press and print its last book, and only then, on an evening in August 1916, did he act.

— Helen Zaltzman
18:11

173 nights, no more than 15 pounds at a time

Thomas could carry at most 10 to 15 pounds of type at a time, and the full set weighed at least 2,600 pounds. By that math he made at least 173 trips to the bridge. Each night he wrapped the pages he had just finished printing in white paper, bagged them, stuffed them into his pockets or into a wooden toolbox with a sliding lid, and sprinkled them gently off the bridge — he didn't want a splash, didn't want to be seen. He was afraid of being caught, because the type was legally promised to Emery.

— Helen Zaltzman
24:24

Nearly a century later, someone found it on the riverbed

Emery had asked the original engraver, Edward Prince, to remake Doves Type, but Prince was nearly eighty and his eyes and hands could no longer do such fine work. Then in 2010, type designer Robert Green began redrawing it. He spent three years making a digital version, wasn't satisfied, and in 2014 went himself to the riverbed under Hammersmith Bridge at low tide, where within 20 minutes he found a lowercase v, and then with a dive team found 151 pieces of Doves Type in all.

— Robert Green
33:46

Whoever leaves more writing controls the narrative

Helen says Thomas left behind a huge number of letters and diaries, while Emery's letters are far fewer, so Thomas had a stronger grip on the narrative. For decades people assumed Emery was the jerk, because he complained less. In the diaries of his last forty years, Thomas edited Emery out completely, and when Annie published those diaries on the fourth anniversary of his death, Emery wasn't in them.

— Helen Zaltzman

Glossary

Doves Type
The exclusive typeface of Doves Press, based on the design of the 15th-century French printer Nicholas Jensen.
apologia
Not an apology, but a written argument defending one's own actions.
photo-engraving
A method of making printing plates using photography, the field in which Emery's company was renowned.
