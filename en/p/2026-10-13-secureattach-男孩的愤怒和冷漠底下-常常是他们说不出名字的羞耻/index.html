Beneath boys' anger and coldness often lies the shame they cannot name

Boys' outward anger, silence, or defiance often aren't the emotion itself, but signals left when their nervous system instinctively severs authentic self-expression to preserve attachment.

Boys shift from proud display to near-silence within five or six years

McCormick observes that kindergarten boys excitedly raise their hands to show off—‘I painted this fire truck’—without caring if the drawing is recognizable, bursting with pride. Yet the same boys by fifth or sixth grade have shed nearly all emotional expression, joy, intimacy, and empathy. He attributes this not to natural maturation but to systematic training that has suppressed traits deemed ‘feminine’—emotional expression, intimacy, connection—ground away bit by bit through environmental pressure, not through explicit teaching.

11:04

One father, strikingly different emotional instincts for his son and daughters

McCormick has four daughters and one son, and admits that when his daughters have big emotions, his instinct is to sit with them and help them through. But facing the same emotions from his son, his reflex is to jump to logic and ‘solve’ the feeling. He stresses this difference rarely comes from deliberate parenting philosophy but from gender-coded emotional scripts absorbed during his own childhood—boys consequently struggle to develop emotional skills not because they lack them innately, but because they've never been given the space to practice.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
24:22

Attachment and authentic self-expression make fundamentally incompatible demands on children

Drawing on Gabor Maté's framework, McCormick argues that childhood contains two equally vital but potentially conflicting needs: attachment (the prerequisite for survival) and authenticity (the essence of truly living). A one-year-old's nervous system knows it cannot survive without its caregiver, so it will do anything to preserve that bond. When authentic emotions—fear, neediness, vulnerability—are met with withdrawn love, the child's nervous system chooses to sacrifice authenticity to keep attachment. This trade happens at the instinctive level, not as a conscious decision.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
32:09

Incarcerated teenagers can only describe themselves through anger and humor

McCormick runs groups at juvenile detention centers and asks 15- and 16-year-old inmates to describe themselves emotionally. The answer is always the same: ‘I'm angry’ or ‘I'm funny.’ Nothing else. When he sends them home to ask their parents what their emotional life was like at age two or three, the description is unrecognizably different. His conclusion: what these boys express now as anger or humor almost never originates as anger or humor—underneath is shame, and he has never seen a boy or man genuinely taught to recognize or tolerate it.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
41:05

Boys will choose certain pain over the uncertainty of not knowing

A boy who cycled through 26 foster homes tells McCormick about a pattern: whenever someone invests emotionally in him and shows care, he sabotages the relationship and pushes them away. He once thought this meant something was wrong with him. Now he understands it as a sophisticated strategy—each rejection confirms the story ‘I am not worth loving,’ which gives him back a measure of control. McCormick frames this as a core human preference: people choose certain pain over the anguish of uncertainty.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
44:36

One poolside sentence can teach a boy his feelings deserve shame

At a pool, an eight- or nine-year-old boy is teased by older brothers until he breaks down crying. His father, sitting across the way, immediately calls out: ‘Don't cry like a little girl.’ McCormick argues that this exchange—commonplace in many families—is devastating to a child's nervous system and sense of self. The message is direct: the moment you show human vulnerability, you will meet shame and disappointment, not comfort.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
45:51

Deep conversations happen shoulder to shoulder, never face to face

McCormick wanted to have a formal conversation with his son about healthy relationships. He called him into the living room and said, ‘Let's talk about this tomorrow.’ His son shut down immediately, wholly unreceptive. Weeks later, sitting side by side in the car, his son initiated the conversation and opened up, and they had a depth of talk he hadn't expected. This taught him a pragmatic principle: with boys, avoid the confrontational setup of face-to-face talking. Instead, sit side by side—in a car, playing a game—where the pressure drops and real conversation becomes possible.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
52:00

Girls articulate their desires; boys have never been taught to ask

In his social work classroom, McCormick asks female students nearing graduation, most looking for male partners, what they want in a partner. They fluently describe ‘a soulmate, someone emotionally stable.’ He and Bren both point out the problem isn't that young men have different expectations—it's that no one has ever asked them this question or taught them the internal capacities required to answer it. This is ‘use it or lose it’: boys are born with these abilities, but without invitation to exercise them, they atrophy.

— Dr. Adam McCormick
