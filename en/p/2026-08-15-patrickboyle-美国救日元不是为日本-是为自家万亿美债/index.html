The US Isn't Rescuing the Yen for Japan — It's Rescuing Its Own Trillion in Treasuries

The US intervened in the yen for the first time in 28 years, officially to help an ally; the real calculation is preventing Japan from dumping $1 trillion of Treasuries to defend its currency — which would push up America's own borrowing costs.

0:00

The Treasury secretary's sticky note, the first time in 28 years

The episode opens on an absurd detail: a sticky note on Treasury Secretary Bessent's desk at a cabinet meeting reading ‘Buy yen, $5–10 billion’, with the currency code JPY carefully appended. Trump later explained it on Air Force One as doing a friend a favour, even dragging in Pearl Harbor. What's truly anomalous isn't the sticky note itself but the nature of the act: the last time the US Treasury stepped in to prop up the yen was 1998, 28 years ago. By convention, treasury secretaries don't do this, so it's worth asking what actually happened.

— Patrick Boyle
4:14

The carry trade doesn't pay free money, it pays you to hold the grenade

The mechanism behind the weak yen isn't mysterious: the Fed raised rates to about 3.75% to fight inflation, while the Bank of Japan held at 0% for years and only recently moved to 1%. The huge rate gap spawned a global carry trade — borrow nearly free yen in Tokyo, convert to dollars, and buy Treasuries paying about 4%. Textbooks say this shouldn't produce a free lunch; a low-yielding currency should appreciate until it exactly offsets the rate gap. Yet markets have persistently done the opposite, which economics calls the forward premium puzzle. The more popular explanation: what you earn isn't free money, it's the commission for holding the grenade on everyone's behalf; when it blows up, everyone runs for the exit on the same day and the yen appreciates violently.

— Patrick Boyle
9:25

Japan, on the watch list, gets rescued by the US anyway

The US Treasury's official position hasn't changed in decades: don't intervene in exchange rates, let markets determine value; intervention is just a short-term band-aid. To police others, it publishes a semiannual report putting trade partners that manipulate their currencies on a watch list. The latest report in July listed 10 countries, Japan among them. Yet in that same month, the US Treasury itself stepped in to buy the yen up for Japan. Strictly speaking, the report criticises pushing a currency down to promote exports, while this was pushing the yen up; but spending American taxpayers' money to move a currency you're currently monitoring shows the rules are clearly only meant to bind others.

12:31

The yen rescue isn't paid for in dollars, it's paid for in quietly sold euros

The execution details are even more anomalous: when the New York Fed placed orders with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley on the Treasury's behalf, what it sold wasn't dollars but euros — with the European Central Bank kept in the dark. The bulk of the euro assets in the US Exchange Stabilization Fund are French government bonds, so there's a strong possibility the US effectively dumped a batch of French sovereign debt. A senior ECB official described it to the FT as ‘an unprecedented breach of central bank etiquette’; the IMF manual even explicitly advises central banks not to intervene in other countries' currencies. It's the equivalent of driving off in a neighbour's car without telling him, hitting a tree, then explaining to him why his insurance premiums are going up.

— Patrick Boyle
14:43

What the US is rescuing isn't the yen, it's the trillion in Treasuries Japan holds

The favour to Tokyo isn't the point; America's own borrowing costs are. Japan is the largest foreign holder of US Treasuries, holding more than $1 trillion. If Japan spends another $50 billion or $80 billion defending the yen, the easiest way to raise the money is to sell Treasuries; heavy selling would push Treasury prices down and yields up. That's what Bessent fears most, so he needs to help Japan steady the yen. His résumé makes it more ironic: in 1992 at Soros's fund he lived through the sniping of the Bank of England, and he later made his name shorting the yen; now he sits in the US Treasury secretary's chair, and the target he's defending against is precisely that same playbook from 30 years ago.

— Patrick Boyle
18:56

A treasury secretary shouldn't take a directional bet on Treasury financing

For most of the past two years, Bessent's issuance leaned toward short-term T-bills, reluctant to issue long bonds. The surface reason was that long-end rates are high now, so there's no need to lock in 30 years; underneath is a directional bet — betting inflation falls and long-bond yields decline, then swapping Treasuries into long bonds. That bet could come from a hedge fund, but not from a treasury secretary, because his job isn't to guess rates but to fund the government regularly and predictably. Unfortunately rates haven't fallen: the 10-year rose to about 4.6%, and the 30-year broke above 5%. As long as rates don't fall, his financing strategy gets stuck in a loop of endlessly rolling short-term debt that gets more expensive each time.

— Patrick Boyle
25:09

$88 billion bought only two weeks, and half the gain was given back

The intervention did work at first: at the end of July, US and Japanese authorities spent about $88 billion over two days, and the yen rose from around 164 to 155, roughly 5%. But within two weeks the yen fell back to 159, giving back half the gain. The reason is simple — the FX market trades trillions of dollars a day, and $88 billion is a rounding error. Obstfeld calls this contradictory approach cakeism: the US wants a weak dollar but no inflation, low borrowing costs but a huge deficit, and Japan to keep buying Treasuries while the yen strengthens. America's own tariffs and pressure on Japan to invest in the US are also pushing the yen down. It's like bailing water while drilling a hole in the bottom of the boat.

— Patrick Boyle
27:21

Treasuries are no longer scarce — that's the real foundation of this whole drama

The real foundation of this drama isn't the yen but the scarcity of Treasuries. A paper by Harvard professor Wenxin Du finds that the convenience yield the world pays for the safety and liquidity of Treasuries has narrowed for decades and has recently vanished or even turned negative. The cause is supply: too many Treasuries are issued, so they're no longer scarce. Thursday's 30-year auction cleared at 5.22%, already the highest 30-year borrowing cost for the US since 2001. If you want a stronger yen, the cleanest way is for the BOJ to raise rates; but Japan's government debt already exceeds 200% of GDP, debt service eats a quarter of government spending, and the new prime minister is also averse to hiking. Intervention doesn't change the trend; at best it buys a few days.

Glossary

carry trade
Borrowing in a low-yielding currency and converting into higher-yielding assets to earn the spread; the yen is the global funding currency.
forward premium puzzle
The theory that a low-yielding currency should appreciate until it offsets the rate gap, yet in reality it persistently diverges.
Cakeism
The mindset of wanting to have it all without paying the cost; used to describe the self-contradictory nature of US fiscal policy.
Treasury Convenience Yield
The extra return investors give up for the safety and liquidity of Treasuries, which has now shrunk to negative.
