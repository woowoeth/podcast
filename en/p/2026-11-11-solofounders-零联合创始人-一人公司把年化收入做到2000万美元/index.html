Seven months of solo scaling: $1.5M to $20M revenue

Ben was introduced as Pulsia's founder on this podcast's first episode at $1.5M annualized revenue; seven months later he's surpassed $20M annual run rate, making all decisions alone—no co-founders, no full-time employees. He's now a one-person company valued at $250M, one quarter of the way to a billion-dollar goal.

— Julian
9:09

Co-founders assembled for funding led to paralysis

At his previous company, he believed he needed a CTO to raise money, so he brought in a college friend who'd worked at Instacart. The problem: both wanted decision-making authority and spent energy debating everything; the company stalled because neither could make the final call. His lesson: successful co-founder relationships emerge from years of shared struggle (classmates, ex-colleagues), not crisis partnerships arranged for fundraising.

— Ben Cera
11:11

Customers never complained about the name or design

Pulsia's name is "AI slop" spelled backward—literally what he texted his lawyer at midnight as a placeholder for the shell company, then kept on the actual product six months later. After six months building Pulsia, customer feedback has never mentioned the name or design; complaints are always about bugs. His conclusion: founders waste effort on naming and terms that customers ignore; survival hinges only on whether the MVP converts users to paying customers.

— Ben Cera
23:16

Most of 50K paying users skip Claude Code entirely

Pulsia now has nearly 50,000 paying customers with a profile distinct from typical AI builders: many are over 40, formerly in traditional industries, newly starting companies; some are 17-year-olds whose fathers recommended them. They pick Pulsia because "you don't have to think about how to build it." Global context: only 10–15% of people have tried a free chatbot, less than 1% have heard of Claude Code, and just 2% of Americans pay for AI. The market is far less crowded than builders assume.

— Ben Cera
29:21

Solo-founded companies doubled from 20% to 40% in five years

The host cited Carta data: five years ago, roughly one-fifth (20%) of companies were solo-founded; this year the proportion reached nearly 40%. Ben credits AI for letting one person do work that once required a team—comparable to how AWS freed founders from building their own servers.

— Julian
34:23

The founder is the producer choosing yes or no

His analogy: a hit album often credits twenty to thirty collaborators, yet nobody questions the lead artist's ownership—because they approved every decision. In the AI agent era, founders work the same way: not writing code or running operations themselves, but constantly telling AI outputs "I like this" or "I don't, fix this," with the final direction and tradeoffs entirely the founder's responsibility.

— Ben Cera
39:25

A co-founder standoff isolates you more than going solo

The idea that solo founders get lonely is misguided, he argues. Real loneliness came in his previous company—disagreeing with a co-founder but unable to leave, like knowing you should divorce but being locked in the marriage. His verdict: making decisions alone and discussing with AI or friends is easier than being stuck in a deadlocked partnership.

— Ben Cera

Glossary

true solo
A founder with no full-time human collaborators, advancing the company entirely through AI and contractors.
free solo
A solo founder who bootstraps entirely, taking no outside investment and funding the company from personal revenue.
juiced solo
A solo founder who has raised outside funding and may hire employees, as opposed to bootstrapping alone.
