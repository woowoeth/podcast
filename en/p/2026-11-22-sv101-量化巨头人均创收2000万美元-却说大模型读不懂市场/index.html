Quant giants generate $20M per person in revenue, yet insist large models can't read markets

XTX Markets, with 25,000 GPUs and just over 200 employees, created finance's highest per-capita profit. But both quant practitioners argue that general large models today cannot understand trading data—the real moat is execution infrastructure, not the model itself.

3:05

Machine-faith GPU accumulation created finance's highest per-capita profits

XTX Markets, founded in London in 2015, has just over 200 employees yet ranks as finance's highest per-capita revenue generator. Founder Alex Kirkal is described as possessing ‘unwavering conviction in compute power’. Guests note that XTX is likely the largest GPU holder in all of finance, with holdings comparable to top AI labs in Silicon Valley. This GPU accumulation is not trend-following—it stems from an early conviction, before ResNet or Transformer even existed, that nonlinear neural networks could predict markets.

— Atlas
34:37

General-purpose large models cannot read the fundamentals of markets

Quantitative models analyze multivariate time series or timestamped tables—fundamentally data-centric, not language- or image-centric. Someone fluent in English and Mandarin who writes Python may still misunderstand markets; the difficulty of knowledge transfer is vastly underestimated. Market data itself carries extreme noise and low signal-to-noise ratio, nothing like the heavily cleaned language datasets. Directly copying language model training paradigms almost certainly fails.

— Atlas
38:00

Execution infrastructure beats data and models as your true competitive moat

Among model, data, and infrastructure, models are least valuable because AI-era models converge, while data is mostly the same raw exchange feeds everyone accesses. True differentiation is infrastructure—both compute and the systems converting predictions into real orders, fills, and risk control. Without execution infrastructure, you cannot access valuable training data. This is especially stark in high-frequency trading, where simulation cannot reliably replace actual market feedback.

— Atlas
45:45

Market feedback feeds your model both signal and toxic noise equally

The claim that ‘market data is abundant and feedback easy to get’is misleading. Raw market data volume is enormous, but independent information is scarce. A single price move may trigger the same event repeatedly. Reward itself is deeply noisy—what you feed your model is simultaneously signal and noise, and whether the model improves or degrades is unclear. This is why superior human traders still function as essential filters.

— Atlas
1:00:54

Single trades teach you nothing; reward only matters statistically

Financial tasks differ fundamentally from domains where AI excels: win rates are low and individual events provide insufficient samples. Reward only becomes meaningful across sufficient statistical samples. Single events or short periods cannot determine signal quality, making the training objective far more complex than other machine-learning tasks.

— Wu Wei
1:04:48

Startups thrive in market niches too small and messy for incumbents

Alpha capacity—the maximum capital a signal can absorb before degrading—creates opportunity. Some signals scale to enormous profits that giants must chase; others apply only to specific trading scenarios, carry high maintenance costs, and cannot scale profitably. Giants skip these due to operational expense, leaving room for startups. Prediction markets like Polymarket are today's closest example. But challenging incumbents like Jane Street, XTX, and Citadel without deep industry experience is nearly impossible.

— Atlas / Wu Wei
1:44:34

Markets as perpetually shifting targets make recursive self-improvement impossible

RSI requires a flywheel: sufficient data plus sufficient feedback. But financial conditions change constantly. If updating a model takes four weeks and a specific market regime lasts two weeks, RSI is fundamentally chasing a past market—always half a beat slow. ‘Never treat the market itself as a fixed dataset or benchmark—the market is a never-ending benchmark. You solve today's problem with today's signal, but tomorrow the market will change.’

— Atlas
2:06:40

Successful quantitative strategies demand the opposite of human instinct

When a strategy shows continuous drawdowns, human instinct is to intervene: tweak parameters, disable the program. Doing so destroys the strategy's statistical distribution, often triggering ‘the final darkness before dawn.’Great quant firms revere risk and discipline. Backtests span years because short-term success is usually survivor bias—you must weather black swans. ‘An ideal quantitative strategy is fundamentally anti-human. That's precisely why it's so difficult.’

— Atlas
