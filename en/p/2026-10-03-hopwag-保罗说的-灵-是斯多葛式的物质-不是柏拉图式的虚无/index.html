The spirit Paul speaks of is Stoic substance, not Platonic void

Paul himself never distinguished the material world from immaterial "spirit"—this dichotomy was imposed only after Platonism took over Christianity later. In Paul and Stoic philosophy, "spirit" is real, physical matter.

Three layers of evidence show Paul understood Stoic philosophy deeply

Judgment of Paul's grasp requires examining evidence in three strata. First: Stoic terms he directly borrowed, such as "appropriate action" and "progress" (prokope). Second: the recurring method of moral exhortation (paraenesis) in his letters matches contemporary Stoics like Seneca's epistles nearly exactly. Third and most crucial: using Stoic concepts to illuminate what Paul himself wanted to express but left opaque—Romans 7's account of struggling under Mosaic law uses the Greek philosophical framework of "weakness of will" (akrasia), precisely what Stoics taught should be entirely overcome as a passion.

10:21

Christian conversion replicates the Stoic sage's cognitive transformation exactly

The Stoics held that initially humans regard food, shelter, and social standing as goods, until recognizing that only "living according to nature" is truly good, making everything else "indifferents"—a purely cognitive shift. Paul describes Christian faith through the identical structure: trust in the fact that God sent his only son Christ from heaven, died on the cross, and rose again triggers the same type of cognitive transformation—not emotional but cognitive, though driven not by reason but by the Holy Spirit.

— Troels Engberg-Pedersen
15:26

The church is Stoic cosmopolitanism made concrete through letters and gathering

Paul calls believers in Philippians "citizens of heaven," echoing Zeno's vision in the Stoic *Republic* of a shared community of sages—belonging to no particular city-state yet constituting a genuine parallel commonwealth. The difference: Stoic cosmopolitanism remains nominal, while Paul's church is tangible—actually writing letters, actually convening, addressing Corinthians and Philippians individually. This groundedness stems from the epistolary form; Stoic philosophical works contain no such specificity.

— Troels Engberg-Pedersen
18:28

Stoic flourishing embeds concern for others within individual virtue itself

Some charge Stoic ethics with being self-centered happiness doctrine, yet this reflects post-Kantian misreading. Aristotle's flourishing merely denotes the state where all one's action-targets are realized; what those targets are is the answer: the virtues regarding others, like justice. Paul's maxim in Philippians—"let each regard not only their own affairs but also those of others"—mirrors this structure exactly: individual flourishing already contains other-regarding content; no separate justification is required.

— Troels Engberg-Pedersen
24:36

Paul's believers already are sages, not striving toward sagehood

Stoics theoretically admitted a perfect sage might exist but claimed never to have met one, rare as the mythical phoenix. Paul instead asserts that believers are not working toward the sage ideal but have already reached it—what Aristotelian ethics asks, "How are virtue and practical wisdom actualized," stands already actualized in Paul's believers. Why then continued exhortation? Because exhortatory discourse itself presumes its audience has already witnessed that ideal, already entered "Christ's circle"; exhortation only reminds rather than persuades from nothing.

— Troels Engberg-Pedersen
27:38

Stoic indifference becomes Paul's instrument to obliterate all status hierarchies

Stoic "indifferents" originally designated worldly values like wealth and family as intrinsically valueless; Paul applies it in two registers. First, to race and station: in Christ Jesus, circumcision and uncircumcision are indifferent, Jew and Greek are indifferent, slave and free are indifferent, male and female are indifferent. Second, to daily living: whether to marry, whether to remain enslaved—all indifferent provided one clings to neither state, because "the form of this world is passing away." This grafts Stoic philosophy onto Paul's own Jewish apocalyptic eschatology.

— Troels Engberg-Pedersen
29:40

The pneuma in Paul is matter, not Platonic abstraction or void

Modern ears hear "Holy Spirit" and default to Platonic immateriality, but that understanding is a later development—when Platonism absorbed Christianity in the 2nd–3rd centuries, the split between material world and immaterial realm crystallized. That distinction is wholly absent in Paul; Stoicism itself is monist, developed precisely against Platonic (and partial Aristotelian) dualism. When Paul in 1 Corinthians 15 compares resurrection bodies to sun, moon, and stars, he concludes a kind of "spiritual body" exists—to be read literally, materially, not as figure.

— Troels Engberg-Pedersen
36:41

Asking if Paul was theologian or philosopher misconstrues ancient categories

Against the claim that Paul is a theologian not a philosopher and belongs nowhere in philosophy's history, the reply is: Plato, Aristotle, and the Stoics all discussed God and ethics together—both fell under philosophy's single umbrella then. The sharp opposition between theology and philosophy is a later categorical distinction. While Paul nowhere claims to philosophize directly, in 1 Corinthians 1 he contrasts the cross's message with Greek wisdom-seeking and Jewish seeking of divine power, yet declares: "Among the mature, we speak wisdom." He is not another Greek philosopher but an anomaly, expressing his message in Greek philosophical terms.
