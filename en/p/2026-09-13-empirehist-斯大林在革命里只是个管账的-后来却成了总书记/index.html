Stalin Was Just the Bookkeeper of the Revolution, and Then He Became General Secretary

In 1917, while Lenin and Trotsky were on the streets calling for insurrection, Stalin was doing the organizing and fundraising nobody else wanted. He became General Secretary not by oratory but by sitting at the table.

5:28

Even his birthday was something he changed himself

The parish register, the seminary certificate, the tsarist police file and his own handwritten questionnaire from the 1920s all say 18 December 1878. But the birthday he announced once in power was 21 December 1879 — a year and three days off. Bob Service offers one judgment in his biography: forgery was too easy for him. Service also has an unprovable but persuasive speculation — that changing his birthday helped him dodge conscription when he was young. The thing itself is the theme of the whole episode: this man's attitude toward facts was instrumental.

6:35

How anomalous it was for a Georgian to rule Moscow

His real name was Joseph Jugashvili, and he was born in Georgia. Georgia is in the Caucasus, next to Armenia, just north of Iran and Turkey, very far from Russia; it has its own language, its own alphabet, and its religion is Georgian Orthodoxy, which is not the same thing as Russian Orthodoxy. The analogy the episode gives is this: a man of Georgian Orthodox background climbing to the top of power in Moscow is equivalent to a man of Irish Catholic background from some remote place like Limerick becoming master of London in the mid-twentieth century. For Georgians at the time, being governed by Moscow meant being colonized.

8:43

His father refused to change trades, then started hitting people

His father Bessarion (nicknamed Besso) was a cobbler with his own workshop in Gori, and by most accounts a good craftsman. But he refused to keep up with the times: he went on making Georgian-style soft leather high boots while the market had already turned to European shoes. He did not adapt; he became bitter, drank heavily, and the violence fell mainly on his wife Ekaterina (Kiki) and little Joseph, who was nicknamed "Crazy Besso." Stalin later told his daughter Svetlana one scene: his father was drunk and beating his mother, he grabbed a knife and threw it at his father, missed, his father charged at him, and he ran to a neighbor's house and hid there for several days until his father's rage passed.

— Anita Arnon / William de Rumpel
11:52

Smallpox, a carriage, and a left arm that was too short

Around age six he caught smallpox, severe enough that his mother thought he would die — two older brothers had already died in infancy, and the family was too poor to afford a doctor, so they could only call a local folk healer. He survived, but his face was permanently scarred, and every official photograph was retouched. Later a runaway carriage hit him and broke his arm; the left arm never healed properly and was noticeably shorter and stiffer for the rest of his life. The episode's formulation is: smallpox did not take him, his father's violence did not take him, and the carriage did not take him either. His mother was extremely devoted to him and had ambitious plans for him.

— Anita Arnon / William de Rumpel
16:07

He wrote poetry, and he was good at it

In his late teens two things happened at once: he began writing quite good poetry, and he entered the seminary. The episode reads one poem aloud: "The pink buds have already blossomed, toward the pale violet violets; brushed by the breeze, the lily of the valley bends down over the grass. Flowers, my Georgia. May peace come to my native land, and may you, friends, make our homeland famous through study." Anita says that in the original Georgian it is quite beautiful. It shows two things: he was a proud Georgian, not a Russian; and in the tsarist context, this way of writing about a "homeland" was already close to sedition.

— Anita Arnon
19:09

The seminary was the tsar's Russification machine

In 1894 he won a place at the Tbilisi seminary. The sign over the school said Georgian Orthodox, but in practice it was under the control of the Russian Orthodox Church, one of the institutions the tsar used to "Russify" the borderlands. Students were kept under close surveillance, and reports were written constantly: were these people good subjects of the tsar, were they talking sedition. And right under that surveillance, he was reading banned books, including What Is to Be Done? and other big questions about freedom and the individual. Anita's comment in the episode is: the surveillance here was not done well enough.

— Anita Arnon / William de Rumpel
21:11

He gave himself the name of a bandit

On the banned list was a Georgian novel, Alexander Kazbegi's The Patricide, whose protagonist is a mountain fugitive named Koba. He took that name as his own alias, and at the seminary his classmates called him by it. The episode calls this a kind of "name determinism": he first took the name of a bandit hero, and then he really did become a bandit. The turning point from seminary student to bandit began when he saw with his own eyes two mountain bandits publicly hanged in the center of Gori for shooting a policeman over a stray cow — he was forbidden to watch, but he went.

— Anita Arnon / William de Rumpel
24:16

They stole 250,000 rubles and could not spend a single one

In 1907 he and a group of friends ambushed a bank carriage in Yerevan Square in Tbilisi, almost within sight of the seminary: some stood up high and threw bombs down, others fired in the street, and the man who organized, financed and directed it from behind the scenes was Stalin, under the alias Koba. They got away with 250,000 rubles packed in linen sacks. But the bank had already published the serial numbers of its largest-denomination notes, so not one ruble of the money could be spent. It was the first time he left a police photograph on file, but he did not go to prison for it and essentially walked away untouched.

— Anita Arnon / William de Rumpel
29:32

The Bolsheviks were the majority, the Mensheviks the intellectuals

Lenin came from a middle-class Russian family; in 1887 his older brother was hanged for attempting to assassinate the tsar, which radicalized the younger brother completely; he was expelled from university for attending too many protests and was exiled to Siberia in the 1890s. By then he was already the leader of the radical Bolshevik wing of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party. The party was an umbrella organization trying to hold many currents together, a judgment that later proved badly wrong. The Bolsheviks were the majority faction within the party, the Mensheviks were more intellectuals and the educated class; what the Bolsheviks wanted was "do it now, do it fast, whatever the cost."

— Anita Arnon / William de Rumpel
30:38

In 1917 he was not at the front, he was keeping the books

Stalin caught Lenin's attention with articles on the national question, and in 1912 he was brought onto the Bolshevik Central Committee. What Lenin noticed was this: the man was highly organized, could handle the somewhat dull organizational work, made sure the money arrived — the classic behind-the-scenes job. In 1917, when Lenin and Trotsky were outside calling for insurrection, he was not there — he was not a good orator, not a man who stood at the front. The episode says this would later sting him: Trotsky would repeatedly point out that while everyone was bleeding for the glorious revolution, this man was keeping the accounts.

Glossary

Koba
Stalin's alias from his seminary years, taken from the mountain bandit protagonist of the novel The Patricide.
Bolsheviks / Mensheviks
The two wings of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party; the former were the majority and more radical, the latter were mostly intellectuals.
Russification
The blanket term for the tsar's policy of remaking the borderlands in Russian culture, language and religion.
