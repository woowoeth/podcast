Canada Wants Into the EU: This Is a Permanent Break With the US, Not a Wait for Trump to Leave

Canada wants to be the EU's first "associate member," but the EU has no such status. It's a generational pivot toward Europe and away from the US — and Europe's economy grows at half America's rate.

Canada wants to be the EU's first associate member, but the status doesn't exist

The Wall Street Journal first reported that Canada is negotiating to become an associate member of the EU. But the show says it plainly: the EU has no such thing as an "associate member" — you're either a member, paying into the budget, accepting free movement of people and seamless truck crossings, or you're not. All 27 current members are in Europe, sharing borders, regulations, and mostly the euro. So what Canada gets will be a status invented for it, with details still under negotiation. What Carney wants isn't another buyer but a group of "like-minded" countries to build, together, the institutions and technological capabilities that America's "wrecking ball" can't reach.

3:01

Canada once saw itself as Europeans living in North America

This isn't a new idea but a return to an old identity. In the first half of the 20th century, some Canadians genuinely saw themselves as "Europeans living in North America" — today's oldest Canadians were born British subjects, and Canada didn't open an embassy in Washington until the 1920s. On the eve of World War II, Canada's trade with Britain still exceeded its trade with the US. After the war America became the center of gravity of the West, the auto industry integrated in the 1960s, NAFTA took effect in 1994, and Canadian elites switched from going to Oxford and Cambridge to going to Harvard and Yale. Trump's tensions reopened a forgotten historical question: where do Canadians belong in the world?

— Drew Hinshaw
6:13

The 51st state isn't a trade negotiation, it's a sovereignty question

When Trump returned for a second term, he didn't just want to renegotiate a trade deal; he questioned the territorial integrity of allies like Canada and Denmark, and said openly he wanted Canada to become the 51st state. The show's judgment: if it were only a trade renegotiation, you could negotiate; once sovereignty is touched, you're touching something highly emotional, sensitive, electorally meaningful and bound up with a country's sense of itself. One Canadian official, after reading every Trump biography he could find, concluded that the 51st state talk was "price discovery" — throwing out an outrageous idea to test the reaction. Whatever the intent, the effect was real: Carney went from 25 points behind in the polls to winning, overnight.

— Joe Parkinson
8:17

Carney defined the trade war as "we are being attacked"

Carney had two signature moments after taking office. One was the Davos speech, landing right in the middle of the Greenland crisis, with Trump due to speak the next day; Carney went first and delivered the clearest anti-Trumpist manifesto yet: Canadians' old comfortable assumption that geography and alliances automatically deliver prosperity and security no longer holds. The second came when the US imposed tariffs — instead of backing down he escalated, hitting back with tariffs of his own, and after the final round of trade talks collapsed he still wouldn't budge. Asked at a press conference why it felt like war was about to start today, his answer was: "Because we are being attacked." The show says that for a technocrat and centrist to escalate is counterintuitive, and that is precisely what made him seen as the leader of the Western allies' anti-Trump movement — for better or worse.

— Joe Parkinson
11:22

Carney's European network matters more than his policies

A prime minister of a North American country has an unusually large number of European friends: a doctorate in economics from Oxford, the first foreigner to run the Bank of England. Macron was a banker, German Chancellor Merz worked at BlackRock, Finnish President Stubb also came from finance, and Carney knows them all. In Ottawa he also has a book club whose members are political heavyweights and industry leaders, with a Fight Club-style rule — "the first rule of book club is you don't talk about book club." Most of them were educated at Oxford or Cambridge, are familiar with Europe, and remain in touch with the prime minister, feeding him exactly the kinds of ideas about how to bring Canada closer to Europe. The show says interviewees were far more secretive about the book club than about Canada's plan to join the EU.

— Joe Parkinson
14:27

What's on the table isn't just trade — it's data cables and Erasmus

The two sides are discussing far more than tariffs: laying data cables connecting Europe and Canada; jointly building data centers, cloud storage and satellite networks that sit beyond the reach of American tech giants; building new pipelines in Canada to send natural gas to the East Coast and on to Europe; using icebreakers to open Arctic shipping routes for fertilizer. They also plan to entwine Canadian and EU research institutions to create an academic and scientific output network that can rival the American system. Canada is even pushing to join the EU's Erasmus student exchange program — the show says this is a big deal in Europe, and has led to a lot of marriages. If a generation of Canadians goes to school in Paris, Spain and Italy, and a generation of Italians comes to Ottawa, Montreal and Vancouver, then what's being discussed isn't just economics but social ties deeper than the flow of natural gas and minerals.

— Joe Parkinson
17:45

The EU's red line: no one can think you can quit and still get the benefits

For Europe, Canada is a "shiny new toy": it has raw materials, critical minerals and energy Europe has long coveted, its values are close, and Carney speaking French and English and making his first trip to Paris and London rather than Washington means a lot to Europeans. But the risks are just as clear. The EU is haunted by Brexit, and its long-standing red line is that no one can ever be allowed to think you can leave the club and keep enjoying the benefits — if major countries start quitting and demanding a Canada-style deal, that would be fatal to the EU. So the puzzle Carney has to solve is: get Canada a large share of trade and connectivity benefits without creating a precedent that lets a country like Italy say, "We don't like these regulations — let's quit and become a Canada-style member, no rules but all the benefits."

— Joe Parkinson
19:54

The real pain hasn't arrived yet, and the White House is betting geography decides fate

There are concerns inside Canada: how much sovereignty would an ambiguous associate membership cost, which regulations would it accept, what happens to immigration — conservative voters and mainstream newspaper columnists are all asking. But the bigger fear is the cost of decoupling from the US, because so far it's only a war of words and the real pain hasn't hit the Canadian economy yet: two-thirds of exports go to the US, and that's a lot of people's businesses and jobs. The White House's judgment is that geography is destiny, Canada is right there, decades of economic ties are real, and in the end Canada will be pulled back to the negotiating table — flirting with Europe won't change economic reality. You can't force Canadian businesses to do business in Europe when a few miles — kilometers, in Canada — across the border sits a tempting market.

— Joe Parkinson
20:56

They judge the problem to be deeper than Trump: it's America itself

Why not just wait for Trump to leave? The show offers a key detail: when the 51st state remark first came out, a Canadian official said they couldn't see many people in Congress speaking up for Canada — not Republicans, and almost no Democrats either — as if when Trump says something like that, everyone just shrugs, another day of the Trump show. But for Canada this was a very serious event, and from it they concluded the problem runs deeper than Trump: it's America itself. The show also concedes whether that reading is fair is another question, but politically that's how things get digested. Canadian officials repeatedly stress they don't want to provoke or retaliate against the US, and that what they want is a new administration and a recalibrated relationship; but given their exposure, they feel they have to fundamentally change themselves.

Glossary

associate member
The EU status Canada wants, but no such tier exists in the EU's current system.
Erasmus
The EU's cross-border student exchange program, letting students from one member state study for a year in another.
NAFTA
Took effect in 1994, dismantled US-Canada-Mexico trade barriers and profoundly changed Canadian society.
