The Vandals weren't barbarians: cutting the tax supply is what killed the Western Empire

In Roman sources the early Vandals were a minor people who lost nine battles out of ten, yet by seizing North Africa and severing Rome's tax revenue they were able to sack Rome in 455; the word ‘vandalism’, meanwhile, was invented as a political metaphor during the French Revolution.

12:29

The Vandals didn't fight their way up — they lost nine of ten battles

When the Vandals crossed the Rhine in 406 they were not a formidable military force. Andy Merrills points out that of the 10 battles recorded for them between the third and fourth centuries and the late 420s, they lost the first 9 — to the point that the fifth-century Christian writer Salvian used them as a byword for people ‘no good at fighting’. But the Western Roman Empire was collapsing at the time, with warlords, usurpers and rebels operating everywhere, and even an armed group that could not win battles could acquire influence in a power vacuum. The contrast makes the point: the Vandals rose not through force of arms but through the disintegration of Roman order.

15:00

Twenty thousand fighters were enough to rewrite a rich province's fate

On the size of the Vandal crossing from Spain into North Africa, the two main sources both give a figure of 80,000, and both say the number is slightly exaggerated. Scholars generally take the 80,000 to cover the whole population, of whom roughly 15,000 to 20,000 were fighting men — in the North Africa of that period, a force on the scale of a single field army, against a local population of two to three million. Andy stresses that whether you treat it as a small war band or a large migration, the order of magnitude is about the same; the numbers were small, but they entered a wealthy province that had long been demilitarised, which is why the impact was disproportionate.

— Andy Merrills
19:39

North Africa was richer than Spain, and was deliberately left ungarrisoned

Why, having already taken Baetica, the richest part of Spain, did the Vandals cross the Strait of Gibraltar into North Africa? Andy offers a fact modern audiences routinely underestimate: in the Roman period, what is now North Africa was far richer than Spain. The province of Africa and Numidia were the wealthiest regions of the Western Roman Empire, and the empire deliberately kept no troops there — emperors worried that a general would build a personal power base on the spot. Rich but undefended, North Africa looked to the Vandals like ripe fruit. This section explains what drew them into the strategic choice they made.

— Andy Merrills
22:53

The kingdom's year one was the fall of Carthage, not an accession

The Vandals did not conquer North Africa overnight. They landed around 428 or 429, besieged Hippo — where Saint Augustine died — signed a treaty with the Western Roman Empire in 435, and took Carthage only in 439. Geiseric treated 439 as ‘year one’ of his reign, and later coins and documents counted from that year rather than from his accession. Carthage was one of the most important cities in the Western Roman Empire at the time, and its fall marks the real beginning of the Vandal kingdom. It also set up what came later: the severing of Rome's tax revenue.

— Andy Merrills
26:32

What destroyed the Western Empire was not the sword but the broken tax flow

The crucial change brought by the capture of Carthage in 439 was not destruction but the rupture of tax flows. Until then the tax revenue of North Africa had been the main artery of the Mediterranean economic system, moving north to pay for Rome's administration and armies; once the Vandals took over, that money stopped leaving. Andy argues that if the Vandals bear responsibility for the fall of the Western Roman Empire, this is the heart of it — they did not use the sword, they cut the fiscal artery and disabled the ‘glue’ holding the empire's politics and economy together. He emphasises that trade continued after the treaty of 442, but the political and economic bond had already become fragile.

— Andy Merrills
30:00

The 455 sack was more lethal to Rome than the one in 410

After Valentinian III was killed, Geiseric tore up the peace agreement, attacked mainland Italy and, in 455, sacked Rome, carrying off enormous wealth — including the treasures the Romans had taken from the Temple in Jerusalem in AD 70 — and also taking the Roman princess Eudocia back to Carthage to marry his son. Andy points out that this sack was in fact more damaging in Roman history than Alaric's sack of 410, but 410 caused the greater shock because Rome had not been breached for 800 years. After 455 the Western Roman Empire had almost no emperor with real power, and its political centre declined rapidly.

— Andy Merrills
41:09

Tolerance was Geiseric's choice; persecution belonged to his successor

The Vandals were Homoian Christians, while the dominant local faith in North Africa was Nicene, and the region carried deep memories of martyrdom and persecution. Geiseric broadly tolerated the coexistence of both confessions, requiring only that people at court be Homoian, and showed little enthusiasm for conversion; his successor Huneric, however, launched clear persecution in the autumn of 484: closing churches, executing and exiling Catholics. Andy cautions that most of these accounts come from the pens of the oppressed Catholics and need to be sifted, but religious tension was genuinely an important source of instability in the later Vandal kingdom.

— Andy Merrills
49:38

The Vandals are forgotten because their kingdom lay outside Europe

The Vandals built a kingdom spanning North Africa and the islands of the Mediterranean, yet today they are far less well known than the Huns and the Goths. Andy thinks the key reason is that their kingdom was in North Africa: after the Islamic conquest of North Africa in the seventh century, this history was absorbed into Africa's own grand narrative rather than into the standard curriculum of European schools. By contrast, peoples such as the Anglo-Saxons and the Franks all entered the founding stories of European nation states, and the Goths left Gothic architecture behind. The Vandal name was never tied to any region or cultural tradition, and in the end all that remains of it is an invented label.

— Andy Merrills

Glossary

Homoian Christianity
The confession holding that the Son is similar in substance to the Father, opposed to the Nicene position and accepted by most Germanic peoples.
Nicene Christianity
The orthodox confession holding that Father and Son are of one substance (homoousios), dominant in the later Roman Empire.
Hasdingi
The royal branch of the Vandals; Geiseric came from this family.
Vandalism
The concept of wilful destruction derived from the Vandals' name, later coined by Bishop Grégoire during the French Revolution.
