The Vandals Were Not Barbarians: Cutting Off Tax Revenue Was What Killed the Western Roman Empire

Early on, the Vandals were a small tribe that lost nine out of ten battles in Roman records, yet by seizing North Africa and cutting off Rome's tax revenue, they sacked Rome in 455; the word "vandalism" was a political metaphor invented during the French Revolution.

This episode uses concrete historical evidence to overturn the stereotype of "barbarians as destroyers": the Vandals' rise depended on timing and geography, not military might; it is more useful than common narratives for understanding the real mechanisms behind the collapse of the Western Roman Empire.

The Vandals' rise was not about fighting prowess; they lost nine of ten battles

When the Vandals crossed the Rhine in 406 AD, they were not a fearsome military force. Andy Merrills points out that in the ten documented battles from the third and fourth centuries to the late 420s, they lost the first nine, so much so that the fifth-century Christian writer Salvian used them as a byword for "not good at fighting." But the Western Roman Empire was collapsing, with warlords, usurpers, and rebels everywhere; even an armed group that could not win battles could gain influence in the power vacuum. This contrast shows that the Vandals' rise relied not on force but on the disintegration of Roman order.

15:00

Twenty thousand warriors were enough to change the fate of a wealthy province

Regarding the size of the Vandal population that crossed from Spain into North Africa, the two main sources both give a figure of 80,000, and both say this number is somewhat exaggerated. Scholars generally believe that the 80,000 included the entire population, with fighting men numbering about 15,000 to 20,000—in North Africa at the time, this was the size of a field army, while the local population was two to three million. Andy emphasizes that whether one views it as a small war band or a large migration, the scale is similar; although the numbers were small, they entered a wealthy province that had long been demilitarized, so they could have a disproportionate impact.

— Andy Merrills
19:39

North Africa was richer than Spain and deliberately ungarrisoned

Why did the Vandals, having already obtained Baetica, the richest part of Spain, cross the Strait of Gibraltar to North Africa? Andy offers a fact often underestimated by moderns: in Roman times, North Africa was far richer than Spain. The provinces of Africa and Numidia were the wealthiest in the Western Roman Empire, and the empire deliberately stationed no troops there—emperors feared that generals would establish their own power bases locally. Rich but defenseless, North Africa seemed like a "ripe fruit" to the Vandals. This explains the incentives behind the Vandal strategic choice.

— Andy Merrills
22:53

The kingdom's year one was not the accession but the fall of Carthage

The Vandals did not conquer North Africa overnight. They landed around 428 or 429, besieged Hippo, where Saint Augustine lay dying, signed a treaty with the Western Roman Empire in 435, and only captured Carthage in 439. Geiseric regarded 439 as the "year one" of his reign, and later coinage and documents were dated from that year, not from his accession. Carthage was one of the most important cities of the Western Roman Empire at the time; its fall marked the true beginning of the Vandal kingdom. This also set the stage for cutting off Rome's tax revenue.

— Andy Merrills
26:32

What killed the Western Roman Empire was not the sword but severed tax revenue

The key change brought by the occupation of Carthage in 439 was not destruction but the severing of tax flows. Previously, North African taxes, as the aorta of the Mediterranean economic system, flowed north to supply Rome's administration and army; after the Vandals took over, that money stopped flowing out. Andy argues that if the Vandals bear responsibility for the fall of the Western Roman Empire, it lies here—they did not use the sword but cut the fiscal artery, rendering the "glue" of imperial politics and economy ineffective. He emphasizes that after the treaty of 442, trade continued, but the political and economic bonds had become fragile.

— Andy Merrills
30:00

The sack of 455 was more lethal than that of 410

After Valentinian III was killed, Geiseric tore up the peace treaty, not only attacking Italy itself but also sacking Rome in 455, carrying off great wealth, including the treasures that the Romans had taken from the Temple in Jerusalem in 70 AD, and taking the Roman princess Eudocia back to Carthage to marry his son. Andy points out that this sack was actually more lethal in Roman history than Alaric's sack of 410, but the 410 sack caused greater shock because Rome had not been captured for 800 years. After 455, the Western Roman Empire almost never had a truly powerful emperor again, and the political center declined rapidly.

— Andy Merrills
41:09

Tolerance was Geiseric's choice; persecution was his successor's

The Vandals were Homoian (Arian) Christians, while the mainstream of North African Christianity was Nicene, and the region had a deep memory of martyrdom and persecution. Geiseric generally tolerated the coexistence of the two sects, only requiring that courtiers be Homoian, and was not keen on conversion; but his successor Huneric launched a clear persecution in the autumn of 484: closing churches, executing and exiling Catholics. Andy reminds us that these accounts mostly come from the persecuted Catholics themselves and need to be scrutinized, but religious tension was indeed an important factor in the instability of the later Vandal kingdom.

— Andy Merrills
49:38

The Vandals are forgotten because their kingdom was not in Europe

The Vandals established a kingdom spanning North Africa and Mediterranean islands, yet today they are far less famous than the Huns or the Goths. Andy argues that the key reason is that their kingdom was in North Africa: after the Islamic conquest of North Africa in the seventh century, this history was absorbed into Africa's own grand narrative, not into the standard curriculum of European schools. In contrast, the Anglo-Saxons, Franks, and other groups entered the founding stories of European nation-states, and the Goths even left Gothic architecture. The Vandals' name was not tied to any region or cultural tradition, leaving only an invented label.

Glossary

Homoian Christianity
A sect holding that the Son is similar in substance to the Father, opposed to Nicene Christianity, and adopted by most Germanic tribes.
Nicene Christianity
The orthodox sect holding that the Father and the Son are of the same substance (homoousios), dominant in the late Roman Empire.
Hasdingi
The royal lineage of the Vandals, from which Geiseric came.
Vandalism
The concept of destructive behavior derived from the name of the Vandals, later coined by Bishop Grégoire during the French Revolution.
