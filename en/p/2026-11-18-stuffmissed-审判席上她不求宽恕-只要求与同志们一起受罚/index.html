In court, she asked not for mercy but to be judged with her comrades

The French government thought exile and imprisonment would tame her; instead, Louise Michel refused to defend herself in court, publicly admitted to every act in the Paris Commune, and demanded a death sentence.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:02

Born outside marriage, she had access to education that most rural girls lacked

Her parents never married, and she was raised by her grandparents in what locals called ‘the grave’—a crumbling castle. This marginal but relatively affluent position gave her something rare for a rural girl of her era: access to books, neighborhood reading groups, and education. This would later fuel her challenge to educational inequality between men and women.

8:07

At twelve, she refused marriage and never reconsidered

When she was twelve or thirteen, two adult men proposed to her grandfather. Unusually, he let her decide. She shamed the first suitor with a quote from Molière, and dismissed the second more directly. This was not teenage rebellion—her memoirs state clearly that she rejected marriage for life, seeing it as fundamentally a torment inflicted on women.

16:20

She aimed to erase the line between boys' education and girls' education

From the start of her teaching career, Michel worked to eliminate the difference between how boys and girls were educated. She observed that schools filled girls with ‘a pile of drivel propping up infantile logic’ while forcing boys to swallow ‘science pill by pill’—both outcomes she found absurd. This was not abstract principle: her school taught boys and girls together and ran night classes to stay open.

23:23

The Paris Commune gave her a chance to implement her program

When the Commune was established, Michel's demands were specific, not abstract: equal education for girls and boys, coordination between schools and families, career paths for young women after graduation, inclusion of disabled children in regular classes, equal pay for male and female teachers, and basic rights to food and shelter for all. Her anarchism was not hollow destruction; it was a complete social welfare platform.

27:30

At trial, she asked the court to sentence her to death

The Versailles military court charged her with attempting to overthrow the government, inciting civil war, bearing arms in military dress, forging documents, and participating in executions of hostages. Michel admitted every charge, asked for no mercy, confessed to setting fires to block the Versailles army, and demanded the death penalty. The court sentenced her to exile in New Caledonia instead.

30:34

In exile, she turned her fight against the colonizers

Exiled to New Caledonia in the South Pacific, Michel aligned herself with the Kanak indigenous people against French colonizers, openly urging them to take up arms. She also taught them French so they could negotiate with the colonists themselves. Her anarchism did not fade in exile; it found new ground.

32:36

Bread, work, or bullets: the 1883 uprising

In 1883, she organized unemployed Parisians in a demonstration that turned into a riot. Protesters chanted ‘Bread, work, or bullets’—a warning that failure to address jobs and hunger would bring violence. She reportedly carried a black flag, which would later become anarchism's symbol, though her role in hoisting it remains unconfirmed. After weeks in hiding, she turned herself in for trial.

37:44

The state tried to prove she was mad rather than try her again

When arrested again in 1890, the authorities did not proceed with trial. Instead they attempted to have her declared mentally ill so they could institutionalize her. After her release, she fled to London, where she spent five years giving lectures, running an anarchist school, and organizing, until 1895 when she returned to Paris, the city she loved.
