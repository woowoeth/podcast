The Cost of Banning Kidney Sales: 130,000 New Cases a Year, Fewer Than 30,000 Transplants

The US sees 130,000 new kidney failure cases a year and fewer than 30,000 transplants; Al Roth unpacks where the repugnance toward organ markets comes from, and argues that regulated compensation could save lives without exploiting anyone.

Ninety thousand people wait for kidneys, and the ban can't say who it protects

The episode opens with a brutal number: about 90,000 people are on the US kidney transplant waiting list, most will never get a kidney, and some will die waiting. And the solution may already exist — many healthy people would donate a kidney for pay if the law allowed it. But society chooses to ban it. From this the host poses the core question of the whole episode: ‘When we say it's not allowed, what exactly are we protecting?’ That question sets up the framework for discussing repugnant transactions: both parties consent, and opponents may not even be able to observe whether the transaction happens.

6:46

Bans with a victim are the ones actually enforced

Al Roth uses a pair of contrasts to show the social basis of market bans: buying heroin and hiring a hitman are both illegal transactions, but society enforces them in wildly different ways. If you ask where to buy heroin, the other person may just be surprised; if you ask where to hire a hitman, they may call the police, and the police will set up a sting. Both are felonies, yet prisons are full of drug dealers and have almost no hitmen. Why? Because contract killing has a clear victim, while drug users enter the transaction willingly. Whether a ban is strictly enforced depends on society's shared judgment about ‘harm’.

— Al Roth
9:28

The organ shortage isn't a technology problem, it's a supply arrangement problem

Kidney failure is one of the top ten causes of death in the US, with about 130,000 new cases a year, yet the country performs fewer than 30,000 transplants a year. Dialysis is not an ideal substitute: half of patients die within five years, and the treatment itself is close to torture — three times a week, four hours each time, leaving people too exhausted to hold down a job. Al Roth stresses that transplant is the treatment of choice, yet it is scarce to the point of being ‘tragically inadequate’. These numbers explain why the organ shortage is not a technology problem but a supply arrangement problem.

— Al Roth
15:00

Seventy percent of kidney sellers are poor, and that still doesn't condemn the market

Iran is the only country in the world that legally pays for kidneys. Al Roth's colleague Akbarpour found that between 2012 and 2017, about 70% of Iranian kidney sellers were below the median wealth level, while only 41% of patients were, and sellers generally did not want their identities made public. But Al Roth refuses to treat this as the market's original sin: poor people work in coal mines and rich people use electricity, and an unequal income distribution by itself cannot condemn a market. What really matters is how to prevent vulnerable people from being exploited, not rejecting compensation itself because the distribution is unequal.

— Al Roth
22:27

Exploitation risk can be managed by design, without banning it outright

Market design can defuse the worry about ‘the rich buying kidneys and the poor selling them’. Al Roth proposes amending the National Organ Transplant Act so that only the federal government can pay for kidneys, then distributing them through a bureaucratic allocation system based on medical need, with no regard to income at all. At the same time, very robust informed consent is needed to prevent someone from impulsively selling a kidney because their car broke down or rent is due, and regretting it afterward. He admits the risk of exploitation cannot be eliminated entirely, but argues these risks can be managed through design rather than banned outright.

— Al Roth
29:49

Two hundred more kidney donors a year would prove compensation works

Al Roth says the death numbers have always weighed on him: when he started his research, the list of people waiting for a deceased donor's kidney had 40,000 people on it, and now it is close to 100,000. He supports an experimental bill called the End Kidney Deaths Act, which would give non-directed donors (people who donate anonymously and start a transplant chain) a refundable tax credit over five years. Today there are about 500 such donors a year; if that could rise to 700, it would prove compensation really can expand supply, and because transplants are cheaper than dialysis, taxpayers would not spend more.

— Al Roth
36:28

Addiction destroys consent, so drugs can't copy the organ playbook

Drugs are different from organs: addiction strips buyers of the basis for genuine consent, so the market solution cannot simply be applied. Al Roth admits we are losing the war on drugs, but stresses the problem cannot be solved by surrender or by pure force either. He argues for treating cities as democratic laboratories and running more experiments: clean needle exchanges have already been shown to reduce the spread of hepatitis and AIDS, and safe injection sites are worth trying too. If addicts are also mentally ill and homeless, then real, usable treatment must be provided in prison, not just talked about.

— Al Roth
47:39

Before arguing whether AI deserves moral concern, we'll fight over its limits

Al Roth thinks AI will be the next source of ‘repugnant transaction’ controversy. We chose pigs over chimpanzees for transplant organs because we feel chimpanzees are almost human; the same moral obligation may someday arise toward ‘silicon-based intelligence’. He predicts that before we reach ‘whether AI deserves moral concern’, we will first argue over the limits of AI use — for example, restrictions on the San Francisco police using facial recognition, and how privacy rights get redefined in an environment full of cameras. All of this will challenge our existing boundaries between ‘natural’ and ‘disgusting’.

— Al Roth

Glossary

repugnant transaction
A transaction that both parties enter willingly but that onlookers believe should not be allowed.
market design
The branch of economics that treats markets as buildable tools and improves allocation outcomes through rule design.
non-directed donor
A living donor who donates a kidney anonymously and does not know the recipient's identity.
