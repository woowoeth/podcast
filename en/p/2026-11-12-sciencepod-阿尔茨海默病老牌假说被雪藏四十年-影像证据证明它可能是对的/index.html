Shelved forty years, an old Alzheimer's theory finds vindication in imaging

A young scientist uses imaging data to show that degradation of deep-brain cholinergic neurons precedes, rather than follows, cortical amyloid deposition—reviving an old theory sidelined by the amyloid hypothesis.

Drug-induced amnesia led researchers to the cholinergic hypothesis

Researchers discovered that drugs blocking acetylcholine signaling induced a memory deficit resembling that seen in aging and early Alzheimer's. Subsequent autopsy studies found that the nucleus basalis of Meynert—a deep-brain hub where cholinergic neurons project to cortical memory and attention areas—was severely damaged late in disease. This led pathologists to suspect it was among the earliest damaged structures. By the early 1980s these findings coalesced into the cholinergic hypothesis: Alzheimer's memory loss and age-related memory loss might be the same process, differing only in degree.

— Jenny Smith
8:14

The amyloid hypothesis displaced both the old theory and old drugs

When the amyloid hypothesis was formally proposed in the early 1990s, a cohort of cholinergic molecules already showing promise—including drugs targeting cognitive receptors with fewer side effects—was shelved outright. The entire field pivoted to focus on the protein deemed the culprit. Mutations in familial Alzheimer's patients all pointed to amyloid generation pathways; the evidence seemed "locked in," and cholinergic research was crowded out of mainstream view.

— Jenny Smith
9:20

Amyloid drugs clear plaques beautifully but barely improve cognition

Anti-amyloid antibodies do cleanly remove plaques and their precursor forms, but the cognitive benefit is only "30% slowing" of decline—not improvement—and temporary and limited. This gap has led researchers to revive shelved cholinergic drugs. Xanomeline, originally developed for schizophrenia (now marketed as Cobenfy), showed in early trials on Alzheimer's patients that it could improve cognition; it's being retested now.

— Jenny Smith
12:25

Imaging data challenges where Alzheimer's begins

A recently graduated young scientist used MRI and PET data to challenge the conventional wisdom: does Alzheimer's really start in the entorhinal cortex? His research showed instead that nucleus basalis of Meynert (the cholinergic hub) degeneration predicts subsequent entorhinal changes, not the reverse. New tools like optogenetics let researchers for the first time observe these neurons in detail, refocusing the field on the overlooked deep-brain structures.

— Jenny Smith
22:50

America's power grid bottleneck is policy, not engineering

Despite heavy investment, America has built high-voltage transmission lines and capacity very slowly over recent decades. Near-term consequences: surging data-center demand pushes up electricity prices. Long-term: harder transition to renewables and less resilience against extreme weather outages lasting days. Technology is not the bottleneck; incentives and policy design are.

— Madeline Yazwick
26:54

Territorial monopolies lock in rules from the 1930s

In the 1920s–30s, multiple utilities duplicated lines in the same regions, inefficiently, while large areas had no power at all. States granted single companies exclusive supply rights for specific territories in exchange for giving up pricing power; the state approved cost recovery. This "territorial" governance model persists, but problems now exceed single-company jurisdictions. Utah solar reaching Las Vegas requires cross-state coordination no one company can deliver.

— Madeline Yazwick
34:06

Direct current lets power lines hide underground

U.S. transmission relies on AC; long-distance DC was historically too expensive. Voltage source converter (VSC-HVDC) technology now makes DC economic in some cases. The advantage: cables bury underground along existing roads and rail corridors—the Champlain-Hudson line in New York runs directly under the Hudson River. This avoids seizing private land and sidesteps opposition to high-voltage towers, potentially speeding project completion.

— Madeline Yazwick
43:22

Every patient carries a drug-response genetic variant

Among ~100,000 All of Us participants with whole-genome data, researchers tested 16 common drug-response genes; every patient carried at least one variant. Critically, roughly one-quarter were already taking drugs affected by their variants—not a rare edge case but a routine clinical issue. The study also uncovered 17,000 previously unreported variants in these genes, providing frequency data needed to decide which populations should be tested and when.

— Philip Empey

Glossary

cholinergic hypothesis
Theory that Alzheimer's originates in damage to the brain's acetylcholine system.
nucleus basalis of Meynert
Deep-brain hub where cholinergic neurons project to memory and attention regions of the cortex.
HVDC
High-voltage direct current transmission technology that enables long-distance power lines with cables that can bury underground.
All of Us
The NIH research program collecting genome and health data from large U.S. participant cohorts.
pharmacogenomics
The study of how genetic variations affect individual drug response and metabolism.
