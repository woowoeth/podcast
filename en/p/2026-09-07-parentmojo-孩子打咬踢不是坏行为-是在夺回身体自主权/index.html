Why Your Preschooler's Hitting, Biting, or Kicking Isn't Bad Behavior—It's a Bid for Bodily Autonomy

Stop asking how to stop the hitting. For a three- or four-year-old, hitting, biting, and kicking are often the only tools left to shout, “I have no say”—when adults control nearly every aspect of their body. Give back a little choice, and the behavior eases.

Most approaches repeat “no hitting” and add punishment, without touching the need behind the behavior. This episode treats aggression as a signal of need and demonstrates scripts and cases for negotiating with teachers.

The argument · timestamps estimated from transcript position

4:21

Hitting is a child's cry that their body isn't theirs

Preschool is often the first place a child is controlled by an adult other than their parents for nearly every bodily arrangement: where to sit, how to sit, when to speak, when to move. In the previous three years, they could largely decide when to run and when to go to the bathroom. Sitting on the carpet for 20 minutes without moving is a real deprivation of autonomy; a three- or four-year-old lacks the language to assert “my body, my rules,” so hitting and kicking become one of the few tools left. So when you get that call from the teacher, don't rush to scold “keep your hands to yourself.” Ask instead: how much say did this child have over their own life before this, and what can we give back?

6:42

Kindergarten readiness is really obedience training

Teachers often say a high proportion of children at a preschool are “kindergarten-ready,” but the core of that readiness is compliance: when the teacher says sit, you sit, even if you're mid-task, even if your body is overloaded. Parents worry that if a child doesn't comply now, they won't get into a good elementary school, college, or good life. But the cost to the child is learning to suppress the feeling of “what I need right now.” Teachers aren't villains either; they need competence, child safety, ease, and even a sense of power. Many entered education to heal their own childhood wounds but only know power-over relationships. Seeing both sides opens room for negotiation.

— Jen
9:24

We accommodate ourselves but refuse to accommodate children

If adults feel overloaded at a party, we arrive early, leave early, retreat to a quiet room, or wear noise-canceling earplugs—we find this natural. But when it comes to children, except for routine things like extended test time, we worry that “too much accommodation will spoil him, and he won't survive in the real world.” The truth is that adults grant themselves far more accommodation than they allow children. The original piece offers a vivid image: a bigger, stronger adult picks you up without explanation from a quiet room and plops you in the middle of a noisy party—if that happened to an adult, you'd immediately see it as a violation of bodily autonomy; when it happens to a child, we look away.

— Jen
12:11

The fix lies before the behavior, not in the behavior itself

Repeating “no biting” doesn't work because that's not the knowledge the child lacks. You need a different set of questions: What happened in the seconds before the bite? What else happened earlier that day? Is there a move or a new baby in the child's life? A sensory-overloaded child who loses control when pushed in line—let them stand at the front, back, or outside the line. A child who struggles with transitions needs a few minutes' warning. Analogy: when your partner snaps at you at night over something trivial, it's often not about that moment but about the morning argument, lack of sleep, and a cascade of stress. If they responded with “your attitude needs work,” you'd explode too; yet we often do only the latter to children.

— Jen
15:10

Talking to the teacher: don't blame, listen to the 'no'

Telling the teacher “change your approach” will almost certainly hit a wall. A safer opening is to set aside judgment and reconstruct the facts: not who's at fault, but what actually happened; and include family changes, which are context the teacher can't see. Once you've figured out whether the trigger is sensory or about autonomy, then acknowledge the three things behind the teacher's behavior: competence, child safety, and the need for a breather. The request must be concrete and actionable, like “Can she sit on a ball outside the circle?” If the teacher refuses, the key isn't the refusal but what need the “no” is protecting—and find a way that meets both needs.

— Jen
16:07

A nap-time conflict resolved cleanly

Jen's daughter Carys, at three, couldn't nap. The teacher required her to lie down; she was bored and frustrated—her need was to play. The teacher had two needs: state regulations required offering nap time, and nap time was also the teacher's break. The solution they found: move her mat to a slightly separate corner, where she could play quietly with soft toys that didn't roll, while the teacher could see her through the window from the break room. The child kept her right to free play, the teacher kept the rule and her break, and Jen escaped the dilemma of tattletale calls. This example shows that “negotiating by need” isn't empty talk: everyone gives a little, but everyone's core need is preserved.

— Jen
17:53

All methods boil down to one question

Compress all the above into one sentence: stop asking “how do I make the hitting stop” and ask instead “what need is this behavior communicating?” Hitting, biting, and kicking are surface signals; the real message might be “I need sensory relief” or “I need some say over my body.” Once the need is correctly named, a path to meet it can be designed; and it's almost always the adult who changes the environment, because changing how adults show up is easier than changing the child.

— Jen

Glossary

autonomy
The basic need to have choice over one's life and body and to act according to one's own will.
kindergarten readiness
A preschool metric for whether a child can sit still, follow instructions, and adapt to group rules.
accommodation
A change to the environment, rules, or schedule to match an individual's sensory or other needs.
power-over model
An interaction pattern where the adult is in a superior position and treats the child with commands and domination, as opposed to a collaborative model.
