What kids really need to learn isn't doing chores—it's noticing what needs to be done

After spending a year teaching her husband to notice household tasks on his own, the author realized she was still doing the 'noticing' for her kids—a generational pattern she decided to break starting with this generation.

Teaching her husband to notice revealed what she'd been teaching her children

After a year of teaching her husband to spot household tasks himself, Sam Kelly realized she'd been creating an identical dependency in her kids. In June 2023, while listing weekend chores for her children, she saw the pattern: she was training them to rely on her brain to notice, just as she'd spent the past year breaking her husband of that habit. That moment convinced her to interrupt the cycle—starting with this generation.

— Sam Kelly
12:25

The noticing walk: tour your home with kids to show what needs doing

The simplest starting point takes ten to fifteen minutes. Walk through the kitchen, living room, bathroom—and demonstrate with specificity bordering on repetition: the cereal box goes in the cupboard, dirty dishes get a quick rinse to prevent crusting, trash needs to be taken out. The crucial move is not to assume kids 'should' know. They've never had the chance to practice noticing because a parent has always done it for them.

— Sam Kelly
14:29

Notice-and-do must come before phone time: task order replaces nagging

After the walk, every day the child picks one 'notice-and-do' task, completes it, reports back, and only then gets access to the phone, a friend visit, or whatever reward already motivates them. This is deliberately ordered task priority, not repeated reminders. For her three ADHD-diagnosed children, she emphasizes that intention alone isn't enough—this has to be practiced daily, like learning an instrument. Children with stronger executive function might not need the reward order at all.

— Sam Kelly
27:04

Ask questions, not solutions: point to the problem, don't prescribe the fix

Carrying mental load for years numbs you to what needs doing—your attention has been trained to track specific patterns, and your children's attention follows yours. Most parents skip straight to the solution ('clean your room,' 'wipe the table') and miss the noticing step entirely. The actual mechanism for training attention is to point to the problem first ('see this cereal box on the counter—what should happen next?') instead of giving a direct command.

— Sarah Brun
31:15

Noticing is the real skill; doing is just what follows from it

Doing is the byproduct. What you're really teaching is attention—the capacity to perceive the environment, see what others are carrying, and recognize where you can step in. Kelly mentions a visitor whose deepest wish wasn't that her partner do the tasks, but simply to hear: 'I noticed you needed this.' That's the ability she wants her children to develop: not to finish chores, but to see.

— Sarah Brun
33:23

The dandelion effect: children's noticing spreads beyond their home

This training radiates outward. At a friend's Christmas party, Kelly's twelve-year-old noticed the snowflakes her host had hand-cut for decoration. While other children looked for games to pair, her daughter asked who had made them and how long it took, then spontaneously led applause. Kelly's conclusion: a child can only truly recognize and honor invisible labor if she's first been given a chance to participate in it. Otherwise, 'thank you' is just what you're told to say.

— Sam Kelly
36:30

The toilet paper roll: even tiny noticing counts and shifts what parents carry

A mother sent Kelly a photo: her child had noticed the roll getting low—not even empty, just reduced—and proactively put out a fresh one. Mental load is built from countless small things. Often it takes feeling these small things leave your hands to realize how much you've been carrying alone. Kelly also acknowledges: pushback and eye-rolling are completely normal. It doesn't mean the practice isn't working. It means you've raised a regular kid. Keep going.

— Sam Kelly
40:37

With ADHD kids: build the scaffold firm, then learn to loosen it

For her three ADHD-diagnosed children and one strong-willed child, Kelly holds three edges: don't get drawn into arguments, stick to 'complete the task, then reward,' and stay as consistent as possible. She likens this to the solid trellis a climbing plant needs—without it, the vine sprawls everywhere. Practical support includes body doubling and counting aloud to help her child sustain focus. As the child grows stronger, you gradually withdraw, the way you eventually let go of the bicycle seat.

— Sam Kelly

Glossary

Mental load
The invisible cognitive work of continuously tracking and planning household tasks.
Noticing walk
A tour of your home with children to demonstrate which details require attention.
Notice and do
A parenting framework where noticing is taught first; doing is what naturally follows.
Preexisting motivators
Things children already want daily, used as rewards only after tasks are complete.
Dandelion effect
A child's learned noticing skill spreads like seeds beyond the family into other situations.
