Beijing's Central Axis Is Not a Relic — It's a City Operating System Still Running

The Ming rebuilt Beijing from a blueprint written two thousand years earlier in the Kaogong Ji, and the central axis still dictates the symmetry and growth of a city of twenty million — even the Olympic Bird's Nest sits on that line.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:13

A two-thousand-year-old instruction manual is still in use

When the Ming set out to rebuild the capital, they dug up a technical manual more than two thousand years old, the Kaogong Ji (“考工记”). It specified a city four to five kilometres on each side, three gates per side, the palace at the centre, an ancestral temple on one side, the court in front, the market behind, and a Temple of Heaven in the southern suburbs. The historian Jeremiah Jenik says the logic behind this layout was cosmic order: there is a fixed point in the sky, the North Star, so there should be a corresponding fixed point on earth, and the most fitting fixed point is the emperor himself — placing yourself in the palace at the exact centre of the city is placing yourself inside a cosmic order that revolves around you.

— Jeremiah Jenik
9:16

They actually built it to the drawing

The most astonishing thing is not that the Ming dared to use a blueprint over a thousand years old, but how closely they built to it. Overlay the Kaogong Ji drawing on the model at the Beijing Planning Exhibition Hall and the two almost coincide. So Beijing today is still a twenty-first-century city running around a fifteenth-century heart, and that heart follows principles laid down nearly two thousand years ago. Compare London, which grew organically along the Thames: Beijing had the blueprint first, the city second.

11:20

The central axis is a spine, and the city grew out of it

From north to south, the axis strings together the Bird's Nest, the Drum and Bell Towers, Jingshan, the Forbidden City, the Temple of Heaven, and finally Yongdingmen at the southern end. The show likens it to a spine: everything is planned and expanded outward from this line. Running true north-south and aligned with the heavens, the line has been Beijing's most distinctive feature for six hundred years, and it determines the shape of the city today.

12:22

The right to tell time is the right to rule

The Drum and Bell Towers were the timekeeping centre of the whole city, and the bell was said to carry twenty kilometres. From 1272 all the way to 1924 — the year the last Qing emperor left the Forbidden City — the time struck here was Beijing's official time. The drum was beaten one hundred and eight times every two hours; it was the first sound Beijingers heard in the morning and the last they heard at night. The show says it is almost as if the emperor were not merely marking time but claiming ownership of time itself.

15:32

Jingshan was piled up out of the previous dynasty's rubble

Jingshan is not a natural hill; it was built artificially by the Yongle Emperor according to feng shui principles. An ideal palace faces south, with water to the south — water that should curve and flow to bring qi and life — and a mountain to the north to block the evil qi coming from that direction and display authority. Beijing is too far from the Yan Mountains to the north, so the Ming took the rubble of the old Mongol palaces and the earth dug out of new canals and built a symbolic mountain inside the city.

18:52

The Forbidden City was a super-project of nationwide mobilisation

Ten thousand logs were shipped from 1,800 kilometres to the southwest, the golden bricks of Suzhou came from 1,100 kilometres to the south — 18 million bricks fired in all — and gold leaf came from Nanjing. One million labourers plus more than 100,000 craftsmen built it in fifteen years. From the 1420s to the early twentieth century, twenty-four emperors lived here. Today it receives more than 15 million visitors a year, making it the most visited historic site on earth.

22:55

A nail-free timber structure can take a magnitude 9.5 quake

These buildings are almost entirely wood, and what lets them stand for centuries is the dougong (斗拱): the dou are the bearing blocks, the gong are inserted and interlock very tightly. Wood shrinks and warps, and this system lets it deform flexibly while keeping the overall structure intact; in an earthquake every small piece of wood can slide a little, rather than shearing the bearing blocks or popping the bolts. Scientists have calculated that this construction could withstand the strongest earthquake ever recorded, magnitude 9.5.

23:57

It was struck by lightning within a year of completion

The Forbidden City was not fireproof. Less than a year after it was completed in 1421, the three great halls were burned to ashes by lightning. Afterwards 308 bronze vats were installed to store water against the next fire. The detail makes the point: no matter how precise the planning, it cannot stop a single point of failure — you can only add redundancy after the fact.

31:13

An imperial procession could halt a city of a million

The winter solstice sacrifice to heaven was the most important ritual in the entire Chinese calendar. The emperor went south from the Forbidden City along the central axis, then turned toward the Temple of Heaven. The proceedings were extremely elaborate: commoners had to shut their windows, and no one was allowed to see the emperor pass; Beijing's newly installed tram system shut down entirely for the day; foreigners were explicitly told not to leave their homes, not even to steal a glance. A city of a million people stopped for this.

35:17

A demolished gate was put back exactly as it was

By the middle of the twentieth century Beijing's population was approaching four million and the city could no longer hold, so Yongdingmen was torn down to build a new ring-road system. But the central axis needed an endpoint, so in the early 2000s Yongdingmen was reconstructed a few metres from its original site. The show points out that the ring roads themselves became an extension of this symmetry — the first, the second, the third, ring after ring. A line drawn centuries ago still dictates the form of a twenty-first-century global metropolis.

Glossary

Kaogong Ji / “考工记”
A technical manual more than two thousand years old specifying the correct way to make cities, palaces, carts and other artefacts.
dougong / 斗拱
A nail-free interlocking timber bracket system that allows deformation and resists earthquakes.
hutong / 胡同
From the Mongolian word for "water": narrow alleyway residential districts formed around a shared well.
feng shui / 风水
Ancient Chinese principles for harmonising people with their environment, governing the orientation and layout of palaces and homes.
