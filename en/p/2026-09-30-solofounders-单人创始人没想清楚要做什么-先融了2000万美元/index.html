Solo Founder Raised $20M Before Figuring Out What to Build

She sees Slack as a fifteen-year-old office building designed for humans—one where AI agents have no place. So as a solo founder, before fully clarifying her strategy, she raised $20M across two rounds to rebuild a messaging platform from scratch.

No competitors doesn't mean no opportunity—it means no one has the patience

She says she's also unsure whether it's early or late, but sees that Slack and Teams are moving too slowly on adapting their platforms for AI agents. She believes team messaging products need enormous patience to build well, and in today's AI gold-rush environment most people want quick returns, so the market is less crowded than it appears—the potential exists but the shape hasn't yet emerged.

— Sara Du
15:22

Investors back the person, not the fully-formed plan

She admits she raised capital as a solo founder before fully clarifying ‘this is the thing’—mainly because one investor shared her judgment on how AI and tool use will reshape work, and believed in her way of operating. She sees this as what early-stage investors do well: assess people, not the plan itself.

— Sara Du
32:08

Slack is an office building built for humans; AI agents can't live in it

Her core claim: Slack's data model has never treated AI agents as entities equivalent to humans. Agents can't truly understand which channels they can join, how to have side conversations with people, or how to start group chats. She compares it to placing a superintelligent being into an office not built for it—the doors and hallways don't work, it has to operate with minimal context from people, and it's blamed when things go wrong.

— Sara Du
36:18

Hide the AI agent's thinking process

She strongly opposes showing AI thinking traces in the UI because watching a progress bar jump from one second to sixty is deeply frustrating. Ando deliberately avoids this interface because the goal is to treat AI teammates more like human ones—people need hours or even days to ‘warm up,’ not instant mind-reading.

— Sara Du
42:33

Humans show zero tolerance for agent mistakes, endless patience for human ones

She observes that people are intolerant of AI error in a way that mirrors the opposite of human trust-building: trust between humans grows slowly but becomes resilient once earned. Trust in AI grows just as slowly but collapses instantly. She thinks this is a hard problem platforms face—deciding how much interface design can help an AI agent earn human user trust, where the design itself bridges that trust gap.

— Sara Du
52:43

Building a messaging platform is closer to game design than B2B SaaS

She says what surprised her most is how much human feeling matters—this is more like making a game than standard business software, which is why messaging founders historically come from games. The reason: what users feel as they move through an interface is something large models currently struggle to simulate, whereas human designers excel at it.

— Sara Du
1:02:56

Before finding product-market fit, fewer people makes for faster learning

She's become radically minimalist in hiring and execution. After easy fundraising, many founders think ‘more money means hire faster, do more,’ but she believes the only thing that matters before product-market fit is learning velocity, and more people slow learning down—too many cooks. So focus on core metrics: what exactly are you validating?

— Sara Du
1:09:06

The cost of solo founding: no one to hold your full context

When asked about solo founding's downside, she says the biggest risk is overthinking from isolation—there's no peer to press on difficult questions with, even if smart people outside the company could help. The upside: a company becomes its own entity with a soul; a solo founder can more clearly hear what it truly needs next, and decide who deserves to become co-founder.

— Sara Du
