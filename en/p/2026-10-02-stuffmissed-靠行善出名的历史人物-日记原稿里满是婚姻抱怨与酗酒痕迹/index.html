A Philanthropist Famous for Good Deeds: Her Diary Revealed Marriage Troubles and Drinking

A King's Bad Poetry Found During Research Made the Outtakes More Entertaining

While preparing the beer riot episode, the host found a newspaper account that included a gossipy aside mocking the "poet king" Ludwig's three volumes of unreadable verse and his self-indulgent travel guide to one of his public buildings. The digression had nothing to do with the uprising itself, so it didn't make the main episode—but it was too funny to leave out of the behind-the-scenes content.

2:08

Ale and Lager Differ in Fermentation Temperature and Yeast Position

Using knowledge from bartending school, the host explained that ale yeast ferments at higher temperatures, settling on the surface of the liquid, while lager yeast ferments at lower temperatures and sinks to the bottom. This also explains why lager is harder to produce: not every place or season has temperatures low enough to support it.

4:11

German Immigration Shifted American Beer Preferences from Ale to Lager

Before German immigrants arrived in large numbers, most American colonists drank ale. German newcomers brought lager techniques, and lager gradually became the dominant beer style in America. The host notes uncertainty about whether brewers of that era actually understood the yeast-science differences involved—the credit usually goes to Pasteur's later research—or whether they simply knew that temperature and method mattered without grasping the underlying mechanism.

9:13

Her Daughters' Memoir Edition Removed Evidence of Her Marital Doubts

She burned many of her teenage diaries herself. After her death, her daughters compiled a memoir from her surviving journals but softened or omitted her reservations about marriage, persistent self-doubt, and anxiety. Historian June Rose, in her 1981 biography, acknowledged in the introduction that she had found the original diaries and discovered her daughters had made the account ‘more gentle’.

11:16

She Complained About Small Household Irritations and Serious In-Law Conflict

Her diary reveals many complaints about married life—notably, that her husband hummed too much and too loudly. Her relationship with her in-laws was fraught: on their first Christmas, she ordered a turkey from Norwich ahead of time, but her mother-in-law brought one too and was offended when she refused to use hers instead. When the mother-in-law fell ill, the household's faith in ‘animal magnetism’ (hypnotic therapy) conflicted with her preference for medical care, adding another point of friction.

14:19

She Applied Stringent Standards to Decide Who Deserved Her Charitable Help

When someone wrote asking for help, claiming fallen circumstances and requesting a visit, she came to their home. Finding their conditions better than expected and their request for funds larger than she anticipated, she decided they were not poor enough—and the distance was too great—so she declined to establish a relief relationship.

15:22

Much of Her Documented Anxiety Stemmed from Self-Imposed Expectations

Her diary shows she couldn't bear being blamed, and she wrote that the strong joy she felt while singing seemed ‘forbidden’—something she didn't deserve to be allowed. The host connects this to her own past experience of self-pressuring anxiety, and to learning coping techniques through therapy in her forties, reflecting that if she had access to modern mental-health resources, she might not have driven herself so hard.

20:26

Her Diary Contains Hints She Self-Medicated Anxiety with Alcohol

As a young woman, she wrote in her diary about wanting to quit drinking entirely but being unable to stop; when she didn't drink, she was uncomfortable. As she aged, she shifted from beer to brandy and referred to it as ‘medicine’. The historian June Rose's biography alludes to this but doesn't state it outright—more like breadcrumbs—suggesting that without modern diagnosis or treatment, this was her own way of managing anxiety or depression, though the evidence is circumstantial rather than definitive.
