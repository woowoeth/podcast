Chinese Labor Shaped US Customs: Modern Border Control Began with the Chinese Exclusion Act

Customs, visas and passports were not endogenous inventions of the West; they were forced into being in the late 19th century to screen out and exclude Chinese immigrants pouring into the United States. The paradox — legal immigration is hard, illegal immigration is easy — persists to this day.

Chinese immigrants shaped the US customs system

In Melancholy Order, McKeown argues that the modern global system of customs and immigration control was not built by white European and American migrants, but developed by the United States in the late 19th century after large numbers of Chinese immigrants poured in, in order to screen who could enter and by what standard they should be classified. To put it in a striking phrase: Chinese immigrants shaped US customs. This system then spread globally, so Chinese immigrants played a key role in the formation of modern border control — and this also refutes Eurocentrism in immigration history.

Foucault's view of power runs through the immigration system

McKeown is deeply influenced by Foucault: power is not top-down and concentrated in the state, but exists in everyday details and operates as a network. When immigrants apply for visas they are required to provide all kinds of certificates and materials; this disciplinary mechanism and knowledge discourse precisely embodies how power operates. Yang Bin points out that the immigration system is full of absurdities and paradoxes — for example, when applying for a student visa, whether or not you actually intend to return home to develop your career, you must check the box saying you will return to your home country after graduation, otherwise the visa officer will reject you outright. On the surface the system operates between legal and illegal, but in reality it is riddled with paradoxes created by power.

— Cheng Yanliang
25:27

The Chinese Exclusion Act established national immigration sovereignty

McKeown dissects the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act and points out that its most important element was establishing the unilateral jurisdiction of the receiving country. Previously the authority of the sending country and the receiving country was unclear; the Chinese Exclusion Act put the decision in America's hands, and from this came today's distinction between passport and visa: the passport is an identity document provided by the home country, while the visa is the receiving country's decision on whether to accept you. In the modern international system, the power to decide on immigration control ultimately rests with the receiving country — this is the key conclusion drawn from the Chinese Exclusion Act.

— Cheng Yanliang
28:30

Illegal immigration is easy, legal immigration is hard

The US immigration system has produced a paradoxical result: roughly 10 million-plus low-skilled illegal immigrants make up 5% of the US labor force and have long been able to stay because enforcement is lax; by contrast, highly educated non-immigrant talent often has to queue for years to apply for a US green card. On the surface the system encourages free immigration, but in reality screening and obstruction are its core. This absurdity, contrast and paradox is what McKeown calls the "melancholy order." So-called free immigration is merely an invention and imagination of the modern state; immigrants are defined, classified and filtered by the state from beginning to end.

34:40

Indentured labor replaced black slaves

In the mid-to-late 19th century, the abolitionist movement surged across the Western world, creating a labor vacuum on New World plantations, and indentured Chinese workers appeared as substitutes for black slaves, known as "sold piglets." Chinese workers in the US usually earned 30 dollars a month, and after deducting living expenses could send back 80-100 dollars a year; at the time the average annual income of a Chinese peasant was only 8-10 dollars, a difference of about ten times. It was precisely this economic drive that propelled the wave of emigration from South China in the late Qing. Take Sun Mei, Sun Yat-sen's elder brother: he started as an indentured Chinese worker and later became a big rancher with 6,500 acres of pasture.

Passports came first, identity cards later

After the US and the West established customs systems, Chinese workers became the first Chinese to hold an international identity document — the passport — and the passport appeared in China earlier than the domestic identity card. Chinese workers crossed clear national borders and arrived on foreign soil, where, suffering racial discrimination and oppression, they shifted from clan and native-place identity to a conscious identification with being Chinese. Overseas Chinese thus became the forerunners of modern Chinese nationalism and revolutionary thought; Sun Yat-sen called them "the mother of the revolution." Yang Bin stresses that overseas Chinese simultaneously exhibit two tendencies — returning to their roots and putting down roots — and that the former should not be the only one emphasized.

Glossary

indentured labor
A labor model in which workers are hired by contract, replacing slavery, used to fill the labor vacuum after abolition.
Natal model
A model in the South African colony of Natal that screened immigrants by language test, later adopted by Australia, New Zealand and others.
Canadian principle
A control principle that decides whether to accept or exclude immigrants based on the country's own economic interests, established in 1909.
