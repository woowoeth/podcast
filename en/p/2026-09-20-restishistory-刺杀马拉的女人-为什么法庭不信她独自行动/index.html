The woman who killed Marat, and why the court wouldn't believe she acted alone

Corday had her declaration written before the assassination and insisted after her arrest that she acted alone, yet the Revolutionary Tribunal kept pressing her on the "chain of conspiracy" — they could not imagine that provincials thought Marat a monster.

0:00

The assassination declaration was written before the act

When Corday was arrested she was carrying a declaration titled "To the Friends of Law and Peace in France." In it she named Marat "the most vile of villains," said he "has been condemned by the universe, and is therefore outside the law," and called on the French to "rise. Strike. Strike." This text was not a post-hoc defence — it was written before the act. What she wanted was not escape, but to turn her own death into a rallying point.

9:30

Marat was the first modern populist

Keith Michael Baker's new biography positions Marat as the first modern populist. His formula included: constantly invoking the capitalized "People," and claiming the people were at every moment in danger of being betrayed by appeasers and traitors; an obsession with conspiracy theory, stating outright that his goal was to keep his readers "perpetually on their guard," lest they be dragged into the abyss by royalists, priests and their ilk. The show points out that this "wake up, sheep" language is used today by both left and right.

21:57

Paper-money inflation pushed the poor into the streets

In early 1793 France had suffered years of poor harvests, the national grain market had collapsed, and the government had no money to fight its wars, so it printed paper money — the assignat — with roughly four billion in circulation. This triggered severe inflation, the price of bread and sugar soared, there were protests in the market almost daily, and women's marches appeared in the streets of Paris. The show stresses: these were the very same conditions that had set off the Revolution and the storming of the Bastille, and the poor would think, "the Revolution happened, yet life hasn't improved — so someone must be scheming."

32:13

The commander-in-chief's defection confirmed the conspiracy theories

On 8 March 1793 the Austrians counter-attacked and routed Dumouriez at Neerwinden; he was forced to withdraw from Belgium and Holland. He wanted to emulate Sulla and march on Paris as a military dictator, and asked his troops, "Shall we march on Paris?" They answered no. On 5 April he rode over to the Austrian lines, taking a number of senior officers with him, including the future King of France, Louis Philippe. One volunteer wrote to his mother: "Who can we trust now?"

38:27

The Terror machine was improvised, not designed

The show stresses that the key institutions of the Terror had no blueprint and no plan — they were built as they went along, and only in hindsight assembled into a machine, and many of those who helped build it were eventually swallowed by it. On 10 March the Revolutionary Tribunal was established to try political crimes, with death sentences to be carried out within 24 hours and no right of appeal; it sat in the Palais de Justice in Paris, and its cellars were the existing Conciergerie prison. Danton said: "Let us be terrible, so that the people need not be."

42:37

The Girondins used a new rule to strike at Marat, and it backfired

The Girondins had just abolished parliamentary immunity, and while large numbers of deputies were away on missions to the provinces, they indicted Marat on 14 April, accusing him of inciting insurrection. Marat went into hiding for three days, then, seeing that street opinion in Paris was on his side, turned himself in for trial. On 24 April the court convened in the Palais de Justice; the prosecutor, Fuquietanville, was a Montagnard sympathizer and let Marat deliver a long self-defence. The ten-man jury acquitted him within a few hours, and Marat was carried back to the National Convention by some 800 people, crowned with roses, to cries of "vive la république, vive Marat." For the Girondins it was a total humiliation.

1:04:31

Corday buys a knife: a 15-centimetre kitchen knife with a black handle

At around eight in the morning on 13 July, Charlotte Corday left her hotel, walked to the arcades of the Palais-Royal, bought a newspaper (which said the Convention would likely sentence the imprisoned Girondin deputies to death), and bought a black Paris hat with a green ribbon, replacing the white Norman-style cap that marked her out. She went into a cutler's shop called Le Coutelier Badin and bought a kitchen knife with a black handle and a blade about 15 centimetres long. Between 11:30 a.m. and noon she took a carriage to Marat's home on the rue des Cordeliers, but she had no appointment and was turned away by Marat's sister-in-law.

1:10:51

The court would not believe a woman could act alone

Corday was arrested on the spot, and one of her interrogators was Jean-Baptiste Drouet, the postmaster who had recognized Louis XVI's flight at Varennes. From the outset she said she had acted alone. But the Revolutionary Tribunal repeatedly interrogated her about the "chain of conspiracy," convinced she must be part of a Girondin plot. The interrogator Montané said: it is impossible, because you say Marat was the source of all France's ills, yet Marat was precisely the man who never stopped exposing traitors and conspirators, so someone must have lied to you. Corday replied: you are mad — only in Paris do people see Marat; in the other provinces he is taken for a monster. The court could not imagine that provincials thought Marat a bad man.

Glossary

assignat
Paper money issued during the French Revolution and backed by confiscated church property, which later depreciated sharply through over-issue.
sans-culottes
The radical common people of Paris during the French Revolution, named for not wearing aristocratic knee breeches.
Conciergerie
The old prison inside the Palais de Justice in Paris, where large numbers of prisoners awaiting trial were held during the Terror.
federalist revolts
Armed uprisings by provincial cities in 1793 against the centralizing Montagnard power in Paris, led mainly by merchants and the middle classes.
