ExtraCredits 2026-11-21

An abused prince became Prussia's greatest warrior king

Frederick the Great, through ‘strike first and win’ and massed infantry discipline, transformed a second-tier peripheral kingdom into a Habsburg-challenging power—though the Seven Years' War nearly destroyed Prussia in the process.

European historyMilitary tacticsDiplomacyEnlightenmentPrussiaWar history
A defiant boy endured his father's militarized brutality, then weaponized it into Europe's fiercest war machine. The character arc and tactical detail grip throughout; a midpoint diplomatic reversal and wrestling-commentary recap of the Seven Years' War are among the series' finest moments.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:10

One man's obsession with militarism built the fourth-largest army in Europe

Frederick William I despised luxury and anything ‘feminine,’ shut down Prussia's Academy of Sciences after taking power, then spent those savings on the military. Using the new ‘Canton System’ of conscription, he expanded the standing army to 76,000 troops—making it Europe's fourth-largest despite Prussia's population being one-tenth of France's. He also raised a unit of giant grenadiers that recruited Europe's tallest men; the cabinet got the nickname ‘Tobacco Cabinet’ from the smoke that filled meetings.

3:11

The son's taste for French culture horrified his militarist father

Before age six, young Frederick was raised by his mother and developed a love of music, poetry, and French as his first language—precisely the ‘decadent French ways’ his father despised. At seven, custody passed to his father. All music and philosophy study stopped; instead, military officers taught him Latin, religion, and military subjects. His Latin tutor secretly helped him assemble a 3,000-book hidden library.

8:07

A failed escape forced him to watch his closest friend's execution

Frederick and his close friend Katte—possibly his lover—tried to flee to England. When caught, Frederick was charged with an officer's treason. His father wanted him dead but lacked legal ground under imperial law, so instead forced him to witness Katte's execution: soldiers held him at the prison window bars and made him watch. He submitted afterward, married a cousin he despised, and waited for his father to die.

15:11

He theorized ‘interest war’ in a book, then practiced it on Austria

In his early Anti-Machiavel, Frederick developed a theory of ‘interest war’: a king predicts his enemy will eventually attack, so he strikes first to seize maximum military advantage. Within six months of his father's death, he followed this logic exactly, launching a six-week lightning invasion of Austria's wealthy Silesian province without formal declaration of war, triggering the War of Austrian Succession.

16:11

His first victory came despite his panic, thanks to his father's infantry

At the Battle of Mollwitz, Frederick commanded using a Gustav Adolf-era formation. When enemy cavalry broke his cavalry, generals told him ‘If the king falls, Prussia has no heir,’ forcing him to flee and hide in a cottage. Unknown to him, his well-trained infantry self-organized into musket volleys that repulsed the enemy cavalry and won him his first victory. He called that humiliation his ‘school’ and never again delegated command.

33:16

His betrayal of France triggered the ‘Diplomatic Revolution of 1756’

To counter the Russian-Austrian alliance, Frederick switched sides and allied with Britain, promising not to attack the British king's Hanover territory in Germany in exchange for British financial support. This enraged his former ally France, pushing it toward Austria—the so-called Diplomatic Revolution of 1756. This move, combined with his earlier broken pledges to Austria (private peace) and his prior exit from the Second Silesian War, also alienated Saxony and Russia, who now allied with Austria. The Seven Years' War's four-front encirclement took shape.

37:18

His signature tactic failed; now he beat stragglers with his sword

At the Battle of Kolin, Frederick's hallmark ‘oblique attack’ collapsed when troops charged prematurely. The frontal assault crumbled under Austrian firepower; Prussia lost nearly 14,000 men. As soldiers began to flee, Frederick swung his sword flat to strike deserters while shouting ‘Do you want to live forever?’ Ironically, the only ‘immortality’ history granted was his, not theirs.

40:20

An empress's death and a madman's succession saved Prussia from destruction

By war's end, Prussia was down to 60,000 troops; Berlin had been occupied by Russia. Annihilation seemed certain. Then Empress Elizabeth of Russia died suddenly. Her successor, Peter III, was a fervent Frederick admirer. He immediately withdrew, returned East Prussia, and allied with Prussia. Prussia reversed course. When Peter was soon after overthrown in Catherine's coup, Russia quit the war. The conflict ended with borders restored to their pre-war lines.

44:22

He bought territorial unity through Poland's partition and finally became a true king

In 1772, Frederick proposed that Prussia, Austria, and Russia partition Poland to avoid another Habsburg war. This unjustified seizure, against Polish will, gave Frederick the region of West Prussia, finally unifying his fragmented territories into one contiguous whole. For the first time, he could be called ‘King of Prussia’ rather than ‘King in Prussia.’

Glossary

oblique attack
Concentrating cavalry force to attack one enemy flank rather than the full front line.
pragmatic sanction
Charles VI's decree permitting female succession to Habsburg territories.
Diplomatic Revolution of 1756
The alliance realignment when Prussia shifted to Britain and France to Austria.
