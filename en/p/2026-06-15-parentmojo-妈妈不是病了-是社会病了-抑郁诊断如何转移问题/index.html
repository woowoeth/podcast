Mothers Aren't Sick, Society Is: How a Depression Diagnosis Moves the Problem

Burnout gets misdiagnosed as depression, and the problem is attributed to the individual brain rather than the social environment. A New Zealand study found that new mothers who had someone help with the housework every day for six months almost never developed postpartum depression, yet medication is still the first-line answer.

The disease model asks about your brain, never about your circumstances

Bob points out that the disease model puts the problem inside an individual's head rather than in the way society is arranged. Women hold jobs and still carry most of the housework, and when they burn out from being overworked, the system does not say this is a question of how society is organized — it says ‘you're depressed, you're anxious, you're stressed, take a pill.’ The disease model does not respond to a person's life situation at all. If one group in a society is under more pressure than another, then the group carrying the greater pressure is necessarily the one that gets diagnosed more.

— Bob Whitaker
6:25

From hysteria to Valium, the goal was a quieter wife

The medicalization of women's distress has a long history: in the 19th century, men had unhappy wives committed to asylums and classified as hysterics. When the benzodiazepines Valium and Librium came to market in the 60s, the advertising was aimed primarily at women. Bob describes the ads of the period: is your wife nagging you when you come home, making you unhappy? Give her Valium and she'll meet you at the door with your slippers and a drink. Milltown was an earlier drug of the same kind. What this pathway does, at bottom, is leave women without emotions so they can serve men better.

— Bob Whitaker
8:18

Psychiatry redefined anxiety as depression to swap tranquilizers for antidepressants

In the 80s, benzodiazepines came under fire for being addictive, so psychiatry redefined anxiety as depression and switched the drug from a tranquilizer to an antidepressant. Antidepressants have the same numbing, quieting effect. Drug companies claimed antidepressants were not addictive, and that drove the reconstruction of the diagnosis. Bob sees this as another form of social control — taking the social environment out of the prescription equation entirely. Today girls face unhealthy environments such as social media, TikTok and body anxiety, and are diagnosed with depression, anxiety and eating disorders, and then medicated.

— Bob Whitaker
11:10

The ads never ask whether a mother is simply doing too much

Jen says she searched YouTube for antidepressant commercials and found 2000s-era mothers sitting on a park bench watching their children sadly, who turn into good mothers once they take the drug. The ads never ask: are you doing too many things because a capitalist society needs you to work and raise children and there aren't enough hands? Is your husband failing to share the load? Jen thinks the world hasn't really changed, only the wording has adapted to how roles are understood today. Bob agrees, and stresses that stress is off the charts for a great many people, but women in particular carry more — housework, homework, taking the children to the doctor are all assumed by default to be the mother's responsibility.

11:59

Depression is not something you have, it is a permitted way to signal suffering

Jen quotes the researcher Marecek: depression is not a thing people possess but a set of culturally sanctioned practices through which people communicate to others that they are suffering. She says she is not allowed to be angry, not allowed to say the system ought to change, but she is allowed to turn the pain inward into depression and then go and get a prescription. Bob agrees emphatically, arguing that this narrative both makes money and excuses society from the obligation to change. Human beings are naturally sensitive to their environment — poverty, racism, working 16–18 hours a day all shape a person's state — but the DSM model removes the environment completely.

— Jen Lumanlan
13:32

Six months of household help worked better than drugs, and was ignored

Bob describes a New Zealand study: new mothers had someone come to the house weekly to help with the housework for six months, compared against usual care. The result, obviously, was that the group that got help had very little postpartum depression. But the intervention did not become a first-line care plan, because the system still believes drugs can solve the problem. Bob says the doctor involved admitted at the time that nobody doubted it was the better response. He stresses that this proves environmental intervention works but is ignored, because the pharmaceutical narrative serves larger interests.

— Bob Whitaker
14:17

The chemical imbalance was never proven; it was a marketing story

Jen sums up the key points of the full interview: depression is understood as a chemical imbalance caused by too little serotonin, a theory that has never actually been proven — a marketing story created by a psychiatry that wanted to repackage itself as legitimate medicine and by drug companies that wanted to sell drugs. STAR-D is the largest US government–funded antidepressant study, with more than four thousand patients, and it publicly claimed a 70% success rate; but independent researchers who went back over the data found that only about 3% were genuinely better a year later. The ads keep evolving, but the message they carry is always the same: the problem is you, not your situation.

— Jen Lumanlan

Glossary

Burnout
A state of physical and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged excessive stress; it is not a DSM diagnosis.
DSM
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, published by the American Psychiatric Association; the interview argues it ignores the social environment.
Medicalization
The process of redefining social or emotional problems as medical illnesses.
Benzodiazepines
Sedative drugs typified by Valium, once widely prescribed to women.
