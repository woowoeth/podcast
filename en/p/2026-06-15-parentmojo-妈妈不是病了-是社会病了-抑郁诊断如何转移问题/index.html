Mom Isn't Sick, Society Is: How a Depression Diagnosis Moves the Problem

Burnout gets misdiagnosed as depression, and the problem is attributed to the individual brain rather than the social environment. A New Zealand study found that mothers who had someone come help with housework every day for six months were almost never postpartum depressed, yet medication remains the first-line treatment.

The disease model ignores the environment

Bob points out that the disease model focuses the problem inside the individual's head rather than on social arrangements. Women both work and carry most of the housework, and when they burn out from overwork, the system doesn't say this is a question of how society is organized — it says "you're depressed, you're anxious, you're stressed, take a pill." The disease model does not respond to people's life circumstances at all. If one group in a society is under more pressure than another, then the group under more pressure will be the one diagnosed more.

— Bob Whitaker
6:25

From hysteria to benzodiazepines

The medicalization of women's distress has a long history: in the 19th century men locked unhappy wives in asylums and classified them as hysterical. When the benzodiazepines Valium and Librium came on the market in the '60s, the ads were aimed mainly at women. Bob describes the ads of the time: is your wife nagging you and making you unhappy when you come home? Give her Valium and she'll greet you with your slippers and a drink. Milltown was an earlier drug of the same kind. The essence of this pathway is to make women no longer have emotions and to serve men better.

— Bob Whitaker
8:18

From tranquilizers to antidepressants

In the '80s benzodiazepines fell into disrepute over addiction, so psychiatry redefined anxiety as depression and switched the drug from tranquilizers to antidepressants. Antidepressants have the same numbing, quieting effect. Drug companies claimed antidepressants were not addictive, and this drove the reconstruction of the diagnosis. Bob sees this as another form of social control — removing the social environment from the prescription equation entirely. Today girls face unhealthy environments like social media, TikTok and body anxiety, yet they are diagnosed with depression, anxiety and eating disorders, and then put on medication.

— Bob Whitaker
11:10

Mothers carry a disproportionate load

Jen says that when she searched YouTube for antidepressant ads, she saw 2000s-era mothers sitting on park benches gazing sadly at their children, who become good mothers after taking the pill. The ads never ask: are you doing too much, because a capitalist society needs you to work and parent with too few hands? Does your husband not share the load? Jen thinks the world hasn't really changed, only the language has adapted to today's understanding of the role. Bob agrees, and stresses that stress is off the charts for many people, but women especially carry more — housework, homework, taking the kids to the doctor all default to the mother's responsibility.

11:59

Depression is not something you have but a practice

Jen quotes the researcher Marecek: depression is not something people have but a culturally authorized set of practices through which people express to others that they are suffering. She says she is not allowed to be angry, not allowed to say the system should change, but she is allowed to turn her pain inward and make it depression, and then go get a pill. Bob strongly agrees, arguing this narrative both makes money and exempts society from the responsibility to change. Humans are naturally sensitive to their environment — poverty, racism, working 16–18 hours a day shape a person's state — but the DSM model removes the environment entirely.

— Jen Lumanlan
13:32

Six months of help, not medication

Bob describes the New Zealand study: mothers were given someone to come to the home and help with housework every week for six months, compared with usual care. The result was obvious — the group that got help had very little postpartum depression. But this intervention did not become first-line care, because the system still believes medication can solve it. Bob says the doctor at the time also admitted no one doubted that was the better response. He stresses this proves environmental intervention works but is ignored, because the pharmaceutical narrative serves larger interests.

— Bob Whitaker
14:17

The chemical imbalance was never proven

Jen sums up the key points of the full interview: depression is understood as a chemical imbalance caused by too little serotonin, a theory that was never actually proven and was a marketing story created by psychiatry wanting to repackage itself as legitimate medicine and by drug companies wanting to sell drugs. STAR-D is the largest U.S. government-funded antidepressant study, with more than four thousand patients, publicly claiming a 70% success rate, but independent researchers who re-examined the data found that only about 3% were truly better after a year. The ads keep evolving, but the message is always the same: the problem is you, not your circumstances.

— Jen Lumanlan
