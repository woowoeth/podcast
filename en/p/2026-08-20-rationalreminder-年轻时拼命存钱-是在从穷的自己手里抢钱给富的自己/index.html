Saving Hard When You're Young Is Robbing Your Poor Self to Pay Your Rich Self

The most popular iron rules of personal finance—save hard while young, dividends drive 40% of returns, index funds only give you average, bonds and cash are safer—mostly get the causality backwards. What actually determines the outcome isn't the rule, it's whether you can hold on.

8:06

Extreme saving when young is robbing yourself

‘Save hard while young and let compounding work’ is treated as an iron rule, but mechanically it's backwards: when you're young your income is at its lifetime low, and each dollar has the highest marginal utility for improving your life—$5,000 at 25 might mean a safer neighborhood, better food, a more reliable car; the same money at 45, even counting the investment growth in between, does far less for your standard of living. So extreme saving when young sacrifices the things that matter most at the time you can least afford it. PWL writer Nick summed it up: you're effectively taking money from the poor person (your low-income present self) and giving it to the rich person (your high-income future self).

— Benjamin Felix
15:11

People who save all their lives can't spend later

Dan offers a counterintuitive observation from the advisor's chair: people who won't spend because they want to build wealth may end up with a lot of wealth but lose the ability to spend it—even when the wealth far exceeds anything they could need, they still can't spend it, because the mindset has hardened. He says this is a chronic problem wealth advisors see commonly; the client base is itself wealthier, so it doesn't represent the whole population, but it's precisely people who saved aggressively and invested well who end up here: they arrive where they wanted to arrive but can't enjoy it. Felix adds the mechanism: cultivating a habit for a lifetime and expecting to flip a switch one day and become a spender is naive.

— Dan Bortolotti
19:17

Why spending and saving arguments get so heated

The topic is a minefield because the decisions are irreversible: someone who saved all their life can't go back and re-experience, and someone who spent on experiences can't go back and re-save. You're facing people who have already made irreversible decisions, and telling them ‘you should have spent more’ or ‘you should have saved more’ feels like a personal attack, because it was a decision they made and can never undo—and thanks to compounding, undoing gets harder over time. Dan adds: as long as the choice was thought through, you shouldn't moralize about others' choices; saying ‘you should save’ easily sounds like judgment, and people get defensive.

— Benjamin Felix
26:23

The stock market isn't the economy; growth is already priced

‘Economic growth is good for stock returns’ is wrong, because the stock market isn't the economy. Stock prices represent real companies' expected future cash flows, and by the time you read the economic news and hear about a market or sector's growth potential, those growth expectations are probably already in the price. Historically this holds at both the industry and country level: industries can grow massively while stock returns are poor, and shrinking industries like railroads have delivered great returns; at the country level, the countries with the highest economic growth often produce lower average stock returns—though Felix explicitly says this isn't a statistically significant result, only that there's no relationship between the two. Dan's version: everyone knows a good company, the market will pay a higher price for it, so ‘good but not as good as imagined’ stocks move opposite to what most people expect.

— Benjamin Felix
29:26

Dividends describe returns, they don't explain them

‘Dividends explain 40% of historical stock returns’ is often used by dividend investors to argue dividends matter, but Felix thinks it gets causality backwards. When a company pays a dividend, your return doesn't increase; it just converts capital into income: the dividend you receive roughly one-for-one reduces the capital value of the shares you hold, and what changes is the character of what you own, not your return. So dividends at most describe returns, not explain them. He offers a comparison: dividend ETFs and buyback ETFs have similar factor exposures (both tilt value, profitability, conservative investment), but buyback ETFs have lower dividend yields yet outperform dividend payers, with an even bigger after-tax gap—if dividends were the source of returns, it should be the other way around.

— Benjamin Felix
36:31

Index funds give you the top quartile

‘Index funds only give you average returns’ is still a circulating myth, usually to set up some strategy that can give you above-average returns. Felix gives two reasons: first, individual stock returns are highly skewed, most stocks do poorly and a few do extremely well, so you're more likely to pick losers, and missing the big winners makes it hard to keep up with the market; second, fees, the average index fund expense ratio is only a fraction of active funds, pushing the entire expected return distribution toward the index side. On the data, the SPIVA report shows that for the 20 years ending December 2025, the asset-weighted annualized return of US actively managed equity mutual funds was 9.41%, trailing US equity index ETFs by more than 1 percentage point. Index funds easily land in the top quartile of active funds.

— Benjamin Felix
42:34

High CAPE doesn't mean low future returns

Shiller CAPE is a valuation measure using real earnings smoothed over the past 10 years; a high CAPE means you're paying more for future earnings, and mathematically expected returns are lower—that part is true. The real myth is the certainty and the use people make of this data: it's often used to sell other products, or to argue against index funds (‘valuations are high now, you should go active, you should allocate to private equity’). In US data, CAPE this high only appeared around the dot-com bubble, and that period was indeed followed by low returns, but that's just one period. Cameron's conclusion is that the data is too noisy and the future too uncertain to be confident that high CAPE necessarily means low future returns; future earnings could be very high, the market might never crash, and CAPE could go higher than at any time in US history—US history isn't world history.

— Cameron Passmore
53:39

Bonds and cash aren't safe assets

Bonds and cash are less volatile than stocks, so they feel safe, but what really matters for a long-term investor is whether you can pay the bills throughout retirement. Scott Cederburg et al.'s 2025 paper Beyond the Status Quo used block bootstrap to simulate the full lifecycles of 1 million US couples, with historical data covering 39 developed countries, from as early as 1890 through 2023, totaling 2,600 years of country-month return data. They tested almost all allocations of domestic stocks, international stocks, bonds, and bills, plus target-date funds and 60/40. The conclusion: bills, 60/40, and target-date funds are all inferior to an all-stock portfolio in retirement wealth, income replacement rate, probability of ruin under the 4% rule, and wealth left to heirs at death. The optimal portfolio is 100% stocks, about 33% domestic and 67% international.

— Cameron Passmore
57:41

Volatility itself can destroy the plan

Dan's attitude toward the above research: the research is fine, but it may underestimate the effect of volatility on investor behavior. He gives a real case—a fairly conservative client watched Cederburg's video and called to ask whether a 100% stock portfolio was right for him; Dan asked ‘can you accept losing 50% in six months’, and the conversation ended there. This happened in 2008; the vast majority of people can't stick to the plan through that kind of decline, and making big moves in asset allocation usually means selling at the bottom, which can permanently damage the portfolio. So nobody adds fixed income and cash to increase returns; they add it to smooth the ride; as long as you can meet all financial goals with a buffer and sleep better, a more conservative portfolio is perfectly fine.

— Dan Bortolotti

Glossary

CAPE
Cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, a valuation measure using inflation-adjusted average earnings over the past 10 years, proposed by Shiller.
block bootstrap
A statistical method that resamples blocks from a historical return series to generate many simulated paths.
SPIVA
S&P's report comparing active fund performance against benchmark indices.
