AI's 'agents' are misnamed: autonomy requires computational closure, not compute stacking

Determining autonomy is not about Turing completeness, but whether a system's internal loops can close on themselves—which is exactly where today's so-called AI agents fall short.

Not all state changes and information transfer constitute computation

Not every information transfer or state change counts as computation. Nima developed a physical computation theory to provide a falsifiable definition, in contrast to casual neuroscience papers where 'that circuit computes X' often functions as an unjustified assertion.

— Nima Dehghani
32:45

Turing completeness does not guarantee autonomy

A French team demonstrated this with oil-droplet walkers: liquid droplets bounce on vibrating oil surfaces, with ripples feeding back to create chaotic trajectories with memory, erasure, and write primitives—Turing-complete, yet not autonomous. The closure comes from the experimenter flipping the wave phase externally, not from the system itself. This gap led Nima to develop the concept of computational closure: closure must emerge from within the system.

— Nima Dehghani
44:04

Calling AI systems 'agents' is a fundamental misnaming

Agency emerges from computational closure—when a system samples the environment, constructs an internal world model, and uses it to regulate its own behavior and state (like biological homeostatic setpoints). Today's AI systems called 'agents' lack this loop entirely. When they fail, it's described as the system 'going out of control,' but that gets the causality wrong—they didn't develop agency and rebel; humans simply didn't set the right boundaries.

— Nima Dehghani
1:06:00

Biology cannot afford backpropagation's weight-update machinery

Feedforward networks are straightforward to design and stack, but recurrent networks are another matter—Nima calls them 'neuroscience's holy grail.' While the brain must constantly change its operations, it cannot afford the rapid, large-scale weight updates that backpropagation requires. Nima breaks the problem into two basic building blocks: 'funnels' (systems with final states) and 'gears' (systems where states can reverse), using this framework to analyze what divisive normalization and winner-take-all circuits actually compute.

— Nima Dehghani
1:09:01

Two circuits combined can compute what each cannot alone

Divisive normalization in isolation produces only a limited set of output states, as does winner-take-all. Connect them in different directions—controlling which pushes which, where inputs flow—and the total state space expands dramatically. Even more striking: purely 'funnel'-based systems, when combined this way, suddenly produce 'gear'-like reversible behavior that neither component could achieve alone. Nima sees this as how evolution reuses modules, and suggests a path forward for designing recurrent systems based on mathematical prediction of combination effects, rather than relying on massive datasets and backpropagation.

— Nima Dehghani
1:18:21

Feedforward networks face mathematical barriers some problems cannot overcome

Nima is preparing a paper (already at the sixteenth revision) proving that certain physical problems are mathematically unsolvable by feedforward systems—they require recurrent architecture. He connects this to embodied knowledge: large language models can describe rain's temperature, paint it in Monet's style, write haiku about it, yet experience no rain. His dog never studied Galileo's physics but genuinely knows what rain is. This lived experience is what feedforward systems structurally cannot access; recurrence can.

— Nima Dehghani
1:22:24

Recurrent systems can learn without changing weights

Nima and collaborator Bahtash Babadi are completing a paper showing how a fully recurrent system can explicitly implement all the mathematics backpropagation performs—but without changing any weights. Learning happens in the system's recursive state rather than through weight updates. Nima is careful to note this is not a claim about how brains actually work, but rather a pointer toward where investigation should focus.

— Nima Dehghani
1:36:45

Not all brain activity is computation—some is information relay

When asked what fraction of brain processing is actual computation, Nima says he doesn't know. But he invokes David Deutsch's standard: good explanations must be difficult to arbitrarily patch when new phenomena appear. Using his current work on E. coli chemotaxis as an example, if he discovers that some component is merely information relay, he'll say so directly—rather than forcing the 'computation' label onto every corner of the system.

— Nima Dehghani

Glossary

computational closure
A system's internal loops that convert environmental sensing and modeling into regulation of its own behavior and state, forming a self-sufficient circuit.
functor
A category theory tool that maps objects and relations from one mathematical or physical system onto another.
divisive normalization
A neural circuit that scales a population's output by dividing by the overall activity level.
winner-take-all
A neural circuit where mutual inhibition between populations leaves only one remaining active.
wave walker
A droplet bouncing on a vibrating oil surface that leaves ripples feeding back to guide motion, forming a Turing-complete yet externally-closed system.
