Ali Got Up Off the Canvas Only After Cooper's Left Hook Woke Him

In that rain-soaked fight in 1963, Cooper's "Henry's Hammer" scrambled Clay's brains and knocked out his illusion that he could win with his mouth — afterward he refused to put the prop crown back on and called Cooper a real fighter.

0:15

Before the fight he called his opponent a bum

Cassius Clay, 21, had just arrived in London with eyes only for world champion Sonny Liston; British heavyweight champion Henry Cooper was merely a transitional opponent to make money and stay sharp while he waited for his shot. He said publicly that Cooper was a bum, a bum, a cripple, not worth training for. Talk like that was unprecedented in 1963 boxing — fighters had never run their mouths like this. British reporters gave him a string of nicknames: the Louisville Lip, the Kentucky Horn, and finally "Gaseous Clay."

— Tim Harford
7:27

His mouth embarrassed his white backers

Clay's employers were the Louisville Sponsoring Group, a set of Kentucky business elites each putting up a few thousand tax-free dollars to fund his training and pay his salary in exchange for 50% of his earnings inside and outside the ring. They sold out Wembley Stadium on the strength of his mouth while rushing to distance themselves to the British press: Cassius is a good boy, all that bragging is just for the box office, his mother used to cook for us. Around the same time, American civil rights activists hoped this smart, successful athlete would work for desegregation, but Clay used his platform to humiliate other Black fighters, and with the same racist tropes that likened Black people to beasts.

— Tim Harford
12:36

Cooper trained a left hook in a pub

Cooper turned the banquet hall of a South London pub, the Fellowship Inn, into a gym, with birthday parties and wedding receptions going on next door. He studied Clay's unusual style — hands low, no guard on the face — and specially imported an American heavyweight sparring partner who could mimic Clay's back-pedaling attack, drilling against him again and again just to find a way to close the distance. His weapon was the left hook, which one boxing writer said caused "acute discomfort," and it had a name: "Henry's Hammer." On paper Cooper was the underdog: older, lighter, inches shorter in reach, and with skin as thin as porcelain — a hiccup could open a cut.

— Tim Harford
15:42

In the first round he was already holding on

Cooper was known for starting cautiously, but this time he charged out of his corner like a tiger, and the left hook worked fast. Within a minute Clay's nose was bleeding, his face blank, desperately clinching the British champion to stop more punishment. The cocky, confident Clay of the pre-fight buildup was gone; the one in the ring was confused and indignant, even arguing with the referee that Cooper was fighting dirty. At the end of the first round Cooper was clearly ahead, and Clay's cornermen were stopping the bleeding while pressing ice on his swollen brow. In the second, a TV commentator said what had been unthinkable four minutes earlier: could Cooper beat Clay?

— Tim Harford
19:47

He insisted on dragging it to the fifth round

In the third round Cooper was cut above the left eye, blood pouring out and blurring his vision, and the referee could stop the fight at any moment. But Clay was "reborn": he dropped his guard entirely, dancing, mugging, talking trash, barely throwing punches. The Louisville Sponsoring Group men at ringside shouted at him to stop playing and finish the fight; Clay arrogantly ignored them. He had told the world he would settle Cooper in the fifth round, so even if he had to drag his opponent to the bell, he would win in the round he had chosen, in his own style. A commentator said bitterly that he was treating Cooper like a toy.

— Tim Harford
21:56

Henry's Hammer fell and he nearly lost

Clay dropped his guard and stepped in with a left jab that landed lightly on Cooper's lowered head. Cooper rose from his crouch and there in front of him was Clay's grinning, wide-open face. In that instant Clay may have realized he had made a mistake, may have seen Henry's Hammer coming, but he almost certainly did not expect its power — when it hit his face it was less like a hammer than a wrecking ball. Clay went down against the ropes, slid to the canvas, his head lolling, eyes blank. Richard Burton shouted from ringside, "Finish him, Henry." It was the bell beating the referee's count that saved him.

— Tim Harford
30:14

For sixty seconds his team skirted cheating

Clay was helped to his corner, dazed, asking "Where am I," unsure whether he was in London or Louisville. The team had only 60 seconds to bring him back. In the confusion someone was seen waving a small bottle under his nose, making him grimace and recoil — smelling salts are banned in British boxing, and using them could mean a loss. But the referee's attention was diverted: Clay's chief trainer noticed a split seam in his left glove, widened the opening, and then asked the referee whether to delay the next round so he could change gloves. Every second hurt the still-bleeding Cooper. The referee thought about it and decided to continue. The delay stretched the rest period by less than ten seconds, but it seemed just enough for Clay to recover.

— Tim Harford
32:18

He won but refused to put the crown back on

Cooper's wound was described by one sportswriter as a "scarlet Niagara," and he felt like he was standing under an open tap. Elizabeth Taylor screamed for the fight to be stopped, and Cooper's trainer finally threw the towel over the ropes. The referee pulled Cooper aside and said, "Sorry, old chap," and Cooper replied, "Not bad for a cripple and a bum." Clay celebrated the win, but when his brother tried to put the prop crown back on his head, he pushed it away. In the post-fight interview he had only respect for Cooper: Cooper was a real fighter, the hardest of nuts, no longer a bum.

— Tim Harford

Glossary

short win / long win
A concept from Catherine Bishop: the thrill of a title can be brief and hollow, while a long win is lasting meaning beyond the trophy.
Bodyline Series
The 1932 England-Australia cricket series, in which England bowled at the batsmen's bodies, winning the matches but destroying its reputation.
smelling salts
Irritant salts used to rouse someone from unconsciousness, banned in British boxing.
