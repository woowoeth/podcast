Before He Was Ali, Cooper's Left Hook Had to Knock the Swagger Out of Him

In that rain-soaked 1963 fight, Cooper's 'Henry's Hammer' left Clay stunned — and knocked out his illusion that he could win on talk alone. Afterward he refused to let the prop crown go back on his head, and called Cooper a real fighter instead.

0:15

Before the fight, he called his opponent a tramp

The 21-year-old Cassius Clay had just arrived in London with his eyes fixed only on world champion Sonny Liston; British heavyweight champion Henry Cooper was just a paycheck and a way to stay sharp while he waited for his shot. Clay publicly called Cooper a tramp, a bum, a cripple not worth training for. Talk like that was unheard of in boxing in 1963 — no fighter had ever run his mouth like this, nonstop. British reporters gave him a string of nicknames: the Louisville Lip, the Kentucky Bugler, and finally, 'Gaseous Cassius.'

7:27

His mouth embarrassed the white businessmen who owned him

Clay's employer was the Louisville Sponsoring Group, a syndicate of Kentucky business elites who each put in a few thousand tax-free dollars to fund his training and pay his salary, in exchange for 50% of his earnings in and out of the ring. Even as his mouth sold out Wembley, they scrambled to reassure the British press: Cassius was a good kid, the wild talk was just for ticket sales, his mother used to cook for us. At the same time, American civil rights activists had hoped this smart, successful athlete would lend his platform to the fight against segregation — instead Clay used it to humiliate other Black boxers, borrowing the same racist language that likened Black men to animals.

— Tim Harford
12:36

Cooper trained his knockout left hook inside a pub function room

Cooper turned the function room of a South London pub, the Fellowship Inn, into a gym, with birthday parties and weddings going on next door. He studied Clay's unusual style — hands held low, face unguarded — and imported an American heavyweight sparring partner who could mimic Clay's backward-hopping footwork, drilling over and over just to find a way to close the distance on him. His weapon was a left hook that one boxing writer said could cause 'violent discomfort'; it had a name — 'Henry's Hammer.' On paper Cooper was the underdog: older, lighter, several inches shorter in reach, and with skin so thin it could split if he so much as hiccuped.

— Tim Harford
15:42

By round one, he was already clinching just to survive

Cooper was known for a cautious, hesitant start, but this time he came out of his corner like a tiger, and the left hook found its mark fast. Clay's nose was bleeding within the first minute; he looked dazed and clinched onto the British champion in desperation to stop the punches. The brash, cocky Clay from before the fight was gone — in his place was a fighter who looked confused and indignant, even arguing with the referee that Cooper was fighting unfairly. By the end of round one Cooper was clearly ahead, and Clay's corner men were stopping the bleeding while pressing ice against his swelling eyebrow. In round two, the TV commentator said something that would have been unthinkable four minutes earlier: could Cooper beat Clay?

— Tim Harford
19:47

He insisted on stalling until round five to win his way

In round three, a cut opened above Cooper's left eye, and blood poured out and blurred his vision — the referee could have stopped the fight at any moment. But this was when Clay seemed reborn: he dropped his guard completely, danced, mugged for the crowd, talked trash, and barely threw a punch. Men from the Louisville Sponsoring Group shouted from ringside for him to stop clowning around and finish it, and Clay arrogantly ignored them. He had already told the world he would finish Cooper in round five, so even if it meant dragging his opponent to the bell, he was going to win in the round he'd chosen, in his own style. A commentator said bitterly that he was treating Cooper like a toy.

— Tim Harford
21:56

Henry's Hammer landed, and he nearly lost the fight

Clay dropped his guard and stepped in with a left jab that landed lightly on the top of Cooper's lowered head. Cooper rose up out of his crouch to find Clay's grinning, completely unguarded face right in front of him. In that instant Clay may have realized his mistake, may even have seen Henry's Hammer coming — but he almost certainly didn't expect its power. It didn't land like a hammer so much as a wrecking ball. Clay went down against the ropes, slid to the canvas, his head lolling, his eyes empty. Richard Burton shouted from ringside, 'Finish him, Henry!' It was the bell, ringing before the referee finished the count, that saved him.

— Tim Harford
30:14

In sixty seconds, his corner skirted the edge of cheating

Clay was helped to his corner, dazed, asking 'Where am I?' — unsure whether he was in London or Louisville. His team had only 60 seconds to bring him around. Amid the chaos, someone was seen waving a small vial under his nose, making him flinch and recoil — smelling salts were banned in British boxing, and using them could mean disqualification. But the referee's attention was pulled elsewhere: Clay's chief trainer found a split in the stitching of his left glove, worked the tear open further, and asked the referee whether the next round should be delayed so Clay could change gloves. Every extra second worked against the still-bleeding Cooper. The referee thought it over and decided to continue. The delay stretched the rest period by less than ten seconds — but it seems to have been just enough for Clay to recover.

— Tim Harford
32:18

He won, but refused to put the crown back on

A sportswriter described Cooper's cut as a 'scarlet Niagara Falls'; Cooper himself said it felt like standing under a running tap. Elizabeth Taylor screamed for the fight to be stopped, and Cooper's trainer finally threw in the towel. The referee pulled Cooper away, saying 'Sorry, mate,' and Cooper answered, 'Not bad for a cripple and a tramp.' Clay celebrated the win, but when his brother tried to put the prop crown back on his head, he pushed it away. In his post-fight interview he had nothing but respect for Cooper: a real fighter, the toughest of the tough — no longer a tramp.

— Tim Harford

Glossary

short win / long win
A concept from Katherine Bishop: the thrill of taking the title can be short and hollow, while the long win is a lasting kind of meaning that outlasts the trophy.
Bodyline Series
A 1932 cricket series between England and Australia in which the English team bowled at their opponents' bodies — winning the match but wrecking their reputation.
smelling salts
A pungent stimulant used to revive someone who's dazed or unconscious — banned in British boxing.
