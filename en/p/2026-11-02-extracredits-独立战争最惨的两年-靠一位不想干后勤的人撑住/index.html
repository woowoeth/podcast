The Revolutionary War's darkest two years, held up by a reluctant quartermaster

Greene won at Trenton and Princeton and lost at Brandywine and Germantown, but what truly kept the Continental Army going was the logistics chief position he grimly undertook.

American Revolutionary WarLogistics ManagementMilitary HistoryOrganizational ResilienceGeorge Washington
If all you know is that Greene was a hero in the Southern Campaign, this episode reveals how he propped up the supply system and how that job nearly broke him.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:05

The Trenton surprise attack proved Greene's worth for the first time

After the fall of Fort Washington, Greene wrote that he felt 'angry, frustrated, miserable, and sorrowful.' But he didn't give up. On Christmas Day, he led one of four columns across the Delaware River in a heavy snowstorm, launching a surprise attack on the Hessian garrison. His men quickly broke through the outer defenses and covered the artillery's bombardment, delivering the Continental Army's first genuine victory. A week later, he used the same surprise tactics to defeat British regulars at Princeton. These two victories reignited the revolutionary spirit and made Greene a regular at Washington's war councils.

5:09

The British used the same trick twice to fool the Continental Army

At Brandywine, Greene's troops held the line south of the ford against the British feint attack—it looked like it would succeed. Then came word: the main British force had already crossed upriver and was sweeping toward Washington's right flank. Greene quickly repositioned his forces to hold back the pursuing British, allowing the rest of the army to escape. He even deployed troops to shield the slow-moving artillery from capture. Philadelphia still fell, but thanks to Greene's rearguard action, Washington's army stayed intact and fought another day.

7:14

In thick fog, Greene's troops opened fire on friendly forces

Germantown was meant to repeat Trenton's multi-column surprise attack. The morning fog should have covered the Continental Army's approach, but it also made coordination impossible. One of Greene's brigades exchanged fire with another column for a long time before realizing they were firing on fellow Continental troops. Both units scattered in retreat. By the time Greene found the actual British, Washington's main assault had already withdrawn and he was surrounded by Cornwallis's forces. Only the Ninth Virginia Regiment's charge held off the enemy long enough for his escape. This battle taught Greene that offense is far harder than defense, especially in the fog of war.

9:23

The quartermaster's position Greene reluctantly accepted

By 1778, Washington's supply lines had completely collapsed—a new army paired with a new government had no functioning supply system. Washington turned to the most organized, most capable man he knew: Greene. Greene had no interest; the job offered no glory and only hardship. But understanding the army's absolute need to spare no effort, he accepted. He rebuilt the supply of grain, forage, and transport wagons. When supply lines broke, he rerouted shipments by barge along waterways, established supply depots along the route, and slowly rebuilt trust with merchants and farmers holding Continental IOUs. The situation gradually improved.

11:28

To save his hometown Rhode Island, Greene boarded a ship for negotiations

Plans to retake Greene's home state of Rhode Island required French naval support. A storm and British attack badly damaged the French fleet. The French commander decided to sail directly to Boston for repairs instead of supporting the landing at Newport—which lay just across from Greene's home. Greene and Lafayette boarded the French commander's flagship to try to persuade him to stay. Greene half-joked that even if the talks failed, at least they'd get a good dinner. The negotiations did fail; the French fleet sailed away and Greene's plan to liberate his homeland ended before it began.

12:29

Commanding troops and managing logistics pushed Greene to the breaking point

Congress had almost no money. Greene cared for sick children while being tormented day and night by quartermaster duties. He submitted his resignation to Congress, writing that he was 'in a difficult situation with neither money nor credit,' and described the exhaustion and anxiety from maintaining the army's fighting strength—what we'd now call total burnout. Washington supported his resignation from logistics and fiercely defended Greene's major general rank when Congress reacted angrily to his harsh words.

13:35

When the Southern theater collapsed, all hope rested on Greene

The war's focus had shifted south: the British retook Savannah in 1778, captured Charleston in 1780, and crushed a Continental force at Camden. Washington needed someone who both understood supply logistics and would avoid catastrophic pitched battles to rebuild the Southern army—only Greene fit. He had to draw on all the skills of both 'Quartermaster Nathanael' and 'Soldier Nathanael' in the series of campaigns that followed, racing Cornwallis toward the Dan River in a desperate effort to turn the tide.
