Literacy Once Democratised Europe; Now It Is Retreating

Writing cooled human communication from an emotional, face-to-face oral culture into a textual culture of evidence and logic; rising literacy once drove democratic revolutions, and screens are pulling public discourse back toward a pre-literate age.

Literacy is historically very recent and very fragile

Marriott stresses that we are so used to literacy that we forget how anomalous it is. By one estimate, in a certain ancient Mesopotamian city-state there may have been only 20 living people who could read and write; literacy in ancient Rome was around 10%. For thousands of years, reading and writing was not a cultural phenomenon but an esoteric code circulating within a wealthy scribal elite — some rulers were literate, some were not. The real turning point was printing: books could be reproduced in quantity, more people could read them, and books became more practical; Protestantism then placed enormous emphasis on the written word, pushing more people to become literate in order to read the Bible. These factors fed each other until the "Reading Revolution" historians identify in the mid-to-late 18th century.

— James Marriott
11:19

The Reading Revolution really did change the shape of politics

Roberts pushes back: if literacy exploded, why doesn't the world look turned upside down? Marriott's answer is that the spread of literacy had a democratising effect — the more people who could access information and ideas, the more people participated in politics and criticised power. Early 18th-century Europe was ruled by absolute monarchs, and political affairs were essentially court secrets discussed among a small elite; by the late 18th and early 19th centuries, newspapers appeared, the standing of absolute monarchs declined sharply, France became a republic, the authority of the British monarch was far weaker than at the start of the century, parliamentary debates were reported, and public affairs were no longer discussed only in court. He calls this the great age of democratic revolution and democratic reform, and ties it directly to the spread of literacy.

— James Marriott
16:32

Writing forces bad ideas into logical form

Marriott concedes books are imperfect, but argues that the way books organise information is more rational and more coherent than conversation, video or TikTok. Writing forces you to polish an idea repeatedly, to externalise part of your mind onto the page and then test it; the reader faces a fixed text and can reread and critically analyse it. More crucially, books restrict the means of non-logical persuasion — you cannot bypass argument with charisma, shouting or tears. He uses the Nazis as a counterexample: Hitler's Mein Kampf is unreadable even for Nazi elites, because Nazism simply does not hold together once it is required to be presented coherently as a written text; yet Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will is treated as an artistic masterpiece, screened in film classes, and regarded as an extremely powerful argument for fascism. Bad ideas are easier to whitewash on screen than in a book.

— James Marriott
25:05

The Republic of Letters filtered out the trolls through writing

The 17th- and 18th-century European "Republic of Letters" was a community of thinkers, philosophers and scientists who wrote to each other and ran journals. The economic historian Joel Mokyr rates it enormously highly, even calling it the single cultural fact that explains the Industrial Revolution. Marriott's mechanism is this: if a clever person sits alone in a village, his ideas can only be told to his neighbours, and scientific innovation struggles to accumulate; the spread of literacy let ideas travel further, more and more people joined the conversation, ideas flowed into one central pool, and thought grew more complex. Roberts adds that the key difference is not public versus private but who the participants are — in conversation with Adam Smith, David Hume and Newton, the standard is naturally high; it is a kind of "submission to that which cannot be enforced". Marriott concedes that the literacy barrier itself carries a benign elitism: you cannot barge in and shout like a troll on X, because nobody will pay attention to you.

— James Marriott
34:49

The novel gives us a passage into other minds

Marriott argues there is a kind of truth about life that only prose fiction can reach: it lets us see the inside of other people's minds. Reading is an active rather than passive process — you use the words on the page to build images in your head, which is why it is immersive, why it feels like having lived another life. Film cannot do this, because what it gives you is the external world and it cannot enter another mind directly; writing is precisely the medium we use to express what is in our minds, mind communicating with mind. He also notes that oral poetry has little interest in the subtle differences between human consciousnesses, and that this capacity to "inhabit another mind" appears only in prose fiction. Roberts does not fully agree, arguing that great films can get close through dialogue, but concedes that film almost never captures the inner activity that is conveyed by more than dialogue.

— James Marriott
47:59

The post-literate commentator is more like Homer

Marriott writes that the post-literate commentator does not explain and analyse the world like the print-age public intellectual, but is more like an oral storyteller, weaving strange and unforgettable tales for a captivated audience. Roberts calls this the deepest insight in the whole piece: faced with the mass of absurd, untenable claims online, people habitually interrogate them by the author's standards — "that isn't logical", "where's the evidence?" — but these people are simply not in that business. Their job is to entertain the audience; truth does not matter, what matters is a story that captivates. Marriott goes further: presenting an argument logically, supplying evidence, driving to a rational conclusion — these are not in fact natural to human communication; in an oral culture, the more effective way to persuade someone is to entertain him, move him, get angry at him. Writing imposes an impersonal, more rational form of communication, and it is not a necessary product of human communication.

— James Marriott
53:59

The richness of pop music also feeds off reading

Roberts gives an example from The History of Rock and Roll in 500 Songs: the opening of Jagger's "Sympathy for the Devil" is taken directly from Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita — the novel opens with two Russian intellectuals mocking God and faith, after which a suave gentleman appears, they think he is mad, and finally they realise it is Satan. Marriott calls this an "annoyingly good" example and regrets not putting it in the book. He concedes it cannot be pushed too far — it is not that you have to move the Penguin Classics into the British Library to join a boy band — but Bob Dylan and Paul McCartney are both obviously heavy readers. He cites research showing that over the past 20 years pop songs have become less complex in both lyrics and melody, partly because the environment favours the simple and the immediate: to go viral on TikTok, a song has to grab you within three or four seconds.

— James Marriott
58:17

The future of reading will be deliberate, like going to the gym

Marriott stresses this is not a new phenomenon: statistics on the decline of serious reading go back to the 1950s and 1960s, television was the beginning of the screen age, and the remote control was the first scroll. Roberts quotes what his father said at the time — books have existed for centuries, television has been a mass medium for only 20 years, let's see if it lasts — and concedes his father should have worried more. Marriott's consolation comes from an analogy with junk food and exercise: McDonald's arrived, many people got fat, but some people realised that health could no longer be absorbed naturally from the environment and required deliberate effort, so they started running and going to the gym. He guesses cognitive health will follow the same path: if reading has a future, it will become more deliberate, less "I'm bored at the airport so I'll pick up a book" and more "I value this experience, so I sit down for half an hour to an hour every night". He admits this makes him a little sad, because reading will become more artificial, no longer the unavoidable part of life it once was.

Glossary

post-literate society
A society in which literacy and reading are no longer the dominant cognitive mode, held to resemble a pre-literate society in some respects.
Republic of Letters
The transnational knowledge community of 17th- and 18th-century European thinkers and scientists who corresponded by letter and journal.
Reading Revolution
Historians' name for the sudden spread of reading and the ubiquity of books and periodicals in the mid-to-late 18th century.
sitzfleisch
A German word for the capacity to sit still for a long time and grind through difficult texts.
