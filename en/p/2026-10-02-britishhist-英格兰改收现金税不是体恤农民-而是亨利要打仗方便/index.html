England's cash-tax reform wasn't mercy to farmers—it was convenience for war

The royal treasurer Fitzneal claimed Henry switched from payment-in-kind to cash taxation to spare farmers, but cash was lighter, wouldn't rot, and let him hire mercenaries overseas directly—that's what Henry actually wanted.

2:08

Tyrants can be gracious hosts—the Victorian logic is faulty

Some historians assume Gaimar's claim that Henry "excelled at entertaining" must be a joke, because Henry ordered castrations and couldn't have been likable. But Gaimar wasn't joking; Walter Map independently confirmed Henry's feasts were magnificent. The host points out that the reasoning—tyrants cannot be charming—actually conflates "throwing good parties" with "being good," which is illogical. Rufus was equally ruthless and equally good at celebrations, yet no one disputes that. Henry could be both cold-blooded tyrant and beloved master without contradiction.

4:17

The royal court wasn't a council—it was a moving village

Most imagine a medieval court as men in wool meeting in a hall. In reality, Henry's court was simultaneously government seat, military retinue, and full administrative apparatus—scribes, guards, judges, clergy. Each nobleman brought knights and servants: bakers, cooks, hunters, women, hangers-on. Add ostlers for the horses, laborers for tents and fires. Every magnate who joined swelled the camp; every departure shrank it. The royal household ballooned and deflated with extreme frequency.

— Jamie
7:25

Norman courts devastated like locusts; Saxon courts had predictable circuits

In Saxon times, kings traveled known routes; nobles prepared grain stores and readied livestock. After the Norman Conquest, new kings moved on whim, unpredictably. This enormous retinue couldn't be forecast or provisioned. By Rufus's reign, the royal tour became open plunder: like locusts, the court drained wine cellars, disrupted markets, left behind debt, famine, and filth. This had gone on for decades. Henry inherited a traveling economic disaster.

— Jamie
14:38

Publishing an itinerary reformed royal governance after decades of chaos

Previously, when and where the king would arrive was unknowable; villagers couldn't prepare. Henry's solution was almost absurdly simple: publish the itinerary like a vacation schedule—where the court was going, how long it would stay, what came next—so subjects could stockpile supplies. The host jokes that years of governance catastrophe could make a second-grader's task look revolutionary. It captures the measure of how broken the old system truly was.

— Jamie
16:46

Henry fixed prices to stop royal officers from extorting merchants

Merchants hauling goods to the royal court faced impossible odds: the king's officers paid fractions of market price, with armed men at their backs. Merchants had no choice; prices were extorted downward into meaninglessness. Henry banned both the physical violence and the "economic violence" his retinue employed. Goods were purchased at fair, fixed market rates. Only with the king present was the market protected. The effect was immediate: merchants began arriving at court willingly. They knew they were safe and would be paid fairly.

— Jamie
19:48

Henry administered his household like a state apparatus

Every royal family member—from the Lord Chancellor to water-tank keepers—had assigned tasks and budgets. Under Rufus and the Conqueror, the method was simple: 'take what you want'; costs ran wild. Henry drafted the Constitutio Domus Regis, detailing exact salaries and allowances for every post—essentially auditing his own household department. The earliest surviving manuscript dates to 1136, but references an earlier major reform; likely this one. Henry's governance of his court matched his governance of the kingdom in precision.

— Jamie
24:07

Farmers brought broken plowshares to court as evidence of ruin

Even after publishing an itinerary, Henry constantly changed plans—a storm, a diplomatic crisis, or simple pleasure at Woodstock. Bakers stockpiled flour anticipating the royal arrival, only to find the king had rerouted; loaves spoiled in their hands. Peasants who'd driven their plow oxen to the moved royal court exhausted them on the road. According to historian Judith Green, losses grew so severe that merchants and farmers pursued the court to complain in person. Fitzneal recorded a group of farmers confronting the king, plowshares in hand: oxen were gone hauling tax grain; without them, next year there would be no harvest.

— Jamie
29:18

The shift to cash taxation solved one problem and created another

To solve the losses from uncertain itineraries, Henry had local officials convert produce to cash equivalents and shifted to cash taxes. Cash wouldn't rot, traveled easily across the sea, and could pay foreign mercenaries directly—far more efficient than collecting grain and having it converted. But cash brought new trouble: English coin was rampant with counterfeiting and clipping. The king nominally collected a pound of silver; he got a pound of 'silver-like' alloy. With fixed tax numbers, a bad harvest pushed prices up while tax revenue stayed flat, gutting purchasing power. In 1124, bad coin and poor harvest together triggered obvious price inflation. Even mercenaries complained their wages weren't good silver.

Glossary

Henry I's detailed ordinance specifying exact salaries and allowances for every royal household position

The royal accounting office named for the checkered board resembling a chessboard used to calculate receipts and expenditures

A ritual feast on Easter, Pentecost, and Christmas when the king wore his crown in public to assert royal authority

The iron blade of a plow that cuts and turns the soil
