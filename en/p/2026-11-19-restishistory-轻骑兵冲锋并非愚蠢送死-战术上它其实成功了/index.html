The Light Brigade's charge was not suicidal stupidity: it was tactically successful

Ambiguous orders and feuding commanders sent 600 light cavalry into a valley of three-sided artillery fire, but they genuinely routed the Russian artillery and cavalry—a strategic disaster, a tactical victory.

Stacked valleys and ridges determine who sees the whole battlefield

The Balaclava battlefield stacked from south to north: South Valley, Causeway Heights, North Valley, Fedioukine Heights. From the 800-foot heights of Sapoon Ridge, Raglan could oversee the entire battlefield like watching a theater, but Lucan and Cardigan at the valley floor could not see what happened on Causeway Heights—only the Russian artillery at the valley's end. This gap in visibility became the geographical root of the misunderstood order and disaster.

20:50

Two thin lines stopped the Russian cavalry surge

Campbell's 93rd Highland faced overwhelming Russian cavalry numbers but, because new rifles had long range and high penetration, deployed in two-line formation instead of the traditional square, maintaining continuous fire. Campbell told his soldiers: "You have no retreat; you must hold this ground or die here"—the dense fire routed the Russian cavalry. A reporter described the moment as "the thin red streak topped with a line of steel," which was later shortened to the famous "thin red line."

— Tom
26:59

Personal animosity cost the light cavalry a chance to annihilate the Russians

After the heavy cavalry routed the Russians, pursuit and complete annihilation was the standard cavalry doctrine. But Cardigan believed his brother-in-law Lucan had previously ordered never to attack; he held his position. Lucan then arrived to deny giving any such order, and the two men argued violently on the spot. The moment was lost—even observing Captain Nolan saw it and was infuriated.

31:08

An ambiguous order triggered the disaster

Raglan saw the Russians preparing to remove captured British artillery—by Wellington tradition, lost guns meant admitting defeat—so he ordered: "Follow the enemy and prevent them removing the guns." But Lucan, standing in the valley bottom, could not see the guns on Causeway Heights; he could only see the Russian artillery at the valley's end. This ambiguous order, combined with the two men's prior mistrust, sent the light cavalry directly into the valley of three-sided artillery fire.

— Tom
44:32

The messenger Nolan tried to correct the mistake even as he died

During the charge, Nolan suddenly galloped ahead of Cardigan, turned, raised his sword and shouted—but artillery fire drowned his voice. He likely realized the order had been misunderstood and tried to correct it. Before he could be heard, shrapnel pierced his chest; his arm held unnaturally high, his body rigid in the saddle emitting an eerie shriek, then he fell dead. Cardigan remained furious at him throughout the charge, believing Nolan was overstepping his authority.

— Tom
58:59

Seven hundred charged, 195 returned, but the artillery fell silent

The charge lasted approximately 20 minutes. Of over 700 light cavalry, only 195 returned; 500 horses were killed or captured. But they did breach the Russian gun positions, suppressed the artillery, and drove the Russian cavalry from the field—by the cavalry charge standard of "scatter the enemy line and frighten them from the battlefield," the light cavalry achieved their objective. The price: the light cavalry that Raglan had carefully preserved throughout the battle were decimated in those 20 minutes.

— Tom/Dominic
1:03:03

Losing one road cost the entire siege its supply line

After Balaclava, the British completely lost Causeway Heights—and with it, the only metalled road connecting Balaclava and Sevastopol. Supply now had to use muddy tracks, directly causing thousands of British soldiers to die from frostbite and disease during the brutal Crimean winter that followed. Raglan himself died in June 1855, nominally from dysentery, but he said: "I can never return to England—they will stone me to death."
