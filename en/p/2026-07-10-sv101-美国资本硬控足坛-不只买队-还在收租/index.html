US Capital Controls 11 Premier League Clubs — Through Finance, Not Football

American money already controls 11 Premier League clubs and has reached into UEFA commercial development, rights distribution and player-transfer finance; with broadcast fees peaking, they keep the game going by pushing valuations and evergreen funds.

0:00

Trump's "suspended sentence" phone call

At this year's World Cup knockout stage, an American player was due to serve a one-match ban after a red card; reportedly, after Trump phoned FIFA president Infantino, the punishment was suspended for a year. The Wall Street Journal analysed that Infantino needs the World Cup held in North America for greater exposure and commercial revenue, and has therefore catered to Trump's preferences for years. The episode is used as an entry point: it is not just FIFA — across European football, whether the Premier League or Europe's big five leagues, Americans loom large behind the scenes.

6:21

Half the Premier League is American-owned

Of the 20 Premier League clubs, 11 are now controlled by Americans, including Manchester United, Arsenal, Liverpool and Chelsea from the big six. Most investment came after 2008, and more than half after 2018. The reasons: US quantitative easing flooded the system with capital seeking assets; some European economies recovered slowly from the financial crisis, leaving football assets undervalued; and around 2020 the pandemic shut down the game, leaving clubs short of cash, so American money bought in heavily at the bottom. American owners do not treat clubs as standalone assets but as part of their sports portfolios, linked with NFL and NBA teams.

16:14

The Ipswich sample: small clubs can pay off big

In 2021, GameChanger Twenty, led by an Arizona pension fund, bought then-League One Ipswich for £30 million. In 2024, another American private equity firm paid £105 million for 40%, meaning early investors effectively made a pure profit of 2.5 times their acquisition cost. That same year the club was promoted to the Premier League, shirt sales rose from 10,000 to 100,000 a year, and season revenue jumped from £13 million to £155 million. American consortia do not have to buy giants; small clubs can also deliver high multiples through promotion within the system and commercial development.

— Mahua
22:47

The Glazers' blood-sucking leveraged buyout

In 2005 the Glazer family used a £790 million leveraged buyout of Manchester United, putting in only £270 million of their own money and borrowing the rest. For over 20 years they have paid only interest each year, never principal, and now they have borrowed again at an interest rate in the low 5s to repay old debt. As of the end of 2024, they had extracted £1.2 billion from Manchester United, including dividends, board compensation and share sales. Meanwhile, Old Trafford has fallen into disrepair — leaking roof, rat infestation — and lost its hosting rights for Euro 2028. This playbook became the template for American acquisitions of European clubs and the root of Manchester United fans' resentment.

— Zhang Bin
30:04

Arsenal's stadium debt

Arsenal spent £450 million to build the Emirates Stadium, securitising the next 25 years of matchday ticket revenue into bonds and raising £260 million in one go. To service that debt, Arsenal pinched pennies in the transfer market for years and was forced to sell its captain, until Kroenke's full takeover and debt restructuring eased the interest burden and restored spending power. Shi Hualun points out that matchday revenue now accounts for only about 20% of big six clubs' income, but 20 years ago broadcast revenue was nowhere near today's levels; those who decided to build the stadium did not foresee that broadcast would replace tickets as the largest revenue source.

— Shi Hualun
52:47

Chelsea's speculation blew up

In 2022, the Boehly and Clearlake consortium took over Chelsea from sanctioned Abramovich for £2.5 billion. They applied American experience, signing players to 8- to 10-year contracts to stretch amortisation, manoeuvring the books, and buying a dozen or twenty players at once. This rule-bending prompted the Premier League to repeatedly amend its rules to close loopholes. The result: Chelsea's form fluctuated, finishing as low as 12th, with 50 players crammed into the training facility. Shi Hualun believes the owners entered at too speculative a moment, and every move in the Premier League is watched by the media; the playbook failed under such high exposure.

— Shi Hualun
57:10

American capital sits at every gate

American penetration of European football goes beyond clubs. In 2012-2013, US-based CAA took over UEFA national-team competition commercial development; in 2024, Relevent Sports wrested the commercial rights to three major tournaments from Team Marketing, effective from 2025. Broadcast platforms are also often backed by American money, such as DAZN. Even instalment payments in player transfers have been turned into factoring products, with banks offering receivables at a discount for cash; such financial tools come mainly from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs. Zhang Bin sums it up: every time money moves through European football, Americans have set up a gate to skim a fee.

— Zhang Bin
1:05:46

After the rights peak, it's a valuation game

Premier League domestic broadcast revenue, adjusted for inflation, is down 31% from the 2016-19 cycle, while player wages and transfer fees keep rising, squeezing costs. From a revenue and margin perspective, football clubs are not a good business; American capital relies more on the valuation game: push asset values up, then exit through consortia, selling 1% stakes, or evergreen funds that roll over indefinitely. In theory, as long as there is a next buyer, valuations can keep inflating. Zhang Bin warns this is essentially like speculating on concept stocks in the A-share market and distributing at the top — you need a continuous stream of willing buyers.

— Zhang Bin

Glossary

Leveraged Buyout
Acquiring a target company with borrowed money, with the target's cash flow or assets used to repay the debt.
Asset Securitization
Packaging future cash flows (such as ticket revenue) into bonds sold to investors, raising funds in one go.
Evergreen Fund
A fund with perpetual life, allowing investors to enter and exit, suited to long-term holding of sports assets.
Super League
A proposed top-tier league with no relegation and fixed members, said to be driven by American financial forces.
Matchday Revenue
Revenue from tickets and spending around the stadium on match days.
