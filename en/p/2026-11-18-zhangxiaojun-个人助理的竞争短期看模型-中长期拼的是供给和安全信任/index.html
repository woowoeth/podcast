Open-source projects proved models crossed the threshold for long-horizon task execution

Cai Wei notes that over this past Lunar New Year, an open-source agent project (called Open Cloud in the transcript) went viral. Though ordinary users spend two or three hours installing and configuring it—the experience is highly technical—it proved that models had crossed a threshold for long-horizon task execution: properly configured personas and skills can then orchestrate and execute numerous tasks automatically. This signal prompted nearly every major player to start personal agent projects after the new year. A hidden secondary theme is security, privacy and compliance. Muse also invested considerable time here, because for personal agents to be truly useful, users must willingly give up context to them—trust is a prerequisite, not a bonus feature.

— Cai Wei
37:31

Muse notifications feel like WeChat, not marketing spam

Cai Wei sees several personal agent products with similar feature lists but divergent experiences: the gap is primarily in what he calls 'boundary sense.' He observes that every Muse notification is carefully curated with sufficient information gain, creating a feedback loop where users think 'this message is worth opening'; but if you send morning and evening digests daily with low information density, users next time treat them as marketing messages and ignore them. This explains why personal agent products generally have higher retention—push has stopped being an engagement tool and become genuine feedback from the real world.

— Cai Wei
44:37

Three generations of AI products map to service industry worth 10 trillion yuan

Cai Wei maps the three generations of AI products unlocked by large models directly to market scale: Chatbots solve information retrieval, corresponding to the information sector at around 100 billion yuan; coding agents like Claude Code that complete tasks upend the software industry at the trillion-yuan scale; Personal Agents point to service industry restructuring—nutritionists, weight loss coaches, tutors and similar services now delivered by humans stand at 10 trillion yuan. He emphasizes the logic: estimate based on what currently solves this problem and how big that market is—the level of problems models solve maps directly to market scale.

— Cai Wei
49:38

Personal agents reshape supply, they don't just shift where scenarios happen

Cai Wei distinguishes two classes of innovation: mobile internet's transformation was fundamentally demand-side—smartphones unlocked location, letting users do what they couldn't before. Personal agents from this wave of large models are fundamentally supply-side restructuring: models can write answers on the fly, can recombine services scattered across different places into entirely new experiences. This distinction matters because a decade of O2O was actually laying the foundation for today's personal agents, letting them directly access offline supply that's now been moved online.

— Cai Wei
1:19:55

KPIs as targets inevitably distort execution

Cai Wei's product management philosophy: compress a direction into a specific metric—5 million DAU, 40% retention—and you necessarily lose massive amounts of high-dimensional information; teams instinctively pick the path easiest to hit the metric, but that's usually not the right path. He prefers explaining the product's principle and value clearly, letting the team understand the 'highest-level core,' so when external conditions shift the team can adjust without worrying whether work done against an old goal counts—especially in large companies where year-end performance reviews happen and constant KPI changes confuse everyone.

— Cai Wei
1:57:19

One error in ten steps means the whole sequence fails

Cai Wei explains with a mathematical intuition why personal agent experience remains far from ready: even if you improve single-step accuracy from 70% to 90%, a task split into ten steps means any failure breaks the whole chain; final success rate is one minus the product of all error rates. Even if each step has only a 3% error rate, multiply that over ten steps and overall success drops to 60-70%. This is why there's a canyon between 'can call tools' and 'calls them well and satisfies users'—leaderboard models only care about the former.

— Cai Wei
2:14:35

Intelligence will plateau; security and supply are the durable moat

Cai Wei breaks down personal agent competitive barriers by time horizon: in the short term, intelligence matters because model capability is still rapidly differentiating; but he judges intelligence will eventually plateau like the smartphone industry after iPhone 4—base models will all be much of a muchness, and what pulls ahead will be supply and trustworthy security. He sees this not as an indefinite hypothesis but as objective industry law, though we can't yet see when it will happen in the near term.

— Cai Wei
2:28:41

Personal agents compete on productivity replacement, not on traffic tolls

Cai Wei identifies two business models for personal agents: productivity logic, measuring whether the AI assistant's value creation exceeds its cost—if it creates 100 yuan monthly value and costs only 10 yuan, users have no reason not to use it, closer to hiring hourly labor; and internet-style traffic platform/toll logic, dependent on centralized traffic aggregation. He judges that if the personal agent ecosystem fragments, the first logic is likelier to hold because it doesn't depend on winner-take-all platform effects, just on individual unit value being high enough.

— Cai Wei
