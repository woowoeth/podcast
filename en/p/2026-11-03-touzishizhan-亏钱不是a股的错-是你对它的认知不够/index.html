Your A-share losses come from understanding that can't keep pace with change

After reviewing his 60+-percent drawdown this year, Zhang Jun discovered that the root cause of A-shares' short bull runs and long bear markets—and investors' short time horizons—was never the market itself, but investors' understanding of intrinsic value and macroeconomic cycles failing to keep pace with change's speed.

The index barely moved, but whole sectors were sliced in half

Zhang Jun highlights a counterintuitive fact: Shanghai Composite's full-year range was under 20 percent, from January highs above 4,200 to lows above 3,600. Yet beneath that, sub-indices swung wildly. ChiNext and STAR Board each fell roughly 40 percent from June peaks; the optical module index dropped over 50 percent from its high. His point: concentrated sector bets faced far steeper drawdowns than the aggregate index suggests.

5:09

Even dividend low-volatility funds got turned into high-volatility assets

Zhang Jun adds that supposedly defensive dividend plays didn't escape the carnage: the baijiu index fell 30-odd percent on its worst days; the dividend low-volatility index—promised stability—was down 10 points in its worst month. More tragicomically, in June when the market rallied and tech soared, this index fell 10 points in a single month, then gained them back in July when the market fell—behaving the exact opposite of its name.

— Zhang Jun
12:12

Every industry has its own Xu Jiayin

Zhang Jun argues Chinese firms cannot sustain stable EPS growth because of abundant ‘entrepreneurial ambition’: CEOs want to expand scale, CFOs and key technologists push expansion and capex to advance their careers, and local governments' investment drives force companies to boost capital spending. Everyone knows Xu Jiayin, he notes, but every industry has its own version—the property sector's just leveraged hard enough to crater publicly.

— Zhang Jun
20:14

Graham never said to discount future cash flows

Zhang Jun revisits years of misreading Graham: value investors picture DCF models calculating intrinsic value, but that was Williams' idea, not Graham's. In “Security Analysis”, Graham calculated working capital and net asset discounts—the goal was finding a margin of safety, not pinning an exact intrinsic value number. He restates Graham's actual logic in three sentences: stocks are stakes in businesses; Mr. Market is a quotation device to exploit; and margins of safety matter.

— Zhang Jun
22:15

Intrinsic value isn't a point—it's a moving range

He cites a cutting-tool stock he sold too early this year: bought at 2 billion+ market cap aiming for 10 billion in three years, but AI-driven M&A turbocharged earnings to target, so he sold per plan—only later discovering the company's intrinsic value was still climbing fast and he'd treated a dynamic company as static. Takeaway: intrinsic value is never a fixed point but a range that shifts up and down with fundamentals; exit timing should track that range's velocity.

— Zhang Jun
31:22

Storage chip prices spike, but intrinsic value may not follow

When asked whether ‘rising demand equals rising intrinsic value,’ Zhang Jun uses storage chips to refute the simple narrative: Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron learned overexpansion's sting, intentionally restrained supply to barely maintain a three-player oligopoly—then AI sent prices skyward, but now they're expanding again while YMTC enters. His conclusion: once a carefully guarded competitive structure cracks, surface price rallies and demand figures don't simply equal long-term intrinsic-value gains.

— Zhang Jun
35:25

Whether to add on dips—the question itself is the answer

Facing scenarios where stock price falls below your entry but intrinsic value falls faster (so margin of safety looks bigger), Zhang Jun offers this heuristic: if you're asking friends whether to add to a losing position, that act alone proves you don't understand it well enough—sell immediately. As long as intrinsic value is still sliding and you can't find new evidence of a bottom, buying purely because the price is low is short-term gambling and probably catching a knife mid-fall.

— Zhang Jun
50:42

Value investors with sweeping sector coverage—I'm skeptical

Zhang Jun offers an ‘immature observation’: no one can be expert in packaged food, consumer electronics, and AI simultaneously. If someone calling themselves a value investor covers too many industries, he doubts their authenticity. He reviews his own past expansion into chemicals, pharma, information security, and solar—convinced at the time he was applying value-investing method, but in hindsight nearly every call was wrong. Example: pharma volume-based pricing policy disruptions happened long before he grasped that industry logic had shifted.

— Zhang Jun
1:12:51

Effort breeds error; comfort invites crisis

Zhang Jun distills two mental models from this year's repeated mistakes: ‘effort breeds error’—the moment you deliberately strain to forecast or trade, bias enters; ‘comfort invites crisis’—the moment you think ‘good enough, time to rest,’ danger usually arrives. He cites late February as proof: market rallied to his year-start target and he was unwinding when an unexpected headline triggered another crash, proving the pattern once more.

— Zhang Jun
