BIS rules block chip smuggling loopholes, but US domestic compute rental remains wide open

New rules block Southeast Asian smuggling, restrict Chinese companies' AI compute rental, and restart TSMC chip fabrication reviews—but do nothing about U.S. domestic compute rental, allowing Chinese capital to flow toward Virginia data centers instead.

Three categories of measures make up the entirety of these new rules

These rules comprise three separate measures: combating Chinese AI chip smuggling, restricting Chinese companies' compute rental, and closing the loophole that lets Chinese companies use TSMC and Samsung as proxies to fabricate AI chips. Chris considers this the first time in 18 months he's seen the U.S. government actually treat export controls seriously. The rules themselves aren't perfect, though, and will require follow-up patches.

— Chris
2:06

Southeast Asia has replaced China as the chip smuggling hub

Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia are now the third, fourth, fifth, and eighth largest AI chip importers relative to their GDP—far exceeding their economic scale. Brazil is included because of ByteDance data center construction. The rules require companies in these countries to get U.S. approval before large chip purchases. But Chris warns: unless standards are globally uniform, smuggling simply relocates to Bhutan, Nepal, and elsewhere.

— Chris
4:10

Chinese companies' compute rental is capped at roughly 800 H100 chips

For the first time, the rules explicitly cap compute rental to Chinese companies without permission: approximately 200 of the most advanced chips or approximately 800 H100 chips. Training or running cutting-edge models typically requires thousands to hundreds of thousands of chips, so this dramatically shrinks available compute for China. The rule has retroactive effect—if more than half a company's compute was already flowing to Chinese companies, that counts as a violation, meaning Megaspeed-type companies could theoretically face liability.

— Chris
5:10

The U.S. domestic compute rental loophole remains unfixed

The largest gap in these rules is that they do not apply to U.S. domestic compute rental. Chris warns that without fixing this, Chinese companies will simply shift their compute demand from Malaysian Johor to Virginia data centers. Virginia could become "the center of Chinese AI." He believes Commerce already has the authority to close this loophole but must act immediately.

— Chris
9:24

Trump's attitude toward his own new rules before China visit is concerning

Julian notes that earlier that day Trump discussed disagreement between Lutnick and Bessent on China policy without seeming aware his administration had just released this major new rule. With Trump heading to China to negotiate with counterparts who want these rules eliminated, there is concern about whether the rules will actually be enforced or softened through negotiation.

— Julian
18:52

Xi's knowledge of Chinese AI's NVIDIA chip dependency is the critical variable

Julian raises a critical unknown: does Xi himself understand how dependent Chinese AI companies are on foreign chips? This week's revelations about Megaspeed and NVIDIA may be the first time senior Chinese leadership learned how serious the situation is. If Xi grasps the stakes, China might escalate; if not, the response will be slower.

— Julian
26:19

Jensen Huang's soup-vendor story reveals why he insists on selling to China

Jensen Huang once told cloud computing CEOs a story about queuing for soup in Japan: the soup vendor prefers to keep prices high and customers diversified rather than sell all inventory to one or two large buyers. Chris sees this as revealing NVIDIA's true logic: worry that if most customers were a few major U.S. cloud companies, any shift to AMD or custom chips would devastate revenue. Only the largest U.S. cloud companies, China, and the Middle East generate that level of purchasing demand.

— Chris
29:22

This story will be remembered for the smuggling problem, not the scandal

Chris predicts that as shareholder lawsuits, Congressional investigations, and actual evidence emerge, coming weeks will show how indifferent NVIDIA was to smuggling concerns. Commerce knew but did not act for months. This corporate and governmental negligence is what will ultimately be remembered from this episode, not the scandal or espionage drama.

— Chris
