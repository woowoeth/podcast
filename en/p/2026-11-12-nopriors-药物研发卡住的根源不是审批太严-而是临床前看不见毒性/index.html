Drug development's constraint isn't strict approval but invisible preclinical toxicity

Over decades, scientific papers increased tenfold to twentyfold and new drug modalities—antibodies, small molecules, proteins, peptides—proliferated, yet annual approvals remained flat. Development costs ballooned from millions to $1.5 billion, timelines stretched from 8–9 years to 15, and every metric worsened. Max attributes this to an industry still trapped in experience-driven trial-and-error: hypothesize a molecule, then iterate in the real world. But molecular and biological design spaces are high-dimensional and combinatorially explosive; empirical search only captures low-hanging fruit.

— Max Jaderberg
14:36

The goal is experimental-grade accuracy, not faster experiments

Isomorphic's north star isn't running wet experiments faster but moving drug design entirely to the computer. Max uses AlphaFold as the exemplar: structure prediction once required months or years of X-ray crystallography; if the model reaches experimental accuracy, real-world validation becomes unnecessary. Internal leaderboards mark an ‘experimental accuracy’ baseline; once crossed, wet experiments shift from ‘learning and iteration’ to ‘verification’, drastically reducing cycles in the physical world.

— Max Jaderberg
17:42

Clinical trials are where drug failure and costs concentrate

Unpacking the 15-year, $1.5 billion timeline: small-molecule discovery and in-vitro work occupy only 2–3 years and ~$50 million; the rest is clinical. Most failure modes—unexpected toxicity, off-target effects, or mechanisms that hit the target yet harm the body—only emerge when the drug reaches patients; animal models and in-vitro tests miss them entirely. Real gains in failure prevention come from understanding these failure modes before patient exposure.

— Max Jaderberg
18:46

Generate proprietary data instead of relying on public databases

Public and industry-wide data are mostly passive byproducts of historical collection, biased, and unfit for training general models demanding generalization. Isomorphic generates its own required data distributions instead of relying on existing large databases. Max noted this approach revealed ‘surprisingly impressive data scaling laws’ in model training.

— Max Jaderberg
20:53

Rat toxicity testing predicts human outcomes with only about 30% accuracy

Regulators mandate animal proof of safety before human use, yet toxicity frequently emerges once drugs enter patients. Max argues directly: rats aren't humans; this ‘gold standard’ predicts human clinical toxicity with about 30% accuracy—and he half-jokes that if his team's AI hit 30%, he'd ask engineers to ‘do better’. He advocates combining in-vitro experiments, AI models, and new tissue-mimicking techniques—organoids and organs-on-chip—to model human clinical toxicity directly, bypassing the five-to-six-decade-old, unreliable animal proxy.

— Max Jaderberg
29:21

Combination therapy fails at safety engineering, not biology

HIV and cancer treatments use cocktail approaches—tumors contain mutation spectra; single drugs prompt resistance; hitting multiple targets simultaneously prevents escape. But real-world combination therapy is rare. Max explains: existing molecules are often intrinsically toxic or damage the liver; multiple drugs generate harmful interactions. The constraint isn't biological understanding but molecular design precision—molecules lack the refinement and safety. Better-engineered, cleaner molecules unlock multi-target therapy against complex pathways.

— Max Jaderberg
32:17

Acceptable risk varies by disease; safety standards shouldn't be one-size-fits-all

When asked whether safety-focused regulation slows drug development, Max grants patient safety must remain central but notes risk tolerance is dynamic: COVID's pervasiveness and severity shifted social acceptance of expedited review; late-stage cancer with no alternatives and chronic low-risk disease demand vastly different risk tolerance. True progress means faster access to better treatments, not uniform safety thresholds across all disease contexts.

— Max Jaderberg
35:42

Extending lifespan isn't enough—drug development must also improve later-life quality

Beating cancer and heart disease still leaves aging itself—accumulated chronic problems: bone and muscle loss, post-fall immobility, compromised wellbeing and quality of life. Decades past 80 aren't inherently well-lived. Max emphasizes the industry must work both directions: treat discrete fatal diseases to extend lifespan, and tackle aging's root mechanisms to extend healthspan.

— Max Jaderberg
