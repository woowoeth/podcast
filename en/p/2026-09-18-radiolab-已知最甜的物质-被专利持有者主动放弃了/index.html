The sweetest known substance, abandoned by its patent holder

Lugduname is 220,000 to 300,000 times sweeter than sucrose. It was synthesised and patented in Lyon, France in 1987, lapsed in 1994 when the maintenance fees stopped being paid, and since then it can neither be bought nor has anyone commercialised it.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:01

The sweetest molecule was abandoned by its patent holder

Lugduname was discovered and patented in 1987 by Claude Bernard University in Lyon, France, and the patent lapsed in 1994 when the maintenance fees stopped being paid. It has not been commercialised, and no one has tried to commercialise it; it is not even sold as a research chemical, and it cannot be bought anywhere. That means there is no safety data for it in humans. Related molecules in the family were tested in pigs and appeared safe. Researchers had to taste it themselves to determine its sweetness, and only a tiny amount is needed to sweeten something, so even ingesting a toxic dose is difficult.

1:02

The sweetness multiple is not "how many times sweeter it tastes"

Potency is measured against sucrose, whose sweetness is fixed at 1. The method is to dissolve a known amount of the sweetener in water and keep diluting until its sweetness matches that of a sugar solution of known concentration. If 0.1 grams of sweetener per litre of water is needed to match the sweetness of 50 grams of sucrose per litre, the sweetener's potency is 500. Lugduname's potency is listed as 220K to 300K, because different patents report different values, and because it is based on human taste it necessarily carries error and may vary from person to person — both taste and smell have genetic components that affect the experience.

7:09

Skip the neutralisation step and the yield drops from 41% to 9%

The first step treats 150 grams of ortho-vanillin with 600 millilitres of glacial acetic acid and 180 millilitres of 48% hydrobromic acid, refluxing for at least 9 hours. It is an acid cleavage of a methyl ether — the hardest class of ether to cleave, so the conditions have to be harsh, and the price is yield. After the reaction the mixture is black, because a lot of tar has formed. The key is that before the next step the residual acid must be neutralised with sodium bicarbonate: when the author ran this reaction earlier without neutralising, the yield was only 9%; this time, after neutralising, he got 56.8 grams, a 41% yield, comparable to the literature.

17:13

The hydrogenation is too slow, a weak point of this route

The oxime is reduced to the amine by catalytic hydrogenation over 10% palladium on carbon, stirred at 30 degrees Celsius until hydrogen is no longer consumed. Partway through, the hydrogen in the balloon is sucked empty and a new one has to be attached. The result is 10 grams of the amine hydrochloride, a 48% yield from the oxime, which the author calls "a bit sad", and the product is certainly still wet. He judges the hydrogenation slow enough that "it would be better to use a different reduction, or run it under pressure". The product, though, is completely white, whereas the literature describes a dirty brown. NMR shows a lot of water, and otherwise matches the literature.

18:14

Of three routes, only the third runs properly

4-isothiocyanatobenzonitrile can be made in one step from commercially available 4-aminobenzonitrile. The author tried three methods: the first was terrible, the second merely bad, and neither is recommended; only the third worked properly. The third uses a biphasic chloroform/water solvent system with sodium bicarbonate as base, adding thiophosgene last — the sulfur analogue of phosgene, a liquid, far less toxic, but still drawn from a septum-capped bottle with a syringe. The reaction turns yellow at once, then orange, with vigorous stirring for 1 hour. The author says the reaction is much better in a basic solvent system but cannot find the exact reason; the attempt with DCM as solvent and triethylamine as base was a total failure.

24:19

TLC fooled him: the thiourea had not disappeared, it had become a urea

The thiourea and methylenedioxybenzylamine hydrochloride are combined in dry DMF, with triethylamine to free the amine and act as base, and mercury chloride added as desulfurising agent in an ice bath at zero degrees; the mixture quickly turns milky white, then black after an hour as mercury sulfide precipitates. The literature says to wait until TLC shows the reaction is complete, but he finds it never finishes even with extra mercury chloride and solvent. His explanation: water present in the system converts the thiourea into the corresponding urea, and the urea has almost the same polarity as the thiourea, so on TLC it looks as if the thiourea has not disappeared when in fact that spot is the urea. He suggests a stain specific for thiocarbonyl, such as 1% silver nitrate in methanol, which lets you judge whether the thiourea is consumed without even running a plate.

30:20

The final deprotection gives a hygroscopic salt

The material is dissolved in 40 millilitres of acetic acid and 20 millilitres of 1,4-dioxane, a septum is attached and hydrogen chloride gas is bubbled in to remove the tert-butyl ester. In the literature the hydrogen chloride is added as a 1,4-dioxane solution; he did not have that on hand and considered bubbling the gas to give the same result. This step turns the ester into the free acid, which is what yields the hydrochloride of the active compound Lugduname — without that acid functional group it has no taste. The author's experience is that its salt form is very hygroscopic, and it also does not much care about losing the hydrogen chloride. After an hour the reaction is complete and the mixture has turned green.

32:21

On the tongue it starts sour, then gets sweeter and sweeter, and lingers a long time

The author took a very small amount straight from the pipette tip onto his tongue. The first hit was a strange sourness — he guesses that was the hydrogen chloride — and then the sweetness slowly built and grew sweeter. It stays in the mouth for a very long time; even a trace gives sweetness, and hours later he still randomly encounters a molecule on his lips. In sufficient quantity it also seems to activate receptors deep in his throat that he did not know existed. He mentions that every attempt made his stomach acid increase and even gave him reflux, and notes that as far as he knows the brain's response to sweetness in the mouth is to increase gastric acid secretion in anticipation of food arriving.

Glossary

potency
Relative sweetness measured against sucrose as 1, determined by the ratio of concentrations needed for equal sweetness.
thiophosgene
The sulfur analogue of phosgene; a liquid, far less toxic than phosgene, used to make isothiocyanates.
oxime
The compound formed when an aldehyde or ketone reacts with hydroxylamine, which can be further reduced to an amine.
carbodiimide
The strongly electrophilic intermediate formed by desulfurising a thiourea, which an amine attacks to give a guanidine.
guanidine
A strongly basic group containing three nitrogens; the sweet-tasting core structure of Lugduname.
