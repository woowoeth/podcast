Harvey's headcount surged sixfold in a single year—trust, not process, sustains this pace

In one year, Harvey's valuation leapt from $30 billion to $110 billion, and headcount from 300+ to nearly 2,000. Yet the VP of Talent credits this pace not to new process, but to founders' trust and employee autonomy that have never been constrained.

Harvey's headcount surged sixfold in a single year

Maggie joined just one year ago when employee numbers were in the 300s, yet Harvey has added over 1,000 new hires and expects to approach 2,000 by year-end. The critical insight: over 70% of the current staff joined after January 1 this year, meaning many recruiting managers, recruiters, and new hires themselves are new to the company. They're figuring out as they work what actually works in Harvey's culture, rather than importing playbooks from previous employers.

— Maggie Landers
7:20

Harvey ships in a month what most companies take a quarter to deliver

Maggie emphasizes that Harvey's pace functions as a filtering mechanism. Even people who felt their prior company moved fast discover Harvey operates on a completely different scale. Most new hires were successful in their previous fields and accustomed to getting things right, but Harvey requires letting go of ‘must be first’ thinking and accepting a higher error rate. The focus shifts to rapid failure and rapid correction rather than getting it perfect the first time.

— Maggie Landers
12:32

Founders eliminate approval chains and stay directly engaged in operations

Maggie describes what makes founders Winston and Gabe distinctive: they combine strategic foresight with deep involvement in daily execution details. Harvey isn't the kind of company where you submit a deck, wait eight weeks, and eventually see executives—staff can reach Winston or Gabe directly on Slack and get near-instant responses. This accessibility cascaded through the entire leadership team, including later hires like COO and CISO, shaping how they approach daily work.

— Maggie Landers
19:10

Harvey's adolescent stage—between startup chaos and corporate maturity—filters candidates

Maggie characterizes Harvey as operating in ‘adolescence’—neither the pre-PMF startup with fuzzy role boundaries nor a mature corporation running like clockwork. This in-between state naturally filters people: some want more structure and stability, others prefer earlier-stage chaos. The recruiting team's job is to make the interview experience reflect the actual working state as closely as possible, letting candidates judge for themselves whether they want to stay.

— Maggie Landers
29:42

Three core values sustain speed without waiting for consensus

Every candidate interviews around three core values: Simplicity, Decisiveness, and ‘Jobs not finished.’ Decisiveness means better to err than over-analyze—decisions aren't made by waiting for 300 people to agree. Simplicity means not over-building; ship first and gather feedback. ‘Jobs not finished’ demands humility, acknowledging that current approaches could always be better. Together, these enable people who are accustomed to perfectionism to release that grip.

— Maggie Landers
34:57

As scale increased, founders chose to preserve trust over control

Winston said in an internal founder Q&A that what he most wanted to preserve during scaling was trust. Maggie believes this trust in staff translates directly to autonomy for front-line employees: no multi-level approvals, no excessive oversight. That's what allows Harvey to continue hiring a thousand people, making mistakes, and experimenting at its current pace. Even with only four years of history, this trust has never been tightened.

— Maggie Landers
41:30

Legal expertise outweighs pure AI backgrounds in hiring

Maggie reminds candidates that transforming legal is completely different from transforming customer service, building AI harnesses, or building models—even though Harvey now builds its own models. The company has many current or former practicing lawyers scattered across product (applied legal researcher), innovation partnerships (legal innovation partner), and sales (legal engineer). Interest in and respect for the legal industry itself is what distinguishes Harvey from companies like Anthropic, OpenAI, 11 Labs, and Sierra. Candidates need to think this through before deciding to join.

— Maggie Landers
44:46

Judgment is the hardest quality to assess, yet determines who succeeds

In an environment of high autonomy and rapid decisions, the most critical but hardest-to-evaluate quality is judgment: knowing when to loop in the right person before you hit send, rather than gathering 20 people for a meeting or going completely solo. Maggie describes it as a voice in the back of your head. It's precisely this judgment that lets employees with substantial autonomy maintain the company's trust in them.

— Maggie Landers
