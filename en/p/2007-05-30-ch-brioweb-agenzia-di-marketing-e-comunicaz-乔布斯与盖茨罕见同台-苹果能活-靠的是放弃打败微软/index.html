Jobs and Gates together onstage: Apple survived only by abandoning its fight with Microsoft

Jobs said Apple's 1997 turning point wasn't a technical breakthrough but accepting that "Apple doesn't need to beat Microsoft"—ending the zero-sum competition is what saved the company.

AppleMicrosoftcorporate historySteve JobsBill Gatesproduct philosophy
Rarely on stage together, they review Mac's origins, Apple's near-death experience, and reconciliation details. High information density with almost no filler—worth hearing in full.

4:14

Partnership began from patent disputes, not from strategic vision

The video's opening explains that the two companies' collaboration talks began from a patent dispute, not any strategic vision. Jobs emphasizes his pride in the two companies resolving their differences "in an extremely, extremely professional manner," then invites Gates onstage to announce they will continue building Mac Office and Internet Explorer. Posterity often romanticizes this as a "love hug," but it actually began as the resolution of a legal dispute.

— Steve Jobs
13:52

Apple paid Microsoft $31,000 for the floating-point Basic that powered Apple II

Wozniak hand-coded an integer version of Basic, perfect but without floating-point capability, and flatly refused to add it. Gates recalls the team begging Wozniak daily—"please, please make this floating point"—but Wozniak wouldn't budge. Apple eventually had to buy floating-point Basic from Microsoft for $31,000, and Gates himself flew to Apple and spent two days installing it using a tape drive. That $31,000 purchase became one of the core pieces of software shipped with Apple II.

— Bill Gates
18:59

Microsoft broke into applications software by gambling on the Mac

Gates points out an overlooked fact: Microsoft wasn't in the applications business at all back then. Lotus 123 dominated the PC applications market, while WordPerfect and dBase controlled word processing and databases respectively. Microsoft broke in by betting that the graphical interface would be the next paradigm shift, and that led them to applications. Put another way: without the Mac bet, Microsoft might never have entered the Office space.

— Bill Gates
23:04

Jobs ended Apple's zero-sum fight with Microsoft and saved the company

Jobs is blunt: Apple was trapped in zero-sum thinking—"for Apple to win, Microsoft must lose"—but that logic only ensured Apple's defeat, because Apple couldn't outfight Microsoft. What Apple needed wasn't to beat Microsoft, but to "remember who it is," because it had forgotten. Breaking that paradigm was, Jobs believed, the essential step to reviving Apple—more fundamental than any product decision.

— Steve Jobs
28:16

Apple sees itself as a software company, not a hardware maker

Jobs explains that iPod beat Japanese consumer electronics giants because those companies couldn't write the right software. iPod is essentially software: the software on the device, the software on your PC or Mac, the software in the cloud store. This leads him to a counterintuitive claim: Apple "sees itself as a software company," and "there aren't many software companies left," Microsoft being one of them. This positioning explains why Apple insists on integrated hardware and software, rather than embracing diversity like Microsoft and letting partners handle hardware for phones, robots, and other markets.

— Steve Jobs
39:55

Apple's custom Google Maps client outperformed Google's own official version

When building iPhone, Apple asked Google for the Maps API and built its own client, rather than using Google's own Java client. Jobs points out that with the same data and the same backend services, the experience was dramatically different—"just blows it away." He uses this to illustrate a broader principle: cloud services plus a sufficiently refined local client beats a purely browser-based approach by far. It's his core evidence against the claim that everything will move to the cloud.

— Steve Jobs
1:04:05

Post-PC devices are where true revolution in interfaces will happen

Asked about how the PC's mouse, icons, and dropdown menus have barely changed in twenty years, Jobs concedes that "truly revolutionary change" will happen on "post-PC devices," because they start as a blank slate without the burden of "zillions of apps" to maintain compatibility with. He offers an analogy: users are accustomed to four wheels and a steering wheel; they don't want six wheels or a joystick. So on PCs, the best you can do is "augment" rather than replace. True reinvention has to wait for devices like phones.

— Steve Jobs
1:22:49

Without deep passion for the work, any rational founder will quit

Asked for advice to founders, Gates emphasizes team and talent recruitment, while Jobs puts passion first: the work is too hard that "if you don't love it, any rational person would give up." Looking back at successful people versus those who failed, the difference usually comes down to this: successful founders love it enough to endure through the hardest stretch, while the others, being rational—"because they're sane"—choose to quit.

— Steve Jobs
