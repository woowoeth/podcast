Commerce kept approving $4.8 billion in chips despite knowing Nvidia's partner was spy-run

Commerce Department possessed intelligence that Megaspeed was operated by Chinese spies, yet chose not to enforce existing regulations that could have blocked its chip supply.

2:22

Real deregulation is choosing not to enforce existing rules

Huang lobbied the government repeatedly to relax China export controls, but what actually changed wasn't which chip model got approved. It was the Trump administration's May 2025 decision to stop enforcing a Biden-era rule that could have blocked large AI chip orders to ‘untrusted’ companies including Megaspeed. The government promised replacement rules; it never delivered. Instead it left a patchwork of what's restricted and what's not—gaps China has exploited massively through Southeast Asia.

— Chris McGuire
5:26

Pillow talk matters not for scandal but for chip allocation control

The real danger isn't the affair itself but what Huang holds: direct private communication with the president on AI and chip export policy; detailed knowledge of Nvidia chip design; and crucially, Nvidia knows global AI chip distribution better than the US government does. Huang personally decides who gets chips when supply is far below demand. Whoever gets close to him gets compute.

— Chris McGuire
11:39

Commerce kept spy-run company buying $4.8 billion in chips for years

What's hardest to understand: Commerce had intelligence that Megaspeed was operated by Chinese spies and was trying to penetrate Nvidia's leadership. For one to two years, it took no action to stop $4.8 billion in chip purchases. The guest said plainly: ‘I can't even think of a plausible explanation.’ He also noted that Cantor Fitzgerald, linked to Lutnik, has its third-largest position exposure in Nvidia stock—behind only S&P 500 and Amazon, ahead of AMD and Marvell. That raises questions about motive.

12:39

Southeast Asia became the hidden pipeline for chips flowing to China

This year, Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand are the world's second-, fourth-, and fifth-largest AI chip importers. Combined, they imported $80 billion in AI chips this year—not all going to China, but the guest believes a large share does. This is the direct result of loosened export enforcement. It's been described as the largest transfer of controlled technology to an adversary nation in US history. Commerce Deputy Secretary Kessler almost never publicly explained the policy logic.

— Chris McGuire
19:57

Huang never grasped he stopped selling gaming chips decades ago

The root of all this, the guest argues, is that Huang was a gaming chip company CEO for three decades. His product suddenly became strategically critical technology. But his argument has always been contradictory: he says these chips are so important that every industry and company needs them, that they'll transform society. And at the same time he insists they won't be used militarily, won't affect global power balance. Carelessness like engaging with a Chinese customer with intelligence ties suggests he never truly internalized how important his chips actually are.

— Jordan Schneider
21:59

Tech industry hasn't learned to treat compliance like banking regulators demand

Banking went through this shift early, Chris notes. It took sanctions compliance seriously, built internal controls, understood its place in national strategy. Tech wants it both ways: enjoy the strategic status of your product while refusing the compliance obligations that come with it. He cites the Justice Department's charges that the Saudi government tried to penetrate OpenAI—unsurprising, because any private company holding this kind of strategic technology becomes a target for foreign intelligence services.

— Chris McGuire
25:05

Congressional bills can't close every loophole in chip export controls

The Chip Security Act, Match Act, Overwatch and related bipartisan bills will likely pass during the lame duck, either standalone or via the NDAA. They can ban Blackwell chips flowing to China and cut off DUV equipment sales to China. But they can't systematically solve transshipment fraud or remote-access compute arbitrage. What's really needed is Commerce tightening its own rules—for example, banning Chinese companies from buying chips in the United States. The irony: most of the chips Megaspeed obtained came legally through a US subsidiary of a company on the Entity List.

— Chris McGuire
27:11

Deputy shared investigation of spy company with its largest business partner

The New York Times reported that Commerce Deputy Secretary Kessler discussed Commerce's ongoing investigation into Megaspeed directly with Nvidia officials. That's equivalent to the FBI investigating a company while briefing that company's largest customer's CEO. The Justice Department then took the investigation away from Commerce, citing Commerce's communication with ‘competitors of the investigated entity.’ The guest was blunt: ‘This is completely inconsistent with how we handle policy or enforcement matters.’ It crossed too many lines.

— Chris McGuire
