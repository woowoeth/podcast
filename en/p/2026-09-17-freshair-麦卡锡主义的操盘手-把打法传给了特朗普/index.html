The McCarthy Era's Operative Handed His Playbook to Trump

Roy Cohn was not an aberration of the McCarthy era but a product of the paranoia that seizes American democracy every few decades — and the playbook did not die with him. It carried through to today via Trump.

Cohn's three rules became Trump's rules

Bird says Cohn's rules became Trump's rules: never admit error, deny and deny again, and when accused, stall and stall again. That is exactly what Cohn did when he was indicted three times in the 1960s — delay the trial as long as possible, deny the charges, and name and attack the judge. He was a habitual rule-breaker his whole life; rules were for little people, not for him. Trump later copied the playbook wholesale: stiffing people on bills, using delay tactics to cut what he owed to ten cents on the dollar, and paying very little tax through various avoidance schemes.

— Kai Bird
3:09

He beat the charges on luck as much as tactics

Bird and his wife and co-author Susan Goldmark argue in the book that Cohn was partly just lucky. In one trial, a juror's father suddenly died and the judge was forced to declare a mistrial; in another, Cohn's own lawyer suffered a heart attack in court, collapsing just as he was about to give the summation. Cohn said on the spot, "Judge, I'll do the summation myself," and got two days, seven hours total, to defend himself — and because he had not taken the witness stand, he could not be cross-examined. The jury acquitted him 12 to 0, and some jurors were said to have been moved to tears by him.

— Kai Bird
5:22

Don't tell me the law, tell me the judge

Another of Cohn's rules was "don't tell me the law, tell me the judge" — he knew which judge favored him and which judge could be manipulated. His father, Albert Cohn, was a Bronx judge appointed by the Roosevelt administration, and from childhood he was introduced by his father to lawyers and judges; after becoming a lawyer at 20, he cultivated judges as friends, very transactionally: do someone a favor first, then knock on the door for the return. Trump went further on this rule — as president he appointed judges and Supreme Court justices, some of whom later ruled on his own cases. Bird says this is Cohn's most insidious and longest-lasting legacy: he was a lawyer, but he did not care about the law at all. He practiced law by telephone, avoided leaving any notes or records, and taught this to Trump.

— Kai Bird
8:26

In four phone calls, Trump kept praising Cohn

Bird conducted four phone interviews with Trump, each about 10 minutes, and they were very strange. The first was a cold call, and Trump picked up and said "Hello?" with no idea who Bird was. Bird said he was writing a Cohn biography, and Trump said, "Oh, great, great, he was a wonderful man, a good lawyer, we won a lot of cases together," gushing about Cohn, genuinely missing him. Bird believes they really were friends. Trump is utterly unashamed of his association with Cohn, which Bird finds baffling — the book's sordid episodes are backed by 1,300 footnotes, so how could anyone read it and not feel shame.

— Kai Bird
12:43

Cohn was McCarthy's brain

Bird says McCarthy was politically an empty shell, not a true ideological believer, who stumbled into this anti-communist witch hunt by accident. But Cohn joined with zeal and professional skill and became McCarthy's brain — McCarthy was by then a functional alcoholic, and Cohn was effectively steering him. They successfully launched a political offensive against the newly installed Eisenhower administration: Cohn was hired in early 1953 and began attacking Eisenhower himself, a Republican president, on the grounds that he was not Republican enough, not anti-communist enough. Cohn was brutal and unrelenting in interrogating witnesses, publicly asking male government employees about their sexual orientation and whether they had had relations with men, destroying the lives of hundreds if not thousands of people.

— Kai Bird
16:49

Trump knew Cohn was gay

Bird says Trump certainly knew Cohn was gay. After Studio 54 opened in '77, Cohn often took Trump there in the 1970s; in the 1980s Cohn was more open, surrounded at his law firm by young gay men. Once Cohn called Trump to plead for a boyfriend who was dying of AIDS, asking whether he could stay at a Trump hotel; Trump arranged it, the man stayed three or four months, and after he died Trump sent Cohn a bill. Bird sees a connection between Cohn's closeted state and the McCarthy-era persecution of gay people, and Trump's anti-DEI, anti-LGBTQ line today.

— Kai Bird
17:53

Cohn's politics was the politics of resentment

Bird traces Cohn's politics in the 1960s and '70s: he resented intellectuals, hated the Ivy League despite being a Columbia graduate, and preferred hiring young lawyers who had not gone to Ivies; he was suspicious of immigrants, anti-black, an isolationist who wanted America first. He opposed affirmative action, seeing it as discrimination against white people. Bird says this politics is exactly what we see in the Trump administration today — the same politics of resentment. And Trump likewise went to an Ivy League school and likewise came from a wealthy, high-profile family; there are many parallels between the two.

— Kai Bird
25:00

The Art of the Deal was arranged by Cohn on his deathbed

Bird says Cohn did one overlooked thing for Trump: before he died, he went to his old Horace Mann schoolmate Cy Newhouse, the Condé Nast magazine publisher and Random House owner, and suggested he have a Random House editor sign Trump to write a memoir. That is where The Art of the Deal came from. The book was published after Cohn's death and became a national bestseller, turning a star developer tucked away in New York into a national figure and famous businessman, which led to the TV show The Apprentice, which led to Trump running for president. Bird says the starting point of that chain is Cohn's relationship with Cy Newhouse.

— Kai Bird
26:01

After Cohn got AIDS, Trump began keeping his distance

Cohn contracted AIDS early in the epidemic. After Trump learned Cohn might die of AIDS, by all accounts he began keeping his distance, relying less and less on his legal advice and turning to other lawyers, but he never broke with him. Trump also invited Cohn to the newly purchased Florida estate Mar-a-Lago and threw a big dinner, where many people toasted Cohn. After the dinner, Trump is said to have had staff wash the silverware in hydrogen peroxide.

— Kai Bird
37:45

McCarthyism was never defeated

Bird says the core lesson of Cohn's story is that McCarthyism was not defeated, it did not die, it was not a 1950s anomaly. As a historian he came to realize that America is a nation of immigrants, a multicultural nation, a nation with civil liberties and the First Amendment, yet every few decades it goes through a period of paranoia: there was a Red Scare in the 1920s, the McCarthy witch hunt in the early 1950s, and now we are in such a period again. In the 1950s several thousand civil servants lost their jobs because of Cohn and McCarthy, and today's situation is worse — a more serious attack on democracy, a more serious politics of vengeance, mass hunting down and deportation of undocumented immigrants.

— Kai Bird
