Water Privatisation Is Not Destiny: Uruguay's Referendum Victory on Water Rights

On 31 October 2004, Uruguayans wrote "water is an essential resource for life" into their constitution with 64.7% voting yes, and stipulated that water supply could only be provided by the state — making Uruguay the first country in the world to lock water privatisation out of its constitution. The movement began when the taps were shut off in a coastal city.

0:45

Water rights entered the constitution through the streets, not the negotiating table

When the referendum result was announced on 31 October 2004, 64.7% of Uruguayan voters backed amending the constitution to enshrine water rights in the fundamental law. Carmen Soa, who was there, recalls that after the vote the streets outside the union hall were jammed with people dancing, calling family, discarded ballots floating in the air. This victory was not the product of elite negotiation but of a street-festival-style popular movement, one that swept up even the people inside the union office.

— Carmen Soa
1:30

The constitution did not just write down a right, it sealed the door on privatisation

The referendum amended the constitution to add the phrase "water is an essential natural resource for life," and further stipulated that drinking water and sanitation could only be provided exclusively by the state, not by private companies. This made Uruguay the first country in the world to write the principle of public water supply into its constitution. The amendment did not merely put a human right into the text; it directly foreclosed the possibility of any future government handing water supply to private companies by law.

2:40

Privatisation did not just raise prices, it shut off the free taps

The movement originated in the 1990s. The coastal region of Maldonado granted a water supply concession to a private company for the first time since 1952. The government claimed private investment was needed to upgrade the network and meet growing demand; the company said it needed to raise prices to fund infrastructure. But activists pointed out that connection fees rose sharply and families who could not pay were cut off; free taps in public spaces such as town squares and schools were also closed, and the poorest families living in informal housing were hit first.

4:00

With no money for ads, 10% of signatures can still force a referendum

In 2002, citizens' groups and trade unions formed the "Commission for Water and Life" and launched a nationwide signature campaign. Under Uruguayan electoral rules, collecting signatures from 10% of registered voters obliges the electoral authority to hold a referendum. Carmen said they went to markets handing out leaflets with the slogans "Water belongs to everyone" and "Water is life"; with no money for media advertising, they could only go door to door. Within a year they had gathered 283,000 signatures, far above the threshold.

— Carmen Soa
5:10

Remote villages were mobilised by 23 people on horseback

To reach the most remote areas with their message, 23 union comrades set out on horseback in early October, crossing the country and entering small villages where there had previously been almost no organised presence. They rode into the capital, Montevideo, arriving at the Legislative Palace on 31 October, which drew widespread media coverage. This equestrian long march turned referendum campaigning into a symbolic social event and linked rural and urban mobilisation together.

6:00

A cup of mate turned a constitutional right into everyday life

Two days before the vote, supporters paid for a television ad themselves: a man holding a mate, with a voiceover saying, "Privatise water and you'll have no water to brew your mate with. Access to water is a basic human right — vote yes." The ad used Uruguay's national drink as a metaphor, turning an abstract constitutional right into a daily scene. It is a classic case of grassroots mobilisation using cultural symbols to move people, and shows that creativity can make up for a budget shortfall.

7:30

64.7% turned a small country's referendum into an international event

Carmen voted at midday and then went to the union building, where about seven televisions were set up showing live election coverage from eight in the morning. That night the result came in: 64.7% in favour. The union phones were overwhelmed; she said it got so busy that sometimes she could not tell which country or which language a call was coming from, because the issue drew enormous international attention. The celebrations and media coverage after the vote turned this constitutional amendment from a small-country campaign into a milestone of the global water rights movement.

— Carmen Soa
8:08

Water is not a commodity: at least 30 countries followed by writing it into their constitutions

After Uruguay's referendum, at least 30 countries wrote the right to water into their constitutions; in 2010 the UN General Assembly formally recognised access to water and sanitation as a human right. Carmen summed it up: water is a human right, because without water there is no life; like air, it must be accessible whether or not you have money. That sentence captures the ethical core of the whole movement: water is not a commodity but a precondition for sustaining life, and cannot be handed over to the logic of profit.

— Carmen Soa
