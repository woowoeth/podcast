The Profit Secret of Wallets Isn't Interest on Balances, It's Money Circulating Within the System

The core accounting of a wallet: money coming in incurs card network fees, internal transfers are nearly free, and withdrawals can be charged again; it earns from the spread of movement, not from idle balances. PayPal, Cash App, and Stripe Link are all betting on this model.

This episode breaks down the real cost structure of wallets: wholesale channel fees are less than a cent, yet platforms can charge users several cents; Japanese peers even open multiple bank accounts to bypass clearing. Even if you're not in payments, this serves as a business teardown template.

A wallet must answer how it makes money from day one

The episode opens with a $100 Venmo payment to a barber, questioning why PayPal still wants to insert itself into such small transactions that already have mature payment rails. It then quickly defines the scope: this is about e-wallet businesses, not crypto private-key wallets. Since a wallet appears to hold balances, it immediately enters the regulatory sensitive zone of deposits, so from day one a wallet must answer how it makes money—if it can't, the product dies.

Card fraud isn't from fake merchant sites; it's from issuer-side leaks

The biggest early obstacle to online transactions was users' reluctance to hand over card numbers, with media constantly hyping card theft risks. The author points out the opposite is true: much fraud comes from massive data leaks on the issuing side, not from fraudsters building fake sites. The conclusion: 'Economies of scale also protect criminals.' PayPal's value was shielding users between card numbers and payees, limiting risk per transaction—this justified charging close to 3% in network fees and planted the seed for the later balance design.

— Patrick McKenzie
6:19

A free wallet is anything but free for the wallet itself

Moving money into a wallet requires card networks, historically around 3%, a cost borne by the wallet. But once balances stay within the system, peer-to-peer transfers are nearly just a database update, too cheap to meter. PayPal charges near list price on both ends, so merely substituting internal transfers for external ones yields extra margin. A wallet that seems free to users is not free for the wallet—the zero marginal cost of existing balances is the real moat.

— Patrick McKenzie
9:21

Driving down network fees beats product innovation for profits

There's no inherent link between wholesale and retail: buying millions of ACH transactions costs about $0.05 each, yet payment providers can charge merchants nearly $0.30; the spread is the tax platforms levy on the underlying rails. Japan is more extreme: interbank transfers cost about ¥200 per transaction, while intra-bank entries are nearly free, so some products open accounts at major banks, move users' money internally, and settle via daily netting. In the financial world, cutting network fees translates more directly into wallet profits than product innovation.

— Patrick McKenzie
12:27

Withdrawals aren't a user perk; they're a second revenue pipe

Cash App prices withdrawals too: for speed, you choose the pricier card rail at 1.75% with a $0.25 minimum; the author tested withdrawing $50 and was quoted $0.88. Complementing this, Cash App partners with institutions like Sutton Bank to issue prepaid cards, so spending outside the system also earns interchange. Wallets no longer treat withdrawals as a user benefit but as a second income stream, because partner banks need the wallet's user base, and the wallet needs the bank's issuing license.

— Patrick McKenzie
20:53

Co-branded cards earn a cut but carry the bad debt

The logic of wallet-issued credit cards is two-way fees, but risk control is the real battleground: wallets typically assume their accumulated spending data can underwrite better than issuers. The industry has repeatedly shown that FICO is already so strong it's hard to beat; additional variables that materially improve losses often cross legal red lines. What truly prices the wallet is the agreement—banks require the entire loan portfolio's loss rate to stay below a threshold, or the wallet's revenue share drops to zero or even goes negative. 10-K language skirts 'loss sharing,' but essentially the wallet absorbs the issuer's bad debt.

— Patrick McKenzie
24:50

Users care about the phone, not the card

Apple's core argument to banks is that users care about their phones, not the plastic credit card; if banks don't pay up, Apple could nurture or build its own two-sided payment network and take users with it. Reports say Apple takes 15 basis points per Apple Pay transaction, while Google reportedly takes nothing—the author quips about the two giants' differing abilities in non-core products. The deeper point: the checkout itself can be like an ad slot. When product and price are set, who gets placed first in payment options becomes an auction. Stripe has shifted from static displays to dynamic sorting based on user preferences, even letting merchants fine-tune options.

— Patrick McKenzie
31:13

Network fees aren't lowered at the negotiating table but in default settings

Extend Link as a ledger-level settlement negotiation layer: if users store their low-fee payment methods in Link, the next checkout can bypass card networks and go through bank accounts; the saved network fees are redistributed between merchants and users. This can't be achieved by one company sitting at a table, but by the aggregation of millions of users' and merchants' decisions, eventually representing a volume on the other side of the payment negotiation close to 1% of global GDP. Only that scale can force network fees to be repriced.

Glossary

ACH
The US interbank batch transfer network; costs are extremely low, with wholesale prices as low as $0.05 per transaction.
interchange
The portion of a card transaction fee that goes to the issuing bank, usually a percentage of the transaction amount; wallet models aim to bypass it.
Durbin-exempt institution
A small bank exempt from debit card interchange caps under US law, often serving as the actual issuer for wallet co-branded cards.
bps / bips
Financial unit of measure; 1 basis point equals 0.01%, so 15 bps is 0.15%.
