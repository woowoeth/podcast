A wallet's profit engine isn't float interest, it's money moving inside the system

The core ledger of a wallet: money coming in pays card-network tolls, internal transfers cost almost nothing, and money going out can be charged again. It earns the spread on flow, not interest on balances — and PayPal, Cash App and Stripe Link are all betting on that model.

A wallet has to answer how it makes money on day one

It opens with a Venmo bill for $100 paid to his barber: for a small transaction that already has a mature payment rail, why does PayPal still want to wedge itself in and monetize it. Then it quickly sets the scope: this is about the e-wallet business, not crypto private-key wallets; because it appears to hold and disburse balances, it immediately enters the regulatory zone that treats deposits as sensitive, so a wallet has to answer how it makes money from the first day of design — if it can't, the product dies.

Card fraud isn't scammers building sites, it's issuer-side leaks

The biggest early obstacle to online transactions was that users didn't dare hand over their card numbers, and the media kept amplifying card-fraud risk. The author points out the opposite is true: most fraud comes from massive data leaks on the issuing side, not from scammers parasitizing checkout pages, and the conclusion is that "economies of scale protect criminals too." PayPal's value was standing between the user and the card number and the recipient, capping the risk of a single transaction — which gave it grounds to charge a toll near 3%, and planted the seed for the balance design that followed.

— Patrick McKenzie
6:19

A free wallet is not free at all for the wallet

Putting money into a wallet goes through the card networks, at a historical list price of about 3%, and the wallet eats that cost; but once the balance stays inside the system, transfers between users amount to changing a row in a database, too cheap to bill for. PayPal charges close to full price on both the in and out ends, so simply substituting internal transfers for external ones adds a slice of gross margin. A wallet that is free to the average user is not free to the wallet — the zero marginal cost of the float is the real dam.

— Patrick McKenzie
9:21

Driving down network fees beats product innovation for profit

There is no necessary relationship between wholesale and retail: buying millions of ACH transactions costs about $0.05 each, while a payment processor can quote a merchant close to $0.30; the spread is the tax the platform levies on the underlying plumbing. Japan is more extreme: interbank transfers cost about 200 yen per transaction while intra-bank entries are nearly free, so some products open accounts at each of the top banks, move the user's money inside the banks, and settle via daily netting. In finance, driving down network fees converts into wallet profit more directly than product innovation.

— Patrick McKenzie
12:27

Cash-out isn't a user perk, it's a second toll pipe

Cash App prices cash-out the same way: want it fast and you pick the more expensive card rail, charged at 1.75% with a 25-cent minimum; the author tested withdrawing $50 and was quoted 88 cents. Paired with that is issuing prepaid cards through partners like Sutton Bank, so money spent outside the system still captures swipe fees. The wallet no longer treats cash-out as a user benefit but as a second revenue pipe, because the partner bank needs the wallet's user scale and the wallet needs the bank's issuing license.

— Patrick McKenzie
20:53

On co-brand cards the wallet takes a cut and carries the bad debt

The logic of a wallet partnering on credit cards is charging both sides, but risk control is the real battlefield: wallets typically assume their accumulated spending data lets them underwrite more accurately than the issuing bank. The industry has repeatedly shown FICO is already strong enough to be hard to replace; the extra variables that could materially improve losses often cross legal red lines. What actually prices the wallet is the agreement — the bank requires the whole loan portfolio's loss rate to stay under a threshold, otherwise the wallet's revenue share goes to zero or it even pays in. The 10-K language dances around "loss sharing," but in essence the wallet is carrying the issuer's bad debt.

— Patrick McKenzie
24:50

Users care about the phone, not the plastic card

Apple's core argument when negotiating with banks is that users care about the phone, not the plastic credit card; if banks won't pay, Apple can also back or build a new two-sided payment network and take users with it. The reported figure is that Apple takes 15 basis points per Apple Pay transaction, while Google reportedly takes nothing — the author uses this to gripe about the two giants' differing ability to do non-core products. The next layer is the conclusion that the checkout itself can be an ad slot: once the goods and price are fixed, who gets to rank a payment method first becomes a bidding war, and Stripe has already turned the traditional display into dynamic ordering by user preference, even opening up merchant fine-tuning options.

— Patrick McKenzie
31:13

Network fees come down not at the table but in the defaults

Extend Link into a general-ledger-level settlement negotiation layer: if users store their low-cost payment methods in Link, the next checkout can bypass the card networks and go through bank accounts; the network fees saved get redistributed between merchants and users. This can't be negotiated by one company sitting at one table — it's the aggregation of decisions by millions of users and merchants, ultimately making the side it represents at the payments negotiating table approach 1% of global GDP. Only that kind of volume earns the right to reprice network fees.

Glossary

ACH
The US interbank batch transfer network; direct debits and credits are extremely cheap, with wholesale pricing as low as $0.05 per transaction.
interchange
The portion of a card transaction fee that goes to the issuing bank, usually a percentage of the transaction amount; the wallet model's goal is to route around it.
Durbin-exempt institution
A small US bank exempt from the debit-card interchange fee cap under US regulation, often the actual issuer behind a wallet's co-brand card.
bps / bips
A unit of financial pricing; 1 basis point equals 0.01%, so 15 bps is 0.15%.
