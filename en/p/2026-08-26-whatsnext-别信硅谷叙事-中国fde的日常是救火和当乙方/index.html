China's FDEs aren't Silicon Valley enablers; they're firefighters for state-owned and private enterprises

The real work of a Chinese FDE isn't presenting AI solutions to empower enterprises; it's putting out fires for state-owned and private companies: internal politics, data governance, uncooperative people—technology is the easy part.

FDE isn't a new role; it's an old intelligence-agency practice

Palantir proposed FDE about 20 years ago, initially customizing data analysis software for US intelligence agencies. Because of data confidentiality, engineers were stationed at client sites, working and meeting alongside clients. In the AI era, large models have become more capable but enterprises don't know how to use them, so OpenAI, Anthropic, and others have formed FDE teams. Over the past two years, global job postings have grown by dozens of times, with salaries often reaching a million yuan annually, dubbed the sexiest job in Silicon Valley.

7:50

In China, FDEs aren't enablers; they're vendors

Shen Le says that in China, FDEs are not the superior role portrayed in media—bringing AI solutions to empower—but are essentially vendors. You have to keep a vendor's mindset, catering to bosses, leaders, and various key client figures to push projects forward; meanwhile, you must deal with pitfalls left by sales, business, and pre-sales solution engineers.

— Shen Le
13:19

The IT lead wants to close the deal but can't mobilize a single business person

In a state-owned enterprise project Shen Le was stationed at, leadership rejected every proposal and the client wanted to cancel the order. He found the core bottleneck: the interim IT lead's only goal was to close the deal, wanting to prove value with a demo, but lacked authority to mobilize the business team, so Shen Le couldn't access real business needs, couldn't build a deployment scenario, and couldn't demonstrate delivery standards to senior leadership. One link jammed the next, and the project fell into a dead loop.

— Shen Le
17:26

The biggest pitfall is people; a leader who can rally the troops fixes it

A week later, a new person took over the project. The new leader didn't understand AI but could mobilize the business team, arranging for Shen Le to meet contacts from eight or nine business departments, adding them on WeChat, calling, and visiting desks to collect materials. Shen Le independently handled one business line, chose a department that writes materials for leaders as the MVP pilot, and the project started moving. He said the biggest pitfall is still people; once you handle people, the rest is easier.

— Shen Le
21:49

The boss doesn't want AI efficiency; he wants to cut 60,000 to 3,000

At a home improvement materials company, a post-80s boss, facing industry downturn and extreme anxiety, hoped AI would drive revenue growth and cost reduction. He gave an example: ideally, with the company's current 60,000+ employees, could AI reduce that to 3,000? Shen Le was startled. The industry solution consultant didn't directly reject the idea but used verbal artistry: rather than focusing on layoffs, consider whether the current 60,000 people could create the value of 12,000.

— Shen Le
28:02

Probabilistic models can't enter production lines that demand accuracy

Shen Le points out that one reason many enterprises are uncooperative is that current models and Agent technology are far from meeting large-scale production conditions. Models are inherently probabilistic, but large-scale production applications don't accept probability; they demand accuracy. Therefore, many FDE projects are just building foundational platforms for experimentation, without the dramatic effect of immediate transformation once deployed.

— Shen Le
