LLMs Industrialize the Scam, and SSO Becomes the Master Switch

When forging interviews, monitoring inboxes and synthesizing voices cost close to nothing, anyone's SSO account can be quietly taken over; who the bank makes eat the loss matters more than teaching users to spot phishing.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:06

You only dodged it because the scam was still too expensive

Manish's starting point: LLMs are attacking the cost side of running a complex scam. The old Nigerian-prince scam let people think "I'm smart enough not to fall for it"; but today the grunt work — standing up a fake company website, simulating an interview process, reading through a victim's emails one by one — can all be automated cheaply. Note that this is not the future; it is happening now.

— Manish
5:22

One SSO account pries open every asset you own

Because users will always reuse passwords, the industry moved to identity providers like Google and Apple, and SSO became "one ring to rule them all." Breaching that identity account means getting the email at the same time; and getting the email means resetting the passwords on bank and brokerage accounts. So the attacker isn't in a hurry to cash out — they quietly sit on the account, monitor your financial rhythm long-term, and move when there's a lot of money in there.

— Patrick
10:37

Impersonating a type of person costs pennies; impersonating you is expensive

Hong Kong has already seen a $25 million deepfake scam: an employee saw the "CEO" and other executives on a video call and was told to press the button and wire the money. Cloning a specific person currently requires thirty-plus minutes of their voice samples; but if all you need is a generic profile like "a man in his forties with a New England accent," the cost can drop to pennies. Remember: today's technology is the worst generation it will ever be.

— Manish
16:52

The harder banks block, the more the money runs to gift cards

Banks are required to interdict illicit money flows, so fraudsters move to payment tools at the regulatory edge. A single gift card can move $500 anywhere, and repeating that action 100 times moves $50,000; gift card companies happen to be very bad at detecting that kind of serial activity. Bitcoin ATMs carry absurdly high premiums that no normal user would pay — the only reason to use one is that a scammer sent a victim to buy. A whole gray industry has grown up around these tools.

— Patrick
22:03

The fake interview is step one of supply chain poisoning

Manish is on the Rust security response team, and two days after he posted, reports came in: someone used a Rust job interview as bait to get candidates to download a library containing obfuscated malicious code, and at least five core community members were targeted. His first reaction was that this is a supply chain attack — compromise the software maintainer first, then poison hundreds or thousands of companies through normal updates, ultimately reaching bank accounts to move money.

— Manish
26:08

A scammer's best money mule is someone already scammed

Scammers go back to people they've already scammed, claim to be case investigators, and then offer a "work from home" job that is really being a money mule: receive money in your own real-name account, take 10%, and forward the balance to the next layer of accounts. This is the "layering" stage of money laundering. The standard three stages are placement, layering and integration — putting cash into the financial system, creating hops to stall tracing, and finally buying assets to clean it.

— Patrick
36:27

Calls and senders can both be forged; trust only what you dial out

The number in caller ID and the sender address on an email can both be completely forged. The only workable principle: don't trust communications that find you — trust only communications you initiate to a known endpoint. Bank employees usually have a "desk name" and an extension, so hang up and call the official number on the back of your card and ask to be transferred; a bank URL in a search ad may also have been bought by a scammer, which is why you can't call the number in an ad.

— Patrick
45:42

Anti-fraud shouldn't rest on individual detection but on institutional liability

Credit card fraud causes tens of billions of dollars in losses every year, yet it hasn't crushed the middle and working classes, because Regulation E established a clear order of liability: a cardholder's out-of-pocket maximum for fraudulent charges is $50, usually waived outright by the bank, and the loss is pushed outward to merchants by contract. Putting accountability on the stronger institution, rather than expecting every person to spot phishing, is the governance model that has been proven out.

— Patrick

Glossary

BEC / business email compromise
A scam that breaches internal email and impersonates an executive to instruct finance to wire money out.
money mule
Someone who uses their own real-name account to receive and forward funds for a fraud ring, taking a cut.
AML / anti-money laundering
The compliance regime by which banks monitor and interdict illicit money flows.
Regulation E
The U.S. electronic transfers rule under which banks bear most of the liability for card fraud.
desk name
An alias bank employees use externally, so criminals can't follow the trail back.
supply chain attack
Compromising a software maintainer and poisoning downstream users in bulk through updates.
