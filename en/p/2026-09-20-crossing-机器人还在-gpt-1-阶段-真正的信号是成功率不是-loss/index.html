Robots Are Still at GPT-1, and the Real Signal Is Success Rate, Not Loss

The Scaling Law signal for embodied intelligence has already appeared, but we're measuring the wrong metric: low loss does not mean high success rate, and fitting the 17 steps that never touch the object is meaningless.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

12:15

Pretraining is not everything; adaptation is what matters

Xu Mengdi's CMU best doctoral thesis is called Building Adaptable Generalist Robots, and its core claim is: pretraining is extremely useful, but you cannot hope a robot will succeed 100% of the time on pretraining alone. The defining feature of the open world is that tasks keep changing — at home there are 20 objects today, a week later 30 of different kinds, and people's preferences change too. So the robot must be able to adapt to change in its deployment environment and improve itself. She explored two paths: In-Context Learning and test-time training; the latter changes only 0.5% of the adapter parameters, but change too much and it forgets the pretrained knowledge.

— Xu Mengdi
19:22

Why adaptation only became important now

Humans generalize well for two reasons: extremely strong priors, and learning things very fast. The mainstream approach until now has been to scale data and models so that multi-task and instruction-following abilities get stronger — essentially giving the model a strong enough prior first. But Xu Mengdi points out this does not mean adaptation is unimportant — it is just that people only recently realized it. And adaptation places demands on the base model: you don't want the robot's self-learning process in a real setting to be terrifying, like smashing a cup. So starting from pretraining is reasonable.

— Xu Mengdi
49:46

Low loss does not equal high success rate

This is the most counterintuitive technical judgment in the whole piece. There is a very tricky point in robotics: loss is not directly tied to success rate. Loss can be very low while the final success rate is not necessarily low — there will be a lot of oscillation. Her example: a task might take 20 steps in total, and the first 17 steps involve no contact with the object. Even if those 17 steps fit extremely well, if step 18, when contact actually happens, has a relatively large error, the task fails easily. This is an imbalance in the time sequence. So a Scaling Law that actually means something should be: as data gradually increases and the model gradually grows, success rate on unseen tasks gradually rises.

— Xu Mengdi
50:47

Robots are still at the GPT-1 moment

Today's mainstream embodied models are still ‘pretraining + fine-tuning on the target domain’, which can be compared to the GPT-1 stage of language models — GPT-1 itself also needed targeted post-training to solve the corresponding tasks. What Xu Mengdi really wants to achieve is the GPT-3 moment: giving the model task information through ICL, through prompting, with no fine-tuning needed, telling the model what to do purely through context. That way the weight of fine-tuning becomes much weaker, it scales more easily to more scenarios and more tasks, and ordinary people can use it too.

— Xu Mengdi
58:49

The data loop is a chicken-and-egg problem

The hardest point is whether you can give the model a stable data loop. That requires putting the robot into an end-user home or service scenario, having a suitable mentor to teach it, and the robot must show obvious progress after being taught for half an hour — only then will end users be willing to use it, and only then can the data loop start turning. But there is a chicken-and-egg problem here: the base model needs to be good enough and learn fast enough, yet the model also needs to get into the scenario first so users start using it, in order to draw out that base capability. So whether the data loop can be made to work is the riskiest point of all.

— Xu Mengdi
1:03:52

Data is defined by working backward from model capability

Data itself gets fed to the model, but the definition of data is not conjured out of thin air — it is defined by the model's capabilities. When people define data, what they are actually doing is: figuring out what capabilities the model needs, and working backward to how the data should be collected. For example, if you need smoother motion, the data should be of the smooth type; if you need fail-recovery ability, the data should be collected with failures and corrections in it. And right now people's definition of model capability is still fairly vague, so the data requirements are vague too.

— Xu Mengdi
1:05:54

Each of the four data types has its own place

Xu Mengdi's position is that of a fusionist, because different data teaches the model different things. Teleoperation data is closest to the robot body itself, suited to the post-training stage, making the model more directly relevant to the target domain. Simulation data easily produces position generalization and texture generalization, making the model more robust to visual change, and suits the front end of pretraining or mid-training. UMI data sits in the middle, more like robot data but cheaper to collect. Human data is the cheapest, can be spread widely across different scenarios, and what it teaches is scene variation and task-relevant information, less tied to the specific shape of the morphology.

— Xu Mengdi
1:19:02

AI-written papers are obvious at a glance

Robotics papers have recently been doubling in number, and Xu Mengdi's way of coping is to scroll through articles promoted by the researchers she follows, because the author list itself has already done a layer of filtering. She can clearly see that some articles have a very large AI-written component. Her judgment: AI-written articles may pass review and actually get into some conferences, but overall the quality still won't be higher than human-written ones, because they look familiar while in fact many arguments are full of holes and there isn't much content. Once she finds out a paper was written by AI, her interest in it is discounted.

— Xu Mengdi

Glossary

In-Context Learning
Adapting a model to a new task purely through context in the prompt, without changing model parameters.
VLA
A model that maps vision and language instructions directly to robot actions.
UMI
A data collection method sitting between robot data and human data, with lower cost.
MAML
Model-agnostic meta-learning: letting a model learn to adapt quickly to unseen tasks; the occasion that brought Xu Mengdi into the field.
algorithm distillation
A DeepMind work arguing that model parameters are equivalent to an RL algorithm.
no free lunch
Once you pick one decision, you have to bear its weaker aspects.
