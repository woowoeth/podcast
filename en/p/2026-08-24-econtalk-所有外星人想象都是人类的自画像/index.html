The UFO Craze Is Not an Astronomy Problem, It's a Belief Problem

When the evidence stays at the level of testimony, whether the universe is crowded becomes a matter of faith. The more science advances, the rarer extraterrestrial life becomes in our observations; when we talk about UFOs, what we're really talking about is ourselves.

6:17

Six military witnesses gave testimony, not evidence

About six witnesses with military or government backgrounds sat before Congress, and to many people that already carries enough weight. But Kirsch cautions: that is only testimony (testimony), not testable evidence. Sagan's famous line still applies: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and the eyewitness record to date has never been matched by a physical object or a clear image. He extends this distinction to religious experience: you cannot deny that the person's encounter with the Virgin Mary was sincere, but subjective certainty cannot become a fact others can verify. More troublesome still, such claims often come from people outside the scientific establishment, and we happen to live in an age that no longer trusts experts, so who has the standing to judge whether it's true or false becomes the real political question.

— Adam Kirsch
17:02

Decades without a clear photo shouldn't count as coincidence

Jung noticed a feature back in the 1950s: UFOs don't photograph well. Even witnesses who claim to have watched for an hour can't produce a clear photo. Decades later, with cameras everywhere, what survives is still just blurry blips on infrared and radar. Kirsch argues this systematic absence can't be attributed to coincidence; the more reasonable explanation is that the claimed events did not actually happen. People think they are reporting an object that has never been confirmed, when in fact memory, narrative and expectation are supplying the image for an originally vague experience.

— Adam Kirsch
21:41

UFOs turned respectable by swapping gatekeepers, not by new evidence

In December 2017, The New York Times and The Washington Post almost simultaneously disclosed Pentagon UFO videos and a Department of Defense program code-named AATIP, with a budget of 22 million dollars over five years. The Pentagon had previously always denied formally looking into such phenomena, and once the reports ran, the mainstream narrative turned immediately. Kirsch points out in particular that the shift in public attitudes toward UFOs did not come from new evidence appearing, but from the gatekeepers changing: once authoritative media moved a topic previously classified as "conspiracy theory" onto the front page, the public gave itself permission to take it seriously.

— Adam Kirsch
31:57

Every renaming is another admission that we don't understand

The term UFO was itself invented by the US Air Force in the early 1950s, to replace the comic-book-sounding "flying saucer" with a cool technological flavor. But Kirsch points to a mechanism: labels can change, but if the phenomenon can't be explained, the word keeps depreciating. Hence the "euphemism treadmill" — after flying saucer came UFO, then UAP; the full name of UAP changed from Aerial Phenomenon to Anomalous Phenomenon, purely to bring underwater, underground and even "from the future" objects into the fold. Every renaming is another admission: we have no idea what it actually is.

— Adam Kirsch
41:01

Belief in UFOs and anti-vaxxing come from the same distrust

Kirsch places UFO interest within a broader social pulse of the present: it appears alongside Brexit and vaccine skepticism, sharing the same distrust of expert authority. People resent being told what to do and would rather take their accounts from social media. Within mainstream media, fact and fiction are still distinguished, but platforms like TikTok give unverified content the same attention as serious reporting, ultimately producing a general ignorance — not an absence of information, but an absence of any basis for judging information. The core of the problem is not whether individual witnesses are crazy, but that the public no longer recognizes anyone as better qualified than themselves to tell true from false.

— Adam Kirsch
50:01

The scientist's puzzle: theory is alive, observation is empty

The ordinary person's puzzle is that "UFOs are always being seen but no one takes them seriously"; the scientist's puzzle is exactly the reverse: theory predicts the universe should be full of life, yet observation shows nothing. Until the mid-20th century, the Copernican principle still led progressive thinkers to assume the Earth was nothing special and alien civilizations must exist; today the solar system has only eight planets, and only one known planet with life. Hence Avi Loeb's "Oumuamua bet": even when the evidence is uncertain, one should first assume an interstellar object might be alien technology, otherwise no one will ever design an experiment to verify it. The anxiety in the scientific community is essentially the gap between a starry theoretical sky and an observational wasteland, unseen by the public.

— Adam Kirsch
58:25

We can only hear civilizations that happen to be in the radio age

Russ offers a time-window argument: if humans survive another million years, today's technology will certainly be obsolete, and radio waves will probably be phased out too. So the alien signals we can intercept can only come from civilizations that happen to be in their radio age; on a cosmic scale that window is as short as the fact that you no longer see horse-drawn carriages today. Kirsch adds the mirror view: our imagination of advanced civilizations has always drifted with our own technology — in the 1950s we assumed aliens were doing radio astronomy, now we guess they long ago miniaturized their technology to invisibility. Together, the two lines of thought repeatedly expose the same present-tense limitation of imagination.

— Russ Roberts & Adam Kirsch
1:11:02

After God exits, aliens take over the position

The final act doesn't stop at skepticism. Kirsch says that on the map of the universe, we always want some coordinate point to be filled with "something." When God exits public discourse, meaning doesn't disappear, it just needs a new vehicle: aliens happen to take on that role — reopening the universe, letting people project hope, and equipping the lonely individual with a larger psyche. Having gone all the way around, he gives a clear attitude: the longing for UFOs should not be mocked; it is a human need worthy of respect. We are always our own frame of reference, and talking about aliens is painting a self-portrait.

— Adam Kirsch

Glossary

The official term replacing UFO, covering unidentified objects in the air, underwater and elsewhere.
euphemism treadmill
The linguistic-social phenomenon of continually swapping in neutral labels to avoid negative associations.
book experience
The experience of gaining an explanatory framework through reading books after an inexplicable event.

A formula for estimating the number of detectable civilizations in the Milky Way, containing several unknown variables.
