AI Safety Language Is Destroying the Debate: It's a Bug, Not a Demon

Calling an AI failure "misalignment" assigns intent and moral judgment to software, leaving Congress and the public unable to understand what actually happened — it should be debugged, reported and fixed like a bug.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

"Misalignment" just means the software has a bug

Sinofsky's core claim: when AI does something we didn't expect, that isn't "misaligned" — it's software not running as intended, i.e. a bug. He says that back when Word ate files and deleted all their contents, nobody thought a demon had possessed Word and made it act against the user's will; and when a spreadsheet miscalculated, nobody circled up to pray to the alignment gods. Calling an AI failure "misalignment" assigns it properties that don't exist: that it's "trying to do something," that it "made a moral judgment," that it "has rules and principles." And if it's just making decisions based on statistics, that doesn't change the standard that it should do what you want it to do — if the statistics are wrong, that's a bug in the product.

— Steven Sinofsky
7:07

Full self-driving running a red light is also a bug

Sinofsky uses autonomous driving as an example: if full self-driving runs a red light because it identified a stop sign as a Christmas tree, we wouldn't say it "failed to align with the spirit of the December holidays and road signage" — we'd say it's a bug, and a dangerous one, because that stop sign has meaning. He points out that both Tesla and Waymo have built in extensive telemetry, diagnostics and extra cameras — things you wouldn't put in a spreadsheet, but would put in life-critical software. And AI models are still at the research-project stage, lacking the tools and telemetry that important software is supposed to have.

— Steven Sinofsky
8:07

Once telemetry ships, there are more bugs than you can fix

Sinofsky recalls that the software industry spent twenty years with no idea why programs crashed, and could only keep a list of crash sites and fix the top ten. Until someone said: why don't we write a program that, when it crashes, tells us over this new thing called the internet? Suddenly there was telemetry, and suddenly there were far more bugs than anyone imagined — the shift from "we have no bugs, we can ship" to "if we stopped all work, we could fix bugs forever." On that basis he says that when people talk about an AI pause, labs absolutely should slow down: stop adding new things and go build telemetry, tooling, logging and step-through debugging. He read the bug report OpenAI had just published, and the conclusion was clear: they just don't know.

— Steven Sinofsky
9:08

Excel wrote "send dogs" to disk

The first Windows version of Excel, in 1987, had a bug: it would write the letters "send dogs" into some Windows settings file on disk. Nobody knew where the phrase came from, and everyone combed the Excel source code and couldn't find it. Finally a tester wrote a script that continuously generated charts and printed them on an old dot-matrix printer for days, and found that after about two days it would write send dogs into that location — it depended on how much memory you had, how long it had been running, and what printer and device drivers you used. Sinofsky says we could easily have concluded that "Excel's brain decided to print send dogs," but we didn't — we actually debugged it, and then added telemetry.

— Steven Sinofsky
13:10

PC software used to claim it was different from mainframes

Sinofsky recalls that IBM people would look at them and say their software was terrible, that Windows was garbage compared to the mainframe software running banks, airlines and power plants. For a long time their answer was "you don't get it, we're different, this is new and cool, we run in 64K instead of 1MB, we sell for $5,000 instead of $5 million." But in the end they found the only thing they had to do was grow up: when people started using Excel to decide how planes fly, using Word to file briefs with the Supreme Court, using PowerPoint to present on the Challenger space shuttle disaster, the standard changed. His conclusion: once your tools are used in those settings, you no longer get to use "we're new" as an excuse.

— Steven Sinofsky
14:10

The virus that cost $12 billion in a day

In March 1998, a virus spread across the internet as a worm. Sinofsky got a call that morning from a reporter gasping "I love you, I love you, I love you." The next day's headline was: $12 billion in damage to the US economy in a single day. He says nobody had previously imagined a bug could cause $12 billion in damage in 12 hours, but it happened. So they held a meeting and decided to pause Outlook development until they understood what had happened. His attitude: the more people use a system, the more damage it can do when something goes wrong, but that's the price of admission, it's what you signed up for — "we're too popular to deal with it" is not an excuse.

— Steven Sinofsky
16:10

Alignment piles a Google-sized problem on top

Sinofsky points out a concrete problem with alignment: to achieve it, you have to have a huge set of rules defining what alignment is — what you can do, what you can't, what's safe, what's unfair. And software engineering learned long ago that piling too many constraints on a system makes it stop working, and produces endless unintended side effects. You set one rule, and someone asks "what about this case," so you add another rule. He says this starts to look like Google's job of deciding how to present search results — which took them 20 years and thousands of full-time people, and is very, very hard. And AI labs have signed up for the upgraded version of that problem: they aren't just retrieving results, they're synthesizing them, so they also have to invent a whole pile of things themselves, and no law is going to invent them for them.

— Steven Sinofsky
17:10

Y2K didn't happen because people did things

Asked whether AI alignment is like Y2K, Sinofsky stresses there's a causal chain here: professionals worried a great deal, then professionals took a great deal of responsibility, and then nothing happened — that's a direct causal chain. They rented generators and RVs, moved computers into secure bunkers, and that's what made nothing happen. And there was almost no legislation — there were some odd regulations about Y2K compliance, but most of the rules were drafted by industry itself, by cross-industry coalitions of banks, insurers and others. He thinks OpenAI needs to sit down with all the frontier model players and labs and build a far better reporting mechanism; yesterday's report is a decent start, but nowhere near what a third party would need to understand what happened.

— Steven Sinofsky
19:13

The FAA doesn't say "we speculate" on the day of

Sinofsky criticizes OpenAI's report as reading more like marketing cover for an incident, because it still says "we're investigating, but our current speculation is..." His comparison is the FAA: the Federal Aviation Administration won't announce "we speculate what happened" on the day of some terrible event — they stay quiet until they know, and once they know they tell you in extreme detail, with a timeline accurate to tenths of a second plus telemetry from every instrument. He says that's how CVE works, and AI labs need to do far more than that, because these are software defects.

— Steven Sinofsky
21:13

Goal seeking sounds like something else to Congress

Sinofsky thinks we shouldn't attribute malice to the people pushing legislation, because that doesn't help anyone solve the problem together. He traces AI back to the Dartmouth summer conference, and notes that academic research has a long tradition of being too clever with language, because that's what makes paper titles catchy — so it's no surprise this vocabulary got inherited. But the problem is that the terminology is dividing people, because the moment you use a metaphor, a fable or anthropomorphism, people assume a whole pile of things. When a technologist says goal seeking, they mean a curve finding its maximum; when a member of Congress hears goal seeking, they picture a person who wants an A; when they hear cheating, they picture doing something you're not allowed to do; when they hear secretly coordinating, they picture spies infiltrating a country, not two pieces of software coordinating via a semaphore.

— Steven Sinofsky
22:13

"Computer virus" was born in the wrong era

Sinofsky says the term computer virus was an accident of birth, and the timing was terrible — it landed right when AIDS and HIV were in the conversation, and the word virus was in the air. So if you go listen to 1980s congressional hearings on the first viruses, it's terrifying, because people were literally thinking about death, when that wasn't what was happening at all — it was just a metaphor a grad student picked up from a friend. He also mentions a piece of trivia: that person wrote a paper on computer viruses that basically demonstrated there was nothing to be done about them and they would exist forever — which itself echoed how people imagined HIV at the time; but he couldn't prove it, because the school wouldn't let him run the tests, on the grounds that he was writing a paper about how dangerous this software was.

— Steven Sinofsky
23:13

Viruses are manageable because industry built defenses

The host asks: viruses were actually avoidable, most of the time we use computers normally without worrying about being hacked — why? Sinofsky's answer is defensive cybersecurity — the industry said this is not acceptable, we can't allow it to happen. He holds up Apple as an example, saying they did it very well, even running TV ads about Mac vs. PC telling people Macs had fewer viruses; that wasn't an accident of nature, it was a huge amount of antivirus work they did on the Mac, work that was very hard to do for Windows because of its business model and a whole pile of other reasons.

— Steven Sinofsky
25:18

From "what counts as a bug" to SevOne, PriOne

Sinofsky recalls that the industry didn't even have a definition of "what counts as a bug": some people only called data loss a bug, others said data being fine but the computer crashing counted too. In the end they decided a bug is when the software and the computer don't do what you want them to do and you're unhappy with the result. They let customers submit any bug in the world, all of it into the database, and so they went from only the bugs they could find themselves to "the universe's bugs," and the count went from a few thousand to hundreds of thousands. Then they introduced severity and priority: severity one is data loss, severity three is intermittent; priority means it must be fixed. The hallway jargon was SevOne, PriOne. The old IBM hands looked at them and said: we've been doing this since 1965, where have you been? And they were still proud of having invented it themselves.

— Steven Sinofsky
26:23

When the forecast is wrong you don't pray to Zeus

Sinofsky says a lot of what's happening now is the hard work of inventing a language for talking about bugs in stochastic statistical systems — but software has actually been doing this for years: weather forecasting is exactly the same thing, a statistical model of what's happening that depends on a pile of inputs and outputs, and it gets things wrong. When a forecast is wrong — say a predicted hurricane track differs from the actual one — meteorologists get together and look at the model, and discuss the model bug that produced the bad forecast. They don't pray to Zeus and ask him to tell them why their weather was wrong.

— Steven Sinofsky
27:24

The Hugging Face incident was an OPSEC failure

Wrapping up, one of the hosts says he sympathizes with the argument but adds: we need software people, and we need AI people, to listen to operational security people, because everything that happened with Hugging Face and OpenAI was an OPSEC failure — no intelligence, no consciousness, purely an operational security failure, and they need to treat it and talk about it that way. Otherwise we'll get legislation nobody is happy with, because legislators can't understand what this industry is saying.

Glossary

alignment
The idea of making AI behavior conform to human intent; Sinofsky argues it repackages software bugs as moral problems.
telemetry
Mechanisms that automatically send back runtime data when software crashes or misbehaves — the prerequisite for locating a bug.
CVE
The numbering system that publicly records software security vulnerabilities; Sinofsky holds it up as the model for defect reporting.
OPSEC
Operational security — the practice of preventing systems from being compromised or abused through process and configuration.
SevOne, PriOne
Microsoft-internal jargon for bugs: severity one is data loss, priority one means it must be fixed.
