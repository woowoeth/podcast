Every founder should be able to say in one sentence whose problem they're solving—so they get directed to the NHS person actually responsible for that issue, then asked if they'll pay for it. That's where healthcare entrepreneurship begins.

2:33

Half of all startups fail in their first year

James cites Y Combinator data: 50% of startups fail in year one, with the high-risk window lasting one to two years. He defines his accelerator work as specifically compressing this risk period—deploying knowledge, mentorship, and network access to push the high-risk curve down and forward, helping teams escape the most dangerous early years faster.

— James
3:33

Co-founders usually find each other by accident, not design

James breaks down how startups actually form: one person spots a problem, then luck brings another person with complementary views and skills. The harder problem: solutions discovered by practitioners are often naturally misaligned with industry needs. When an industry receives the solution, its first questions are always the same—Is this a real problem? Do customers exist? Can it scale? What's the addressable market?

— James
4:34

First explain whose problem you solve, then find who pays

James repeats this: every startup must articulate in one sentence which problem it solves—because then you know exactly who in the NHS owns it (perhaps a deputy chief nurse, a transformation director, or a CCG leader), and you get directed straight to that person. Only then do you ask: will you pay for this? That's when a real business model takes shape.

— James
5:34

Procurement caution, not technical limits, slows NHS innovation

James explains why NHS innovation moves slowly: healthcare can't pause and redesign mid-cycle; any change requires solid evidence; procurement makers carry real accountability, not fashion risk. The NHS also lacks spare budget for non-essential products. Add near-zero interoperability across the health system, navigation friction for patients, and long CQC certification and regulatory cycles, and the innovation timeline stretches significantly.

— James
6:37

HS separates building founders from scaling companies into two distinct programs

HS's solution: build and scale. Build targets individuals with ideas, early teams, and seed-funded companies—taking them from zero to a working tech company with customers and investment. Scale, in partnership with the Department for International Trade, exports UK's best health-tech globally and gives foreign entrants a soft landing—helping them navigate data protection, information governance, and adapt their business model to the NHS.

— James
7:37

Investment approval range between £10k and £2m sets an achievable first target

HS is surrounded by a pool of VCs and angels who can enter at any incubation stage. HS also partners deeply with reinsurer RGA, so teams can pitch directly to RGA's investment committee and apply for funds in the £10k–£2m band. This is a relatively clear funding path HS can unlock.

— James
8:41

HS is recruiting data scientists to balance its doctor and business founders

The HS team itself blends a trauma orthopedist, serial entrepreneurs, an accelerator evaluation specialist, and a former company CFO. James ends with a direct call: most build-program applicants come from medicine or business backgrounds; what the team needs most is data scientists. Anyone ready to join a startup can talk to him and get matched directly into a build program.

— James

Glossary

CCG (Clinical Commissioning Group)
The local NHS body responsible for purchasing and funding healthcare services in a region.
CQC (Care Quality Commission)
The UK's health and social care regulator; products and services require its certification before entering the NHS.
SME (Scale-stage health-tech company)
