The real money in K-Beauty is not in brands, but in contract manufacturers and distributors

Korean beauty brands excel at making consumers feel their products work, but what truly endures across cycles is the contract manufacturers like Cosmax (科丝美诗) and Kolmar (科玛) who produce them, and distributors like Olive Young and Silicon2 who stock them—the brands themselves are more like fireworks, each one bursting and then moving to the next.

Korean beauty shifted from brand-driven to product-driven sales

In the 1.0 era, brands like Sulwhasoo and Innisfree operated on ‘brand-to-product’ leverage: consumers remembered the brand and bought whatever it sold. In 2.0, it reversed to ‘product-to-brand’: consumers remember product nicknames like ‘little yellow oil’or ‘flight attendant spray’but can't recall the brand behind them—and the brand doesn't expect them to. With short-form video attention spans at two or three seconds, blowing up a single product pays better than building brand equity.

20:16

APR built the highest K-Beauty market cap with one spray

Among Korea's new beauty giants, APR (parent of Medicube), founded in 2014, is now Korea's highest-valued beauty company, surpassing legacy Amorepacific. International sales account for 80%, of which approximately 30% from the US. Another contender, Goodai Global, began as a distributor and parlayed its success distributing Beauty of Joseon into acquiring brands. Its sales grew from 100 million won to 1.4 trillion won (approximately $1 billion) in just years—the company filed for IPO this month.

— Han Xiao
24:17

The THAAD crisis forced an unexpected Korean beauty pivot to global markets

Korean beauty brands that emerged between 2014–2016 initially targeted the Chinese market, where resellers could profit over 200,000 yuan per month. The 2017 THAAD incident abruptly closed China's door, but these young brands—still small enough to be agile—exited China immediately and pivoted to Southeast Asia and Western markets. Ironically, this reversal let them globalize faster than legacy brands already running dozens of stores in China; what could have been a disaster became, in Leng Xiao's phrase, an unexpected alignment of timing, geography, and circumstance.

— Han Xiao
34:23

Three major brands' identical eye patches exposed contract manufacturing dependency risks

In September, Fenty Beauty, Huda Beauty, and Glow Recipe each launched nearly identical ‘slice eye patches’ within the same week, each accusing the others of copying—until it emerged they all sourced their ‘proprietary technology’ directly from the same Korean contract manufacturer. Cosmax (科丝美诗) and Kolmar (科玛) are no longer simple manufacturers; they handle everything from product design to regulatory compliance, making Korean brands astonishingly fast at launching new products. But this structural advantage creates structural vulnerability: product homogenization across brands becomes virtually inevitable.

— Han Xiao
46:34

Korean beauty excels at performing efficacy, not achieving it

Beauty consumers globally demand efficacy, but ‘making it feel effective’and ‘actually being effective’are different things. Chinese brands focused on serious R&D—like Youshiyan (优时颜) with peptide products or Juzi Shengwu (巨子生物) with collagen—may deliver more robust efficacy but struggle to explain it clearly. Korean brands took ‘efficacy theatre’ to an extreme: through ritual-heavy product designs like micro-needle bottles, flight attendant sprays, and jelly patches you twist apart, they create strong visual and tactile feedback. The long-term efficacy median may actually be lower than brands doing rigorous R&D, but the perception wins.

— Han Xiao
1:01:45

K-pop, K-Food, and K-Beauty were written into the same national policy

After the 1997 Asian financial crisis, Korea's government elevated cultural exports to national strategy through the Framework Act on the Promotion of the Cultural Industry. K-Drama, K-pop, K-Food, and K-Beauty were then deliberately synchronized: TV dramas repeatedly featured fried chicken and beer, K-pop idols ‘naturally’ used Korean beauty products in behind-the-scenes variety show footage, and BTS members' use of Mediheal sheet masks and sunscreen once directly boosted sales. These four pillars work together to amplify Korea's overall cultural reach globally.

— Han Xiao
1:22:57

Acquiring Korean beauty brands means buying a windfall, not future capability

L'Oréal's acquisition of 3CE mirrors Estée Lauder's botched play with Dijiyating (蒂佳婷)—the company considered selling it, then bought it back. Both cases reveal the same problem: international conglomerates acquire Korean brands at peak hype, capturing near-term upside rather than sustained growth. Korean brands excel at launching fireworks—constant novelty—but rarely sustain a single long-running product. Once absorbed into a multinational's ESG-compliant, diversity-focused corporate system, they lose the scrappy execution that drove their rise.

— Han Xiao
1:26:40

Chinese beauty brands have deep roots but lack the storytelling canopy

A brand or industry resembles a tree: the unseen roots are R&D, raw materials, and foundational research; the trunk is organization and distribution; the canopy is marketing and brand narrative. Korean brands' strength lies almost entirely in the canopy; their roots and trunk may not be more solid than China's. Chinese beauty has already reached the global first tier in foundational research and efficacy—the gap is in storytelling, global marketing, and distribution systems, all relatively easier to build.
