Ukrainians Are Resisting the War With Poetry and Artifacts, Not Weapons

How the war turned a culture reporter into a language learner, and a prose writer into a poet: Ukrainians resist the war's erasure of identity and memory by rescuing artifacts and writing poems.

Charlotte spent four years deep inside Ukraine and offers a perspective rare in war coverage: culture as a lens for understanding resistance, not decoration. Her firsthand experience makes the conclusion more credible.

2:20

Culture isn't something you look at, it's a lens for seeing the world

Charlotte says that after years of writing about culture, she grew increasingly unsatisfied with merely judging whether a work was good or bad. She came to see culture as a lens for viewing the world, not a thing to be viewed. The idea took shape gradually through 2020-2021. After the full-scale invasion, a Guardian colleague suggested she go cover Ukrainian culture, and she agreed at once. She believed artists would document the war the way they did in World War I, and would leave behind a memory deeper and more lasting than news reporting.

— Charlotte Higgins
4:41

The war isn't on the border, it's in the center of Kyiv

On her first trip to Kyiv, Russian forces had already withdrawn from around the city for several months, and outside the front line everything was calm. Then, on the day she arrived, Kyiv came under its first large-scale cruise missile attack in months. She calls it a "fairly brutal awakening." In that moment she realized this was not a country that merely happened to be at war on its border — the war had returned to the urban center of civilian life, and from then on that became the norm. This personal experience lets readers feel directly what she was reporting on: cultural workers doing their jobs under that threat every day.

— Charlotte Higgins
10:00

Writing poetry isn't lyricism, it's that everyday language ran out

The host notes in a question that many people in the book who wrote prose or drama turned to poetry once the war began. Charlotte agrees and gives two reasons. The practical one: poems are short and can be finished on the march, in the moment; a novel inherently needs time and a place to sit down. The more fundamental one: the shock of the full-scale invasion was so great that everyday language was no longer enough, and poetry is good at reassembling the meaning of fragments. Ukrainian poets told her that in wartime poetry retreats to its most basic forms — elegies for the dead, lullabies for children, curses for the enemy.

— Charlotte Higgins
13:08

Soldiers starting to write long prose is worse news

She says that at a recent Kyiv literary festival she found large numbers of soldiers writing prose and long memoirs, and that this is in fact a sadder sign. Writing a novel or long-form nonfiction takes time, and the war has now dragged on four years, so soldiers finally have time to write long things. This shift in genre is a mirror of the war's protraction, and it shows the war has entered a sustained, destructive state. Poetry was the immediate response; prose is now the delayed sediment.

— Charlotte Higgins
22:15

Once artifacts are lost, the past can no longer be read out of them

She tells of a group of young people at a museum in Lviv whose leader, while moving around at the front, rushed into dangerous areas to rescue artifacts — semi-officially, acting first and reporting later. Charlotte says she found it hard to understand at the time: who would stand in front of a rocket launcher just to save a poet's hat and a few relics? But the answer is that these people would. Her explanation: what makes you feel you belong to a national community is not only intangibles like history and language — objects and artifacts matter too. Objects can be broken, lost, stolen, and once they vanish you can never again draw knowledge from them. They have to exist for the past to keep being understood.

— Charlotte Higgins
27:44

The war doesn't just destroy artifacts, it kills the people who document them

Victoria Amelina was a prominent Ukrainian novelist who switched to working as a war crimes investigator after the war began, and died in a missile strike in July 2023 at just 37. Charlotte recalls meeting Victoria at her first Lviv book fair, when Kyiv was under attack, and the first person to message and check that she was safe was Victoria. Later, when Charlotte wrote a poetry review, Victoria helped her enormously. In her investigations, Victoria discovered that the children's writer Volodia had gone missing, and dug up the war diary he had buried under a cherry tree in his garden. Charlotte suggested turning the story into a piece of reporting, and Victoria agreed. The day Charlotte finished writing it, Victoria was wounded, and never got to read it. This detail shows the personal cost behind the work of cultural preservation.

— Charlotte Higgins
34:31

Not being able to name a Ukrainian writer isn't Ukraine's problem

Charlotte makes a sharp point: the West knows Russian culture inside out — everyone knows Tolstoy, Dostoevsky — but can't name a few Ukrainian novelists, poets or painters. She admits she was the same, until she spent four years deep inside Ukraine. She argues this isn't because Ukraine lacks good culture, but because the tides of history submerged it: in the tsarist and Soviet eras, writing in Ukrainian was severely restricted, Russian was the language of education, entertainment and ambition, and Ukrainian culture was pushed into second place. The cultures of former imperial territories like Georgia and Belarus were likewise pressed down beneath Great Russian culture. The good things are still there, but you have to go looking for them.

— Charlotte Higgins
