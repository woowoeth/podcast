Numerical Control Didn't De-skill Machinists — Average Skill Barely Moved

Since the 1950s, people have feared that numerical control would de-skill machining trades. But Class A machinists and NC operators earned nearly identical hourly wages, and over three decades overall skill fell only about 1%.

AutomationManufacturingSkill ChangeAI CodingEmployment
Worth hearing for anyone tracking how automation rewrites skills and wages: this episode tests a widely believed claim against three data sets — training time, headcount and pay.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:04

A machinist's job isn't just cutting metal

A machinist doesn't just run a machine that cuts metal. They read two-dimensional drawings with designers, reconstruct the three-dimensional part in their head, and spot in advance what can't be made, which is why they need geometry, trigonometry and algebra. What's truly hard to replace is feel: the 1887 book The Complete Practical Machinist says lathe work is most demanding in feed and speed, the most varied, most complex and most deceptive part of the whole trade, requiring judgment, perception and alertness — the tool can sound and look fine while it's already drifting, even producing scrap or injuring someone. An experienced hand, using gauges plus feel, can detect dimensional changes as small as one half thousandths of an inch. Skill here equals judgment, and that is the bullseye of the later de-skilling debate.

3:06

Four years and eight thousand hours to make journeyman

In the first half of the twentieth century, machining was the largest single skilled manufacturing job for American men, and the standard entry path was a formal apprenticeship: generally four years, eight thousand hours; five years and ten thousand hours for tool and die makers. Someone who apprenticed at GE in the 1940s recalled spending the first half year learning basics in the shop, with classes three evenings a week in shop math, college physics, blueprint reading and electricity; then three and a half years rotating through various tools, starting at the drill press and switching about every six months. On finishing, the company certified you a journeyman, gave you a first set of tools or a cash bonus, and a raise — in his words, you were finally qualified for marriage. But it was never the only path: some came up purely on shop experience and self-study at home, and there were courses claiming to teach you how to run a machining shop, which you could call the 1930s version of a coding bootcamp.

6:10

Riveting a boiler: nine skilled men become one

Industrialization repeatedly does the same thing: pack the skill into the machine, then break the work into pieces. When Edison started making light bulbs in 1880, the factory was full of skilled craftsmen who understood glass and wire and knew from scratch how a bulb was made; as specialized machines built their skills in, the work was simplified and segmented, and a few all-round craftsmen were replaced by a large group of workers who each knew one operation. Riveting a boiler took nine skilled men; with a hydraulic riveter it became one skilled man plus eight unskilled ones, the latter doing things like fixturing. In 1883 the young machinist John Morrison testified to Congress that the machining trade had been subdivided from the mid-1870s, and subdivided again after that — in sewing machine work, the jobs were split so finely that a single person didn't count as a machinist at all. NC is just one more step on that timeline.

8:14

Bosses hoping NC would cut workers were disappointed first

NC controls the machine and the workpiece with pre-written instructions, with no need for a machinist to intervene manually: a programmer translates the drawing into coordinates, punches them onto paper or plastic tape, and feeds it into the controller. It was invented in the 1950s, but at first served only the Air Force and aerospace, with specifications far beyond what ordinary shops and small job shops needed. Crucially, early NC was hard-wired, with limited functions, and a major change meant tearing the controller apart and rebuilding it — very inflexible. So shop owners hoping to lower worker skill with it were thoroughly disappointed: a mid-1960s survey by Lawrence Williams and Brian Williams found shops still needed operators with the same skills and technical knowledge, especially right after introduction; owners thought the risk of NC breaking down and causing expensive downtime was too high, and instead assigned their best employees to run the machine. Only once it matured did the advantage show — one NC drill press was estimated to equal three conventional machines.

11:19

Training shrank from five years to half a year

In the 1970s computer technology accelerated NC's spread, and by 1980, 25% to 30% of machine tools sold in developed economies already had NC. The real de-skilling evidence shows up in training time and headcount: a 1982 paper studying Swedish machining shops noted that NC operators needed at most 6 to 12 months of training, while lathe hands spent 4 to 5 years in the shop. In the cases the paper cites, one plant needed only 22 NC operators instead of 44 lathe hands; another replaced 63 qualified machine operators with 21 NC operators. In a developed country like Sweden, skilled workers willing to take night shifts were hard to find, and NC operators accepted stranger hours, so the machines didn't have to stop. As for whether that means lower skill, nearly 80% of Canadian hand-tool machinists surveyed in the 1980s agreed NC required less skill, yet only half of those who had actually run NC saw it that way.

15:21

NC operators earned just twenty-one cents less an hour

Wages are a crude ruler for skill, but the result is counterintuitive. A 1989 study used 1981 US Bureau of Labor Statistics data to compare average pay for 80,000 Class A machinists and NC operators in the machine tool industry: Class A machinists earned $9.72 an hour, NC operators $9.51. For comparison, Class A machinists still earned more than Class B and C machinists doing more repetitive work ($8.54 and $6.41); interestingly, there is evidence some NC operators were recruited from Class B and C machinists, meaning they were actually the ones getting a raise. Of course wages are affected by region, unions and so on, and don't reflect job quality or the number of positions. Bill Bowman is the other side: as more people learned the skills he had, his CNC premium flattened, and a few years later, with layoffs on top, he was making only $20,000 a year.

18:25

Over thirty years overall skill fell only about 1%

Jeffrey Keefe's 1991 study examined three US Bureau of Labor Statistics surveys spanning 30 years and found overall skill levels fell by about 1% — essentially zero. That number directly undercuts the claim that numerical control de-skilled machinists. But the author also stresses that the unchanged average hides changes in the composition of jobs: the work of skilled setup hands, semi-skilled machine operators and unskilled workers changed or disappeared, and other tasks migrated to NC operators, so underneath it was very turbulent. So the more accurate statement is not that skill vanished but that its form changed — deep mastery of one thing was weakened and replaced by several other kinds of skill, such as understanding how the whole machine runs and reconstructing the logic of a failure, which is why operators say you can't just go tear into the fault, you have to work backward to how the accident happened.

19:25

AI coding is replaying this history right now

What the author sees is a parallel between AI coding and machining back then: early GitHub CoPilot and early Cursor only did simple completion or inserted a line, while AI coding systems have now evolved into agentic — something modern CNC machine tools have not achieved. He specifically mentions a case study written by Ryan Lopopolo of OpenAI in February 2026, about a brand-new product built by a team of agents, with humans monitoring and steering. Programming, like machining back then, is one of America's dominant high-paying jobs; some things don't change — programmers still read code and judge where the problem is, it's just that when it's time to fix it you tell the coding agent instead of opening a text editor, the same skills expressed differently. Some jobs will de-skill and decline, new areas may appear, and those are the NC operators of the AI coding era. What he remembers is Bill Bowman: you have to keep learning.

Glossary

Numerical Control (NC)
Controlling a machine tool and workpiece with pre-written instructions, with no need for a machinist to operate it directly by hand.
CNC
The upgraded NC that followed the arrival of microprocessors in the 1970s, with instructions generated and edited on a computer.
journeyman
Someone who has completed a formal apprenticeship and been certified by the company as a qualified tradesman.
agentic
An AI system that can carry out multi-step tasks on its own, rather than just completing a line of code.
