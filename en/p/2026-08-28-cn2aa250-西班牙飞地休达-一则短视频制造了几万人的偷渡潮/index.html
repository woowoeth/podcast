Spanish Enclave Ceuta: A Short Video Creates a Tide of Tens of Thousands of Migrants

What really drives tens of thousands to risk their lives swimming to Ceuta is not Moroccan unemployment, but a short video twisting a Spanish judicial ruling into 'swim over and get a status'; most who land find it's not so and return on their own. Ceuta thus becomes Morocco's border leverage against Spain.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:23

The end of the world can be rewritten as boundless

Look at the map: Ceuta is a peninsula on the Moroccan coast jutting toward the Strait of Gibraltar. Greek myth called the Rock of Gibraltar on the north shore and this mountain on the south the twin pillars Hercules erected at the end of the world. In late Roman times, the city was named Septem Fratres (Seven Brothers) after its seven hills, and the name evolved into today's Ceuta. Medieval Spaniards added a Latin motto to the pillars: non plus ultra, meaning 'no further beyond.' In Charles V's time, an advisor suggested deleting the negative non, leaving plus ultra—'still further beyond'—as the emperor's motto. Removing one negative word reversed how an empire saw the world.

15:48

Barbarians crossing the sea can also become Mediterranean masters

Most Germanic barbarians were poor sailors, but the Vandals broke that rule. In the early 5th century, the Huns' westward push disrupted tribal order; the Vandals crossed the Rhine, traversed Gaul and Iberia, and their leader Gaiseric was especially keen on seafaring. In 429 AD he led his whole people across the Strait of Gibraltar into North Africa. He first besieged Hippo, where St. Augustine was, then established a kingdom centered on Carthage, raiding Sicily and many Mediterranean islands, even sacking Rome in 455. These 'barbarians' fleeing the interior cut off North Africa's grain supply and briefly became masters of the Mediterranean, until finally destroyed by the Byzantine general Belisarius, and the people dispersed.

28:14

Count Julian of Ceuta is the European Wu Sangui

Before the Muslim crossing in 711, a legend circulated about 'Count Julian of Ceuta.' With Byzantium having lost all of North Africa, the commander Julian, holding out in Ceuta, submitted to the Visigothic king Roderic across the strait, sending his daughter as a hostage—but Roderic raped and impregnated her. In a fury, Julian allied with the Muslims, handing over Ceuta as a landing point, and Muslim armies then overran the Visigothic kingdom. The story's truth lacks much documentary support; it's closer to a medieval Iberian moral imagining of 'how the conquest happened': Muslims could cross because Christians opened the door, like Wu Sangui inviting the Qing army through the pass.

41:24

The medieval pinnacle of geography came from a Ceutan's hand

Before the Portuguese arrived, Ceuta was a commercial hub ruled by the Marinid dynasty: wool, grain, and Saharan gold transshipped here, with merchants from Ethiopia, Alexandria, and Syria appearing. The local-born geographer al-Idrisi later went to Sicily, compiling the Book of Roger for the Norman king Roger II and crafting a world map in silver. He wrote that 'the earth is round like a sphere, with water adhering to its surface,' and recorded England's topography and ports and roads across regions. The book's value comes precisely from Sicily's position—Latin, Byzantine, and Islamic knowledge systems met in one court, allowing cross-cultural knowledge to converge.

47:21

Portugal didn't attack Granada because it aimed across the sea

Portugal didn't attack Granada not out of mercy, but because the spoils agreement was already written. By 1249 Portugal had expelled Muslim rule from its southernmost point, completing its Reconquista two centuries before Spain; Granada, the last Muslim territory on the peninsula, was reserved for Castile. So the Portuguese turned expansion toward North Africa, capturing Ceuta on August 21, 1415. Half the Portuguese fleet's ships were foreign; the battle lasted a single day, followed by three days of looting. But Ceuta didn't become a source of wealth: Muslim merchants fled, trade broke, and the city became a garrison and place of exile, described as 'Portugal's Siberia.'

1:13:02

Letting migrants through is also Morocco's leverage

Morocco demands Ceuta's return; Spain says Ceuta was never ruled by Morocco for centuries, and Morocco only became independent in 1956, so it has no claim. When diplomacy fails, Morocco more directly uses the border as a lever: deliberately relaxing controls at the Ceuta boundary, letting unemployed youth try to cross, even urging them 'go go go.' In 2021, some 8,000 people flooded into Ceuta, many of them minors. Moroccan police don't stop those entering but instead block those trying to return, effectively shoving a hot potato into Spain's hands, making Spain bear pressure within the EU, then using that pressure to extract concessions.

1:14:35

Short video turns a judicial ruling into a migration horn

This wave of tens of thousands isn't rooted in economic deterioration but in a distorted law. In July, Spain's Supreme Court ruled that people arriving by swimming or with flotation devices cannot be immediately expelled; they must go through asylum screening. Short-video platforms took the phrase out of context, simplifying it to 'swim to Ceuta and get EU status,' and after viral spread, tens of thousands rushed to the coast. Once ashore, people found the rumor untrue, and many returned to Morocco in large numbers; some drowned in the process. The long-standing backlog of Spain's immigration status issues is evident from the 'amnesty' receiving at least 1.2 million applications—policy misinterpretation is just one visible corner of this vast undocumented population.

Glossary

Reconquista
The centuries-long process by which northern Christian kingdoms of the Iberian Peninsula gradually recovered territory from Muslim rule, starting in the 8th century.
enclave
A territory belonging to one country but located within another; Ceuta is a Spanish enclave in Morocco.
Septem Fratres
The Latin old name of Ceuta, derived from its seven hills, which linguistically evolved into Ceuta.
plus ultra
The imperial motto of Charles V after deleting 'non', meaning 'still further beyond'.
taifa
The dozens of small Muslim states that emerged from the fragmentation of al-Andalus in the 11th century, later becoming pawns in the struggles between northern Christian kingdoms and North African Berber dynasties.
