Bypassing CUDA for inference chips is inevitable, but the SRAM window may not stay open forever

Training is a compute game, inference is a bandwidth game. Groq and Cerebras both bet on SRAM, but one discounts for determinism and the other discounts for yield; OpenAI chose the most expensive HBM4, because what it lacks is power, not money.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:17

Training bets on compute, inference bets on bandwidth

In the training era nobody could make money from training itself, so costs couldn't be quantified and everyone just wanted to bet on the single strongest cluster; in the inference era the math is clear — how much the user pays per million tokens, and how much it costs me to produce those million tokens. More fundamentally it's about compute density: training has massive data to saturate the compute, while the decode phase of inference is strictly autoregressive, producing one token at a time, and every token produced requires reading the entire model from storage into the compute units. So the core tension of inference shifts from compute to memory bandwidth.

— Ziyang
16:39

SRAM is the consensus, but the two companies part ways

Both Groq and Cerebras saw the bandwidth requirement before Transformers appeared, and both converged on SRAM — because SRAM's absolute bandwidth and bandwidth per dollar are two to three orders of magnitude higher than HBM. The price is capacity: DRAM stores one bit with one transistor plus one capacitor, SRAM needs six transistors, so per-chip capacity may be two orders of magnitude lower than HBM. So you have to scale the system up, putting all the model weights across hundreds or thousands of chips, and the bottleneck immediately shifts from capacity to cluster communication and scheduling. Here the two diverge: Groq moves all scheduling ahead into compile time, while Cerebras simply stuffs a whole rack of chips into one wafer.

— Mark
19:34

Groq's static scheduling collides with MoE's dynamism

Groq designs hardware around the compiler, pushing determinism to the extreme, with no runtime scheduling overhead. But after MoE appeared, each token activates 8 of 128 experts, and which ones is decided at runtime, unpredictable at compile time. A static approach has only two options: conservatively send the token to all of them and let some chips idle; or change how the model is partitioned, but today's models are hard to partition cleanly along any dimension other than experts. Idling only affects cost-effectiveness, not the fact that it's still fast, so Groq remains a good choice outside of GPUs.

— Mark
25:18

The price Cerebras pays for yield

The limit on a single die is about 800 square millimeters; a wafer yields thirty or forty dies, and at 50% yield you still get 20 good ones. But for Cerebras the entire wafer is one product: if defects exceed the 30% it can tolerate, the whole wafer is scrap. This isn't mask cost (masks can be amortized), it's that out of every 10 you build, maybe 9 are unusable. So it may achieve 10x Nvidia's bandwidth, but at 3x the cost of HBM, and the bandwidth-per-dollar has to be discounted. Groq discounts for determinism, Cerebras discounts for yield cost, and the discounts all land on token cost in the end.

— Mark
33:58

In the inference era CUDA will definitely be bypassed

TPU and Trainium have zero interest in CUDA compatibility, yet people still find TPU software hard to use — Anthropic can buy them at scale because many of its engineers came from Google and know JAX. The key change is this: in the training era people were willing to pay for good software; in the inference era the scales tip toward performance and cost-effectiveness. As long as it's one hard problem rather than many, writing all of the Transformer's operators on a new chip makes the business case work; add AI coding accelerating operator optimization, and the software-stack barrier drops sharply. DeepSeek would rather write low-level PTX assembly for optimization — that's the same logic.

— Ziyang
44:25

The faster inference runs, the smarter the model gets

After agents exploded, a huge share of tokens aren't read by humans but by other agents; human reading speed tops out at 100 tokens per second. But the point of inference speed isn't faster interaction — it's that in the same one minute of waiting, the model can use 10x more tokens for more internal thinking, and thus be smarter. The x-axis on Jensen's slide is literally Smarter AI, the y-axis tokens per watt. Conversely, some applications that answer quickly may simply be thinking less. Time is the fundamental constraint — ask an AI to judge tomorrow's stock market and if it thinks for two days, the answer is meaningless.

— Ziyang
49:04

Grab the invariants: once FFN becomes MoE, it's stable

Model architecture will always influence chip design — diffusion models, for instance, are compute-dense and suit a different paradigm, so you first make the heterogeneity choice: do this or not. More critical is finding the invariants — within the Transformer, attention is still being reinvented, with every new lab publishing papers proposing new ideas; but the FFN behind it has been a relative invariant ever since it became MoE. RL in post-training also has no fundamental effect on model architecture. So solve the part that has already converged first, and the chip won't be left behind by algorithmic iteration.

— Mark
1:09:40

Everything revolves around locality

Bill Dally's exact words are that computer architecture is like real estate: everything is location, location, location. Temporal locality is that data you've used will probably be used again; spatial locality is that once you use a piece of data you'll soon use the data around it; the CPU cache is the application of both. But in the decode phase of AI inference, algorithmic temporal and spatial locality are both flattened out, so you have to restore locality in hardware at the architecture level — putting model weights in SRAM is essentially buying the best possible locality.

— Mark
1:17:30

OpenAI picked HBM4 because it lacks power, not money

Jalapeño is a general-purpose inference chip that wraps prefill, decode, attention and FFN all into one chip, can even run game demos, and the next generation will support training too. It stacks HBM4 bandwidth to 15.4 TB per second per package, taking the most expensive road. The reason is that what US data centers lack isn't capital or space but power, so it cares intensely about tokens produced per watt, does a lot of work on power, and produces more tokens per megawatt than Nvidia's corresponding Rubin architecture. In China electricity is about 1/3 of cost, in the US about 2/3; different constraints lead to different convergence points.

— Ziyang
1:27:03

Heterogeneity inside the chip, or at the system level

OpenAI proposes the concept of Dark silicon: when one chip does different things, you can shut down or power down some of its parts, spending more cost to buy power efficiency. This differs from Groq's and Cerebras's system-level heterogeneity — the latter is one chip working with other chips, putting the attention KV cache on another chip. SRAM's capacity-per-dollar is poor, making it good for storing fixed model weights but not for a KV cache that keeps growing. So for scenarios where power is the first constraint, OpenAI's approach is great; for scenarios demanding extremely high bandwidth-per-dollar, things will still converge on SRAM.

Glossary

arithmetic intensity
The amount of computation you can do per unit of data read; determines whether a chip is compute-bound or bandwidth-bound.
roofline model
An architectural analysis framework that uses arithmetic intensity to judge whether an application is stuck on compute or bandwidth.
MFU
The ratio of actual compute to theoretical peak, mainly measuring utilization of the matrix multiplication portion.
MBU
The share of memory bandwidth actually put to use; a key metric for high-speed inference scenarios.
Dark silicon
Parts of a chip being shut down or powered down during a given task, in exchange for power efficiency.
Amdahl's Law
Overall system speedup is limited by the unoptimized portion, which explains why a heterogeneous system only gains 10x.
