The main cause of educational inequality isn't school resources — it's family and classmates

The 700-page federal report of 1966 set out to prove that poor schools lack money; the data pointed to a different answer: family socioeconomic background and the class mix inside the classroom are the strongest predictors of student achievement.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

The report's author was himself at the scene of the arrests

James Coleman's son Thomas recalls that on the weekend of July 4, 1963, the whole family went to a segregated amusement park in Maryland — not to have fun, but to be arrested. Staff paired white families with Black families and asked whether you were there to play or to be arrested. The Colemans were taken into custody at an armory; Thomas fell asleep on a bench, while his older brother John stayed awake and went with their parents to be fingerprinted. A few years later, on the same July 4 weekend, the report bearing his father's name was published. That image explains why Coleman took the job: he was not a detached data expert.

9:27

The entire investigation was hidden in a clause nobody read

The Johnson administration had two weapons in hand: the legal authority of the Civil Rights Act, and more than a billion dollars in school funding appropriated by the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. But it did not know how to spend it — at the time, no one really knew what was happening in American schools. The answer was hidden in Section 402 of the Civil Rights Act, a dry little paragraph requiring the Commissioner of Education to report to the President and Congress on the lack of equal educational opportunity in public educational institutions because of race, color, religion or national origin. The clause also set an urgent deadline: deliver the report within two years. In the summer of 1964, the clock started running.

15:40

He asked what the law did not require him to ask

The wording of Section 402 was vague; it did not specify how to measure schools or how to design a study on this scale, so Coleman had a great deal of design freedom. He first measured educational inputs: library holdings, whether there was a science lab, how big the budget was, how many sound projectors. But he went beyond what the law required and measured educational outputs — what actually changed in students during their time in school. He asked students how many people were in their family, how far their parents had gone in school, where the family's money came from, whether anyone had read to them before they started school, and even whether they felt they could control their own success. It was precisely these questions nobody cared about that produced the most explosive findings.

18:50

Six hundred thousand questionnaires, once lost on a train

When the survey was first sent out, it received zero responses, and a student on the team had to write to schools: this is an act of Congress, please call ETS this week. Many schools in the South refused to take part for fear of revealing how deeply segregated they were, and Chicago and Los Angeles also withheld their data. But in the end Coleman obtained questionnaires from six hundred thousand students in grades one, three, six, nine and twelve, sixty thousand teachers, and roughly four thousand schools. Hundreds of boxes of documents traveled by train; one postdoc left a box on a train seat, and the box and Coleman headed in opposite directions, so he had to turn back and chase it. The data ran on a giant mainframe at Princeton, and as the episode puts it: the computing power in your iPhone today is stronger than what he could call on then.

20:57

The government wanted an answer about money; the data did not give it

The Johnson administration expected the data to prove that marginalized groups were being sent into segregated schools, that those schools were badly funded, and that those children therefore scored poorly — and then the segregated system's funding could be cut. Coleman's report confirmed the first half: schools remained segregated nationwide, and Black children lagged several grade levels behind their white peers, which is what is called the achievement gap today. But the report gave a cause no one had anticipated. What truly shaped the academic achievement of students of color and low-income students were two factors: first, the family's socioeconomic background and educational expectations; second, the socioeconomic mix inside the classroom — poor children sitting alongside affluent children for whom college, a high school diploma and honors courses were taken for granted. Student achievement cannot be raised by adding resources alone.

23:01

The most wounding thing in the report was the students' resignation

Coleman's data also revealed a mood of resignation, even despair, among low-income students of color: they did not feel their educational future was something they could influence. No matter how hard they worked or how good their grades were, they did not feel they had much control over their fate. The episode's assessment is that this seems to betray the entire idea of the American dream — if you work hard and follow the rules, you move up; but these children were not living in that world, and they did not expect it to happen to them. This is harder to address than the achievement gap, because it hollows out the promise of education itself.

24:03

The government buried it over Independence Day weekend, and it blew up

The Johnson administration, worried the report would cause controversy, effectively buried it — trying to release it quietly over the July 4 weekend in the hope that no one would notice. Instead the press pounced. The New York Times got Coleman's findings wrong in a short piece headlined "Black Education Found Inferior," saying that predominantly Black schools were inferior to white schools and that this widened the racial achievement gap. Television news followed. The report was described as a Rorschach test: people found in it whatever they wanted to find. Social scientists and local politicians began picking out the conclusions that suited them, using it to tell the story they had already wanted to tell.

25:05

The same data was rejected by conservatives and Black nationalists alike

Conservatives read the report as saying "money doesn't matter" and "schools don't work" — the episode states plainly that this was not at all what Coleman meant, but that reading was commandeered by a large group of people who wanted to cut taxes and stop the federal government from pouring money into schools. Opposition on the other side came from the Black nationalist and community control movements: they pointed out that the report did not consider the confidence and security a Black child might gain in a predominantly Black classroom facing a Black teacher, and they felt the report belittled Black children — Black children do not need white children in order to learn. The same data tore every side apart on the question of race.

31:40

He opposed the forced busing his own data supported

In 1971 the Supreme Court ruled that busing students was a constitutional remedy for achieving racial integration in public schools. Coleman's data was cited in the court's busing orders. But Coleman himself began publicly opposing it almost immediately — not opposing integration, but fearing that busing would not create more opportunity and would instead produce more segregation. In his view, busing would trigger white flight, and that is indeed what happened. In 1975 he published follow-up research explicitly opposing busing, and was subsequently called to testify before Congress. The position was extremely unpopular, his reputation suffered, and some even tried to expel him from the American Sociological Association.

34:49

His method was only a snapshot, and offered no road map

Critics said Coleman was doing "bumper sticker sociology," and Robert Crane of the RAND Corporation was harsher, saying he had set out from the start to advance the cause of private schools. The episode's key judgment is this: what Coleman did was not a scientific experiment that could prove causation; his method was only a snapshot of the system, and it came with no road map telling you where to go. For decades, people believed they knew the answers to questions that were in fact only correlations. This explains why, for decades after the report, education policy swung back and forth between charter schools and the mandatory testing of No Child Left Behind, and never arrived at an answer.

35:53

The report's most harmful legacy is the cult of quantitative testing

The criticism the episode raises is this: one of the more harmful legacies of the Coleman Report was treating quantitative testing as the means of measuring school quality. Using standardized tests to evaluate school quality and to examine individual student performance is a rather reductionist form of measurement; it flattens the idea of what schools should do, reducing schools to places that can produce students who are good at taking tests. And in a democratic society, what we ask of schools should be more than that. This criticism connects to the earlier judgment that the data was only a snapshot: a report born to prove inequality ended up narrowing the purpose of education into a test score.

Glossary

achievement gap
The systematic gap in academic performance between students of different races or classes.
educational inputs
Resource conditions that help students reach learning goals, such as library holdings, laboratories and budgets.
educational outputs
The changes that occur in students as a result of education, which are harder to measure than inputs.
white flight
After forced integration, white families move out of the school district, which in fact worsens racial segregation.
No Child Left Behind Act
A federal education law that sought to close the achievement gap through mandatory standardized testing.
