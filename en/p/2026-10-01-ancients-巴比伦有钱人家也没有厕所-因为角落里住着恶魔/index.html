Even Babylon's wealthy had no toilets—because demons occupied the dark corners

5:11

Babylon exists in clay tablets alone, erased by groundwater from the earth

Hammurabi's Babylon (circa 1770–1750 BCE) was Mesopotamia's business and knowledge center, but archaeological evidence has almost vanished. The city sat on the Euphrates riverbank with extremely high groundwater; when German archaeologists reached that layer in the early 20th century, they found nothing. Scholars reconstruct street layouts from neighboring sister cities like Nippur and Sippar with better-preserved remains. The real evidence for daily life comes not from buildings but from thousands of surviving clay tablets: private letters, marriage contracts, shopping lists—the intimate record that stone cannot leave.

10:19

Beer, not water, kept Babylon alive—low-alcohol and served with every meal

Babylonians did not drink the Euphrates water, which had dead sheep floating in it. Instead they drank beer, especially the low-alcohol variety that was more like porridge and a vital calorie source. A clay-tablet recipe from Yale's collection reads like ‘take one piece of meat, one leek, some peas’—with no cooking times or methods. The period also spawned satirical ‘luxury menus’ that mocked the wealthy: eat donkey feces and sour milk in Kislim month, eat a donkey's rear packed with fly shit in Sabat month. These were jokes about how the rich chased seasonal foods for status.

19:56

Babylonians chose public shame over private darkness: toilet demons decided the outcome

Even wealthy Babylonian homes had no toilets—only baskets that slaves emptied into the street or the Tigris. Urination and defecation happened openly in the street. Babylonians even developed an entire divination system around it: urinating on a doorstep would bring sterility; urinating into irrigation channels risked the weather god's flood-punishment. What surprises is that they willingly defecated in public, seen by neighbors, rather than venture alone into dark corners. Those corners were believed to house toilet demons—creatures that waited in shadows to strike.

— Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones
25:04

Architects, not homeowners, paid when roofs caved in

Babylonian roofs were built from mud brick mixed with straw, laid on cheap timber, and frequently failed. Historical records repeat accounts of roof collapses and people falling from heights. Hammurabi's code explicitly required builders to take full responsibility for repairs and reconstruction if the house proved unsound or the roof or walls collapsed—considered one of the earliest building-quality accountability laws.

— Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones
31:32

Babylonian mothers died delivering their first child—yet families still needed more

Women were expected to bear many children. A proverb said ‘a woman who has borne eight children can finally lie down in peace’. Families ideally contained seven sons. Yet first-delivery mortality ran approximately 60%, and mothers were often only twelve to fourteen years old. Folk belief held that Lamashtu—a demon with a lion's head, covered in hair, nursing dogs and pigs with her hanging breasts—would steal infants and mothers during labor. Pregnant women wore amulets carved with her image for protection.

— Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones
36:49

Taverns and brothels merged into one category—with different legal fees

The boundary between Babylonian taverns and brothels was almost nonexistent. In the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the protagonist's final destination is a tavern at world's end, called ‘the tavern at the edge of the world’, where the owner Siduri serves as a half-divine tavern-girl and prostitute prototype. The law even published a price list: normal intercourse inside a tavern cost half a shekel of silver; quick transactions standing in the street cost half that. But the law also explicitly banned Naditu priestesses from entering taverns—and the ban's very existence suggests people had complained about it happening.

— Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones
42:06

Kings fed the poor for a month; Babylon offered no other welfare

Babylon had no official system of poor relief. The Akkadian language contains no word for charity. Begging was common. The epic *The Poor Man of Nippur* opens with the protagonist's grain store empty and his family starving. The only thing resembling welfare was the king's New Year banquet, which could last twelve days or even thirty, funded by the state to let ordinary people feast. But this looked more like a display of royal justice than a systematic relief program.

— Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones
49:22

Dead Babylonians never left home—the family floor was tomb and ossuary both

Babylon left no large cemeteries. The dead were buried directly beneath the family's house floor in stone chambers or pits. After a few years, old bones were moved aside to make room for the next family member. Babylonians believed the afterlife was only empty darkness ruled by the goddess Ereshkigal, where the dead retained awareness but lost all pleasures of the living. Each new moon, families held a table for the dead—an empty place set at the family meal for the ancestors below the floor. Spirits without memory, without regular offerings, became ghosts that haunted the living.
