Defense contracts foot the bill for American factories—the real winners are robots and medical devices

Defense contracts fund the most expensive startup costs for American factories and mines, and the resulting capacity and manufacturing know-how eventually flow into robotics, energy grids, and consumer products—not just weapons.

2:11

America's hollowed-out industrial base now carries real costs on the battlefield

Aaron Price Wright points out that over the past few decades America's industrial base has been gutted, and this year's multiple conflicts have revealed the real price tag—not just economic, but direct impact on American soldiers and allies in the field. This became the direct trigger for American Dynamism to shift its investment focus from individual defense systems to the entire industrial foundation.

— Aaron Price Wright
5:19

Defense contracting accidentally creates entire new civilian industries

Take DJI: it started in consumer drones, then discovered public safety and defense market demand in the late 2010s and built military production lines; today DJI is one of the world's largest robotics manufacturers, because drone motors and robot actuators are nearly identical in manufacturing. The capacity and expertise built by defense orders eventually feed back into completely unrelated civilian robotics markets.

— Connor Love
7:24

Defense orders subsidize the costliest phase of industrial expansion

Building a new PCB production line requires massive equipment investment. If a defense customer becomes the first buyer, it's essentially subsidizing the company's upfront capital expenditure. The key is that PCB circuit boards aren't just for missiles—robots and hearing aids need the same boards. Defense procurement actually funds the ‘0-to-1’ phase that's most expensive for the entire industry, lowering downstream costs for robotics, automotive, and medical devices.

14:40

Line workers with AI tools can now reprogram factories in 20 minutes

American manufacturing is dominated by ‘high-mix, low-volume’ production; in the past, reconfiguring production lines meant calling system integrators, waiting three months, and spending tens of thousands of dollars. Now the change is this: factory technicians without software engineering backgrounds, relying on intuition about physical production plus AI tools, can reprogram and reconfigure lines themselves—what Claude can do in 20 minutes once took months.

23:11

Hardware companies now dominate the world's most valuable businesses

For ambitious young people, it used to make no economic sense to enter mining, defense, or energy. But Palantir, Anduril, and SpaceX proved that you can build enormous companies in these sectors—today most of the world's most valuable companies manufacture physical products. This change in economics is why this generation's most ambitious founders are rushing into the physical world.

— Connor Love
30:26

Local supply chains raised defect-free rates from 50% to 70-90% in three years

Three years ago, one portfolio company ordering PCBs had to buy double quantities because it expected to scrap half for quality issues; when parts arrived and got tested, nearly 50% were defective. Now new suppliers are reengineering PCB production with software, and the same company's defect-free rate is now 70%-90%. Being close to customers and co-developing with suppliers is systematically lifting quality across the supply chain.

32:29

Supply chain middlemen have avoided innovation for forty years

When Boeing needs parts for the 737, it calls Honeywell, and Honeywell's rational choice is to keep making the old-design landing gear that meets spec—orders and margins don't change whether they use new designs or 40-year-old ones. The problem is that end-system requirements keep evolving while old subsystems stagnate. The opportunity now is to shift supply chain middlemen from passive order-takers to active participants in ‘co-engineering’ system redesigns.

— Connor Love
43:56

Defense venture funding has already exceeded all of last year—energy is just a choice

In the first half of 2026, venture capital flowing into defense has already surpassed all of 2025, closing the ‘valley of death’—the funding gap before government contracts—that used to trap defense startups. But when discussing America's energy production gap with China, Aaron Price Wright argues it's not a technology problem but a matter of whether Americans are willing to build power plants and mines next to their communities; capital and technology will follow where the will goes.

— Aaron Price Wright

Glossary

dual use
A technology or product that serves both military and civilian markets.
valley of death
The funding gap when startups are most likely to run out of cash before securing first major orders.
co-engineering
Suppliers partner with systems integrators in design, rather than passively taking orders.
high mix low volume
Manufacturing many product types in small quantities—harder to automate than mass production.
AD50
a16z's annual list of the 50 companies exemplifying American dynamism.
manufacturing middle
Supply chain layer between component makers and systems integrators that has rarely innovated in recent decades.
