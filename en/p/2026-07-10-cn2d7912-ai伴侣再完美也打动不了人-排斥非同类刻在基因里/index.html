3:10

Whether to take the fidelity drug is really an age question

The show posed a drug that eliminates attraction to anyone but your partner, though it doesn't guarantee lasting attraction to that partner. Guest choices didn't actually depend on the hypothetical scenarios presented—they depended on how many unexplored possibilities each person felt they had left. Sun Shuhong said her 30-something self would refuse, fearing it would prematurely lock down her future; but at 54, she already has limited options to explore, so she'd accept. Age, not the drug's risks, is the real variable that determines the choice.

— Sun Shuhong
26:19

Mid-life partner switching doesn't pay; fishing makes better sense

While discussing the fidelity drug, the conversation shifted to a harder calculus: when two people in their 50s start over, they realize decades of their lives have no overlap, and little time remains to share. The math doesn't work out—fishing or banging your head against trees (amusing yourself alone) makes more financial sense. This explains why guests became more conservative about taking romantic risks as they aged.

— Sun Shuhong
49:25

The real reason to refuse an AI companion: it would spoil you

One guest mentioned an article describing parents who let their children converse with AI for extended periods. The AI affirmed every thought and idea. The parents later regretted it—unconditional praise distorted the children's sense of their actual abilities. The guest worried that living long-term in AI-generated ‘perfect resonance’—where an unfunny joke gets praised as witty—would erode his own grip on reality. This artificial reflection bothered him more than the fact that AI is artificial.

— Wang Jiye
54:28

AI companions lose to genes, no matter how perfect

The scenario offered an AI companion with perfect experience in all respects, its only flaw being that you know it's a machine. Most guests refused anyway. The ‘magnetic field’ explanation, previously dismissed as a joke, was reframed by Luo Yonghao as a serious evolutionary argument: humans carry an animal instinct against non-self species in their genes—something that doesn't disappear just because the other party performs perfectly. The audience vote split roughly 50-50.

— Luo Yonghao
1:00:34

Fewer than one in five would share pain for love

When ‘love’ shifted from abstract to concrete cost, attitudes flipped. If technology could let you and your partner split 50% of negative emotions, only about 20 people in the audience voted yes—far fewer than those who refused. Guests were blunt about why: some admitted their baseline suffering was already lower than their partner's, so mutual burden-sharing would leave them worse off. The gap between ‘willing to sacrifice for love’ and ‘calculated whether you'll lose’ became visible.

1:29:19

Keeping the memory of infidelity is a form of leverage

Faced with technology that could erase infidelity and restore the relationship, most people chose deletion—to stop torturing each other. But Sun Shuhong disagreed. If she were the betrayed party, she wouldn't erase it, because memory itself is a constraint—keeping that remorse makes the other person remember the lesson and correct their behavior. Without the memory, next time temptation came, the partner would think ‘no consequences, why not try again,’ and infidelity would repeat.

— Sun Shuhong
1:58:01

Most people won't abandon a partner for a better match

When the scenario shifted from ‘perfect AI’ to ‘real person in your city, 100% compatibility, one chance only,’ attitudes diverged. The guests said they wouldn't abandon their current partner unless the partner left first—because years of shared history are irreplaceable assets, and switching to a 100-point match incurs the cost of rebuilding memories from scratch. Luo Yonghao quantified it: if the current partner scores around 70, he'd switch; but at 90+, it's not worth the trouble. The older you get and the less time remains, the worse the deal looks.

— Luo Yonghao
