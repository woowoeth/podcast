AI Didn't Kill Jobs — Herding Agents Is the Real Bottleneck

Jia Yangqing, from Caffe to two startups, looks back on fifteen years of AI: model capability is already sufficient, the bottleneck is how to make a swarm of agents collaborate like a human team, and he asserts AI hasn't brought mass layoffs — it's just the scapegoat.

8:19

The industry once declared AI dead

Jia Yangqing first encountered the concept of artificial intelligence in 2005 and 2006 while looking for a graduate research direction, but at the time the entire industry had already declared the field ‘dead’, treating artificial intelligence as a historical concept; the more common terms were machine learning and pattern recognition. He recalls studying automation as an undergraduate and switching to pattern recognition in graduate school, when people believed the wave of artificial neural networks had passed and that more direct mathematical methods were needed to identify cats and dogs. It wasn't until AlexNet appeared in 2012 that AI returned to the center of the economy. This background explains why the later rise of deep learning was seen as such a huge leap.

— Yangqing Jia
17:46

Caffe was born from a GPU in a courier box

During his PhD, because AI compute demands exploded, Jia Yangqing wrote the open-source framework Caffe. It started as a side project, because at the time there was almost no open-source code to reproduce AlexNet. He applied to Nvidia's academic hardware donation program, and they sent him a single GPU, packed in a courier box. Jia says that back then you received the package and assembled the machine yourself. That GPU drastically cut training time from 1 to 2 months on CPU, and also led Nvidia to establish a collaboration with Berkeley. Caffe later became a foundational open-source AI tool, and it gave him his first taste of the positive impact of open source.

— Yangqing Jia
37:56

AlphaGo didn't keep him, pay did

For Jia Yangqing, AlphaGo beating Lee Sedol in 2016 was ‘a pretty big shock’, but his colleagues at Google Brain weren't surprised, because the model's predicted win rate never dropped below 50%. He admits he never imagined at the time that an algorithm for playing Go could upend every industry a decade later. Even so, he chose to leave Google and join Facebook, because Facebook was ‘more chaotic and vibrant’, more like undergraduates getting together to do things. Another very practical reason was ‘pay good’ — he had just had a child and was carrying a mortgage, so a bit more money was nice. This also reflects the pragmatism of Silicon Valley talent flow.

— Yangqing Jia
50:28

What he learned at Alibaba was guts

In 2019 Jia Yangqing joined Alibaba Cloud, heading the computing platform business unit. Alibaba Cloud CTO Xing Tian told him: you can keep deepening your technology, but do you want to try spreading this technology across all industries? Jia believes Alibaba gave a technical expert a stage, and took a big risk. His biggest gain was ‘guts’: at Google and Facebook he was more like a good student producing perfect answers, while at Alibaba he faced problems from ‘half of China's internet’ and had to define the problems himself. The ability to handle complexity that he learned there was later applied directly to his startup, for example helping customers solve supply chain problems rather than just providing a software platform.

— Yangqing Jia
1:05:05

GPT-3.5 turned AI into a generalist

Jia Yangqing recalls that when GPT-3.5 came out in November 2022, it was still a rough prototype and couldn't reliably answer math questions. But the whole industry spent months understanding its capability boundary, and the mindset shifted from ‘there's something to use slowly’ to ‘try it to death’. He says AI used to be ‘general technology, specialized products’, requiring domain knowledge to specialize; after large language models it became a generalist you can interact with in natural language. His startup idea actually came slightly earlier than GPT — he was already talking with partners in September and October — but the AI inflection point amplified demand for a new kind of cloud and GPU systems.

— Yangqing Jia
1:28:38

Don't copy big-tech frugality when you start up

Lepton AI became profitable in its second year, and Jia Yangqing was very proud, but in hindsight he reflects that he could have been more aggressive. He brought the big-tech mindset of building a ‘solid business’ to his startup: valuing profitability, idle efficiency, saving money. But the task of a startup isn't profitability, it's growth; when the rocket is taking off you shouldn't be calculating fuel efficiency. He says the untold context of decisions differs between big tech and startups — for example, idle resources are a huge waste in big tech, but in a startup with rapid business growth, idle resources quickly get filled. This reflection resonates with many people who leave big tech to start companies.

— Yangqing Jia
1:37:56

A bunch of agents isn't a team, it's a gang

Jia Yangqing's motivation for his second startup came from seeing that a single agent is already smart enough, but multiple agents without a good coordination mechanism are an ‘agent gang’ rather than a team. AI agents slack off like humans: for instance, if they can't iterate to a result they suggest recording the requirement as a to-do item, and the review agent will also say ‘you have a point’. He believes a verifiable mechanism is needed, such as validating with business systems or simulated environments, rather than letting AIs chat with each other. What his new company wants to build is the organizational relationships between AIs, letting people define ‘what is wanted’ and ‘what counts as done’, and leaving ‘how to do it’ to the agents. This may be the direction that breaks through in the next 6 to 12 months.

— Yangqing Jia
2:04:51

In the layoff wave, AI is the scapegoat

Facing the social backlash against AI, Jia Yangqing's view is somewhat unwelcome: AI has not brought mass unemployment the way the media portrays it — it's mostly speculation. He gives the example of a company like Brex (which does SRE) announcing layoffs because of AI more than half a year ago, but the more fundamental reason was that it pushed into crypto and doubled its headcount during the pandemic, and is now paying for earlier mistakes, with AI conveniently serving as a ‘plausible reason’. He also says any organization will find reasons for itself, and AI has become the scapegoat. He admits AI practitioners do pay relatively little attention to trust issues, and Silicon Valley's arrogant posture has intensified the antagonism, but positive changes exist too, such as electronic payments increasing trust between people.

— Yangqing Jia
