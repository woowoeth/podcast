Former NASA executive: current lunar landing plan is too complex, 2028 is fundamentally unrealistic

Doug Cooke argues that the lunar lander relies on unverified cryogenic propellant orbital transfer and a tall, narrow structure, each reducing mission success probability, while China's simplified plan more likely reaches the Moon first.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:18

Single lunar landing needs ten to twenty-plus launches—simply not credible

Cooke flatly states that the current Artemis architecture requires ten to twenty-plus rocket launches just to deliver the lunar lander to lunar orbit. ‘That's simply not credible to me.’ He notes that SLS and Orion are already proven, but NASA hasn't used SLS as originally designed. Instead, all complexity has been dumped into the lander and orbital refueling—opposite to the direction he and colleagues studied over thirty years. China's approach, by contrast, aligns more closely with those original research conclusions.

— Doug Cooke
23:19

The lunar lander's cryogenic fuel transfer has never been validated at scale

Both contractor teams need to transfer large quantities of cryogenic propellant—liquid oxygen, methane, and others—in lunar orbit to give their landers enough thrust to leave orbit and reach the Moon. These fuels evaporate continuously, so the refueling spacecraft must be resupplied repeatedly and quickly before losses mount. Large-scale cryogenic fuel transfer has never been validated before. Cooke says he's been raising this since congressional testimony in 2019; now that landers are selected, the problem is more concrete.

— Doug Cooke
26:39

A lunar lander over 50 meters tall: like standing a pencil on the Moon

Cooke points out the new lander exceeds 50 meters—an elevator rises roughly 30 yards. Successful past landers—Apollo, prior robotic ones—were squat, low center of gravity, wide footprint: drop a foot into a crater and they stay upright. This tall, narrow design has extremely high center of gravity; lean beyond the footpads and it topples. ‘Like balancing a pencil upright on a table.’ The Moon's south pole is already jagged, shadowed, and its regolith properties remain poorly mapped.

— Doug Cooke
28:44

Returning to Apollo-style storable fuel design would actually work

Cooke's alternative—Plan B—abandons cryogenic fuel for storable propellants used in Apollo, paired with a shorter, wider lander matching the SLS cargo bay diameter. No reusability required, no new technology invented, just existing techniques. He concedes this approach has lower reuse potential and might not hit 2030, but within that timeframe it would actually be feasible.

— Doug Cooke
33:58

More launches and critical operations means lower overall mission success rate

Cooke notes each critical operation—orbit insertion, fuel transfer, rendezvous, docking—carries its own success probability. Multiply them together: more operations mean lower overall success. He uses Artemis II as example: even a relatively simple mission demands near-perfect execution on every orbit correction. The current lunar architecture demands far more critical operations than before, further degrading overall mission success.

— Doug Cooke
42:07

Human-scale Mars entry and landing is a technology humanity hasn't solved

Cooke admits that human-scale Mars Entry, Descent, and Landing (EDL) is a major unproven technology. Mars has a thinner atmosphere than Earth but still needs a heat shield; past robotic landers were much smaller and already pushed systems to their limits. A crewed landing vehicle is vastly larger. ‘This isn't a detail, and it's not a scale we've accomplished before.’

— Doug Cooke
46:13

NASA's engineering talent is hemorrhaging, decisions shift to approve-then-fix

Cooke says new administrator Jared Isaacman has acknowledged NASA's capabilities have declined. The problem is decision-making has become top-down—decide first, fix later—rather than grounded in solid engineering data. The so-called ‘commercial’ model also denied engineers the deep design participation they had during Space Shuttle and Space Station eras. This drove talented engineers out in last year's buyout wave. ‘They didn't see a path forward, so they left.’

— Doug Cooke
51:16

Artemis III rendezvous without docking is just wasting irreplaceable hardware

Cooke notes Artemis III plans Orion rendezvous with the lander, but he's heard little about ‘docking.’ Rendezvous itself will be verified in Artemis II with the upper stage. If Artemis III just repeats rendezvous without docking, ‘you're burning through precious hardware.’ The real leap—verifying cryogenic fuel transfer and absorbing the major probability hit—actually falls on the next phase, Artemis IV.

— Doug Cooke

Glossary

EDL (Entry, Descent, and Landing)
The process of a spacecraft slowing through atmosphere and safely landing on a planetary surface.
cryogenic fuel transfer
Pumping extremely cold, volatile liquid propellant from one spacecraft to another in orbital space.
new space / commercial
Procurement model where NASA buys completed services from private companies rather than providing deep design oversight.
