Africa Doesn't Lack Resources, It Lacks Population Density

Dangote built his fortune on a monopoly concession and spent $20 billion of his own money on a Nigerian refinery to become Africa's richest man, but Studwell argues the variable that decides whether Africa can replicate Asia's miracle is the urban market size created by population density — not minerals or aid.

5:10

He traded four years of monopoly for a factory, and beat the Swiss giants

Aliko Dangote came from a Muslim merchant family in Kano and started out in soft commodities trading. Under President Obasanjo he struck a deal with the government: in exchange for a four-year monopoly on cement imports, he would build and operate a plant locally, at a time when Nigeria produced no cement of its own. He did it, then replicated the model across more than a dozen African countries, beating Swiss multinational cement giants like Holcim — the first signal that he was someone to reckon with.

— Joe Studwell
6:11

The refinery the government couldn't build, one businessman built

The Nigerian government had tried several times to build its own refining capacity and failed every time. Dangote spent $20 billion to build and successfully operate a giant refinery east of Lagos, making him Africa's richest man. He promptly built a urea fertilizer plant next door, which now supplies most of Nigeria's fertilizer consumption, and has signed on to replicate the same project in Ethiopia, Africa's second most populous country. The IPO is expected to be heavily oversubscribed, because it is the first time investors can buy a genuinely high-quality listed industrial asset on the African continent.

— Joe Studwell
9:12

Africa looks resource-driven because there was nothing else

Facing the charge that this is a resource curse, Studwell counters that minerals and oil and gas actually make up a far smaller share of Africa's GDP than outsiders assume; it only looked resource-dominated because other economic activity was absent. Dangote's business is fundamentally demand-driven: population and population-density growth are creating real urban markets, so the demand for cement, refined fuel and fertilizer is far larger than it used to be.

— Joe Studwell
10:12

Africa's growth was weak because people were too few and too scattered

The continent now has 1.5 billion people, a population density roughly equal to Asia's in 1960; at the end of World War II Africa had only 220 million people, a density comparable to Europe in 1500. Studwell sees this as the root cause of past weak growth: the urban market determines who buys what you produce, the per-capita affordability of infrastructure depends on population size, and concentration of people also brings finer division of labour and more creativity — the fact that 80% of global GDP comes from cities is the proof.

— Joe Studwell
20:36

Africa's most capable actor isn't the government, it's the private sector

Unlike East Asia's developmental-state model, African governments are generally weak in capacity, and the private sector is often the most capable actor — Dangote being the prime example. Studwell's reason for optimism is that rising population density will bring bigger cities and more tax revenue, which in theory should gradually strengthen government capacity, but he concedes this remains an expectation to be tested, not an established fact.

— Joe Studwell
27:46

China builds factories in Africa to sell locally, not to export

To the worry that robots and AI will make Africa miss the manufacturing window, Studwell counters with data: last year Chinese manufacturing FDI into Africa reached $12.5 billion, for a straightforward reason — margins in Chinese domestic manufacturing are already thin, while producing in Africa for local consumption yields higher margins. Steel, for instance, sells for $500 to $600 a ton in China but around $1,000 in Africa, which explains the Chinese steel plants that have gone up in Zimbabwe, South Africa, Algeria and Ethiopia one after another. He also notes that robots are sunk cost and inflexible, whereas labour at $60 a month in Madagascar can be hired or fired at any time — flexibility that beats robots when demand fluctuates.

— Joe Studwell
30:50

India bet on services and lost to China's bet on factories

Manufacturing can pull people out of the agricultural economy into the modern urban economy with a relatively low education threshold; the factory itself is a vocational school, and the whole process is affordable for a poor country. High-value-added services demand far more education spending, which neither poor-country governments nor individuals can afford. Studwell cites India: even with the IITs underpinning its IT industry, that sector employs only about 6 million people, and India has grown 4.2% a year on average since the 1991 reforms, while China, which went all in on manufacturing, grew 10% a year over the past three decades. He puts the question to Nigerians: do you want to be India, or do you want to be China?

— Joe Studwell
42:10

In a land-for-everyone Africa, landless farmers are appearing

After World War II East Asia was extremely land-scarce, which directly drove the Chinese Communist Party to seize power through land redistribution and pushed the US to promote land reform in Japan, South Korea and Taiwan. Africa is the opposite: sparse population means almost everyone has land, and the only case of land reform among Africa's 55 countries is the overthrow of the feudal Omani elite in Zanzibar. But with agricultural growth above 4%, the fastest in the world in 25 years, wealthy urbanites have begun buying or renting land and squeezing out smallholders, and for the first time in African history a class of landless farmers is emerging.

— Joe Studwell

Glossary

flying geese paradigm
The development theory that East Asian industries shift in sequence to lower-cost countries and upgrade rung by rung.
resource curse
The phenomenon where abundant natural resources actually drag down long-term economic development.
developmental state
A model in which the government directs capital allocation and industrial policy to drive economic development.
kulak
Borrowed from the book to describe Africa's emerging class of wealthy urbanites who buy land and squeeze out smallholders.
demonstration effect
The policy and psychological chain reaction triggered by seeing a neighbouring country succeed at development.
