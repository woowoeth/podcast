Good Inside with Dr. Becky 2026-10-27

What kids need most isn't parents—it's an outsider who won't judge them

She's not a parent, but without the daily drain of child-rearing, she can offer the unconditional stable presence that parents struggle to maintain—and kids, in turn, increasingly prove worthy of trust.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:01

Those without custody duties have more capacity for consistent presence

Chelsea cares for three unrelated girls who call her "dad" rather than "auntie." She explains that as a single woman without children of her own, she has more "bandwidth" to notice what these girls are missing in their original families and consistently fill that gap.

— Chelsea Handler
9:21

Show up when kids are angry, don't argue back

In a parenting class, Chelsea learns a core rule: never confront a child at the height of their emotion. Whether they're screaming or cursing, the only thing the adult should do is tell them this must be hard, and that you're not going anywhere. This is neither punishment nor reward—just presence.

— Chelsea Handler
11:24

Don't use companionship as a tool for punishment or reward

Chelsea initially wanted to motivate kids with "good grades means we travel," but a therapist quickly corrected her: food, safety, and companionship shouldn't be tied to behavior. Instead, they should be like a marble jar—you keep steadily adding to it, and even if a child messes up once, the trust you've already deposited won't drain all at once.

— Chelsea Handler
14:35

Take responsibility first; accountability and shame will follow naturally

A newly licensed driver scrapes the neighbor's car—entirely because Chelsea forgot to close the door—but the girl breaks down in fear of punishment. Chelsea immediately tells her it wasn't her fault and goes knock on the neighbor's door to apologize. Months later when something similar happens, the girl walks over to explain to the neighbor herself—Chelsea sees this as evidence that the child's fear of authority has been quickly repaired.

— Chelsea Handler
28:02

Trust a teen completely, and they'll regulate themselves

Chelsea keeps cannabis and mushrooms at home that need to be secured when kids visit. She gradually stops monitoring a 16-year-old boy and lets him stay unsupervised. One time she comes home to find he's discovered an improperly hidden cannabis gummy and $200 in cash, left them untouched with a note saying he figured she wouldn't want these things around the kids.

— Chelsea Handler
30:09

Adolescent trust accumulates in moments that seem to mean nothing

Becky points out that parenting teenagers isn't an instant-gratification game—the times you stay quiet in the kitchen waiting for them to come out of the shower may seem pointless, but kids are quietly noting whether you're consistently there. When a child suddenly wants to talk about something deep at 11:30 p.m., it's because you've been in the kitchen several times before, asking nothing.

— Dr. Becky
33:15

Assume kids are good inside, and good behavior unlocks itself

The core premise of their discussion is this: assuming kids have generosity and responsibility built in is the starting point for changing how you interact with them. If you first assume kids are fundamentally bad and require punishment to "install" good qualities, that approach has never actually worked in human history. Chelsea adds that sometimes you have to tell kids they're amazing before they grow into it.

40:32

Name what kids do well; don't fixate on problems

Chelsea believes you need to deliberately practice reminding kids of concrete strengths like "you're great with animals" or "you're skilled at making bracelets"—it's a habit that needs feeding. No child doesn't want to hear good things about themselves. At the end, Becky says inspired by this, she's going to tell each of her children one concrete thing she genuinely admires about them today, even if they roll their eyes.

— Dr. Becky
