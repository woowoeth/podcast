British misjudgement lit the fuse in Northern Ireland: the Provisional IRA meant to fight all along

A run of British government misjudgements in the early 1970s — the Falls Curfew, the refusal to send more troops, indiscriminate internment — turned the Provisional IRA from a fringe armed group into the central actor of a thirty-year conflict, and radicalised British soldiers along the way.

The Provisional IRA was born of a split over strategy, not community defence

At a secret meeting in Belfast in December 1969, a group of veteran IRA members unhappy with the movement's Marxist leadership joined forces with younger radicals and decided to break away, founding the Provisional IRA. They argued for achieving a united Ireland by force, parting ways with the Official IRA and its case for political struggle. The armed wing rose fast in the early 1970s and became the central actor in the thirty years of conflict that followed. What is worth noting is that it was not a spontaneous neighbourhood defence organisation but a group of veterans with explicit political aims and years of armed experience, actively choosing the path of violence.

14:32

Civilian deaths were a price the founders had already calculated

Tom insists that the Provisional IRA cannot be described as a defensive organisation. Its founding leaders — Mac Stíofáin, Brady and others — were long-standing IRA members with years of struggle behind them, explicitly committed to unification through violence, and openly acknowledged that this would cause civilian casualties while holding that the price was worth paying. This account breaks the narrative popular at the time that the IRA was forced into resistance, and it explains why any government crackdown only strengthened the organisation's legitimacy: it had already decided to fight.

— Tom
22:08

The curfew and house searches did the IRA's recruiting for it

In June 1970 the newly installed prime minister, Heath, decided not to ban the Orange Order parades — a choice one British official called the worst misjudgement he had seen in his life. Violence duly broke out, and the Provisional IRA made its first appearance. On 3 July the British Army imposed a curfew and large-scale house searches on the Falls area of Belfast; weapons were seized, but the brutality of the operation sharpened the community's hostility and delivered a flood of new recruits to the IRA. This became the turning point in the escalation of the conflict: in the name of maintaining order, the Army in effect completed the insurgency's recruitment campaign.

— Tom
33:04

Most Britons wanted withdrawal; Heath preferred to let soldiers die

In February 1971 the IRA member Billy Reid shot dead Robert Curtis, a 20-year-old British soldier, with a Sterling submachine gun — the first British soldier killed in the Troubles. Curtis left a pregnant wife, and his father said he did not know what his son had died for. Polling showed that most people in Britain supported withdrawal, but Heath's cabinet judged that pulling out would bring international condemnation and sectarian slaughter, and refused. The decision let the situation slide further into the abyss, and it pinned the British government to a position of preferring dead soldiers to a political settlement.

— Tom
40:29

Escalation did not come only from the IRA; British soldiers were radicalised too

A Scottish working-class Catholic soldier describes in his memoir the silence that fell over the barracks as news of these killings came in. He looked at the faces of the veterans around him and read the same emotion in all of them: wait until it's our turn. This marks the point at which the British Army, too, began to radicalise. That mood created pressure on the British government: rank-and-file anger would translate into action, which would in turn provoke the IRA further, producing a spiral of violence. Tom uses this first-hand account to show that the escalation was not driven by the IRA alone — the psychological shift inside the Army mattered just as much.

— Tom
46:53

The biggest killer was not the loyalist militias but the IRA

The Ulster Defence Association (UDA), founded in the autumn of 1971, was the largest loyalist self-defence organisation, with 25,000 to 40,000 members at its peak and a women's unit as well. Over the course of the conflict the UDA committed more than 400 murders, almost all of Catholic civilians. But Dominic points out that the IRA killed far more people than that, making it the single biggest killer of the entire conflict. The point breaks the simple narrative that the IRA was the only violent party, and it shows that the Troubles were a two-way sectarian war rather than a straightforward anti-colonial struggle.

— Dominic
1:05:00

Arresting only Catholics amounted to recruiting for the IRA

Operation Demetrius arrested 342 people, all of them Catholics and not one a Protestant. Maudling later argued that a Protestant organisation behaving as the IRA did would have been treated the same way, but the fact was that the UVF had already begun killing. Because the intelligence behind the operation was out of date, it arrested the wrong people and made the conflict worse. Dominic notes that such a blatant imbalance made the Catholic community feel systematically discriminated against, and became the best recruiting advertisement the IRA could have had. Internment was a failure both militarily and morally, and it drove the violence straight onward.

— Dominic

Glossary

The Troubles
The sectarian and political violence in Northern Ireland from roughly 1968 to 1998, with the British government fighting republican and loyalist paramilitaries.
Operation Demetrius
The British Army's 1971 programme of preventive internment in Northern Ireland, which arrested 342 Catholic suspects on intelligence so poor that it inflamed the conflict instead.
UVF
The Ulster Volunteer Force, a loyalist paramilitary organisation founded in 1966 to fight republicans by violence, responsible for numerous murders.
