Britain's Miscalculations Lit the Northern Ireland Conflict: The Provisional IRA Meant to Fight, It Wasn't Forced Into Rebellion

A series of British government miscalculations in the early 1970s — the Falls Curfew, refusing to send more troops, indiscriminate internment — let the Provisional IRA swell from a fringe armed group into the protagonist of a thirty-year conflict, while British soldiers were themselves radicalised by the killing.

The Provisional IRA was born of a split, not community defence

In December 1969, at a secret meeting in Belfast, a group of veteran IRA members dissatisfied with the Marxist leadership and younger radicals decided to split and found the Provisional IRA. They advocated achieving Irish unity by force, parting ways with the Official IRA, which favoured political struggle. This armed wing rose rapidly in the early 1970s and became the protagonist of the next thirty years of the Northern Ireland conflict. Notably, it was not a spontaneous grassroots defence organisation, but a deliberate choice of the violent path by veterans with clear political goals and years of armed experience.

14:32

Civilian casualties were a cost the founders had calculated

Tom stresses that the Provisional IRA cannot be described as a defensive organisation. Its founding leaders, such as Mac Stíofáin and Brady, were long-standing IRA members with years of struggle behind them, who explicitly advocated achieving unity through violence and openly acknowledged it would cause civilian casualties, but considered the cost worth it. This account breaks the then-popular narrative that ‘the IRA was forced into rebellion’, and also explains why any government crackdown only strengthened its legitimacy — because it was going to fight anyway.

— Tom
22:08

The curfew and searches did the IRA's recruiting for it

In June 1970, the newly installed Prime Minister Heath decided not to ban an Orange Order march, which British officials called ‘the most serious miscalculation I have seen in my life’. Conflict followed, and the Provisional IRA made its first appearance. On 3 July, the British Army imposed a curfew and mounted large-scale searches in the Falls area of Belfast; although weapons were seized, the heavy-handedness inflamed community antagonism and brought the IRA a large number of new recruits. This became the turning point in the escalation of the Northern Ireland conflict: in the name of ‘maintaining order’, the British Army in effect did the recruiting for the insurgent organisation.

— Tom
33:04

Most Britons wanted withdrawal; Heath preferred soldiers to die

In February 1971, IRA member Billy Reid shot dead 20-year-old British soldier Robert Curtis with a Sterling submachine gun — the first British soldier killed in the Northern Ireland conflict. Curtis left behind a pregnant wife, and his father said he ‘did not know why his son died’. Polling showed most Britons supported withdrawal, but Heath's cabinet believed withdrawal would bring international condemnation and sectarian massacre, and refused. That decision let the situation slide further into the abyss, and pinned the British government to the position of ‘preferring soldiers to die rather than reach a political settlement’.

— Tom
40:29

Escalation came not only from the IRA; British soldiers were radicalised too

A Scottish working-class Catholic soldier described in his memoir how the barracks fell silent on hearing of these killings. Looking at the faces of the veterans around him, he read the same emotion: ‘wait till we get over there’. This marked the beginning of the British Army's own radicalisation. That mood put pressure on the British government: the anger of ordinary soldiers would turn into action, which in turn further provoked the IRA, forming a spiral of violence. Tom uses this firsthand account to show that escalation in the conflict was not driven by the IRA alone; the psychological shift among British soldiers was equally crucial.

— Tom
46:53

The biggest killer was not the loyalist militia, it was the IRA

The Ulster Defence Association (UDA), founded in the autumn of 1971, was the largest loyalist self-defence organisation, with 25,000 to 40,000 members at its peak and even a women's unit. During the conflict the UDA carried out more than 400 murders, almost all of Catholic civilians. But Dominic points out that the IRA killed far more than that, and was the biggest killer in the entire conflict. This breaks the simple narrative that ‘the IRA was the only violent party’, and shows that the Northern Ireland conflict was a two-way sectarian war, not a straightforward anti-colonial struggle.

— Dominic
1:05:00

Arresting only Catholics amounted to recruiting for the IRA

Operation Demetrius arrested 342 people, all Catholics, with zero Protestants. Maudling later argued that if a Protestant organisation had acted like the IRA it would have been treated the same way, but the fact was that the UVF had already begun killing. The operation, working from outdated intelligence, seized the wrong people and instead intensified the conflict. Dominic points out that this glaring imbalance made the Catholic community feel systematically discriminated against, and became the best propaganda for IRA recruitment. The internment operation was a failure militarily and morally, and directly drove the violence to continue escalating.

Glossary

The Troubles
The sectarian and political violence in Northern Ireland from roughly 1968 to 1998, pitting the British government against republican and loyalist paramilitary organisations.
Operation Demetrius
The British Army's preventive internment operation in Northern Ireland in 1971, arresting 342 Catholic suspects; the intelligence failure instead intensified the conflict.
UVF
A loyalist paramilitary organisation founded in 1966, which fought republicans with violence and carried out numerous murders.
