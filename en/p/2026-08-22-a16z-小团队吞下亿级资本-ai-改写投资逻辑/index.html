A 20-Person Team Burns $2 Billion: AI Rewrites the Ceiling on Capital Efficiency

AI lets a 20-person team spend $2 billion efficiently, and venture capital shifts from zero-sum to strategic control points; the frontier labs' financing advantage will eventually fade, and the application layer captures more value.

Adding people can't accelerate, but adding money now can

Martin gives the example of a famous multimodal model that everyone uses: the team is about 20 people, and the cost exceeds $2 billion. Ten years ago, getting $1 billion just meant hiring a huge number of people and screwing it up; now teams know how to turn capital directly into capability and growth. This is capital efficiency unprecedented in the history of engineering, and it means the ceiling on venture capital's scale has been redefined. The old ‘Mythical Man-Month’ held that adding people can't accelerate things, but in the AI era capital can directly buy compute and data, letting small teams match enormous sums of money.

10:33

Early investing looks at stack position, not financials

Early-stage investing can't look only at the balance sheet, gross margins and churn. Martin points out that if you ignore strategic position in the technology stack, you'll miss companies that can command enormous value-transfer power even while losing money. A company may be a key control point in the future stack, and whether it monetizes on its own or gets acquired, it will generate enormous returns. This is the core lens for understanding the Cursor and OpenRouter deals, and the dividing line between early-stage investing and late-stage financial thinking.

— Martin Casado
11:41

On whether labs take all, both sides have equally strong arguments

Martin distinguishes two paths: the labs win everything, or they don't win. In favor of a sweep: over the past three years they've held 95% of the market, raised more than the entire downstream combined, have pricing power as long as they stay a notch ahead, a self-catalyzing effect that widens the gap, and control of supply. Against: the application space keeps expanding into areas that require services and deep customer connections, the open-source model ecosystem is maturing, and cheap capital and GPU supply will eventually rationalize. He states plainly that both sides have strong arguments.

— Martin Casado
16:09

By dollars the labs win; by tokens the long tail wins

Martin offers a specific guess: supply constraints ease around 2028; the big labs still take 80% of the market on a dollar-weighted basis, because that's how historically large companies have worked; but on a token-weighted basis, 60% will belong to the long tail and open source. That means fragmentation at the model layer will be higher than people think, and application companies will start eroding the labs' profit share. He cautions that this isn't a conclusion but his ‘complete wild guess’.

— Martin Casado
17:19

OpenRouter's value isn't routing, it's aggregating the long tail

OpenRouter looks like an API router on the surface, but it's actually a two-sided marketplace: developers get a unified entry point, visibility and analytics, while model providers get distribution and demand. Its value lies in aggregating long-tail supply, not in intelligent routing. Martin says intelligent routing is an AI complete problem, and the gains actually deployed today are mainly cost-performance optimization — cutting token consumption while maintaining quality.

— Martin Casado
25:00

A $200 plan gets resold for $20

Martin discloses that highly savvy operators in China subscribe to a $200-a-month model plan, burn through the tokens within three days, cancel the subscription, get a prorated refund for the remaining 27 days, and then resell the full service to others for about $20. This kind of arbitrage reflects how subsidies from the leading models get captured by gray markets, and it also shows that marketing spend is turning into a precisely calculable financial decision — the CMO's job has become the CFO's job.

— Martin Casado
28:11

$60 billion for Cursor isn't a premium

SpaceX acquired Cursor for $60 billion, the largest independent private M&A deal in history. Martin thinks the standalone valuation is worth that, because it would be easy to raise money at that valuation. Cursor has data, product and distribution; SpaceX has compute and resources; both sides agree that code is the path to general intelligence, and together they're worth more. He stresses that this is two companies added together, not a single asset.

— Martin Casado
34:22

It's not one layer taking all, it's every layer growing value

Martin argues that it's not one layer eating all the value now — applications, models, inference and media are all creating value. This is the biggest wealth unlock he's seen in his career, far beyond the 1990s. He advises founders to stop thinking zero-sum, to stop worrying excessively about moats, and instead to think about what is strategic in the new world. This judgment directly explains why a16z is willing to pay a premium for strategically loss-making companies.

Glossary

autocatalytic effects
Using AI tools to accelerate AI's own R&D, creating a positive loop between capital and capability.
AI complete
A problem that can only be solved once general artificial intelligence is reached, such as model routing.
token path
The intermediate layers that tokens flow through during model inference, such as API gateways — a strategic chokepoint.
two-sided marketplace
A platform connecting the supply side of models with the demand side (developers/users).
founder-market fit
When a founder's experience and endowments align closely with what the target market needs — a core investment criterion.
