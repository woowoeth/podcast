Lowbrow aesthetics aren't designed—they naturally emerge from poverty

He explains lowbrow aesthetics not as a style strategy but as something naturally weathered into being by poverty—the way ancient Greek temple columns fade and become beautiful over centuries.

5:01

Poverty in his childhood taught him to welcome loss

Ren Ke (仁科) recalls being a "rich local kid" until elementary school, when his father's businesses—a karaoke bar, hair salon, and tea restaurant—failed in sequence. His own grilled meat stall folded within two months after he kept giving free food to friends. This boom-and-bust pattern shaped his later "unambitious" creative mindset: he never expected music to earn money or make him a rock star, just enjoyed making it.

— Ren Ke
1:02:24

Picture someone always too late to be born, and panic fades

Around age 17, Ren Ke conceived of a fictional person named Mr. X (X先生)—someone who always missed every era, forever waiting to be born, never arriving even before the universe ended. He applies this thought experiment to himself: perhaps he's the person who "never gets an album out," but that's not failure, just not his turn yet. This coherent worldview let him spend years selling books and singing in Guangzhou with zero commercial expectations while maintaining emotional stability.

— Ren Ke
1:04:24

No sales training, but he made over 100 yuan a day

After arriving in Guangzhou to meet A Mao (阿茂), Ren Ke made money selling pirated books—clearing over 100 yuan on day one, faster than A Mao's bootleg CD business. He refused A Mao's pirated records and tried selling ties but found it "more shameful than selling pirated books." He describes himself as "overconfident" and "lucky," even reserving stall space for philosophy by Freud and Nietzsche that barely sold, reasoning that "someone eventually buys everything."

— Ren Ke
1:33:54

They pushed the unplugged format to its logical extreme

During their record shop era, they invited Zhou Yunpeng (周云蓬)—who was accepting an award—to play an "unplugged" set. Zhou expected typical unplugged: speakers but no effects. Instead he arrived to find they literally had no PA system, only speakers for playback—genuine a cappella plus acoustic guitar. Zhou nearly fell apart but still performed and sold albums. This poverty characterized their early tours: sound equipment stolen by audience members to charge phones, PA and monitor mixed on one channel, lights never changing once all night.

— Ren Ke
1:53:35

Aesthetics don't get installed—they're naturally worn into being

Luo Yongzao (罗永浩) proposes a theory: humans don't initially see peeling paint and cracks as beautiful—that was stuff needing immediate repair. But after decades of weathering accumulate, people suddenly notice rough surfaces have intrinsic beauty. Ancient Greek temple columns started painted but only became the gray-white we know today after centuries of finish loss. Ren Ke agrees and adds that plastic buckets and salon signs have "touch" with his real experience—authentic lived texture, not a style strategy grafted afterward.

— Luo Yonghao
2:00:36

One forgotten poster becomes their most iconic visual symbol

Wu Tiao Ren (五条人) wanted a striking logo like Rolling Stone's tongue. Designer Hu Zi (胡子) drew over 30 options but nothing clicked. The solution came from a poster Hu Zi had painted for a previous tour—a plastic bag caught in wind like a sunset above workers, captioned with "Tomorrow's sun will rise for you." Ren saw it and realized: why not just use this as the logo? One plastic bag became the band's most recognizable visual identity.

— Ren Ke
2:56:02

One defiant song choice makes the whole country smile

A Mao (阿茂) initially refused The Summer of Bands entirely. Ren spent two hours on an emotional "guilt trip" call convincing him to perform. By late-night taping, both were exhausted in the wings—even the backing band urged A Mao not to take risks, worried about job security. Ren chose the most "frivolous" track deliberately, thinking they'd bomb fast and be eliminated anyway. Instead that spite-chosen song made the whole country laugh, becoming one of the show's most viral moments.

— Ren Ke
3:01:05

Post-fame fans nearly drove him into the ocean

At an Alaya (阿拉雅) beachside show that sold thousands of tickets, at the post-show party, 40-50 fans chased Ren Ke for autographs and photos, pushing him deeper toward the water until crew extracted him into a car. He says that's when he finally understood why celebrities like Leslie Cheung and Andy Lau complained about crowds—he'd briefly tasted "Four Heavenly Kings" problems himself. Since then he wears plain masks and hats, bought expensive ones then kept losing them, so switched to cheap ones.

— Ren Ke
