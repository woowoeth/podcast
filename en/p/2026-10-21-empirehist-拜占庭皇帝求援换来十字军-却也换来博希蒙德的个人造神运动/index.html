An emperor's plea for aid summoned the Crusade and Bohemond's rise

When Alexius called the West to arms, it sparked the First Crusade. But the real beneficiary was Norman Bohemond, who faked his death and packaged himself through a bestseller. Byzantine honor became the price of the deal.

1:02

Manzikert's true power came from myth, not the battle itself

In 1071, at Manzikert, Byzantine emperor Romanus IV was captured by Turkish general Alp Arslan—a humiliation that history barely recovers from. But Frankapan points out that the battle's true weight comes from later: today, millions in Turkey take to the streets each year to commemorate it, viewing it as the birth of the Turkish nation. This symbolic meaning far exceeds the actual impact it had in 1071.

— Peter Frankopan
7:14

A Norman mercenary's rebellion revealed the Byzantine empire was hollow

Norman Roussel de Bailleul was hired as a mercenary officer to clear Turkish raiders, but in the 1070s he judged the empire was losing and took his troops to set up his own domain in the heart of Anatolia. This wasn't because of grand ambition, but because the empire was trapped in currency devaluation, calcified leadership, and economic stagnation. Young general Alexius Komnenos was sent to suppress the rebellion and eventually captured him—Frankapan describes this as 'a bit like Apocalypse Now, a bit like going to catch Colonel Kurtz'.

— Peter Frankopan
13:24

Alexius delayed the Turkish problem because it hurt his nobles most

After taking power, Alexius faced three threats at once: the Turks, Pecheneg nomads, and Norman Robert Guiscard. He chose his order: stabilize the Normans first, deal with the Pecheneg next, and delay the Turkish problem in Anatolia. Frankapan explains why: Anatolia was where Byzantine nobility held their richest estates and wealth. The longer he delayed, the more pain the aristocracy felt—like Putin making his oligarchs take the hit while he stays away from the highest-risk zone.

— Peter Frankopan
26:44

Crusaders swore fealty to Alexius despite fierce resistance from some lords

When crusaders arrived in Constantinople in waves, Alexius's real worry wasn't the rough northern knights outside the walls, but the local elites inside who already resented him and could use the crusaders to overthrow him. He used three tactics to control events: ferry the armies across the Bosporus quickly, meet each lord separately from the main force, and make them formally swear to become his vassals in the Western feudal fashion, pledging that conquered lands would return to the empire. Some lords resisted hard against becoming vassals, but all were eventually persuaded to comply.

— Peter Frankopan
28:46

Nicaea fell to crusaders but was given to Byzantium, planting seeds of rage

Crusaders expected the siege of Nicaea to take months. Instead, they cut trees to build siege engines themselves and took the city in days—even Alexius didn't expect it that fast. But as soon as the city fell, it was handed over directly to Byzantine officials, not left to the crusader knights. Frankapan points out the contradiction: the knights who'd hewn trees and built engines weren't looking to become local administrators. But the feeling of being bypassed planted the seeds of resentment.

— Peter Frankopan
30:48

A year of starvation couldn't break the crusade, but a lie stopped relief

Antioch was a tougher nut than Nicaea—a true fortress. The crusaders camped outside the walls for nearly a year. After food ran out, they ate seeds from horse manure, picked off by tower archers, harassed by Turkish forces from behind, repeatedly on the brink of collapse. But they didn't lift the siege because of their oaths to Alexius and because Antioch was a strategic bottleneck they had to take. In 1098, they broke through with help from Firouz, an Armenian officer inside. But immediately after breaking in, Turkish reinforcements arrived and surrounded them. Stephen of Blois fled mid-siege and told Alexius the crusade army was about to collapse, so Alexius didn't send relief—a decision that later became Bohemond's biggest weapon against him.

— Peter Frankopan
37:14

Bohemond staged his own death, then used a bestseller to rewrite history

Through a bestselling text called Gesta Francorum, Bohemond painted himself as a hero always three steps ahead and Alexius as a schemer ready to betray anyone. Later, when expanding from Antioch, he was captured by Turks and ransomed back. To build buzz, he climbed into a coffin surrounded by dead chickens to spread the rumor he'd died in battle, and the stench kept anyone from checking. With this stunt, he returned to Europe as the talk of the continent—the number-one idol everyone discussed. He eventually married the daughter of the King of France.

— Peter Frankopan
39:18

The empire's honor was the price of calling the Crusade into being

Now married to a French princess, Bohemond raised armies, crossed the sea, and attacked Byzantine territory itself. He cast the war as a holy crusade against Alexius as the Antichrist—a brilliant PR move. But Alexius was ready this time. He trapped him, bled him dry, and forced him to sign the humiliating Treaty of Devol. A few years later Alexius died, and by then the world's attention had shifted to Antioch under Bohemond's nephew Tancred. Frankapan's conclusion: calling the West to aid may have been the lesser of two evils for Alexius at the time, but the damage to his personal reputation and to Byzantine honor was catastrophic. Yet the Norman principality of Antioch endured because of this for about two hundred years.

— Peter Frankopan
