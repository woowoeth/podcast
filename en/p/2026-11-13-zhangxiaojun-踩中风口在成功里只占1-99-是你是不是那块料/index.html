Riding a trend is 1% of success; being the right person is 99%

Liang Qiwei draws on years of game-making to argue: trends and capital narratives can sustain short-term revenue, but what truly determines fate is the creator's character, not external circumstances.

1:05

The core of the knight-errant is not goodness, but willingness to risk greatly

Liang Qiwei rejects equating the knight-errant with the everyday good person (like helping an elderly woman cross the street). True knights-errant must, driven by hatred and injustice, choose to bear risk with superhuman resolve—if you're not exposed to real danger, you're just a good person. This also explains why knights-errant are always outsiders: the rivers and lakes stand against the state; knights have no power to enforce law, only to maintain justice where order collapses.

— Liang Qiwei
33:31

Players see through any moral label you attach

When the team tried to add direct dialogue like "For nation and people, that is the great knight-errant" to give the game moral weight, players almost universally rejected it. The problem wasn't disagreement with the sentiment itself, Liang explains, but that players saw through the creative intent: you're not thinking about the game itself, you're trying to borrow external moral associations for mainstream approval. The lesson became: convey the spirit obliquely, never through self-congratulatory statements.

— Liang Qiwei
1:12:10

In school he was the invisible man in the dorm

Liang Qiwei recalls having Asperger's-like traits—indifferent to the real world, acutely sensitive to the virtual. In his dorm, he wasn't bullied; he was "transparent." Roommates gradually stopped inviting him to meals, and he felt no loneliness, because loneliness presumes a desire for companionship he simply didn't experience. The term itself mischaracterized his state.

— Liang Qiwei
1:52:48

One failed exam erased his entire life plan

Liang was on track: fifth in his class, accepted to Tsinghua for graduate study through the direct enrollment track. Then he failed engineering economics, an unprecedented system failure—the rules said you could advance to Tsinghua but couldn't graduate. Like learning there is no final boss before the climax, the world suddenly unraveled. But it was also the first time fate loosened its grip on his predetermined path as a "problem-solver."

— Liang Qiwei
2:34:31

During the capital boom, the team forgot how to do real work

After Blade of Shadow I found early success—twenty people, a couple million yuan monthly profit—the company dreamed bigger: anime, series, IP matrices, even pitching for a New Third Board listing. For years, Liang's actual work was pitching to investors, cutting deals at dinners, spinning IP narratives. Real coding and art happened less than a day per week. He compares it to martial artists who haven't drawn a real blade in years; when actual combat comes, they're finished in one stroke.

— Liang Qiwei
2:44:38

Professing purity is the start of impurity

Liang reflects on an article he wrote years ago: humble-bragging about colleagues' raises and a new girlfriend abroad. He sees it now as commodifying pure creative experience into capital-market small talk—using claims of "purity" to prove he'd learned the money game. Two selves were fighting: one faithful to first principles, one increasingly shrewd, hunting for external validation.

— Liang Qiwei
3:01:53

Chasing trends is 1% of success; capability is 99%

Blade of Shadow II deliberately stacked two trends: mobile esports and anime culture. It succeeded at neither. Liang's conclusion: catching a trend accounts for maybe 1% of success; 99% depends on internal capability—whether the team actually has what it takes to execute the vision. External conditions are irrelevant if you're not the person built for this work. No market opens for those not suited to seize it.

— Liang Qiwei
3:23:06

Brilliant Murong Fu always loses to clumsy Guo Jing

Game Science and miHoYo succeeded through what Liang calls "strong-mind thinking": the conviction that markets aren't fixed, that force of will can reshape them. Liang admits to the opposite—the "problem-solver" mentality: solve what's given, never imagine changing the game itself. In wuxia metaphor, Murong Fu—brilliant, elaborate, overthinking—always falls to apparently simple Guo Jing. True strength is internal, inward-facing, not in displayed talent.

— Liang Qiwei
