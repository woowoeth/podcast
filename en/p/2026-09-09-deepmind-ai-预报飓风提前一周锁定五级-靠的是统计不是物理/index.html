AI Forecasts a Hurricane at Category 5 a Week Out, Using Statistics Not Physics

Classical numerical forecasting solves fluid equations; AI learns statistical patterns from historical weather. Hurricane Melissa was locked in as a Category 5 by the model while still a tropical depression — earlier and more confidently than traditional models.

2:10

The model locked in Category 5 before the storm had formed

Hurricane Melissa hit Jamaica in October 2025. At a stage when it wasn't even a tropical storm — possibly just a tropical depression — DeepMind's model began showing it would become a Category 5 hurricane, with confidence rising over the course of a week from lower levels to about 80%. Peter says that as far as he knows, other models were also predicting intensification at that point, but none had that kind of confidence, that specific track, and that specific intensity. The National Hurricane Center officially determined on Saturday that it would become a Category 5 and hit Jamaica, when it wasn't even a Category 1 yet. It was the highest-intensity forecast the hurricane center had ever issued from the lowest intensity, and they later said the call was "heavily influenced" by the DeepMind model's confidence.

12:11

Forecasting is hard because you can't observe that butterfly

Peter traces the difficulty of forecasting to a fundamental limit: small things can have large effects, and we can't observe every butterfly. So there is a fundamental limit on observation, and that limit translates into a fundamental limit on prediction. But he adds a key turn: that doesn't mean we can't detect subtle patterns in the full historical record that were previously overlooked. These important features that drive weather, though unobservable, leave "little clues and breadcrumbs" that statistical learning can pick up. He uses the analogy of dropping a small pebble into a pond: if you can catch that little splash and understand it means concentric ripples will spread out, you can use it.

— Peter Battaglia
20:14

An AI model can see the whole hurricane at once

GraphCast was one of the first models in the third phase to simulate global weather as a whole out to 10 days. Its approach: take the complete state of global weather, run the same learned local function everywhere (roughly corresponding to "physics is the same everywhere"), process these into a larger representation covering the globe, then predict back down to the local. Traditional numerical forecasting doesn't do this — it only makes predictions at very local scales. Peter says this may be why AI models are so effective: a large hurricane can span hundreds of kilometers, and the structure on its west side can tell you a lot about the east side. If the model can put the entire hurricane into one representation, it can better constrain what happens locally next.

— Peter Battaglia
24:16

Probabilistic forecasting works by stuffing butterflies into the model

A probabilistic model makes many guesses rather than just one. DeepMind has two approaches. One is diffusion models: start from a large number of different noise images, refine them into what weather looks like, and each time you land on a slightly different scenario — that's where the diversity comes from. The other is functional generative networks, which they invented: inject different input scenarios and slightly alter the neural network's weight parameters, producing diverse outputs. Peter's analogy is "what would the world look like if there's a butterfly here and a butterfly there" — run it hundreds or thousands of times and you can see what the future most likely looks like. When all scenarios give the same prediction, confidence is high; when they fan out like spaghetti, the model is saying "we can't tell what will happen" — Peter believes a model must admit this, and that it's a form of "humility."

— Peter Battaglia
26:17

WeatherNext 3 compresses the whole weather tree into one model

Peter uses the analogy of a "weather tree": the roots are data, the junction of root and trunk is the estimate of the current weather state, the trunk is the operational model predicting global weather, the branches are regional models for specific uses like energy and extreme events, and the leaves are applications — you pull out your phone to know the temperature right now. Traditionally this value chain is done in at least three or four stages. WeatherNext 3's change: it doesn't just take the weather state estimate given by meteorological agencies, it eats raw satellite imagery directly; and it doesn't just predict a global weather estimate, it directly predicts observed values at high-quality weather stations like airports. That is, one model eats from the roots all the way to the leaves. Peter says many groups are thinking about this idea, but WeatherNext 3 may be the first to significantly exceed all previous AI and traditional models in accuracy.

— Peter Battaglia
29:18

A forecast every hour, because satellites update every hour

Several specific changes in WeatherNext 3: higher resolution — no longer blurry or pixelated images — and it can make predictions at different resolutions; forecast frequency goes from once every 6 hours to once every 1 hour, because the satellite imagery it consumes is itself updated hourly; the forecast's own temporal resolution is also higher, with the model natively predicting a series of weather states within a 6-hour window — what the weather is at 1, 2, and 3 o'clock, say — rather than only saying what it is at 6 and 12. Peter says much of this design is based on users telling them what they want from the next generation of weather models — renewable energy forecasting and load forecasting, for instance.

— Peter Battaglia
35:22

The AI model's failure mode is regressing to average weather

Hannah asks: using the current time step to predict the next, won't small errors accumulate and amplify? Peter says that's more a problem of traditional methods — there's nothing in the equations that pulls a diverging solution back to the most reasonable state; once you enter a rare state, the equations won't say "you must behave like normal weather." AI models fail the opposite way: when they start to go wrong, they tend to predict average weather — that is, "regression to the mean." And predicting average weather is actually a fairly good way to forecast, and the oldest way — farmers planting in spring rely on seasonal patterns. So AI models are less likely to "blow up" the way traditional numerical methods do. But Peter also concedes that why statistical learning can predict the strongest hurricane on record is something that "probably needs to be explored better."

— Peter Battaglia
39:22

Most weather observations aren't being used at all

Peter says weather itself affects roughly a third of the economy, but better forecasts don't necessarily help that third — it's just that weather touches energy, agriculture, insurance, transportation, and disasters all at once. He thinks the room for the next step is enormous: industries like energy are themselves collecting large amounts of weather observations that could be folded into models, and they haven't done it yet; many weather phenomena happen at scales smaller than a kilometer; and "most weather observations aren't actually used by meteorological agencies." His judgment: we've only proven we can make very accurate forecasts — now the real work begins. He also says he'll move away from "approximately solving equations," but not away from physics — the data itself was made by physics, it's just approximating physics a different way.

Glossary

numerical weather prediction
The classical forecasting method that uses supercomputers to approximately solve the equations of fluid motion and extrapolate step by step.
GraphCast
DeepMind's earlier global AI weather model, predicting global weather 10 days out in one pass.
GenCast
DeepMind's probabilistic weather model, predicting multiple possible scenarios rather than a single average weather state.
functional generative networks
DeepMind's in-house probabilistic forecasting technique, generating diverse scenarios by slightly adjusting network weights.
spaghetti plots
Plotting the tracks of multiple probabilistic scenarios together; the more they fan out, the greater the uncertainty.
load forecasting
Predicting electricity demand on the grid, driven mainly by temperature.
