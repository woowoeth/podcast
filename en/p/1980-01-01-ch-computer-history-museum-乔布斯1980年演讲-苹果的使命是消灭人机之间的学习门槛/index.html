Steve Jobs' 1980 speech: Apple's mission is to eliminate the learning barrier between people and computers

Jobs believed Apple's true product wasn't hardware and software volume, but empowering anyone without programming knowledge to solve problems directly; he was confident this barrier would fall within 36 months.

AppleSteve JobsStartup historyPersonal computersProduct philosophyEarly hardware
A raw early recording of Jobs, stripped of theatrical flair, capturing him working through how to explain what Apple actually does—essential for anyone wanting to see how early-stage founder rhetoric takes shape.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:07

Personal need, not market research, created the first Apple

Jobs said they had no idea what others would do with the machine initially—they designed it purely because Wozniak and Jobs couldn't afford commercial computer kits, so they "liberated" parts from HP and Atari and spent six months designing it themselves. Wozniak worked through the night to 4 a.m.; once built, friends wanted copies, but assembly and debugging took 60-70 hours per unit, becoming an unsustainable burden. This overturns the narrative that Apple had clear market positioning from the start—the product came first for themselves, market demand came later.

— Steve Jobs
3:08

Founders' personal possessions, not investor money, funded Apple

Wozniak sold his HP calculator; Jobs sold his van. Together they raised $1,300 to pay a friend for PCB layout design, planning to sell blank circuit boards to friends at double cost. Then Paul Terrell, owner of the first Byte Shop in Mountain View, asked for 50 assembled and tested units—a new demand that pushed them to spend five days convincing component distributors to extend $10,000 in credit. They built 100 units, sold 50, and cleared the distributor's debt within 29 days. This is Apple's origin in its plainest form, without mythologizing.

— Steve Jobs
5:14

The bicycle analogy: technology amplifies human capabilities beyond natural limits

Jobs cited an early Scientific American study measuring efficiency across species—vultures ranked highest, humans ranked lower, but give a human a bicycle and human efficiency doubles that of a vulture. His conclusion: Apple wasn't building "bicycles for riding between Palo Alto and San Francisco," but tools to amplify some innate human capability, the way the Industrial Revolution amplified physical strength. This analogy became one of Jobs' most famous public speaking points; here we see its earliest form.

— Steve Jobs
8:18

Apple's three-year mission: eliminate programming as a barrier to computing

Jobs was candid: anyone buying a computer system to solve a problem now faced a giant obstacle—learning how to use a computer. After crossing that wall, the tool was powerful, but the wall itself was the barrier. He believed Apple had a 36-month window to genuinely solve this, letting people with zero computer knowledge use the tool to solve their problems without first learning to program. His reasoning: Apple's entire company philosophy rested on "one person, one computer," a different foundation from bigger computer companies.

— Steve Jobs
10:22

One person, one computer determines all of Apple's business decisions

Jobs attributed all of Apple's early advantages in retail channels, interactive software, and interactive video to the core belief of "one person, one computer"—serving this one-to-one relationship required lower-cost distribution channels and pricing that individuals could afford. His example: Apple's terminal running on a DEC 1170 minicomputer communicated at 9600 bits per second, yet that machine cost $300,000 and couldn't run VisiCalc. His secretary on an Apple could do budgeting "far, far better."

— Steve Jobs
17:34

The line between software and hardware is constantly blurring

When asked how Apple would use hardware performance to remove the learning barrier, Jobs sidestepped specifics and offered a philosophical judgment instead: software is whatever is "still changing rapidly, or you're not yet clear on what you want, or the technology isn't mature enough to build into hardware." Yesterday's software becomes today's hardware, and that line will "get finer and finer." Apple tried to predict years ahead where hardware and software technology would converge, while simultaneously researching the high-level tools customers needed, aiming both toward the same target.

— Steve Jobs
19:37

Apple II won't become obsolete; different models serve different needs

Facing concerns from existing Apple II users about becoming obsolete, Jobs first offered a happenstance argument: two people from Los Altos and Cupertino happened to want exactly what millions wanted, and no one else came closer in two and a half years, so Apple II wouldn't become obsolete. Then he used a Volkswagen Beetle, dump truck, and Mercedes analogy to explain that Apple's future product line would have different emphases but shared core functionality—users wouldn't need to abandon Apple II. He acknowledged Apple I had indeed been displaced, but Apple arranged an upgrade path for Apple I users to Apple II and promised never to repeat this.

— Steve Jobs
21:43

Talented employees took pay cuts to do important work right

Jobs mentioned Apple then had about 500 employees and $150-200 million in annual sales this year, possibly the industry's highest per-employee sales. Many had initially joined Apple while voluntarily taking 50% pay cuts—they came not for money but for the chance to "do one thing right, do it quietly without getting it screwed up," and to work alongside equally capable people. His measurement for management: could they maintain over the next decade a culture that tolerated failure, gave people enough rope, but hoped they wouldn't hang themselves with it—a creative atmosphere.

— Steve Jobs
