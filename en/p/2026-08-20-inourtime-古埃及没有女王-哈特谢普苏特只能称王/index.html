Ancient Egypt had no queen: she could only be king, and was written out 20 years after her death

Hatshepsut seized power step by step from her position as regent, building kingship on temples, trade and oracles — and roughly 20 years after her death Thutmose III had her largely written out.

5:13

Egypt had been unified for only 70 years; the trauma of division was still fresh

When Hatshepsut came to power, Egypt had been re-centralised for only about 70 years. Before that the north was ruled by the Hyksos (possibly from Canaan) and the south was the kingdom of Kush; the Thebans gradually conquered from the south and unified Egypt. She came from the families of these conquerors who reunified Egypt, and the cultural memory of division was still present.

8:42

Women in power were not unusual; a woman ruling alone was

Women holding power in Egypt was not rare, but mostly as king's mother or regent. Sobekneferu ruled alone for about four years around 1700 BC, the exception to the regency pattern; there is also the documented ‘Amazon-style queen’ Ahhotep. Hatshepsut did not lack precedents of strong women around her.

— Campbell Price
22:28

She was appointed regent on the strength of power she already held at court

Thutmose II ruled only about three years before dying. He and Hatshepsut had only a daughter; the successor, Thutmose III, by the latest estimate may have been only two to five years old, and his mother was a low-ranking queen with no title at all. Hatshepsut was appointed regent on the strength of the power she already held at court, effectively exercising royal authority in his place.

— Kate Spence
24:42

She did not kill the child king, because the family was her power base

She advanced very cautiously, step by step: in the second year an inscription at Semna already has her presiding over rituals as ‘heir of the gods’; then images appear of her in a woman's dress wearing a crown; and between years two and seven — closer to year seven — she formally declared herself king and adopted the full royal titulary. She did not assassinate the child king, because a stable family and court were the foundation of her power.

— Kate Spence
29:17

For the first time a king's mortuary temple became a temple of Amun

Her large temple complex at Deir el-Bahri sits right next to the burial place of the Middle Kingdom unifier Mentuhotep, and began as a small project before expanding to cut away the cliff face. This was the first time a king's mortuary temple was presented primarily as a temple of Amun, uniting kingship and god in death; at the same site she also built a cult place for her father and embedded it in the ritual route of the ‘Beautiful Festival of the Valley’, transforming the whole religious landscape of Thebes.

— Kate Spence
32:40

She transplanted part of Punt into her own temple

In her year 9 Hatshepsut sent an expedition to Punt, a place probably on the Red Sea coast, near Arabia or Somalia. She deliberately revived ancient trade routes, and in the temple scenes emphasised the myrrh trees brought back — transplanting part of Punt into her own temple environment, both advertising open trade and displaying her command over that strange land.

— Elizabeth Frood
35:57

The lover rumour does not hold up; the 26 statues are the real anomaly

Her chief official Senenmut comes across as a rare ancient Egyptian who could have had a biography written about him: of ordinary birth, he later became tutor to the princess Neferure. He received 26 statues, even one carved together with the young princess, which was unprecedented. He was unmarried and left graffiti depicting sexual scenes, giving rise to the lover theory, but all three guests consider the evidence insufficient.

— Campbell Price
44:24

The erasure came 20 years late; the motive may have been succession rivalry

After Hatshepsut disappeared, the erasure did not happen immediately but on a large scale roughly 20 years later, in the late reign of Thutmose III. Kate suggests this may be tied to succession rivalry: several children in the royal nursery could have succeeded, several family lines competed, and she was written out. Elizabeth adds that evidence from Karnak shows some of the chiselling actually began shortly after her death, making it a complex process.

Glossary

Hyksos
A foreign people who ruled northern Egypt during the Second Intermediate Period, possibly originating in Canaan.
Kush
A Nubian kingdom centred on Kerma, located south of the Third Cataract.
God's Wife of Amun
A priestess title holding independent estates and religious power, sharing authority with the pharaoh.
Regent
One who exercises royal authority on behalf of a child king during his minority, usually the queen or king's mother.
Deir el-Bahri
Hatshepsut's temple complex, cut into the cliff and facing Karnak.
Punt
A semi-mythical trading land on the Red Sea coast, producing myrrh and other luxury goods.
