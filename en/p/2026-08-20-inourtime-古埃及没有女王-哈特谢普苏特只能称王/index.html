Ancient Egypt had no queens: she could only be king, and was erased 20 years after her death

Hatshepsut took power gradually from the position of regent, building royal authority through temples, trade and oracles; about 20 years after her death Thutmose III erased her on a large scale.

Egypt had been reunified only 70 years; the trauma of division was still fresh

When Hatshepsut came to power, Egypt had been recentralised for only about 70 years. Before that, the north was ruled by the Hyksos (possibly out of Canaan) and the south was the kingdom of Kush; the Thebans conquered and unified Egypt step by step from the south. She came from the family of those conquerors who reunified Egypt, and the trauma of division was still present in cultural memory.

— Elizabeth Frood
8:42

Women holding power was unremarkable; ruling alone as king was the rare thing

Women wielding power in Egypt was not rare, but usually as king's mother or as regent. Sobekneferu ruled alone for about four years around 1700 BC, the exception who was not a regent; there is also Ahhotep, the "Amazon queen" recorded in the texts. Hatshepsut was not short of precedents for strong women around her.

— Campbell Price
22:28

She was appointed regent on power she already held inside the court

Thutmose II reigned only about three years before he died. He and Hatshepsut had only one daughter, and the successor, Thutmose III, may have been only two to five years old on the latest estimates, his mother a junior royal wife with no title at all. Hatshepsut was appointed regent on the strength of power she already held at court, and in practice she exercised royal authority herself.

— Kate Spence
24:42

She did not kill the boy king, because the family was her power base

She advanced very carefully, in stages: by year two, an inscription at Semna already shows her presiding over rites as "heir of the gods"; then came images of her in a queen's dress wearing the king's crown; and between years two and seven, closer to year seven, she formally called herself king and adopted the full royal titulary. She had no one assassinate the boy king, because a stable family and court were the foundation of her power.

— Kate Spence
29:17

For the first time, a king's mortuary temple became a temple of Amun

Deir el-Bahri, the site of her great temple, sits immediately next to the burial place of Mentuhotep, the unifier of the Middle Kingdom; it began as a small project and was later enlarged until the cliff face itself was cut back. This was the first time a king's mortuary temple was presented primarily as a temple of the god Amun, with kingship and god united in death. At the same site she also established a cult for her father, and embedded it in the processional route of the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, changing the religious landscape of Thebes as a whole.

— Kate Spence
32:40

She transplanted a piece of Punt into her own temple

Hatshepsut sent an expedition to Punt in her year 9, a place probably on the Red Sea coast, near Arabia or Somalia. She deliberately set out to revive the ancient trade routes, and in the temple scenes she gave prominence to the myrrh trees brought back — transplanting a piece of Punt into the setting of her own temple, which both advertised open trade and displayed her command over that numinous land.

— Elizabeth Frood
35:57

The lover rumour does not hold up; the 26 statues are the real anomaly

Senenmut, her chief official, is one of those rare ancient Egyptians whose biography can actually be written: of ordinary birth, he later became tutor to the princess Neferure. He was granted 26 statues, and was even carved in a single statue together with the young princess, which was unprecedented. He never married and left behind graffiti with sexual scenes, which has fed the story that he was her lover, but all three guests think the evidence is too thin.

— Campbell Price
44:24

The erasure came 20 years late, and the motive may have been succession rivalry

After Hatshepsut disappeared, the erasure did not follow immediately; it was carried out on a large scale only about 20 years later, late in the reign of Thutmose III. Kate thinks this may have to do with competition over the succession: several children in the royal nursery could all have taken the throne, and with several family lines in contention she was written out. Elizabeth adds that the evidence at Karnak shows some of the effacement in fact began not long after her death, so it was a complex process.

— Kate Spence

Glossary

Hyksos
A foreign people who ruled the north of Egypt during the Second Intermediate Period, possibly out of Canaan.
Kush
A Nubian kingdom centred on Kerma, lying south of the Third Cataract.
God's Wife of Amun
A priestess title carrying independent estates and religious power, sharing authority with the pharaoh.
Regent
The person who exercises royal power while the boy king is under age, usually the queen or the king's mother.
Deir el-Bahri
Hatshepsut's temple complex, reaching into the cliff and facing Karnak.
Punt
A half-mythical trading land on the Red Sea coast, the source of myrrh and other luxuries.
