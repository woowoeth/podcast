Envy Can't Be Eliminated, Only Regrown Somewhere Else

Eliminate one source of envy and people will invent another immediately — because envy sits on top of hierarchy, and hierarchy is animal, and it doesn't go away. A good organisation doesn't kill envy; it converts the energy spent pulling others down into energy spent pulling yourself up.

6:03

Eliminate one bias and people will invent the next

The medium of envy can be destroyed; envy itself cannot. Once cars, houses and luxury goods are the same for everyone, people look at desk size, whether the office has a window, how close you sit to the chairman. Level those too and they look at how much hair you have, black or white, whether your skin has lustre. Wang Yajun's explanation: what sits behind envy is hierarchy, and hierarchy determines your ability to obtain resources. That comes from the animal part of the human mind, so people will always keep manufacturing things that separate them from others. The implication for managers is: don't expect to cure envy by flattening one particular difference. All you get to decide is which difference gets invented next.

— Wang Yajun
9:46

The bigger the gap, the less envy; the more alike, the more

The intensity of envy is inversely proportional to distance. Two people far apart generally won't envy each other. Two people close together — or former peers at the same level — go through one year-end review that separates them, and next year a new position opens and one becomes the other's superior. That is the most awkward way to work together. Which is why some companies require several people to compete for the CEO job at once, and when one goes up, the other two or three have to leave. GE did exactly this, on the logic that ‘I don't quite believe people can still work together calmly and amicably after something like that happens’. Wang Yajun distinguishes this from McKinsey-style up or out: the latter is more a question of ability, the former a question of human nature.

— Wang Yajun
13:12

Envy itself is life force; the question is where it goes

Wang Yajun doesn't treat envy as purely negative: at its most basic it is about raising the odds for your own survival and the transmission of your DNA, and it is a manifestation of life force. Imagine a world without envy, where everyone thinks it's all the same whether others are better or worse than them, and nobody wants to change their current situation — that world stops moving and becomes something static. So the question isn't whether envy exists but which road it takes once it does: pull the other person down, or lift yourself up. The six-pack example: one option is to make sure he doesn't have one, the other is for me to go train. The starting point is roughly the same; the action and the outcome are completely different.

— Wang Yajun
20:13

Luxury goods don't sell function, they sell hierarchy

Wang Yajun says he used to find it hard to understand why one Hermès bag costs so much more than another bag that is unknown, or even slightly known — a bit more expensive makes sense, that much more doesn't. Then it clicked: what you're ultimately buying isn't its function of holding things, it's its function of hierarchy, of telling other people I have the money to buy this. Same with luxury cars. School uniforms are the reverse operation: make everyone identical in dress, so kids from very wealthy families and kids from ordinary families wear the same thing and no pointless envy arises. But even then, some kid will find a small way to be different from everyone else. Same inside a company: everything else is about the same, but the watch has to be very expensive.

— Wang Yajun
22:11

If your rating came in low, don't fight it within one cycle

Performance evaluation is an abstract standard, and the higher up you go the more it resembles an ideal — there may be no one who is exactly what an A or an A+ looks like. The person being rated feels it's unfair, and that is essentially a form of envy too, just normally called unfairness. Wang Yajun's advice is to look at whether the process is fair: if you believe this company has reasonableness and fairness in how it handles these things, stay one more cycle and see what happens next time. The reasoning is that information can never be 100% symmetric, so there will always be error, but a good system, given two or three tries, will most likely regress to the mean — this time it pulled you down, next time it may be better; this time it pushed you up, next time it will pull you back down. Conversely, if every single time you think you deserve an A and every single time you get a B+, then you need to figure out whether it's systematic targeting or whether your self-perception is off. Ask several people. Don't sit on it.

— Wang Yajun
41:32

An office near the chairman is a scarce resource

Wang Ziru said he wanted an office fairly close to the chairman, and Wang Yajun reads it through envy: that spot is a scarce resource — if you have it, I don't; if you're close to him, I still have to go through you. Envy from colleagues under normal circumstances is to be expected. His judgement is that this sort of thing is inspiring for the individual but not good for the organisation as a whole. Longfor's approach was that executive offices could only be in the middle of the floor with no windows, and window seats were left to employees, because executives already hold plenty of power — if they take all the perks too, everyone else naturally feels the imbalance. He also mentions companies that give executives a dedicated elevator, saving all of thirty seconds, but the negative spillover is extremely strong and it is very much not worth it.

— Wang Yajun
53:51

Deliberately stoking envy between reports never works well

There really are managers who deliberately set two people in one group against each other, on the assumption that the organisation doesn't need much collaboration, only individual capability. Wang Yajun says the setups he has seen of this kind — leadership teams split into an A side and a B side, for instance — never work particularly well: this kind of counterbalancing only pushes the organisation toward mediocrity, because it can't produce a system everyone believes in, there is very little trust, and nobody is safe — you can be swapped out at any moment, and the B side is just waiting for you to slip. When people don't dare make mistakes, they do the safe, conservative thing, or they go for something big, skim a little off it for themselves, and get out. He names real estate and insurance as industries that have companies like this, and not small ones, but not one of them has managed to produce innovation, and they can't build systems in their traditional business either.

— Wang Yajun
1:04:06

Before investing in a company, diligence its envy content

Wang Yajun treats envy as a usable decision variable: an employee weighing whether to join a company can first look at how strong the atmosphere of envy is there; a fund considering an investment can look at it too. His reasoning is that once you're in, your only choices are play by their rules or leave — but before you're in, doing this diligence from both the people angle and the money angle is a good lens. He also offers a distinction: if it's malignant envy and it's running hot, there's probably a problem; but positive envy usually doesn't get called envy — it gets called healthy competition, keeping each other on your toes. With a sales team you want that; with R&D you probably don't, unless you set up two teams on the same project.

— Wang Yajun
1:08:38

Changing your reference point beats suppressing envy

The technique Wang Yajun gives the envious person isn't suppression, it's swapping the frame of reference. First, you can choose to compare with different people, and to compare across a person's different dimensions in aggregate, rather than fixating on one point and magnifying where he beats you. Second, don't only compare with others — compare with your own past; being better than you used to be is also a good way to think, and that's the logic of a growth mindset. Third, check whether the thing you envy is actually scarce — money is scarce, but the marginal cost of learning, knowledge and cognition is zero; you either have it or you don't. These days with ChatGPT your breadth of knowledge seems about the same as the next person's, so there's no need to envy him — and so people start inventing new objects of envy. His own way of making peace is ‘thankfully I'm not classmates with Jack Ma’: envy depends on how far the reference point you've chosen is from you. It only appears when it's within a jump's reach and you didn't reach it. When it's very far away, you don't care.

— Wang Yajun

Wang Yajun's explanatory framework for behaviour: sex, fear, love and belief — envy is assembled from sex and fear.
