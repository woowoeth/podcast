Bank of America sent 60,000 credit cards to one city in 1958 with no applications

In 1958, Bank of America mailed credit cards to 60,000 Fresno residents with zero screening or applications—a gamble that nearly bankrupted the bank but unexpectedly launched the nationwide credit card era.

Early bank card systems collapsed when customers didn't know who to pay

Starting in 1944, Flatbush National Bank took over merchants' informal credit systems for regular customers—the bank handled credit checks and billing, a model almost identical to today's credit cards, only confined to a small Brooklyn area with 13 merchants and 3,600 accounts. Customers didn't know whether to pay the bank or the merchant, though; many merchants left as a result. The bank was then acquired and the project was killed. Not until 1950, when designer Biggins moved to Patterson Savings Institution in New Jersey, did the system succeed—he created three repayment periods of 30, 90, or 180 days, with merchants paying 6% to the bank for credit screening.

The Diners Club forgotten-wallet founding story was completely made up

The official Diners Club story: in 1950, founder Frank McNamara forgot his wallet while treating clients to dinner in Manhattan, his wife came to pay the bill, and this sparked the buy-now-pay-later credit card. But years later a former Diners Club executive admitted the story was entirely fabricated to make the founding sound better. The likelier version: McNamara worked at a credit company and noticed customers lending their own credit limits to neighbors to buy things, then collecting debts on themselves; this led him and his lawyer friend and Bloomingdale's heir to devise a centralized billing system for restaurants, which paid 7% in fees for customer traffic.

Blanket-mailing unsolicited cards got merchants on board—and nearly sank the bank

On September 18, 1958, Bank of America's Fresno Drop sent credit cards to 60,000 Fresno residents at once, each carrying $300 or $500 in credit with no screening and no application process—any Bank of America account holder got one in the mail. It did convince many merchants who'd rejected credit cards to join, suddenly gaining thousands of customers with buying power. But the bank badly underestimated fraud, counterfeits, and defaults; it lost millions in the first two years. Only after it adjusted collections and reduced merchant fees did the program start profiting by the end of 1961.

23:44

The credit card's magnetic stripe was designed for CIA IDs, not banks

In 1960, IBM engineer Forrest Perry invented the magnetic stripe on the back of credit cards, but his original assignment was encoding data for CIA ID cards—nothing to do with banks. The real challenge wasn't writing data to the tape but fixing it to the card. According to IBM, Perry's wife suggested using a hot iron to fuse the plastic to the card, which solved it. The stripe later spread to bank cards; by 1969 it was standard on credit cards worldwide, so merchants no longer had to call the bank to ask ‘does this person have enough credit?’ Perry also helped pioneer UPC barcode technology.

26:45

Women couldn't apply for credit cards alone until 1974

Before 1974, most U.S. banks had a policy that women needed a husband, father, or male guardian to sign for a credit card; men needed permission to guarantee for women too—not law, just banking practice, rooted in the coverture tradition where married women's property belonged to their husbands by default. The 1974 Equal Credit Opportunity Act broke this, explicitly banning creditors from discriminating on sex and other grounds. A key force behind it was Ruth Bader Ginsburg's ACLU Women's Rights Project, founded in 1972, which pressed through legal suits.

29:11

When South Dakota abolished rate caps, card companies moved in and profits soared

In 1979, South Dakota repealed its usury law, so credit card interest had no ceiling; banks moved entire credit card divisions there, and South Dakota quickly became the U.S. credit card hub, Delaware following suit. This made credit card operations one of the bank industry's highest-margin segments. The U.S. still has no federal credit card rate cap: Congress proposed a bipartisan 10% cap in early 2025, but it stalled in the Senate Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs Committee and hadn't reached a full vote by November 2026 when this episode was recorded.

— Tracy V. Wilson
33:18

America took twenty years to catch up after France went all-in on chip cards

French banks began rolling out microprocessor chip cards in the 1960s—requiring a PIN to transact—and by the mid-1990s chip cards were standard on all French credit and debit cards. In 1994, Visa, MasterCard, and Europay jointly launched the EMV standard for global use (the name is their initials), spending two years standardizing chip specs; the first contactless terminals appeared in 2005. Yet chip cards only became mandatory in America on October 1, 2015—more than twenty years after France's nationwide rollout.

Average Americans now carry over $6,000 in credit card debt

According to a 2004 PBS Frontline report, the average American household then held 8 credit cards. Experian data shows total U.S. consumer credit card debt is now $1.23 trillion, with an average balance of $6,768 per person. The hosts note that number might sound high or low depending on who you are—they saved the full credit card debt story for a paid Behind the Scenes episode.
