Fed Official: Inflation Hasn't Left, Rate Cuts Can't Be Bet on 'Temporary'

Goolsbee worries the economy is overheating: the inflation decline is mostly supply-side repair, services haven't responded; cuts below neutral need evidence, not front-loading.

This episode features a rare, clear self-account from Goolsbee: why he voted against last year, how he views AI investment and overheating, and the real changes at the FOMC under Warsh.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:03

If inflation stays above 3%, rates aren't tight

Asked whether current policy is restrictive, Goolsbee declined to answer directly. He said the key is the real rate—nominal minus inflation expectations or actual inflation. His long-run landing point is 3% nominal, 2% inflation, 1% real. If inflation is above 3%, the real rate is much lower than it appears. He's willing to wait, but he's nervous about the last six months of inflation data. This opening sets the episode's premise: you can't discuss tightness apart from inflation.

— Austan Goolsbee
6:07

Saying 'wait three months' every quarter isn't temporary

Goolsbee breaks down current inflation: tariffs and war-driven oil prices are one-off price-level shocks that should fade, but he warns from the COVID experience—a large enough supply shock can drag on far longer than initial forecasts. What really worries him is services inflation, which is neither tariffs nor oil, a deeper problem. His test: you can't say 'wait three more months' every quarter; he needs evidence of actual fading.

— Austan Goolsbee
12:21

Poaching electricians isn't overheating; wage spillover is

On AI data-center construction, Goolsbee says what he hears from Midwest businesses are complaints: data firms buy up land, push up prices, and there aren't enough electricians or HVAC workers. But he stresses that industry-level resource competition isn't economy-wide overheating. Only if AI investment pushes wages and prices beyond its own sector, lifting national unemployment and non-GDP indicators, would it be traditional aggregate-demand excess. If it gets to that point, current policy isn't restrictive enough.

— Austan Goolsbee
18:28

Two-thirds of this inflation cycle came from supply, not demand

Looking back at the aggressive 2022-23 hikes, Goolsbee offers a split: roughly two-thirds of this cycle's inflation rise and fall came from supply, one-third from demand. He credits the Fed for ensuring 'the other shoe didn't drop': TIPS inflation compensation stayed stable at 2.3% even as CPI neared 10%, showing the 2% target held. He admits the Fed was slow to start, but insists you can't have it both ways—you can't blame stimulus for the rise and credit supply-chain repair for the fall.

— Austan Goolsbee
22:36

A 5.25% long-end yield isn't a credit panic

On rising long-term Treasury yields, Goolsbee says it takes time to distinguish drivers: inflation expectations, market expectations of high Fed rates, or increased issuance supply. He rejects the 'market panicking about US credit' read, because if default were truly a concern, 5.25% is historically just a normal level. He quotes Volcker: the Fed acts, the market reacts, and the order can't be reversed. Market signals can inform, but the market shouldn't tell the Fed what to do.

— Austan Goolsbee
28:44

The dot plot isn't a reaction function; the median isn't from one person

Goolsbee separates forward guidance from reaction function. He opposes guidance that 'promises a hike or cut at a specific meeting,' as it ties hands and adds volatility. But markets need to understand how the Fed views data—that's the reaction function. The problem with SEP and the dot plot: the median inflation and median rate don't necessarily come from the same person, so the dot plot can't truly serve as a reaction function. He says he's opposed SEP for years, because members write forecasts and later get slapped when wrong, hurting credibility.

— Austan Goolsbee
32:54

Without evidence of inflation fading, don't cut early

He gives his own reaction function: high attention to the inflation side; if he sees inflation genuinely falling back toward 2%, he's willing to return to the '3-2-1' path; if especially services inflation is still rising with no progress, he gets nervous. He explains his dissenting vote late last year: with the government shutdown and missing data, he was uncomfortable front-loading a cut without evidence, not wanting to act on the assumption that 'inflation will disappear on its own.' That explains his divergence from the current majority.

— Austan Goolsbee
39:10

Powell just moved a few seats; the FOMC has shifted gears

Asked how a Warsh-chaired FOMC differs from the Powell era, Goolsbee says the personal feel is very different. The new chair's posture at press conferences, worldview, and willingness to revisit communication tools all differ; Powell is still on the committee, just moved a few seats over. He won't comment on the chair's personal reaction function, saying everything waits for the verbatim minutes four years and nine months out. For listeners, this signals the Fed is in a gear-shifting period, and market speculation about the reaction function will continue.

— Austan Goolsbee

Glossary

R-star
The real neutral rate that theoretically measures whether monetary policy is restrictive; not directly observable.
SEP
The Fed's quarterly Summary of Economic Projections for inflation, unemployment, and rates; the dot plot is part of it.
Forward guidance
Central-bank communication pre-committing to a future rate path; Goolsbee thinks it was needed at zero rates but should be reduced now.
Reaction function
The rule for how a central bank responds to economic data; Goolsbee stresses it isn't a specific rate commitment.
Taylor rule
An empirical rule mapping inflation and output gaps to a suggested rate; it computed negative values under zero rates.
