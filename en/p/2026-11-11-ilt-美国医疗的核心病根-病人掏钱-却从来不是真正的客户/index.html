Healthcare's core malady: patients pay but have never been true customers

Ro founder argues healthcare spending is inevitable—the real problem is patients never being true customers. GLP-1's cash-pay model proves what works when they control the payment.

Patients can't become customers when others control healthcare payment

Patients' out-of-pocket expenses represent only a small fraction of total healthcare costs; employers and taxpayers pay the bulk. This means whoever controls the payment flow—not patients—determines what services healthcare offers. Only in cash-pay markets like LASIK and cosmetic surgery do prices fall and quality improve, because those customers face direct consequences. GLP-1 proves the principle: when patients pay themselves, the market works like any consumer market, with drugmakers competing on price and convenience.

7:22

Bypass middlemen: prices fall, volumes surge, total profit grows

GLP-1 manufacturers sell directly to consumers, bypassing both PBM and insurers. A drug with list price of $1,300 reaches patients for $149. He compares PBMs to an appendix—possibly once useful, but nobody remembers what problem they solved, and software can do their work if removed. The counterintuitive part: as prices drop, volume rises so much that total manufacturer revenue increases, not decreases. This proves patient control simultaneously lowers prices and grows the entire market.

23:42

Proven safety should deploy even if workers lose employment

Autonomous vehicles are 10 to 20 times safer than human drivers. The U.S. sees approximately 40,000 car-crash deaths annually—roughly equal to breast cancer deaths. Yet public debate centers on driver unemployment, not accelerating deployment of the solution. His view is unambiguous: when a technology is proven far safer, society should not delay it to protect old jobs. Those who block it bear responsibility for preventable deaths that follow. He worries healthcare will make the same mistake—prioritizing who delivers care over whether patients survive.

30:57

When employees debate packaging, the company has surrendered already

In early 2022, Ro had closed a $500 million round at $5 billion valuation less than a year earlier, but grew only 9% that year—becoming a 'zombie company' that neither thrives nor collapses. The turning point came when employees complained on Slack about holiday macarons in plastic packaging. He realized winning companies simply don't care about packaging: 'We are, literally and figuratively, a company as soft as a macaron.' He wrote a six-page memo, cut approximately seven VPs and 20% of staff (reducing the team from over 600 to 400), and revenue subsequently became ten times larger.

43:10

Plans longer than two years are fiction, not strategy

Ro eliminated long-term roadmaps entirely, calling them 'false precision'—like printing directions instead of using Google Maps. Any new variable (a new drug, regulation, or AI breakthrough) invalidates everything from step three onward. Instead, the company held daily 8:50 a.m. all-hands standups with leadership for two consecutive years, forcing every person to show progress within 24 hours rather than waiting until Monday. They also deliberately sequenced work—tackling one thing at a time—rather than parallelizing, because compounding returns from undivided focus dwarf the benefits of pushing five initiatives forward simultaneously.

— Zach Reitano
51:28

Twenty years from discovery to treatment reveals medicine's broken cycles

The Dallas Heart Study in 2000 tracked 3,500 patients. In 2006, researchers discovered a subset carried a 'defective' PCSK9 gene that kept their LDL cholesterol at 30 to 40, reducing heart disease risk by 85% to 90% regardless of smoking, drinking, or eating hamburgers. Merck launched an oral drug mimicking this genetic effect this year—a gap of nearly 20 years from discovery to treatment. He sees medicine-AI fusion's best opportunity in compressing feedback cycles: mathematics and programming enjoy structured data and rapid feedback, but medicine's feedback is death and disease—slow and chaotic. The cycle from discovery to therapy must be radically shortened.

1:04:54

The fiercer the backlash, the more suppressed demand exists

Ro's early subway advertisements simply showed eligible people using GLP-1 injections for weight loss. The campaign triggered enormous backlash—people felt uncomfortable with 'injection weight loss' while underestimating obesity's health harms. He interprets fierce negative reactions as a signal: the greater the criticism, the more demand the market is suppressing. His standard for pushing through controversy is 'moral high ground'—if history will eventually vindicate the decision, amplify the message maximally. This logic drove Ro to recruit Serena Williams and Megan Fox to normalize GLP-1 use in a field burdened by stigma.

— Zach Reitano
1:14:07

A father's dying wish taught him to end with love

While Zach was at boarding school and homesick, he was short with his parents during a visit. As they left, his father—unusually—had his mother drive while he sat in the passenger seat, rolled down the window, and asked, 'Tell me you love me.' Zach immediately replied that he loved them. Years later, Zach learned his father was experiencing his fourth heart attack at that moment and thought he might not survive; he wanted his last words to be 'I love you,' not an argument. The experience became habit-forming: Zach now says 'I love you' before ending every phone call—with friends, family, and business partners alike.

Glossary

PBM
Middleman between pharmaceutical manufacturers and insurers; commonly blamed for raising drug prices and obscuring pricing.
Overton window
The range of ideas society finds acceptable; shifts as advocates push the boundaries.
Demand aggregator
Business model aggregating dispersed individual demand and matching it to suppliers, such as Costco-style membership.
Strong attract, strong repel
Company culture sufficiently clear that aligned people strongly commit while misaligned people self-select out.
