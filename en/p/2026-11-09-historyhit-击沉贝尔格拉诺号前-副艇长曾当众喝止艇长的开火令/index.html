The Submarine That Almost Changed the Outcome of the American Revolution

In 1776, David Bushnell designed the Turtle with the core elements of a modern submarine—ballast tank, ventilation pump, drilling explosives. It successfully approached HMS Howe, but couldn't find a suitable wooden section on her iron hull to drill through for the explosive charge. According to the curator, success that night would have directly altered the course of American independence.

The Royal Navy Treated Submarine Warfare as Piracy

When the Royal Navy built Holland One, its first submarine, in 1901, Admiral Arthur Wilson publicly called submarines dishonorable and unfair weapons, threatening to hang anyone who used them as pirates. The submarine was therefore commissioned in secret. Its first commanding officer complained that the control room was stuffed with wheels, levers, and valves—as if operating it required one person to simultaneously fire torpedoes, dive, and steer.

One Hour After War Was Declared, Three Ships Sank Before Breakfast

One hour after Britain declared war on Germany, the U-boat U-9 sank three British warships, earning the name ‘Three Before Breakfast’. Compared to expensive battleships, submarines were far cheaper, and this attack first demonstrated to the Royal Navy the asymmetric power of submarine warfare.

The Seaplane Experiment That Sank a Submarine With All Hands

Between the wars, M-class submarines experimented with adding seaplane hangars to the hull, launching aircraft by catapult. In one mission, the hangar hatch wasn't sealed properly; seawater flooded in, and the M2 went down with all hands lost. The salvaged note read only: ‘Help, M2 sinking, Hatch 2, open’. This tragedy later directly prompted improvements in submarine design.

In World War II, British Submarine Crews Died Five Times Faster Than Surface Sailors

During World War II, British submarine crews suffered a 36% casualty rate versus 7% for surface vessels. Danger came not only from enemy attack; underwater identification was extremely difficult, and many losses actually resulted from friendly fire. Even veteran submarines like Upolda, which sank 120,000 tons of Axis shipping, eventually disappeared on routine patrol; all 33 crew members perished, and the cause remains unknown.

The X-Crafts That Slipped Through Defensive Nets to Cripple a Battleship

To disable the German battleship Tirpitz, hidden in Norwegian fjords and protected by layers of torpedo nets and anti-submarine nets, Britain designed the X-Craft—a micro-submarine so compact that only one person could stand upright inside. Each carried two tons of explosives in side-mounted cargo containers. They threaded through the net barriers and entered the harbor to plant charges that disabled the Tirpitz for months. Churchill called destroying or even severely damaging this warship the greatest naval victory of the time.

One Second Before Opening Fire, the Deputy Commander Called Stop

In the 1982 Falkland War, nuclear submarine Conqueror was ordered to attack the Belgrano—a decision approved by the Prime Minister. When the captain ordered firing preparations, Deputy Commander Maikelaimen (麦克莱蒙) publicly shouted stop: the firing angle wasn't optimal, and waiting would give them a zero-gyro angle with far higher accuracy. After pausing several seconds, the captain calmly asked if they could fire now, and the crew's confidence immediately refocused on him.

The First Sea Lord, Gwyn Jenkins, discussing unmanned systems, noted that AI can certainly beat any human in strategy games, but a human team augmented with AI can beat pure AI. He believes submarines' future advantage doesn't lie in replacing large nuclear submarines with unmanned systems, but in combining both with well-trained crews.
