The October Revolution was not a street uprising, but a coup decided by twelve men voting in the dead of night

On the night of October 10, 1917, twelve Bolshevik Central Committee members voted 10–2 to authorize armed uprising; that night trams ran normally in Petrograd and theatres remained open, with almost no street bloodshed.

Lenin disguised as a priest infiltrates back into Petrograd

Lenin disguised himself as a priest to slip back to Petrograd and met with twelve Central Committee members at the home of Mensheviks journalist Sukhanov—considered safest precisely because Sukhanov himself was hostile to the Bolsheviks; his wife Galina was actually a secret Bolshevik sympathizer and deliberately stayed away that night. The twelve debated for seven and a half hours, Lenin repeatedly pounded the table, and finally passed the resolution for armed uprising 10–2, hastily written in a child's notebook.

The February Revolution was spontaneous, and the Bolsheviks were weak at the time

When the February Revolution erupted, Lenin was still in Switzerland on exile, writing newspaper articles with almost no followers; the Bolsheviks had neither organized nor led this revolution, and the real drivers were starving workers, deserting soldiers, and angry women taking to the streets of their own accord. Victor notes that the Bolsheviks only committed fully to supporting it after seeing that the revolution might actually overthrow the tsar and might actually succeed.

18:40

Peace, land, bread sold better than democratic institutional design

Lenin, supplied with vast German funding, ran newspapers and propaganda promoting the slogan ‘Peace, land, bread’—no more war, divide the nobility's land among peasants, have enough to eat. By contrast, the Provisional Government wanted to maintain war against Germany while building modern representative democracy, a complex program with no appeal that could fit on a leaflet.

— Victor Sebestyen
23:50

Without Lenin present, this coup would not have happened

At the October 10 meeting in the dead of night, Lenin initially stood in the minority; old comrades Zinoviev and Kamenev warned that armed uprising was gambling the revolution's future—if it failed, everyone would hang. Lenin convinced skeptics through threats of resignation and various arguments, getting them to vote yes by dawn. Trotsky later said that if Lenin had not been in Petrograd that night, the Bolshevik Revolution would never have happened.

— Victor Sebestyen
26:52

Storming the Winter Palace was essentially fabricated myth

On the night of October 24–25, trams ran normally through Petrograd, restaurants and theatres continued operating, and most residents had no idea political power had changed hands. The Bolshevik Red Guard numbered no more than 2,000 and occupied only key nodes—telephone exchange, bridges, post office, train stations. So-called ‘storming of the Winter Palace’ was largely fiction: shells from the Aurora cruiser mostly missed their targets, and when Lenin announced ‘power has been seized,’ the palace actually had not yet been truly taken.

— Victor Sebestyen
33:04

Election results expose the fiction of liberal democracy

In late November Constituent Assembly elections, Bolsheviks took only 22 percent of the vote, Socialist Revolutionaries 39 percent, liberal Constitutional Democrats just 6 percent—showing that Russians wanted not Western liberal democracy but radical left-wing transformation. Lenin allowed the Assembly to sit for 13 hours, then closed it; Russia never again had a free parliament for the next 75 years.

— Victor Sebestyen
39:14

The White armies' unpopularity made them lose the civil war

Not until mid-1920 did the Bolsheviks truly control all of Russia. The White armies' three forces lacked coordination, and their policy was reactionary—returning estates to landlords and moving backward toward the old tsarist order; the Red Army could say ‘we will keep distributing land.’ British and American aid fell far short of what had been promised, and after World War I ended, the West had no appetite to send troops back into Russia. The White armies' reactionary character became the decisive factor in the Red Army's victory.

— Victor Sebestyen
46:44

Lenin's will ordering Stalin's removal from general secretary was never read aloud

In his final years, Lenin suffered a stroke and lay bedridden, yet still struggled to dictate his political testament, explicitly ordering Stalin removed from his position as party general secretary. But this testament was never publicly read; it was kept secret for a long time, and by the time it was finally exposed, Stalin had already installed his people throughout. Victor notes that in Lenin's view, no one was fit to succeed him.

— Victor Sebestyen

Glossary

Bolsheviks
The radical faction of the Russian Social Democratic Party; the party to which Lenin belonged.
Mensheviks
A Russian left-wing party with more moderate positions than the Bolsheviks.
Soviet
Russian for ‘council’; self-organized deliberative bodies composed of workers and soldiers.
Cheka
The secret police apparatus established by the Bolsheviks; later evolved into the KGB.
Treaty of Brest-Litovsk
Peace treaty signed between Russia and Germany; ceded Ukraine, Byelorussia, and vast other territories.
