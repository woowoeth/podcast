You Can't Make a Kid Want to Work: Structure and Consequences Beat Enthusiasm

Building a work ethic doesn't run on making a kid enjoy the work. It runs on three things: rules that structure the household, privileges tied to responsibilities, and the sense that someone is counting on him — plus, when needed, letting him take his own fall at school.

You cannot make a kid want to work, only make him do it

Rena's near-reflex on hearing the letter was "this kid is only 11" — though she also admits that at 11 she hated being asked to do things just as much. Lisa's verdict on this parent is that she's half right: the instinct that the kid should be doing more is sound, but "we can't make them want to do it." She then breaks a work ethic into three sources. Some people care intensely whether the adults are pleased — in the research that's more often girls than boys. Some do the work because the work itself suits them. The boy in this letter has neither going for him: he isn't bothered by his parents being annoyed, and his interests run entirely to reading and screens. So stop fighting on the ground of getting him to want it, and work on the ground of him having to do it anyway.

5:28

Interest cannot be assigned; a kid has to collide with it

Where does intrinsic motivation come from? Lisa offers two known routes. Choice: when a kid gets to pick, motivation runs high. And interest, which cannot be required — only encountered. She describes a type she sees often in clinical work: a kid who coasts through school with his parents feeling like they have a foot on his neck, then hits one class in 11th grade that lights him up, and from that point can throw himself at it completely. She likens the encounter to a chemistry problem — the kid has to run into the thing that matches him. So this book-loving boy is not short on intrinsic motivation; his motivation simply has nothing to do with chores right now. Rather than pushing him to fall in love with cleaning the chicken coop, start by accepting that he won't.

— Lisa Damour
9:00

If you can only have one, structure beats warmth

Lisa cites the baseline finding from family research: kids do best when both warmth and structure are high. But when researchers compared the configurations 14 different ways, structure won the trade-off — kids in homes with structure and no warmth turn out "okay," and better than kids in homes with warmth and no structure. In daily terms: a kid looking thoroughly unhappy because he's been told to do something is a perfectly acceptable outcome. What matters is that the parent holds high expectations, stays predictable, and follows through. Lisa puts it bluntly: it's fine for your kid to be mad at you, as long as what you're doing is fair — "our expectations are high and we're going to hold you to them." That runs directly against the parental instinct to wonder whether you've done something wrong.

— Lisa Damour
13:24

Lock in homework habits while the work is still easy

Fifth and sixth grade, when the coursework is still light, is precisely the window for establishing homework habits. Lisa's advice is a fixed time and a fixed place, and her reasoning is practical: by the time the work gets hard, you want the routine to already be automatic. When a kid dashes something off, a parent doesn't have to demand a polish job, but should hold out for a decent job — so he first learns what it feels like to do work well. Practicing on the easy things is what makes it possible to carry that into the hard things later. She even concedes that some families get through the middle school years on exactly the foot-on-the-neck approach. That isn't a parenting failure; it's the reality of the stage.

19:34

He will find something he loves, and it won't be chores

Asked whether this is a developmental phase that will improve, Lisa says nothing in the letter strikes her as developmentally off. She also isn't worried that this boy will fail to develop strong interests: a household full of work, order and structure is often exactly what lets a kid run into something he loves at some point and then dive straight in. But she adds a plain truth: he will probably keep making a face about chores right up until the day he leaves home. Equating a distaste for chores with a lack of prospects is the parent over-reading it.

— Lisa Damour
21:09

Purpose is not a grand mission; it's being counted on

Lisa cites the research of UCLA psychologist Andrew Fuligni: a sense of purpose need not be some sweeping mission — it can be "feeling like somebody's counting on you," and that is what young people need. Applied to chores: rather than just handing out assignments, make it explicit that the household depends on him — the dog is waiting on him for a walk, the counters only get clean if he wipes them, everyone here relies on everyone else. A teenager will say out loud that he doesn't want to do it, but if he's genuinely confirmed as someone this house can't run without, some kids get activated by that. Lisa admits she often does the job herself because it's easier that way, and ends up living as the nag — which is exactly what she argues for avoiding.

— Lisa Damour
25:00

Complaining while doing it is already a passing result

Rank parental expectations in three tiers. Tier one, ideal but rare: the kid is moved by "you're an important member of this family" and picks up the sponge on his own; Lisa says plainly she wouldn't hold out that hope for most families. Tier two, more realistic: you have enough genuinely good times with your kid that when he goes slack, your displeasure spoils the mood, and he gets the thing done to get back to the easy part — "a good time with your kid is your number one discipline strategy." Tier three, completely normal: privileges tied to responsibilities — homework before play, chores before allowance. The kid may complain the whole way through and still do it in the end: complain but comply. The complaining and the compliance don't conflict.

— Lisa Damour
30:00

The work ethic your nagging produces is yours, not his

When Lisa asks kids in schools what they wish their parents knew, the line middle schoolers write again and again is: please let me deal with the consequences of my own choices. Her inference is sharp: if the only reason your kid never misses an assignment is your nagging, then the person with the work ethic is you, not him. She suggests handing the academic consequences back to the kid — assignment not turned in, he faces the teacher himself. That's especially powerful when he cares about a particular teacher: one "this isn't like you" or "you can do better than this" outperforms ten reminders from a parent. And falling on your face in middle school is cheap: botching sixth grade and learning the lesson beats being escorted all the way to ninth, by which point the transcript starts following him. She even suggests telling the teacher directly: you can hold him to a high bar, and I'm prepared for him to have some ugly moments during the middle school years.

Glossary

work ethic
The internal capacity to get work done on your own initiative, without someone standing over you.
intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation
Doing something because you like the thing itself, versus doing it for a reward or to avoid a punishment.
warmth and structure
The two dimensions of parenting research: love and acceptance on the emotional side, clarity and predictability on the rules side.
complain but comply
Letting the kid grumble all he wants while still requiring that the task get finished.
purpose
Defined by Fuligni as feeling that somebody is counting on you — not necessarily a grand mission.
nagging
A parent repeatedly reminding and hounding a kid to do what he's supposed to do.
