You Don't Have to Cure a Kid Who Won't Do Chores: Structure and Consequences Beat Passion

Building a work ethic isn't about getting your kid to love work. It's three things: a family structure with rules, privileges tied to responsibilities, and making him feel that someone is counting on him — plus, when necessary, letting him fall on his face at school.

You can't make a kid want to, only make him have to

Rena nearly blurted out "this kid is only 11" when she heard the letter, but she admitted she also hated being told to do things at 11. Lisa's verdict for this parent is "half right": the instinct that the kid should be doing more is correct, but "we can't make them want to." She then breaks out three sources of a work ethic: some people care intensely whether adults are pleased with them, and in the research that's often more girls than boys; some people do it because the activity itself suits them; and the boy in this letter has neither — he isn't afraid of annoying his parents, and his interests are entirely in reading and electronics. So don't fight over "getting him to enjoy it," work on "he still has to do it."

5:28

Interest can't be commanded, only encountered

Where does intrinsic motivation come from? Lisa gives two known paths: choice — when kids get to pick, motivation is higher; and interest — it can't be demanded, only encountered. She describes a type of kid common in her clinical practice: coasts through school, parents feel like they're stepping on the kid's neck, then in 11th grade hits a course that suddenly ignites him, and from then on he can throw himself into it fully. She likens that encounter to a chemistry problem: the kid has to collide with the right thing. So this book-loving boy isn't lacking intrinsic motivation, it's just that his motivation is temporarily unrelated to chores. Rather than forcing him to love cleaning the chicken coop, first accept that he won't.

— Lisa Damour
9:00

When you can only pick one, structure beats warmth

Lisa cites the basic finding of family research: high warmth and high structure together produce the best outcomes for kids. But when the research compared configurations 14 different ways, it found that if you can only pick one, structure wins — families with structure but no warmth turn out "fine," and better than families with warmth but no structure. In daily life: a kid being visibly unhappy about being made to do something is completely acceptable; what matters is that the parent keeps expectations high, predictable, and follows through. Lisa puts it bluntly: it's fine for your kid to be angry at you, as long as what you're doing is fair — "we have high expectations, and we will hold you to them." This is the exact opposite of the instinct of parents who worry they've done something wrong.

— Lisa Damour
13:24

Homework habits have to be locked in while the work is still easy

Fifth and sixth grade, while the work is still easy, is precisely the window for building homework habits. Lisa recommends a fixed time and place for homework, for a practical reason: by the time the work gets hard, you want the action to already be automatic. When a kid rushes through it, parents don't have to demand it be "polished to a shine," but they should insist on a decent job — let him first feel what doing a job well feels like; practice on easy things, and later he can bring it to hard things. She even admits that some families in middle school get through it by "foot on the neck," and that this isn't a parenting failure, it's a stage-of-life reality.

19:34

Kids will find a passion, but it won't be chores

Asked whether this is a developmental stage and whether it will get better, Lisa says nothing in the letter makes her think anything is developmentally abnormal. She also isn't worried that this boy won't develop strong interests later: a family full of work, order and structure tends to lead a kid to "collide" with something he loves at some point, and then dive in headfirst. But she adds a blunt truth: he will probably keep a look of disgust on his face about chores until the day he leaves home. Equating "hates doing work" with "has no future" is the parent's overreading.

— Lisa Damour
21:09

Purpose isn't a mission, it's someone counting on you

Lisa cites UCLA psychologist Andrew Fuligni's research: purpose isn't necessarily a grand mission, it's "feeling that someone is counting on you" — that's what young people need. Applied to chores: rather than just assigning tasks, make "this family depends on you" explicit — the dog is waiting for you to walk it, the counter only gets clean if you wipe it, everyone in the family depends on each other. Teenagers say "I don't want to do it," but if it's genuinely confirmed that "this family can't run without you," some of them get activated. Lisa admits she herself often does things for her kids because "it's easier to just do it myself," and ends up turning herself into a nag — exactly what she argues against.

— Lisa Damour
25:00

A kid who complies while complaining is already a passing result

Rank parental expectations from high to low in three tiers. Tier one (ideal but rare): the kid is moved by "you're an important member of this family" and picks up the sponge on his own; Lisa says outright she wouldn't count on this for most families. Tier two (more realistic): you've had enough fun with the kid normally that when he slacks off, your displeasure spoils the mood, and he hurries to get it done so he can get back to the easy times — "good times with your kid are the number one discipline strategy." Tier three (completely normal): privileges tied to responsibilities — homework done before play, chores done before allowance. The kid can complain the whole time but ultimately complies — complain but comply; complaining and complying don't conflict.

— Lisa Damour
30:00

The work ethic you nag into existence is yours, not his

Lisa often asks kids at school "what do you wish your parents knew," and one line middle schoolers write over and over is: "Please let me bear the consequences of my own choices." Her inference is sharp: if your kid only never misses homework because you nag, then the one with the work ethic is you, not him. She recommends handing academic consequences back to the kid — homework not turned in, he faces the teacher himself; especially when he cares about a particular teacher, a single "this isn't like you" or "you're capable of better" does more than ten parental reminders. And the cost of stumbling in middle school is low: blowing it in sixth grade and learning the lesson beats a parent escorting him all the way to ninth grade, when the transcript starts following him. She even suggests telling the teacher directly: you can hold him to high standards, I'm prepared for him to have some ugly moments in middle school.

Glossary

work ethic
The internal capacity to complete work proactively without anyone watching over you.
intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation
Doing something for the love of it versus doing it for a reward or to avoid punishment.
warmth and structure
The two major dimensions in parenting research: emotional love and acceptance, and clarity and predictability in rules.
complain but comply
Allowing a kid to grumble while still requiring the task to get done.
purpose
Fuligni's definition: "feeling that someone is counting on you," not necessarily a grand mission.
nagging
A parent repeatedly reminding and pestering a kid to finish what they're supposed to do.
