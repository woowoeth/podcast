Buying Syngenta for $43 Billion: The Peak and the End of China's Buying Spree

China's golden age of overseas M&A began with an official signal in 2009 and ended with the 2017 turn against globalisation; Syngenta was both the high point and the finish line. Buying cheap matters less than buying right — integration decides success or failure.

The starting point of the era was an official statement

People in the circle generally hold that this wave of overseas M&A began in 2009 and ended in 2017. In July 2009 Beijing held the Global Think Tank Summit, where then head of SASAC Li Rongrong said in his keynote that Chinese companies' economic strength had risen markedly but their global share remained very limited, out of proportion to China's national power, and that China would create a more favourable environment to actively support and encourage companies, especially central SOEs, to go out and conduct cross-border M&A. Wang Wei says there had already been uncoordinated private moves before that, but nothing systematic; only after the official signal could everyone start doing this.

— Wang Wei
22:00

Lenovo and Geely share four things in common

Wang Wei considers these two deals benchmarks no one can ignore. First, before the deals both companies were confined to the domestic market with unremarkable industry positions — Geely was not even a first-tier domestic brand before buying Volvo. Second, the acquired businesses themselves were not favoured by the market: IBM's PC business had low margins, and Volvo's passenger car unit had already been passed on by Ford. Third, neither deal was favoured at the time; people thought the price was high and might drag down the existing business. Fourth, integration was measured in three to five years — Lenovo finished in 2004 but results only showed in 2009 — and in the end both became Fortune Global 500 companies.

— Wang Wei
32:12

Western buyers cut staff first; Chinese buyers keep management

Wang Wei explains why overseas sellers welcome Chinese buyers: when Western companies buy each other, the first thing they do is cut staff and get rid of management, because the synergy usually comes from saving on salaries and costs, possibly hundreds of millions of dollars. The first thing a Chinese company does is talk management retention, because Chinese companies are unfamiliar with overseas markets and face many challenges. This is the reputation Chinese buyers have earned: willing to pay, willing to invest, willing to keep management.

— Wang Wei
37:50

There are no bad deals, only bad prices

Wang Wei says there is a saying in investment circles: there are no bad deals, only bad prices. It is always about value for money — even junk has room to make money if it is cheap. But companies look at things differently: a deal must serve a strategic purpose, so figure out the purpose first and then decide the path; M&A is only a means. If the right price happens to solve my problem, it is a good deal. He likes to compare M&A to marriage: only the two people know whether they suit each other; outsiders see that the degrees do not match, but what I value may be that she is a good wife.

— Wang Wei
43:24

Syngenta was once the jewel in the crown

When Wang Wei entered the industry in 2009 he already looked at the Syngenta deal, but at the time Chinese companies could not afford it, and the company was hailed as the jewel in the crown of the industry, described as untouchable and not for sale. The industry had six proprietary agrochemical companies: Syngenta, Monsanto, Dow AgroSciences, DuPont Pioneer, Bayer's agrochemical business and BASF's agrochemical business — all jewels in the crown. The second tier was generic manufacturers, whose gross margins fell from around 40% to the low 20s. He turned instead to non-patent agrochemical companies, such as Australia's Nufarm and Makhteshim Agan, the latter renamed Adama after being acquired by ChemChina.

— Wang Wei
52:13

Four stages of M&A, and execution is the easiest

Wang Wei divides M&A into four stages: defining strategy (the original intention), screening targets, execution, and integration. The stage everyone focuses on is execution, because all the intermediaries come in — investment banks, accountants, lawyers, consultants — and if you fail everyone curses you, while if you succeed everyone blesses you. But he thinks the strategy stage is the hardest, because it is the origin, and finding the original intention is very hard; harder than finding it is not losing it. Execution is in fact the easiest, because a large number of intermediaries and professionals come in to help you.

— Wang Wei
1:12:40

Syngenta was both the peak and the curtain call

Wang Wei says the Syngenta deal was the end of the era, because the era reached a peak and it is hard to imagine a larger deal afterwards. First, the macro environment changed: after Trump took office, the tide turned against globalisation, with talk of the China threat and US-China confrontation, and every country had government review mechanisms and no longer approved Chinese companies buying sensitive assets and businesses. Second, failed investment cases appeared in this wave, with some companies using the opportunity to move funds and even assets overseas, causing capital flight — HNA and Xiamen Huaxin became hiding places for offshore assets. But he thinks the first point matters more.

— Wang Wei
1:22:45

Two reasons China's M&A culture is thin

Wang Wei sees two reasons China's M&A culture is not vigorous. First, SOEs are all organised uniformly by the state, so there is no need for them to buy each other. Second, well-run private companies are usually still run by the first generation, and that generation has a very strong personality and ego; two big bosses find it hard to recognise each other — each is sure he is better, so why should I sell to you — and it is hard to find synergy. Abroad there is a professional-manager culture, many companies have passed through several generations and are listed, with no absolute likes or dislikes: business is business.

— Wang Wei

Glossary

Synergy
The incremental profit created by combining after a merger — one plus one greater than two.
management retention
Keeping the original management in place to run the business after acquisition, a common practice for Chinese companies.
offtake
An arrangement in which equity is invested to lock in a fixed supply chain and preferential prices.
EV/EBITDA
Enterprise value divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, a standard international valuation metric.
DCF
A model that discounts future cash flows to estimate a company's value.
HSE
Health, safety and environment — the environmental, health and production-safety issues common to chemical companies.
