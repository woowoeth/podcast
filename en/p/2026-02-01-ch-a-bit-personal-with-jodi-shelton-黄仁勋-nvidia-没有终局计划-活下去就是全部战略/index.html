Jensen Huang: NVIDIA has no endgame plan; survival is the entire strategy

Jensen Huang says the company never had an endgame; ‘staying in business’ is the only strategy. What he values most is not IQ but ‘character’—something that can't be tested in an interview, only forged through the company's suffering.

10:23

Belief demands first principles, not others' words

Jensen Huang recalls telling a journalist at 29 that ‘NVIDIA is neither a church nor a prison’. He explains his early confidence came from a methodology: before believing something, decompose the reasoning to first principles and regularly verify whether those principles still hold. If the principles are anchored in physical or real-world facts, once premises change you must reassess and adjust accordingly—not simply believe what others say.

— Jensen Huang
18:51

Company success is built on character, not just intelligence

Jensen Huang says NVIDIA is actually the smallest GPU company in the world by volume—‘anyone else makes more GPUs than we do’—but the real moat is the team's ability to maintain integrity under extreme pressure. He cites the Grace Blackwell production process, which ‘nearly broke the company's backbone’, yet no one left or was blamed during that test. This team culture that remains intact after such pressure, he believes, is more important than technology and intelligence, and genuinely hard to replicate.

— Jensen Huang
25:16

Accountability exists for mistakes, but no one gets fired

He states plainly that in teamwork ‘someone must be accountable for dropping the ball’, and the company doesn't shy from this. But over all these years no one has been fired for dropping the ball, including himself, who has dropped it many times. This culture of tolerance and learning from mistakes is, he believes, the core reason NVIDIA has navigated crises repeatedly without people leaving each time.

— Jensen Huang
41:01

Being twenty is fast but teaches no wisdom

On the debate of whether to build a business or enjoy life in your twenties, Jensen Huang argues that your mind is faster then, your focus stronger, but wisdom, discernment, and long-term thinking ability are nearly impossible to learn—acquired only through genuinely experiencing suffering, fear, and vulnerability. He says his thinking speed now is slower than before, but with accumulated experience and pattern recognition, he can actually arrive at correct answers faster. ‘I could spar with a 20-year-old all day and they'd not come close to me’.

— Jensen Huang
52:25

AI makes problems smaller, humans busier, not jobless

Jensen Huang presents a counterintuitive claim: the faster computation gets, the smaller problems look—like how jets made the world smaller. He predicts this won't cause joblessness; instead it will embolden humans to solve problems previously shelved as too expensive or hard, so ‘we'll be busier than ever’, because human imagination will keep generating new problems to consume the extra compute. He further predicts that in five years, ‘it's more likely 100% of work will change than 50% of jobs will disappear’.

— Jensen Huang
1:14:25

True intelligence is seeing around the corner, not raw IQ

Asked ‘who is the smartest person you've met’, Jensen Huang refuses to answer directly because traditional technical smartness is exactly what AI is conquering first—software programming was once thought the ultimate intellectual work, yet it's the domain AI cracked first. He offers his own definition: true smartness stands at the intersection of technical acuity and human empathy, can infer from ‘vibe’ what goes unsaid, see problems beyond the corner in advance, and such a person might score poorly on the SAT.

— Jensen Huang
1:16:30

CEOs are the company's most fragile people, not strongest

Jensen Huang says the world may cast the CEO as a powerful leader, but it's the opposite—he's the only person in the company who can accomplish nothing without others' help, entirely dependent on everyone's goodwill. He calls this condition ‘solitude in the mind’: facing hard problems in transition, he often must spend thousands of hours in internal dialogue to think them through.

— Jensen Huang
1:21:44

NVIDIA was built by ignorance, not knowledge

Jensen Huang says if he could choose again, he'd pick his twenties back then, not his twenties now, because ‘ignorance is a superpower’—if he'd known how hard building NVIDIA would be, with all the setbacks and disappointments it would bring packed ahead of him, he would never have done it. He believes optimists are optimistic precisely because they're ‘oblivious to the truth’, while this generation's youth, drowning in information, more easily turn cynical.

— Jensen Huang
