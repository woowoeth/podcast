How Do We Grow Old? Glimpses of Real Old Age in China Through "Qianlang"

After digging into roughly fifty families, the director found that growing old together from youth to white hair is rare, not the norm; most marriages are lukewarm or worse. Many people of that generation didn't enter relationships for love but were pushed by social expectations, and muddled through caring for each other for a lifetime.

4:08

Supporting yourself through children no longer works — you have to appoint your own guardian

In the first episode, Gu Nainai, a childless widow in Shanghai, dies in a nursing home. Before her death she had made a voluntary guardianship notarization with notary Li Chengyang, so he handles her affairs. She left no will, only repeated oral statements that she wanted to donate her estate to society and not give it to relatives, but this wish was never fixed in legal form. Li Chengyang chooses to follow the law, digs out relatives she hadn't seen in years from the archives, hands over five million, and then suggests taking two hundred thousand of it to donate to a fund helping old people with no one to rely on; the other side agrees. He insists on finding a solution because many solitary elderly people in the community die leaving empty apartments that no one claims, and by law they can't simply be confiscated, so they sit empty for years.

12:14

"Were they sleeping together" decides who gets the estate

An elderly man with a terminal illness leaves his entire estate to the woman who has long cared for him, not a cent to his biological daughter. The notary office debates fiercely, and one point of contention is whether the two were sleeping together: if they weren't, she is just a nanny or live-in helper, and leaving an estate to a nanny is morally acceptable; once the two have a sexual relationship, it no longer fits propriety. And the caregiver herself still has a marriage that has had no contact for years and effectively doesn't exist. The daughter eventually shows up, saying it's not about the money, just to give herself closure; the money goes to the caregiver. The same father is a responsible partner to the caregiver and, to the daughter, an out-and-out scumbag, a terrible and absent father — both things exist at once.

17:17

Older people seeking companions increasingly don't register the marriage

Li Kangmei and Lao Li were together eighteen years without ever registering, and with no de facto marriage relationship. The program says this is far more common than people imagine: after divorce or losing a first marriage, many people choose not to marry when entering a new relationship, because what they actually want is someone to keep them company and care for them, and behind that is the deep loneliness of old age; the form isn't necessarily that important to the person. But a large number of viewers in Tencent's danmu felt Li Kangmei wasn't worth it, and the family's line — you two have feelings, but those feelings can't help with anything practical — was treated as clear-eyed realism. The program calls this the water level of attitudes: love doesn't matter, or ranks very low; objective material conditions matter more.

29:23

You only know an old person's daily difficulties if you live with them

The host talks about his grandmother in her nineties: his aunts and uncles live downstairs, grandmother lives upstairs, and every time he goes back he insists on staying in her apartment, because only by living together do you truly learn her daily situation. For example, toilet paper with a hard cardboard core: for someone with very poor eyesight, hanging it into the bathroom dispenser is extremely difficult, so she usually gives up and leaves the roll on the counter, and the paper is permanently wet from water. Details like this you only know if you actually live together. Recently grandmother hired a live-in nanny, something she had long resisted because she didn't want a stranger to see the unseemly parts of her life, including incontinence; now she occasionally says that living too long isn't necessarily a good thing.

34:24

"I'm not sleeping well" rather than "I'm depressed"

Medical anthropologist Arthur Kleinman found that today's young people have a rich psychological vocabulary and can quickly say I'm depressed, I'm anxious, but the older generation still expresses psychological needs through somatization — saying I haven't been sleeping well lately, I have a bit of a headache, rather than saying the poor sleep is because I'm thinking too much, a bit depressed or overly anxious. Because in our culture it's easier to talk about physical pain; anyone can have a headache or a fever, there's no stigma; once you say depression or anxiety, that's another layer of stigma. Kleinman studied more than a hundred people in Changsha, Hunan, who had lived through the Cultural Revolution and later developed long-term chronic symptoms, and first proposed the concept of somatization.

45:29

A pitch that got killed: she wanted to play mahjong

Fan Shiguang describes a pitch he killed himself: the most diligent auntie in a hospice ward, whose husband has terminal bladder cancer, takes the bus every day from a home far away, arriving at seven or eight in the morning and leaving at five or six in the evening, like going to work. At first he couldn't see her love, only her repeatedly asking doctors and nurses how much longer he had. Later he learned her son had died of a heart attack two years earlier — a family that lost its only child; the tears may have run dry two years before. When someone asked what she wanted to do in the future, she said she wanted to play mahjong, because her husband had never let her. The first scene shot after the husband's death is her hiring workers to smash the legs off a two-meter bed one by one with a sledgehammer, and putting a mahjong table in its place.

— Fan Shiguang
51:33

Growing old together isn't the norm, it's rare

Fan Shiguang says the team did field research on roughly fifty families, and going from youth to truly growing old together, to the very end, isn't the norm — it's rare; most marriages are lukewarm or even bad. In season one's "Lovers" episode they filmed an old man with Alzheimer's and his wife: the man loses control and hits his wife, the daughter sends him to a nursing home specializing in Alzheimer's, and the old woman sneaks off to see him every few days; the old man grabs her hand and says today is a good chance, you must take me and run. After the man becomes seriously ill, the old woman can finally wait on him — cutting his nails, washing his hair and feet — and two weeks later he dies. More than a month later they visit the old woman again; she cries for over ten minutes, then says she looked down on him her whole life, and just muddled through a lifetime with him.

— Fan Shiguang
1:04:42

The label in your mouth is a prison

In season one there's a story about a ninety-five-year-old learning to drive and taking the license exam, and Fan Shiguang instinctively says "but he is ninety-five after all," then immediately realizes: in that sentence, the ninety-five-year-old has already been stripped of his rights — as if he doesn't deserve them. The Ministry of Public Security's laws and regulations never say a ninety-five-year-old can't learn to drive, but in that verbal violence, he stripped the old man of his rights. When the film aired, danmu was full of people questioning whether this old man could drive on the road, whether this old man was too selfish. He says average human lifespan has risen from the fifties to seventy or eighty, and in another fifty years may reach ninety or a hundred — why can't consciousness keep up; once you stick on a label, it's like locking a person in prison, and word after word is violence.

— Fan Shiguang

Glossary

Voluntary guardianship
An adult, while of sound mind, designates a guardian in writing who will make decisions on their behalf once they lose capacity.
Somatization
Expressing psychological distress through physical symptoms, e.g. saying only that you're not sleeping well or have a bit of a headache.
