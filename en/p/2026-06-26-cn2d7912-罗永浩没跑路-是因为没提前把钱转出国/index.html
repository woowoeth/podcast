Pursuing fandom requires 3,600 yuan just to sit on a bench far away

Invested 3,600 yuan for a black-market ticket to see a live show recording—3,000 for admission, another 600 to sit in the fan section, still only a small bench in the back row. Five-plus hours of taping. Phones and bags confiscated. By 9 p.m. end time, elaborately dressed fans with LV and Chanel bags sat roadside eating street snacks, having gone without food the entire session. The gap between the aspirational vision and the actual texture of fandom became visible.

Campus loans recruit new victims by hiding risk behind tiny gift incentives

In a six-person dorm, one roommate spent freely on sneakers, then borrowed from a campus loan app. Unable to repay, he convinced bed-1 to open an account to pay him back. Bed-1 couldn't repay, so recruited bed-2. The chain continued until all six beds were trapped by the same debt for two to three years, taking ten months after graduation to fully clear. Why everyone enrolled: senior promoters required zero cost—just download an app, register for a task, get a small gift. First-year students nearly universally pre-installed it. The actual risk surfaced much later.

1:09:27

One keystroke sent 3 million yuan; character determined what the company did next

While running operations at Zhihu (知乎), needed to distribute end-of-year points to creators. The backend confused points with yuan denominations—sent 3 million actual yuan instead of 30,000 yuan in points. Realized within five or six minutes. First instinct: froze at the computer, hands shaking while opening his bank app to confirm he had only 84,000 yuan in the account. His thought: if he had to repay that alone, it was impossible. One hour later, the tech team reversed the transaction. The company fined him 80,000 yuan and issued a public apology, neither firing nor formally disciplining him.

1:21:37

Physical inventory counts revealed billions in debt; forecasts never would have

On the day the company initiated bankruptcy liquidation, finance reported debt of approximately 3 billion. Luo had already mentally prepared to repay it over five to ten years. But phone manufacturing requires hundreds to thousands of discrete components—missing even one prevents assembly of any finished units, which blocks cash conversion. With thousands of parts sitting in inventory, a single missing component ruins an entire batch. Finance kept recounting daily: 500 million more today, another billion tomorrow. The tally kept compounding. Final count: 600 to 700 million. The debt wasn't imposed once; it was discovered through inventory.

— Luo Yonghao
1:23:39

Without assets abroad, escape becomes impossible; staying is the only choice

Luo observes that entrepreneurs who actually flee usually pre-position assets overseas—once the domestic situation becomes untenable, they leave. He transferred nothing. So where would he go? There would be nothing to gain. He considered personal bankruptcy through specialized lawyers, but that path required him to litigate full-time for one to two years, with no freedom to leave. The more difficult factor: many creditors were small suppliers and individual small business owners. If his company invoked limited liability to escape, those smaller enterprises would likely go bankrupt. That personal, human pressure mattered more than the legal algebra. He decided to stay and bear it.

— Luo Yonghao
2:19:09

Lent 17 million, recovered 1.7 million; faith in people somehow survived intact

When a friend faced economic crime charges and needed urgent funds, Luo tried to legitimately call in old debts. Finance and legal walked through the account and discovered 17-plus million in loans he wouldn't recover—far more than his rough estimate of a few million. He says 90 percent were never deliberately withheld; his friends were struggling themselves. When he lent, he'd already treated it as a gift. Years later, a few friends actually repaid, which reinforced his near-naive faith that people ultimately honor relationships over money.

— Luo Yonghao
2:24:31

Lending through strangers lets you keep friendships while demanding repayment anyway

A business-owning friend taught him a trick: if a friend asks for a loan and you want to help but fear non-repayment, don't lend directly yourself. Find another friend who doesn't know the borrower and broker the loan through them. When the due date arrives and payment doesn't, that third party can legitimately demand repayment, write promissory notes, even litigate—you've still solved your friend's problem, but you don't have to be the villain. He also mentioned that when collecting his own debts, he manufactures stories (family conflict) that reframe repayment as the debtor helping him—that's how you manage to ask without tearing the relationship.

— Luo Yonghao
2:30:22

Debt collectors who sit in offices become part of the debtor's daily routine

Xianyujiang, who worked collections for a Fujian guarantee company, described the actual mechanics: the company charged interest on bridge loans far exceeding the legal limit of four times the principal. When a borrower defaulted, they stationed someone in that company's office daily, set up a typical Fujian-boss tea table, made small talk, and applied steady pressure—nothing dramatic, just persistent presence asking for priority repayment. Over three to four months, they recovered 2.9+ million of the debt. He described the constant contact as creating an almost intimate, ambiguous relationship between collector and debtor—the debtor's company eventually failed.
