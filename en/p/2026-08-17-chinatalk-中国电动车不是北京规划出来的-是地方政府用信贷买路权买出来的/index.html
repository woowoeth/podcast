China's EV Industry Wasn't Planned — Local Credit Bought Its Way In

This episode takes half the credit for the rise of Chinese automakers out of the hands of central industrial policy: the real engine was local governments trading loans and equity for factories, and entrepreneurs using connections to route around the SOE licensing regime — at the cost of today's overcapacity that nobody can unwind.

5:12

The number "150,000 vehicles" wrote the automaker roster

In the 1990s China's annual passenger car sales were only about 500,000 units, a scale comparable to Poland at the time or Vietnam today, yet every province except Tibet had its own car plant, with roughly 130 automakers counted in the mid-1990s. The reason wasn't absurd: under the planned allocation system, if you wanted cars you had to build them yourself. Foreign partners like Volkswagen told Chinese regulators that a car plant needed at least 150,000 units a year to be efficient, so "150,000" became the magic number in policy circles — with a market under one million, only four to six players could fit, and that simple arithmetic directly produced the "three bigs and three smalls" designated licensing list.

11:19

Chery isn't a private company — it's a prefecture-level city's SOE

Chery is not an SOE of any major province, major city, or the center, but of Wuhu (芜湖), a city in Anhui. In the early 1990s Wuhu couldn't find a pillar industry, and discovered that a township enterprise on the city outskirts, using engineers from a Hunan ordnance factory and Korean KD kits, was producing three to five thousand cars a year selling for about $10,000 (70,000-80,000 RMB), far below the joint-venture cars of the three bigs and three smalls, making it Wuhu's first company to break 100 million RMB in revenue. The city concluded from this that "making cars can work." It first tried partnering with FAW and failed, then used 200 million RMB from selling the Saiwen plant (later Conch) plus bank loans coordinated by the province to buy an engine production line from Wales in the UK — because the lesson FAW had taught them was that without your own engine you can't build your own car.

— Fan Minglu
21:27

The barrier back then wasn't technology or capital — it was the license

For its first few years Chery couldn't get a central production license and could only sell within Anhui, and Anhui's market alone couldn't support a car company. Their solution was to sell cars in Chengdu but have buyers accept Anhui license plates. The first to crack wasn't consumers but the Chengdu Public Security Bureau, which simply notified buyers they had to register locally. This detail shows the barrier back then wasn't technology or capital but administrative permission itself — the cost of routing around the license was high enough to require inventing this absurd transaction structure.

— Fan Minglu
35:34

The acquisition money wasn't Geely's — it was local banks'

Ford's condition was "if you can raise the money, I'll sell," so Geely's core work through all of 2009 was assembling about $2 billion. Investment bankers involved in the deal say Geely talked to as many as 60 cities — out of roughly 300 prefecture-level cities nationwide, one in five. The pitch was to show localities Volvo's next-generation product line (the eventual XC60 and XC90): I'll let you build some of these models, in exchange for the "five fingers" — 500 million RMB, about $70-80 million at the time, as the ticket to ride, in the form of local bank credit or equity injected into an SPV. Put together four or five cities and the system turns over. The biggest early supporter was actually Beijing, with a letter from a Beijing municipal leader to Ford's CEO as endorsement, but at the end of 2009 BAIC objected to the city backing a competitor, and Beijing pulled out.

— Fan Minglu
41:44

The NDRC gave permission, not money

Geely didn't force its way in: it waited until it had the NDRC's "unwritten approval" before moving. The earliest candidates to buy Volvo in 2007-08 were SOEs like Changan, and the SOEs ultimately all said they didn't have the money and it wasn't a priority; only after the NDRC confirmed no SOE wanted to buy did it clear the way for Geely. The counterexample is another Sichuan private company that wanted to buy Hummer and announced the deal on its own without approval — regulators in Beijing only learned about it from newspapers and TV, and the deal was killed outright. After approval, the center's attitude was: you sort out the finances yourself. Policy banks like the Export-Import Bank of China refused loans at first, and only once Geely had assembled all the money did China Development Bank agree to lend for the subsequent plant construction.

— Fan Minglu
55:01

BYD wasn't selling cars — it was selling localities big projects

"Ten Cities, Thousand Vehicles" handed targets to localities, and BYD found arbitrage in that: localities needed deliverables, it needed sales. In partner cities like Shenzhen and Changsha it swapped entire taxi fleets to its own EVs (the Changsha government sold a bus plant outright to BYD in 2008-09), and it set up its own financing company to help taxi drivers and bus operators buy the cars; Shenzhen had no Olympics or World Expo, so it used the Universiade to stage its own demonstration project, with BYD accounting for a quarter of the 2,011 vehicles in operation. The more crucial chip was that it created a dedicated rail transit division — telling local governments that if you want more, I can build you a light rail line, letting you hand your superiors a "big project." In the 2010s its monthly sales were only about 30,000, earning it the nickname "Thirty Thousand," and this government-enterprise exchange is what kept it alive.

— Fan Minglu
1:04:06

The Hefei model made three to four times its money; latecomers got only capacity

NIO was initially very reluctant to take local government money because of the many attached conditions (requirements to invest a certain amount locally, etc.). After its cash broke in late 2019 and its US stock price collapsed, it had no choice. Beijing once again pulled out at the last minute because of BAIC's objection, and Hefei stepped in with about $1 billion (7 billion RMB) in equity, exiting the stock entirely about a year later at a three-to-four-times return. The problem came afterward: the Hefei model was taken nationwide as a template, just as the NDRC had already stopped approving new vehicle capacity in 2020-21. Guangdong province and Guangzhou injected about 2 billion RMB into XPeng, Ningbo injected about 10 billion RMB into Geely's Zeekr, all hoping to replicate that "three-to-four-times exit," but after 2021 Chinese ADRs and EV valuations were hit simultaneously, the exit path was gone while the money had already turned into capacity.

— Fan Minglu
1:12:13

Changan and Dongfeng pushed for a year, stuck on two cities

The other route to absorbing overcapacity is SOE consolidation, but that route is harder than it looks. Since last year the center has had not just rumors but more concrete moves, requiring the relatively successful Changan and Dongfeng to merge; after nearly 12 months of pushing there's no result, because their two home cities, Chongqing and Wuhan, each refuse to give ground, and it's now basically shelved. This contrasts with the center's decades of successful SOE mergers in other industries, and explains why autos has never been a centrally managed industry: in the 1980s it tried setting up a national-level company to oversee all of China's car plants, which survived only a few years before dissolving, because localities wouldn't hand over their car plants.

— Fan Minglu
1:28:33

Exports are twice Japan's peak, and that curve can't hold

At this year's pace, China's full-year auto exports could reach about 12 million units. The reference point is Japan's export peak (around 1986) of about six to seven million units, which triggered the US-Japan and Europe-Japan trade wars — China is already approaching double that. So Fan Minglu (樊明路) argues this curve is unsustainable: you can't imagine exporting 15 million next year. The EU is patching the holes, and proposals to extend high tariffs from pure EVs to plug-in hybrids have already appeared. The real way out is localized production, and the opportunity lies precisely in European automakers' own idle capacity: European carmakers are being hit by Chinese cars (not just EVs but combustion-engine vehicles too), over-expanded because they overestimated demand during the pandemic, and face younger people buying fewer cars or even getting fewer driver's licenses — so the European market may be facing not stagnation but decline.

— Fan Minglu

Glossary

three bigs and three smalls
The passenger car designated-production list set in the late 1980s; anyone not on it had no production license.
township and village enterprise (TVE)
Collectively owned firms that emerged in the 1980s; Chery's predecessor started out making cars from a TVE.
10 city thousand vehicle project
A demonstration program from about 2011 requiring pilot cities to operate a thousand electric buses or taxis within two to three years.
Hefei model
Local governments injecting equity to rescue EV startups, then exiting in the stock market at a profit.
special purpose vehicle (SPV)
A shell company set up for a single acquisition; Geely used one to receive equity funds injected by various cities.
