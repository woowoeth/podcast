The 'Foreign Plot' Evidence That Toppled Danton Was Actually Lies Concocted by Two Corrupt Officials Deflecting Blame

Danton, leader of the French Revolution's moderate faction, went to the guillotine. The fatal evidence came from two deputies who, to hide their own embezzlement of public funds, each fabricated 'foreign conspiracy' charges against their political enemies, which Robespierre assembled into a single conspiracy network.

Danton's Corruption: From Widespread Practice to Political Liability

In the Revolution's early years, bribes and real estate deals among deputies were commonplace; Danton's purchase of land in his hometown of Arcis with money of unclear origin was unremarkable at the time. What truly changed his position was Robespierre's cultivation of his image as ‘incorruptible’—as republican virtue became the political standard, Danton's unchanged behavior shifted from a widespread practice to a glaring liability, his dealings starting to look outdated. This is why charges of corruption and moral decay could later be established so easily.

17:51

Robespierre Was Not the Dictator; Real Power Lay Elsewhere

The Committee of Public Safety had 12 members. One faction—Robespierre, Saint-Just, Couthon—spent their time discussing virtue and moral authority, while the machinery of state actually ran through military bureaucrats like Lazare Carnot (managing the army) and Robert Lindet (overseeing grain supplies), who were responsible for winning wars and maintaining administration. When Robespierre waxed poetic about a republic of virtue, these men were quietly signing logistics orders beneath the table and rolling their eyes. The episode emphasizes that this split would later become more acute.

— Dominic
19:56

Law of Suspects: Doing Nothing Was Also Evidence of Treason

The Law of Suspects decreed that any words, associations, or writings suggesting you might be an enemy of the people or freedom constituted a crime. Among the ‘suspicious characteristics’ listed by the Paris Commune, the most absurd was ‘displaying excessive severity to appear patriotic’—too zealous support for the Revolution looked suspicious. The list explicitly identified people who ‘neither opposed liberty, but also never did anything for liberty’ as enemies of the people. In other words: opposing the Revolution was a crime; appearing too supportive was also a crime; there was almost no safe middle ground.

The Death Toll of the Terror—And the Paradox That It Actually Worked

Historian Timothy Tackett gives an official execution count of 17,000; adding those who starved or died of illness in prison brings it to roughly 40,000; including purges in regions like the Vendée, total deaths ran into the hundreds of thousands. But the episode stresses this was not aimless slaughter—the Terror was an emergency response to the total crisis of summer 1793, and it actually worked: the Maximum Price Law stabilized the currency and Paris's grain supply, and the French army won several critical battles that autumn against Prussia, Austria, and the Federalist rebels. This is the backdrop for the later question ‘If we won, why not stop now?’

The 'Conspiracy Evidence' That Brought Down Danton Came From Two Corrupt Officials Protecting Themselves

Fabre d'Églantine, who designed the new calendar, reported to Robespierre and Saint-Just that there was a foreign spy network funded by Britain, infiltrating clubs and committees, orchestrated by Pitt to manipulate the Hébertists into attacking the government. But this entire account was fabricated by Fabre himself—his true purpose was to cover up his own embezzlement, together with others, of the liquidation funds of the French East India Company. Almost simultaneously, Deputy Chabot, implicated in the same East India Company scandal, reported yet another ‘conspiracy’ to Robespierre. Robespierre stitched together these two unrelated yet entirely fictitious accounts, concluding they revealed ‘the same spy network’—and Fabre happened to be a friend of Danton, who was himself known for corruption and attacks on the Terror.

58:53

Robespierre's Logic: If You Ever Criticized, You Were Already No Citizen

In his February 5 speech, Robespierre said that without terror, virtue has no power; but as long as you are a loyal patriot and citizen, terror will never touch you. Historian R. R. Palmer points out that this formula is actually a recipe for permanent terror: once you have criticized or doubted, you have already placed yourself outside the republic, ceased to be a citizen, and thus have no rights whatsoever. The episode argues this is the blueprint for twentieth-century totalitarian states, even though Robespierre himself was utterly convinced he was building a better world.

Hébert's Street Uprising: The First Time No One Answered the Call

On March 4, 1794, Hébert announced at the Cordeliers Club that 73 moderate deputies must be purged, draping the portrait of the Declaration of the Rights of Man in black cloth—a signal that had always triggered uprising in the past. But this time, only the Cordeliers' own district responded across Paris; the Paris Commune didn't even summon the National Guard. The episode interprets this as the first time street radicalism in the Revolution hit a ceiling—every previous leftward surge had gone smoothly, but this time the wind didn't fill the sails.

— Tom
1:19:30

In the Courtroom, Danton's Voice Broke the Procedure

During the trial, Danton spoke so loudly that people on the street outside could hear him; the prosecutor Fouquier-Tinville rang his bell several times to silence him without effect. He ignored the judge and jury, speaking only to the gallery, saying his voice would be heard across France and the world. On the third day he demanded to call a long list of witnesses; when the judge refused, the courtroom erupted in chaos. Saint-Just hastily invented charges that Danton and his allies were plotting a prison escape and that Lucile Desmoulins was also involved in the conspiracy, then pushed the National Assembly to pass an emergency decree overnight—‘defendants whose conduct insults justice can be expelled from court’—before finally silencing Danton.
