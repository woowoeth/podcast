Bin Laden wasn't a poor kid who made good — he was the son of a Saudi royal-family builder

Osama bin Laden was Mohammed bin Laden's 17th son, drew a dividend in the hundreds of thousands of dollars as a child, and attended Jeddah's only elite day school — his radicalism was not a product of poverty but of privilege and Brotherhood recruitment.

The elder bin Laden started out carrying sacks on the docks

Mohammed bin Laden came from Hadramawt in Yemen; as a boy in his early teens he walked to Jeddah with his uncle, first worked as a dock porter, then learned bricklaying, then became a contractor. He lost an eye in a construction accident. His rise was bound up with Saudi Arabia itself: first the pilgrimage economy, then, after oil arrived in the 1930s, the royal family's building boom, which made him ‘the royal bricklayer’. Steve Coll stresses that he was an outsider — no amount of money could make marrying his children into the royal family conceivable, just as a blue-blood family in the American Northeast would not marry its daughter to the Sicilian contractor who built its swimming pool.

— Steve Coll
9:24

The fourth wife was a rotating post

Mohammed bin Laden kept four wives at once; the first three were long-term fixtures, while the fourth slot was rapid marriage and rapid divorce, which Steve Coll says can only be described as his own libido. Osama's mother Aliyah was a 15-year-old girl from the Syrian coast, introduced to the elder bin Laden through a Syrian business deal, and about a year after Osama's birth she was passed on to a mid-level manager in the company. Osama was therefore ‘the child of a divorced wife’, but he was also a ‘golden child’ — he held shares in this highly profitable company and drew a dividend in the hundreds of thousands of dollars a year in the 1960s and 70s.

— Steve Coll
15:46

Bin Laden boys grew up watching demolitions

The elder bin Laden's biggest project at the time was the modernisation of Mecca and Medina, which meant tearing down much of the old city. What the sons saw as teenagers was the daily routine of placing explosives in old buildings, stepping back, boom. Steve Coll says this cannot be causally connected to what Osama later became, but it is the true picture of their upbringing. At the same time the elder bin Laden was strict with his sons, repeatedly preaching simple living, hard work and study, and periodically summoning all his sons to company headquarters; daughters were not part of that gathering.

— Steve Coll
17:51

In 1967 his father crashed, and the eldest son Salem took over

Mohammed bin Laden's plane hit a mountainside; an American pilot judged it a landing error. The king wept, because the royal family was itself a debtor to the bin Laden organisation, and losing him meant every construction project would be thrown into disorder. The eldest son Salem was sent to London for his education, the only son sent to the West, and he loved the Beatles, played the harmonica, flew his own plane and threw parties around the world; he was described as a playboy and practical joker. He took over in his early twenties; European engineers kept the projects going, but every decision landed on him, including where Osama went to school and who could marry.

— Steve Coll
26:17

What changed him was a PE teacher's after-school group

Osama attended the elite day school in Jeddah founded by Irish and British educators, relatively secular. But there he came under the influence of a Syrian PE teacher, a follower of the Muslim Brotherhood who ran an after-school club and study group for children of elite families — the Brotherhood believed in gaining influence by recruiting and educating elites. Osama gradually became the family's ‘Puritan among Puritans’: no music, opposed to photographs, no modern plastic toothbrush, only a twig, and married for the first time at 17.

— Steve Coll
29:26

Among 54 siblings, he was the right-wing minority

Steve Coll likens Mohammed's 54 children to a parliament, divided between modern and traditional. The vast majority were globalists: property in Europe and America, dancing in clubs, selling perfume in Geneva, running a Hard Rock Cafe in Beirut. The middle tried to balance Saudi conservatism with occasional trips abroad. Osama belonged to the family's right wing, with only a few siblings equally devout, and among them he became the leader and advocate, frequently scolding his siblings for what they said and did, and isolating himself in the process.

— Steve Coll
31:33

In a Saudi context, going to Pakistan was entirely normal

Steve Coll stresses that Osama volunteering in Saudi Arabia and on the Pakistan-Afghanistan border was in no way abnormal in a Saudi context — like a family of six children where one is more devout than his siblings, goes abroad through the church to build houses for disaster victims, and the family is proud of him, perhaps a little worried, but does not object. Salem supported him too, giving him money and access. Only later, when he turned against his own family and the kingdom, did he cross that line. Throughout the 80s, in our eyes, he was a supported, entirely conventional radical.

— Steve Coll
33:43

At the Mecca siege, the royals wanted the bin Laden family's blueprints

In 1979 hundreds of armed men seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca, their weapons smuggled in beforehand inside coffins, and the siege lasted two weeks. The militants hid in the basement, and the people who held the basement blueprints were the bin Laden family, then building the mosque, who were asked to help the special forces storming it. Osama, 22, watched it all on television. Steve Coll argues that what he learned was not the specific methods of this self-proclaimed Mahdi's group, but that a small group acting boldly in a public symbolic space can stir an entire society and attract the world's attention.

— Steve Coll
39:05

His real edge was understanding media, not public speaking

The Osama of the 80s was consistently described as shy and soft-spoken, not a natural leader and not an orator. Steve Coll argues the key is that he grew up in a globalised business family with access to electronics and cutting-edge communications technology; he was trained as a marketer himself, and his sons worked in marketing in Jeddah too. He was first a consumer of media, and later his own communicator. When he turned against his family and the kingdom and began exporting his own vision, he was extremely effective, because he mastered the tools of the day earlier than his contemporaries.

— Steve Coll

Glossary

Hadramawt
A region of eastern Yemen that historically produced large numbers of sailors and overseas merchants.
Wahhabi
The state religion of Saudi Arabia, advocating a return to the lifestyle of the Prophet's 7th-century era, extremely puritanical.
Muslim Brotherhood
An Islamic political organisation originating in Egypt that emphasised changing political structures by recruiting and educating elites.
globalists
Steve Coll's term for the majority of bin Laden children who lived in Europe and America and were secularised.
