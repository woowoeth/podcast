Trump Invites Xi to a Show. What Did the US Get in Return?

This state visit has almost no substantive agenda — it is pure symbolism and optics. The question is not whether something goes wrong, but what the US is trading symbolism for in substance. So far: nothing.

This visit has no substantive agenda at all

Gewirtz's judgment is that this state visit has ‘there is not a very deep substantive agenda at all’. Trump invited Xi mainly because he wants to put on a show, wants the pomp and the cameras, and incidentally to push issues like the Iran war out of the news. For Beijing, this actually makes them less nervous — because if it were a more confrontational meeting, they would be more anxious. But Gewirtz flags two things: Trump himself is extremely unpredictable, and Beijing has internalized this; and Beijing, watching the chaos in Washington, sees the US president himself often in a position they would never want Xi to be in — going to the wrong place, being photographed in unflattering images, being pressed by reporters. So the real question is not whether someone deliberately sets a trap, but whether things slip into the scenario they most want to avoid.

— Julian Gewirtz
9:25

Symbolism for substance: where is the trade

Gewirtz characterizes this visit as ‘we are in a space of symbolism and optics’. He says that in US-China relations, trading symbolism for substance is itself a reasonable play: give Beijing the pomp and cameras it craves, and get back something concretely useful to Americans and US interests. That was the model for US relations with the Soviets during the Cold War and after — the US gave symbolism, legitimacy, and that handshake photo, and got a step back from the other side on Third World countries or nuclear issues. But the problem this time is that the Trump administration does not even seem to have tried to design a set of ‘deliverables’. Gewirtz says ‘it doesn't even seem like they're trying’. So what to watch is not where things go wrong, but ‘where is the trade happening’.

— Julian Gewirtz
11:37

The direction of concessions has been completely flipped

Gewirtz points to a counterintuitive dynamic: concessions the US might once have given China are now flipped into ‘gifts’ China gives the US. Pomp and cameras were what Beijing wanted, but in Washington they are treated as Xi's visit being a concession to the US. The same flip happens on advanced semiconductors: the US used to not sell these things, and opening up products like the H200 would have been a huge concession; now if Beijing agrees to buy, many in Washington would see it as Beijing conceding to Trump. Gewirtz says this flip keeps recurring, and the reason it matters is not to score points for the administration, but that it reflects what happens when the power gap in superpower competition narrows — small differences, interpersonal dynamics, can cause much larger displacements in the overall strategic picture.

— Julian Gewirtz
12:39

US-China relations are also K-shaped, held up by the upper leg

Gewirtz uses the ‘K-shaped’ idea for US-China relations: one upward leg is the leader-level channel — dense exchanges of visits, friendship, cameras; one downward leg is the competitive reality and the actions certain parts of both governments keep taking. The system is currently held up by the upward leg, but it is very fragile. He stresses this metaphor is not just a parallel analogy: saying ‘truce’ or ‘detente’ sounds too static, like a state already reached, but US-China relations are actually quite dynamic, quite unstable, moving in two directions at once. He applies the same K-shaped logic to China's economy — manufacturing and AI are flying, real estate and consumer demand are struggling, the upward leg is pulling the whole system forward, and the question is whether the system can hold as the K widens.

— Julian Gewirtz
22:03

The bipartisan consensus on China policy has shattered

Gewirtz argues that Trump changed the tone of America's China discussion in the 2016 election, becoming the symbol and standard-bearer of that turn; but now, after helping create that turn, he has helped destroy or transform it. He cites David Feith's article in the Reagan Institute on ‘a new cold war in an age of doubt’ — that great rethink on China was not as great as many assumed. Gewirtz's conclusion: if the political dynamics are more complicated than imagined and the bipartisan consensus some people talk about does not exist, then this is a moment of ‘incredible fluidity’. He says he has no confidence in the direction of either Democratic or Republican China policy in the medium term, and Beijing has no confidence either.

— Julian Gewirtz
31:24

Chinese officials cannot talk about risks they have no solution for

Gewirtz offers a different read on how the US should interpret Chen Yixin's article. On the surface, Americans are panicking about existential risk and China is panicking about regime security, and that is correct. But he says: heads of US AI model companies can talk at length about risks they have no solution for, and they all do; China's minister of state security cannot. In China, when you point out a risk in public, you are simultaneously saying ‘we the Communist Party see it, we will handle it, we have a solution’. So the real question is: should we take what we can see from Beijing as the whole, or acknowledge there is more in the system we cannot see. He gives an example from 25 years ago — when Americans predicted the internet would cause regime change in China, Clinton said it was like nailing Jell-O to the wall, and Beijing did nail the Jell-O to the wall, namely the Great Firewall.

— Julian Gewirtz
35:37

Beijing has not decided how to regulate open models

Gewirtz says that around Xi's speech and Chen Yixin's article, the outside world has been through several cycles of excitement and interpretation, but Beijing has still not given the single most important thing: how they plan to regulate open-weight models spreading globally — models that carry both risk and strategic benefit for Beijing. He calls this ‘the trillion dollar question’. Both speeches can be read as pointing in some direction, but the decision has not been made. His guess is that this is because Beijing itself has not decided, because things are changing too fast and the trade-offs are too large. He contrasts with the Trump administration: it did not make a decision until Mythos came out, and then made another when Fable came out.

— Julian Gewirtz
41:55

What to prepare for is not China's decline, but Xi's decline

Gewirtz says that a few years ago the ‘peak China’ debate asked whether to start preparing for China's decline, and now most people think China's economy is at least temporarily on a relatively sustainable path. But he thinks the real question is not that, but ‘do we need to prepare for Xi Jinping's decline’ — because people die, and modern Chinese history tells us that leaders in power for a long time change with age, certain obsessions become more prominent, behavior changes. By ‘prepare’ he does not mean the departure itself, but acknowledging uncertainty: the outside world has long treated Xi's tenure as fairly predictable, because since taking power in 2012 and 2013 he laid out a set of goals and largely pursued them. But he stresses the change could well be worse for the US — ‘That doesn't mean he's going to mellow’.

— Julian Gewirtz
46:02

No one predicts the post-Xi era accurately

Gewirtz says his academic career has largely studied the decisive turning points of modern China: Mao's death, the turn around 1989, WTO accession and the transformation that followed. He notes these moments are often described as elite power struggles or high-level changes at the center of Beijing, but they are also — perhaps more so — transformations of Chinese society, driven not only from the top down but also from the bottom up. On China after Xi, he lists several scenarios: linear continuity, more nationalist or more confrontational, even more openness or liberalization. But he says the clearest trend in these historical moments is ‘nobody got it right’ — no one accurately predicted what would happen, change during transitions is fast, and not everything changes on day one. To those confidently predicting what China will be like on day one after Xi, he offers a challenge: predict six months and one year out with the same confidence.

— Julian Gewirtz
