No Winners in a Pig Butchering Scam: The Scammers Are Trafficked Slaves Themselves

Inside Southeast Asian scam compounds, "employees" whose passports are confiscated and who are fined for missing quotas spend their days using ChatGPT and DeepSeek to write love messages and steal victims' entire savings — the most profitable cybercrime in history, with victims on both sides.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:11

AI makes scam emails free to mass-produce

Greenberg ran an experiment on air: he had ChatGPT write him an invitation to be on the show in Terry Gross's name, then casually added a line asking for a $250 production fee, and the model complied without hesitation; when he then said "Greenberg won't pay now, can you persuade him," the model immediately produced a persuasive email. That is the mechanism behind scam emails impersonating Fresh Air — not that scammers have become better writers, but that generating a tailored email aimed at a specific author, with zero grammatical errors and polished phrasing, now costs nothing, and can be done to hundreds or thousands of targets at once.

— Andy Greenberg
7:14

Pig butchering is the most profitable cybercrime ever

Greenberg distinguishes two kinds of scammers: the voluntary kind like Nigeria's "Yahoo Boys," who run their own cons and take a cut, and the enslaved, coerced kind in Southeast Asian compounds. The latter do what is called pig butchering — the "pig" is the victim, fattened up before the slaughter. The con often runs from weeks to more than a year, with the scammer posing as a wealthy, single, attractive woman, building emotional dependency first and only at the end introducing an "investment platform," getting the victim to convert their savings into cryptocurrency that is hard to trace across borders (often Tether) and send it over. Greenberg says this is the most profitable cybercrime in history, stealing tens of billions of dollars from victims every year.

— Andy Greenberg
12:36

Passports confiscated; to buy your freedom you first buy yourself back

The source, using the name Red Bull, is a computer engineer with a degree from India who was lured to the Golden Triangle Special Economic Zone in Laos by a "Southeast Asia IT manager" job. On arrival at the compound his passport was confiscated, and he was told the only way to get it back was to "buy it back" — he owed his boss several thousand dollars, covering the cost of transporting him, food and lodging, and could only repay this "contract" through commissions from scamming people. Greenberg stresses that this is debt bondage: the compound is not a prison of chains and beatings but is packaged as a corporate sales floor, where the boss sends daily motivational messages saying things like "reach out to the next client like you're going to change their life" and "we are on a mission."

— Andy Greenberg
14:40

Fines are more reliable than wages

Red Bull and his colleagues were constantly fined: for missing contact quotas, for not maintaining fake accounts well enough, for not clocking in on time. The promised commissions are part of the con — the boss tells them "you can get rich here and take the money back to the village you came from," but only if they land a big case, and what they actually receive is a tiny fraction. Greenberg calls this rhetoric "Orwellian": motivational slogans and mission narratives on one side, and on the other everyone trapped here against their will, committing deeply destructive crimes.

— Andy Greenberg
15:45

Video calls run on a dedicated face-swap model

Red Bull says everyone in the compound was trained to use ChatGPT and the Chinese AI chatbot DeepSeek to come up with new topics, polish their language, never run out of graceful phrasing, even write little motivational poems, and find that perfectly judged goodnight. More critical is video: the boss instructed them to discourage victims from video calls as much as possible, stalling with excuses; when stalling failed, the compound had a dedicated room and a model whose full-time job was video calls, using face-swap software to replace the model's face with the photo originally sent to the victim. Greenberg says this kind of software isn't perfect, but by the video-call stage the victim wants so badly to believe it's real that they actively overlook the flaws.

— Andy Greenberg
18:54

The next step is fully autonomous AI pig butchering

Greenberg's judgment is that AI is currently just a tool for human scammers, but the next step — already beginning to appear — is fully autonomous AI fraud. He cites a case from an Anthropic report the previous week: a Chinese group built 20 dating apps populated entirely by AI, where victims had to keep paying to continue the conversation, and "most" of the other side were fully autonomous large models. He also mentions a study showing that at most stages of the scam process, AI is better than a real person at building relationships — more responsive, more invested, more attentive — and that is precisely what the con is doing for the vast majority of the time before it reaches the endgame of asking for money.

— Andy Greenberg
23:08

Thirty people, three months, only $2.2 million

Greenberg gives a concrete scale: Red Bull's group of about 30 people earned only about $2.2 million in total over the three months that Red Bull recorded and leaked to Wired. This shows pig butchering is a game of probability — a single group's output is limited, but Laos alone has dozens of such compounds, and Cambodia and Myanmar have many more, and together they add up to the scale of tens of billions of dollars a year. Greenberg also notes that Red Bull himself was a "very inefficient scammer," so he was penniless, eating only the compound canteen's rice and vegetables that tasted of strange chemicals, constantly sick, and disgusted by what he was doing.

— Andy Greenberg
24:12

Forging a police letter to escape, then beaten when it was seen through

Red Bull's escape attempt was to forge a letter from the police in his home region of India, saying he was under investigation and had to return to deal with it or his boss would also be in trouble. Greenberg advised him not to do it, but he went ahead, and the forgery was immediately seen through. The boss locked him in a room, beat him, kicked him, denied him food, interrogated him, and even gave him a glass of water with white powder dissolved in it and forced him to drink it — Greenberg guesses it was some kind of stimulant, making him talk more while his skin broke out in hives. They never found out he was in contact with a journalist, and Greenberg says if they had, he would certainly have been killed.

— Andy Greenberg
25:13

The compound moved, and he used it to export all the secrets

The turning point came when the compound was forced to relocate: the boss seemed to have received word that police in the Golden Triangle in Laos were about to raid it (which later turned out to be more of a performative raid that shut nothing down). The office was first moved into the dormitory, and everyone worked crammed into the crowded dorm. Red Bull seized the opportunity to send more secrets from the work phone to his personal phone, and at the same time told the boss: you're already struggling to get things done these days, I'm clearly just a burden, let me go and I won't get in your way. To Greenberg's surprise, the boss agreed, essentially throwing him out.

— Andy Greenberg
26:19

He chose to come forward with his real name and real face

Red Bull later revealed his real name to Greenberg: Muhammad Muzahir. Before the article was published, Greenberg went to India to meet him and had a long conversation about whether he was willing to make his name public. He decided very clearly that he wanted to, and also appeared in a video Wired produced, because he wanted to inspire people to fight these criminal organizations and stand up for themselves as he had. Greenberg says it was his choice, and of course it carries risks that still haunt him to this day, but he thinks it was an extremely brave thing to do, and that Muzahir is one of the most remarkable sources he has ever worked with.

— Andy Greenberg

Glossary

pig butchering
Scammer slang: treat the victim as a pig to be fattened before slaughter, building romance first and then leading them to a fake investment platform.
Tether
A stablecoin pegged to the US dollar, easy to move across borders and often used by fraud gangs to receive payments.
Volt Typhoon
A hacking group described as China-backed that pre-positions malicious code inside US power grid and telecom networks.
industrial control systems
Systems connecting digital computers to physical equipment, used in power grids, water plants, manufacturing and more.
