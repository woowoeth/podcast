You Can't Vote an Investor Director Off Your Board: Common Stock Never Gets the Right

An investor board seat is elected only by its corresponding class of stock, and the founders' common stock has no vote to replace it; the only realistic paths are a private ask to step down, or waiting for the investor to voluntarily become a board observer.

2:23

Picking directors is picking your future co-signers

Becky starts by framing what a board is for: the board sets the company's strategic vision and delegates day-to-day operations to management; but every major action must be approved by the board — issuing securities (even a single share), options, any financing (SAFE, convertible note, preferred stock, bank debt), M&A, major contracts, and hiring or firing the CEO. So choosing who sits on the board is essentially choosing the people who will co-sign your future major decisions.

— Becky
3:35

The first board should have only founders and a lawyer

Jason's principle: before product-market fit you probably don't need one, at Series A you must have one, and in the window between, use numbers to decide — once capital raised plus revenue exceeds two or three million dollars, you can put together a small board with just the founders and a lawyer. You don't have to hold meetings; written consent can pass resolutions, but every director has to sign. Becky adds a key warning: don't stuff five directors in on day one just to make the company look good, because you will immediately lose control of the company.

— Jason、Becky
7:47

On a balanced board the independent director is the deciding vote

Investors usually ask for a board seat when they come in on a preferred round, not at the SAFE stage; Jason's observation is that they only speak up once they hold 10%–20%. Becky's structural evolution: at Series A founders are usually still the majority, and at Series B it becomes a "balanced board" of 2 common + 2 preferred + 1 independent director, where the independent director is often the tie-breaking vote when the count is even.

— Becky
8:18

The director investors call most independent is often the least independent

The mechanism for choosing an independent director is usually one side designates and the other side vetoes: the founder can propose and the investor can veto, and vice versa. Jason calls out the trick in this seat — the "most independent director" in an investor's telling may be in the same fraternity, the same stint at Bain, and have vacationed with him seven years running; Becky's advice is to pick people for the gap in the business, e.g. Airbnb needed a hospitality expert, and Paris Hilton was a candidate no board member could refuse. Compensation at early-stage companies is almost purely equity, roughly "a bit more than you'd give an advisor," vesting monthly over two to four years; at Apple's level, cash plus stock plus audit committee pay comes to about $400,000.

— Jason、Becky
13:29

Observers have no confidentiality duty, yet hear the same secrets

Directors have a vote and fiduciary duties; observers have neither and are just "guests" invited to the board meeting. Becky stresses that although observers can't vote, they get the full board package and can speak up to sway the real directors — so they must sign a separate confidentiality provision. Director status carries confidentiality obligations with it, observers don't have them, yet they hear equally sensitive information, and that's a hole many early-stage companies overlook.

— Becky
15:36

For a small fund, being an observer beats being a director

Jason's current preference: small funds shouldn't take a board seat, they should drop to observer and send an analyst to track two things — how the business is doing, and when the next financing or major corporate action is coming. The reason is that he has seen too many forced signings where "you have to sign within 24 hours": the company sends Series A documents overnight, issues 50% more shares in them, and makes you the last person "standing in the way of the company's success." Rather than be held hostage by that game, keep information rights as an observer and still learn first on Slack whether the company is short on cash or making money.

— Jason
17:46

Even at 100 votes per common share, you can't vote out an investor seat

Becky lays out the legal structure: within a VC company, different seats are elected separately by different classes of stock, and common stock and the designated preferred seats are two separate voting pools; even if your common stock had 100 votes per share, you couldn't vote it onto the investor's seat. The lead investor usually also gets designation rights in the financing documents, so as long as it keeps holding 25%–50% of the shares purchased in that round, combined with the voting agreement, everyone must vote for the person it designates. So the "marriage analogy" for board membership breaks down here: a marriage can be ended unilaterally, but a preferred director can only leave when he chooses to.

— Becky
21:07

A walk and a lunch are the only ways to get a director off

Legally there's no solution; in practice it comes down to relationships. Becky's advice is concrete: take the director for a walk along the Embarcadero, invite him to come a day early for lunch, talk an extra twenty minutes on the drive to the airport — build the relationship so that when you need him to step down, "a little sugar helps the medicine go down." Jason's matching posture: if he's no longer the person the seat needs, he'll voluntarily become an observer — "if people don't want me at the party, I don't go to the party." Founders toward investors, and investors toward founders, can both use this logic.

— Becky、Jason

Glossary

Board observer
May attend board meetings and receives all materials, but has no vote and no fiduciary duty, and must sign a separate confidentiality agreement.
Designation rights
The lead investor's contractual right to name the preferred director seat for as long as its ownership threshold is met.
Voting agreement
Requires common and preferred holders to vote in an agreed way to elect directors, giving effect to designation rights.
Written consent
A board resolution made without a meeting, but it requires every director's signature.
Fiduciary duty
Directors must put the interests of the company and all shareholders first, not the interests of their own fund.
