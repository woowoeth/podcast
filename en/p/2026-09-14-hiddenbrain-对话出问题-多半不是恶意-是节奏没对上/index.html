When Conversations Go Wrong, It's Usually Not Malice — It's Mismatched Rhythm

The same silence reads as polite waiting to one person and as a dead air to another; an interruption is completed by two people — one starts talking, the other stops. Misunderstandings are hard to resolve because we have no language to talk about this layer at all.

The same silence, two meanings

While doing her PhD, Deborah Tannen recorded three hours of conversation at a Thanksgiving dinner with friends and found she simply couldn't analyze the conversational style of the Californians — it wasn't that they didn't talk, it was that they couldn't get the floor. The reason was that each side had a different default for how long a normal pause is: New Yorkers assumed a shorter pause, so while Californians and English women were still waiting for a normal pause to jump in, the New Yorkers had already decided ‘they have nothing to say, so I'll fill the gap.’ The same silence reads as polite waiting to one person and as a dead air to another. When styles clash, we don't say ‘the pause you expect is one millisecond shorter than mine’ — we jump straight to a judgment about the person.

— Deborah Tannen
7:10

An interruption is completed by two people

To capture every detail she could perceive, Tannen annotated the transcript with the difference between the pronunciations of the and the, measured perceptible pauses with a stopwatch, and then had the participants themselves listen to the recording to check. It was this precision that let her see that a New Yorker's ‘interruption’ is sometimes an expression of enthusiasm, not rudeness. From this she proposed a counterintuitive mechanism — an interruption is completed by two people: one starts talking, the other stops, and it's the latter who defines ‘talking at the same time’ as an interruption.

— Deborah Tannen
10:21

The more you rush to yield the floor, the more the other person goes silent

In the recording, Tannen heard an embarrassing side of herself: she calls it machine gun questions. To be friendly to Chuck, a guest from Los Angeles, and hand him the floor, she fired off questions — you live in LA? Here on vacation? What do you do there? What do you do at Disney, a writer? An artist? You write ad copy? She already knew the answers; she just wanted to give him an opening. It backfired: Chuck was caught off guard by the speed of the questions and, instead of picking up the thread, went even quieter. She realized that the more she asked, the more urgently she asked — she was machine-gunning him.

— Deborah Tannen
20:48

Going indirect is a way of asking ‘he cares’

Why not just say what you want? Tannen uses birthday gifts to explain: you want someone who loves you to give you the gift you want, because that proves they care, that they're willing to make the effort to guess, that they know you well enough. If you tell them outright, you get the gift but not the things you actually wanted — ‘he cares,’ ‘he gets you.’ Indirect expression works the same way in other situations. She also corrects a common attribution: this isn't always a male-female difference — between her and her Greek husband, she is the more direct one and he the more indirect; region, ethnicity, and culture all shape style.

— Deborah Tannen
36:41

Only people with power can afford to go indirect

A career naval officer told Tannen that this is how the military works: when he was a cadet, the instructor walked into the classroom and said ‘it's a bit cold in here,’ and everyone nodded; the instructor said ‘when I say something, I expect you to do something about it — let's try again — it's a bit cold in here,’ and everyone jumped up to close the windows. Tannen's conclusion is that it's precisely because he has power that the instructor enjoys the luxury of indirect expression. The same sentence — ‘could you grab me the update on that report’ — can be heard by one person as the boss being unpretentious and treating me as one of his own, and by another as the boss being too soft, why doesn't he just give an order.

— Deborah Tannen
47:16

Treating good intentions as the default is a rhetorical strategy

Tannen calls her position ‘a rhetoric of good intentions.’ People often push back: what if the other person really is malicious? Her response is — that's not news. People are already always ready to see others as ill-intentioned and personally flawed. The news her work can offer is this: step back and consider that the other person may not be malicious, just using a different conversational style, pursuing his positive goals in a different way. Thinking about conversational style gives you a channel to imagine different intentions.

— Deborah Tannen
1:12:22

Silence isn't a compromise — it loses on both ends

On ‘lies of omission,’ Emma Levine points out that speakers' and listeners' judgments are completely misaligned. The speaker feels silence is a moral compromise: more moral than lying, less moral than telling the truth. But the listener can see you're avoiding the issue, and what he wants is usually comfort, affirmation, to feel a little better. If you turn away or change the subject, he neither hears the truth nor gets the social goal of being loved and affirmed — so silence is sometimes not a compromise but the worst option, stripping away both kindness and honesty at once.

— Emma Levine
1:32:03

Whether you dare to tell the truth depends on context

Research by Emma Levine, Yenna Kim and others compared critical feedback in the US and China: in China, people are more willing to give critical feedback because both sides share an understanding of what the norm is and where things fall short, and criticism is seen as a shared responsibility; in the US, the default is ‘you do you’ — who am I to comment on your workplace behavior. Levine's conclusion is that whether you dare to tell the truth is not a matter of personality but is determined by context and cultural components.

— Emma Levine
