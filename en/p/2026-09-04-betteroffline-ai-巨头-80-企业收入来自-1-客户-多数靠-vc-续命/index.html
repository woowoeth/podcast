AI Giants Get 80% of Enterprise Revenue From 1% of Customers, Most of Them Living on VC

OpenAI and Anthropic get 80% of their enterprise revenue from 1% of customers, and many of those are AI companies burning VC-subsidized tokens; the only ones willing to pay real money are tech itself, and when VC stops, the trillion-dollar compute bill blows up first.

This monologue turns the AI bubble argument from a matter of stance into a matter of structure: who the customer is at every layer, where the money comes from, whose cash flow can break. Ten minutes holds a complete transmission chain, no filler.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:03

80% of enterprise revenue rides on 1% of customers

Ramp's enterprise spending data shows that about 80% of OpenAI's and Anthropic's enterprise customer revenue comes from the top 1% of customers, and that ratio has not improved at all over the past three years. Ramp chief economist Ara Karazian points out that this 1% is heavily skewed toward the tech industry and AI product/service companies, a level of concentration he has not seen in any software category he tracks. The host adds the data's boundary: it does not include players like Microsoft and large banks, but it is still representative of the overall state of AI industry spending. In other words, what actually props up the enterprise revenue of the two leading model makers is not a dispersed mass market, but a small handful of extremely free-spending customers.

— Ed Zitron
2:05

The big AI spenders' money comes from VC, not revenue

This spending structure holds up largely because AI startups are kept burning money by VC transfusions. The common play is to sell subscriptions but subsidize tokens: a user on a $20-a-month subscription can burn $30, $40 or even $100 worth of tokens, with the difference covered by capital. That is, the money OpenAI/Anthropic receive from these customers depends on those customers being able to keep raising. The host admits this is his intuition: the loudest voices on AI Twitter are basically venture-backed startups, and the tokens they burn internally to write code may far exceed what outside customers pay. So-called real demand is, at least in large part, actually propped up by ‘the ability to raise’.

— Ed Zitron
4:08

The only ones willing to pay big for AI are tech itself

Lay out this data and the conclusion is more awkward than most people want to admit: AI has occupied every media outlet and every meeting room for three straight years, almost every company has tried it, and plenty have approved big AI budgets, but the ones actually willing to spend big turn out to be, it seems, only the tech industry itself. Industries outside tech and AI barely pay high prices for large models, and there is no sign of any reversal. Worse, this small set of leading customers is itself unstable — they could well switch to cheaper self-built open-source models, or eventually move to on-device models. The host points out that this customer structure is the real underlying exposure of OpenAI and Anthropic.

— Ed Zitron
5:09

Cloud vendors have their fortunes riding on two model companies

The risk does not stop at the two labs themselves. The compute purchase commitments on OpenAI and Anthropic exceed $1.1 trillion, and they are take-or-pay contracts — no matter how much is actually used or earned, the agreed amount must be paid. The compute revenue of Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Oracle and CoreWeave is all betting that these two can pay. The figures the host cites: OpenAI contributes about 70% of Microsoft's FY26 AI revenue; UBS estimates OpenAI and Anthropic will account for 48% of Google Cloud's total revenue next year, corresponding to roughly $84 billion to $100 billion; Barclays also gives 2027 forecasts of about $40 billion for AWS and at least $50 billion for Azure. And both companies are still losing billions of dollars a year.

— Ed Zitron
6:10

The compute bill hits a reset wall in 2027

Why hasn't the bubble burst yet? Because the mortgage-style structure has not yet reached its peak repayment. These compute contracts usually start with a small prepayment, the data center capacity is not yet online, and the real payment obligation gets heavier month by month starting next year. The host cites an analogy from a Groundbreaker article: this is like a mortgage's low teaser rate expiring — the rate was extremely low at signing, and after the reset the monthly payment balloons to unsustainable levels; at signing, every borrower assumed house prices would keep rising and that they could refinance or sell, but when everyone tries to run at the same time, no one can get out. OpenAI's and Anthropic's enormous compute commitments are the subprime of the AI bubble. The pretty big orders leave cloud vendors carrying huge revenue backlogs, while the ability to pay rests on a belief in continued growth and on VC money that keeps arriving.

— Ed Zitron
8:11

NVIDIA sells cards on its customers' debt

The very top of the chain is just as dangerous. The reason hyperscalers and neoclouds buy more GPUs is the demand brought by OpenAI and Anthropic; even those who build data centers to sell to third parties are only referencing the revenue backlog on the books of companies like CoreWeave and Nebius — and those companies' customers are mainly OpenAI, Anthropic, or the cloud giants behind them. More glaring still, NVIDIA's customers can no longer buy GPUs out of operating cash flow alone; every purchase depends on debt remaining available. Broadcom's latest earnings also show that Anthropic and OpenAI will become its top two customers. The host closes this layer with irony: ‘Everything is normal, everything is fine, no one needs to panic.’

— Ed Zitron
9:12

Bloomberg pops a new mega-deal, and no one worries

The host points out that sell-side analysts and financial media will reassure you: the smartest people and the most powerful companies don't burn money for no reason, AI demand is real, and skeptics are just cherry-picking data. But just as his own monologue is about to end, a news item pops up on the Bloomberg terminal: Crusoe signs a roughly $13 billion cloud computing contract with Jane Street. The irony is that Jane Street is itself both a major CoreWeave customer and a CoreWeave shareholder — strung together, these identities are exactly a sample of the concentration risk he has been describing all episode. He judges this to be a catastrophic capital misallocation, and also the biggest collective failure to report in the history of news.

— Ed Zitron

Glossary

concentration risk
Revenue or investment overly concentrated in a few customers, businesses or assets, so that it collapses once they leave.
take-or-pay agreement
A long-term purchase agreement in which the buyer must pay the agreed amount regardless of how much it actually uses.
neocloud
A new generation of cloud vendors that specialize in renting out GPU compute, such as CoreWeave.
revenue backlog
Future revenue already contracted but not yet delivered, often used to prove that demand is real.
