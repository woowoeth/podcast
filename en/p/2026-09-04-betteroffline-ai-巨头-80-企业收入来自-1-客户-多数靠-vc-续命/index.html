AI Giants' Enterprise Revenue: 80% from 1% of Customers, Most Kept Alive by VC

OpenAI and Anthropic get 80% of enterprise revenue from 1% of customers, mostly AI companies burning VC-subsidized tokens; only tech pays big, and when VC dries up, trillion-dollar compute bills blow first.

This monologue turns the AI bubble debate from stance to structure: who the customers are at each layer, where the money comes from, and whose cash flow can break. Ten minutes pack a complete transmission chain with no filler.

1:03

80% of enterprise revenue rides on 1% of customers

Ramp's enterprise spending data shows that for OpenAI and Anthropic, about 80% of enterprise customer revenue comes from the top 1% of customers, and that share hasn't improved in three years. Ramp's chief economist Ara Karazian notes this 1% is heavily skewed toward tech and AI product/service companies, a concentration he hasn't seen in any software category he tracks. The host adds a data caveat: it excludes players like Microsoft and large banks, but remains representative of AI industry spending overall. In other words, what really props up the enterprise revenue of the two leading model makers isn't a broad mass market but a tiny set of extremely heavy spenders.

2:05

AI burners' money comes from VC, not revenue

This spending structure holds largely because AI startups rely on VC funding to keep burning cash. A common play is selling subscriptions but subsidizing tokens: a user paying $20 a month can burn $30, $40, or even $100 in tokens, with the difference covered by capital. That means the money OpenAI/Anthropic receive from such customers presupposes those customers can keep raising. The host admits this is his intuition: the loudest voices on AI Twitter are mostly VC-backed startups, and the tokens they burn internally for coding may far exceed what external customers pay. So-called real demand, at least a large chunk of it, is actually propped up by 'fundraising ability.'

— Ed Zitron
4:08

Only tech itself will pay big for AI

Spread these numbers out and the conclusion is more awkward than most care to admit: AI has dominated every media outlet and every boardroom for three years, nearly every company has tried it, and many have been approved for large AI budgets, but the only ones actually willing to spend big turn out to be the tech industry itself. Industries outside tech and AI hardly pay premium prices for large models, and there's no sign of reversal. Worse, this small set of top customers is itself unstable—they could easily shift to cheaper self-hosted open-source models or eventually switch to on-device models. The host points out this customer structure is the real underlying exposure for OpenAI and Anthropic.

— Ed Zitron
5:09

Cloud giants' fortunes rest on two model companies

The risk doesn't stop at the two labs. OpenAI and Anthropic carry compute procurement commitments exceeding $1.1 trillion, and they're take-or-pay contracts—whether they use or earn as much, the agreed amounts must be paid. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Oracle, and CoreWeave are all betting their compute revenue on these two being able to pay. The host cites figures: OpenAI contributes about 70% of Microsoft's FY26 AI revenue; UBS estimates OpenAI and Anthropic will account for 48% of Google Cloud's total revenue next year, corresponding to roughly $84–100 billion; Barclays also gives 2027 forecasts of about $40 billion for AWS and at least $50 billion for Azure. And these two companies are still losing billions annually.

— Ed Zitron
6:10

Compute bills hit a reset wall in 2027

Why hasn't the bubble burst yet? Because the mortgage-like structure hasn't reached its peak repayment. Such compute contracts typically involve a small upfront payment, data center capacity isn't online yet, and the real payment obligations start next month and grow heavier each month. The host cites an analogy from a Groundbreaker article: it's like the low teaser rate on a mortgage expiring—the rate is extremely low at signing, and after reset the monthly payment skyrockets to unsustainable levels. Borrowers sign assuming house prices will keep rising and they can refinance or sell, but when everyone tries to run at once, no one can. The massive compute commitments of OpenAI and Anthropic are the subprime of the AI bubble. The pretty big deals hang huge revenue backlogs on cloud vendors, while the ability to pay depends on the belief in continued growth and a steady stream of VC money.

— Ed Zitron
8:11

NVIDIA's card sales rest on customer debt

The top of the chain is equally dangerous. Hyperscalers and neoclouds buy more GPUs because of demand from OpenAI and Anthropic; even those building data centers to sell to third parties benchmark against the revenue backlogs of companies like CoreWeave and Nebius—whose customers are mainly OpenAI, Anthropic, or the cloud giants behind them. More starkly, NVIDIA's customers can no longer buy GPUs from operating cash flow alone; every purchase depends on debt remaining available. Broadcom's latest earnings also show Anthropic and OpenAI will become its top two customers. The host closes this layer with irony: 'Everything is normal, everything is fine, no one needs to panic.'

— Ed Zitron
9:12

Bloomberg pops a new big deal, no one worries

The host notes that sell-side analysts and financial media will reassure you: the smartest people and most powerful companies don't burn money for no reason, AI demand is real, and skeptics are just cherry-picking data. But just as his monologue is about to end, a Bloomberg terminal pops up with news: Crusoe and Jane Street sign a cloud computing contract worth about $13 billion. The irony: Jane Street is itself both a major CoreWeave customer and a CoreWeave shareholder—these roles strung together are a sample of the concentration risk he's been talking about all episode. He judges this a catastrophic capital misallocation and will be the biggest collective miss in news history.

— Ed Zitron

Glossary

concentration risk
Risk that revenue or investment is overly concentrated in a few customers, businesses, or assets, such that if they leave, it collapses.
take-or-pay agreement
A long-term procurement contract where the buyer must pay the agreed amount regardless of actual usage.
neocloud
A new generation of cloud vendors that specialize in renting out GPU compute, such as CoreWeave.
revenue backlog
Future revenue already contracted but not yet delivered, often used to demonstrate real demand.
