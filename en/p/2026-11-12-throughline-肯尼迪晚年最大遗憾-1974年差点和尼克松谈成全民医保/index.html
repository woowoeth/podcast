Kennedy's greatest regret: almost striking a deal with Nixon on universal healthcare in 1974

The healthcare reform plan Nixon proposed at his lowest point during Watergate was more radical than Obamacare later, but Kennedy surrendered to pressure from allies and abandoned negotiations, calling it his greatest political regret.

Son's cancer treatment convinced Kennedy to champion universal healthcare

Kennedy's son Ted Jr. was diagnosed with bone cancer at age 12 requiring amputation. Early chemotherapy was free through clinical trials, but after the trial ended, each treatment cost $2,700 with no insurance coverage—except Kennedy himself enjoyed government insurance as a congressman. In hospital corridors, he encountered families who had mortgaged homes or faced bankruptcy unable to pay treatment costs. This experience made him embrace universal healthcare as ‘the cause of his life’.

— Ted Kennedy / narration
10:30

Nixon proposed healthcare reform to deflect from Watergate

At the 1974 State of the Union address, Nixon, mired in Watergate, pivoted the conversation to healthcare, proposing a plan requiring all employers to either provide insurance or pay penalties—radical for a Republican president by today's standards. The episode notes this was not Nixon's ideological shift but a ‘president desperate to change the subject’, creating unequal motivations for the subsequent negotiations.

— narration
12:30

Kennedy saw Nixon's plan as insufficient compromise

When Nixon spoke, cameras caught Kennedy sitting unmoved—because just a year earlier, he had proposed a European-style government-led single-payer system akin to Medicare for all. Nixon's plan, in Kennedy's view, merely compromised with private insurance companies rather than constituting genuine reform.

— narration
16:36

Kennedy stunned observers by offering to work with Nixon

In April 1974, Kennedy joined the House Ways and Means Committee chairman in publicly announcing willingness to work with the Nixon administration on legislation, with media interpreting it as universal healthcare ‘a done deal’. But the decision simultaneously angered Nixon's cabinet (accusing him of ‘socialism’) and liberal allies like unions (seeing it as extending the dying Nixon's political life).

— narration
18:40

Union leaders called Kennedy a traitor to labor

Labor leaders believed Democrats would dominate the midterm elections and control both chambers, so shouldn't bargain with the departing Nixon. During negotiations, a top labor lobbyist called Kennedy a ‘traitor to labor’ to his face. Kennedy stood up, opened the door, and said ‘Then I guess you'll just have to call me a traitor’—demonstrating his determination to push forward despite pressure from allies.

— narration / Ted Kennedy
19:46

Staff negotiated in secret church basement for months

Because of political sensitivity, Nixon and Kennedy's healthcare staff met secretly in a room called ‘the pub’ in the basement of St. Mark's Episcopal Church starting in June 1974, as carefully as ‘sending an envoy to the Soviet Union’, trying to find balance between government control and free competition. Negotiations continued through July.

— Stuart Altman / narration
22:02

Negotiations continued even as Watergate hearings intensified

Even as impeachment hearings unfolded in the House and White House tapes were turned over to prosecutors, church basement negotiations continued discussing technical details like payroll taxes and insurance rules, because participants still saw this ‘historic window’ as open. On July 8, 1974—just one month before Nixon resigned—his health secretary still told Kennedy ‘reaching agreement is possible’.

— Stuart Altman
23:06

Kennedy quietly walked away from the historic opportunity

After assessing how much he had compromised and how strongly allies had backlashed, Kennedy told his staff ‘They're prepared to crucify me’, then arranged no further meetings or calls. The negotiations simply faded away. A month later Nixon resigned and the healthcare plan disappeared entirely, ‘just like that’.

— Ted Kennedy / narration
25:15

Kennedy called it the greatest regret of his career

Years later, Kennedy repeatedly told staff and associates ‘We should have done this’ and ‘I thought we could pull it off’, calling it the greatest regret of his political career. The episode notes that he couldn't have foreseen how public trust in government would continue eroding after Watergate; in the 30 years afterward, he never came close to such a comprehensive healthcare proposal again.

— Ted Kennedy / Barbara Souliotis
