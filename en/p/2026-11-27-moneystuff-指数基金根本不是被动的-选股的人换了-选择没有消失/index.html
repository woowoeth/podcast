Index funds fundamentally aren't passive—the stock picker switched, not the stock picking

The S&P 500 and Russell indexes aren't laws of nature—they're products built by committees using chains of subjective rules. Buying a "passive" fund transfers stock-picking power to the index maker; it doesn't eliminate stock picking.

Index investing isn't passive—it transfers picking, doesn't eliminate it

Robertson's thesis is titled "Passive in Name Only"—with "index investing" in scare quotes for effect. She expected to find an authoritative paper on how indexes are actually built; it didn't exist. So she spent one and a half years reading through the methodologies of every index in the U.S. stock market. Her finding: passive index investing is entirely built on subjective rules and human decisions, requiring as much judgment as active management—except the judgment happens at the index-maker level, not the fund manager level.

Even ‘large-cap’ itself is a committee's subjective choice

The boundaries between large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap are entirely human constructs. Should an index include the top 500 companies or the top 1,000? Russell and S&P draw these lines differently, and the placement directly determines which companies get included or excluded. S&P 500 maintains an actual committee that meets regularly to decide which companies to add or remove based on criteria they themselves define. The result is nothing natural or inevitable.

— Adriana Robertson
14:22

Index rules weren't designed for a company as large as SpaceX

When SpaceX went public, market attention turned to whether index makers would modify their rules to include it in their indexes sooner. The rules were originally sensible, but designed around an assumption—that no company would arrive on the market already large enough to rank in the top 5-10 of U.S. public companies by capitalization. Robertson draws a parallel to product design: clients have needs, index makers adjust their rules accordingly, and if the adjustment is poor they lose market share to rival indexes.

19:26

The top-seven concentration looks worse than it actually is

The S&P 500 is not the even distribution of mid-size companies most people imagine. It's dominated by a small number of giants. Robertson confirmed that seven large companies comprise roughly one-third of the index; the top 10 holdings of Vanguard 500 represent 36% of the fund. But she offers a counterargument worth hearing: Amazon's retail and cloud services are fundamentally different businesses bundled together, as are Apple's hardware and software. If you disaggregated the conglomerate structure, actual concentration might look less dramatic than the raw numbers suggest.

— Adriana Robertson
22:29

Equal-weight sounds fair and costs you constant contrarian rebalancing

Equal-weight indexes sound appealing—each holding gets exactly the same allocation. But there's a cost. With a market-cap-weighted index, when Apple rises, its weight automatically increases without any trading. Equal-weight requires constant rebalancing to maintain equal size across holdings, which means selling the winners and buying the losers. Robertson jokes that this sounds like ‘the world's worst hedge fund,’ but emphasizes it's not a question of right or wrong—you just need to know what strategy you're actually buying.

— Adriana Robertson
25:32

One rule actually matters for index funds: keep fees under three basis points

Despite understanding index construction is loaded with subjective decisions, Robertson herself keeps nearly all her wealth in low-fee, broadly diversified index funds and money market funds. What worries her: index funds have become such common advice that the market is now flooded with high-fee, narrowly specialized ETFs. Many investors believe they're buying cheap, diversified passive products—when in fact they're buying expensive, ill-suited investments, forfeiting the low-cost advantage that made index funds valuable in the first place.

— Adriana Robertson
29:37

Robertson told the SEC to keep quarterly earnings reports required

Robertson joined the SEC's investor advisory committee in June and participated in debate over eliminating mandatory quarterly earnings reports. Her stance is carefully restrained: she doesn't believe zero disclosure is desirable, and she acknowledges mandatory quarters may discourage some companies from going public. But there's no real empirical evidence that this causal link is actually strong. With a touch of irony, she suggests the ideal solution: randomly divide companies into two groups, require one to file full quarterly reports and the other only unaudited financial statements, then measure whether outcomes actually differ.

equal-weighted index
An index that maintains equal weight across all holdings, requiring frequent rebalancing that forces the fund to sell winners and buy losers.

Markowitz's framework showing that diversification reduces risk without sacrificing returns.
