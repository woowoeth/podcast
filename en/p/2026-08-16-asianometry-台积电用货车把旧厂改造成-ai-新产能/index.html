TSMC Trucks Half-Finished Wafers: The Secret Weapon of AI Capacity

TSMC exploits over 90% tool overlap between N5 and N7 to truck wafers half-made at its Tainan fab to Taichung for finishing, freeing up AI capacity during the N5-to-N3 transition. The foundation of the AI boom is a fleet of ordinary trucks.

This episode uses Mark Liu's and C.C. Wei's own words from earnings calls to puncture the stereotype that a node change requires a new fab. Information density is high, and the back half on trucking wafers is especially counterintuitive.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:05

Money spent this year becomes capacity only two years later

TSMC is building new fabs in three places in Taiwan at once: Fab 20 in Baoshan, Hsinchu; Fab 22 in Nanzih, Kaohsiung; Fab 25 in Taitung, which so far has only land; plus the AP7 advanced packaging plant in Chiayi. More than $60 billion in capital expenditure has already been allocated for 2026. Yet C.C. Wei and other executives admit that money spent in 2026 won't reach the market until 2028. AI demand still can't be met, and Nvidia wants chips now. TSMC still has a card to play: cross-node utilization.

1:08

Node names mean nothing; the real node is a set of tools

The most open secret in semiconductor manufacturing is that logic node names mean nothing. TSMC's node roadmap isn't a straight line like N3→N2→A14; it splits like ASCII art: N3 branches into N3E, N3P, N3X, N3C, and N2 branches into N2P, N2X, A16. After a node enters the fab, the manufacturing team keeps experimenting, yields climb point by point into the mid-80s, and once there are enough improvements they repackage it as a new node — N6 was born out of N7; or they may hold the improvements for a future node. A so-called node is ultimately a set of tools and recipes.

4:10

Depreciation ending doesn't mean pure profit; old fabs get stranded

One of a fab's biggest costs is depreciation, a non-cash expense representing the useful life of the tools. After five to seven years a fab is fully depreciated, and in theory revenue minus operating costs is all profit — but in reality tools always break, which is why Charlie Munger called EBITDA "BS earnings." When a big customer upgrades nodes, the old fab is left with a huge capacity hole: when Apple moved from N7 to N5, the Taichung fab had to find other customers. N2 is too new and too odd, N3 is the sweet spot, N4 is mature and leading, and N7/N6 are stuck in the middle — too far from the leading edge, yet expensive compared with 28nm. Once utilization falls below 70%, a fab starts losing money.

7:10

Above the transistors, the layers can be identical across nodes

Semiconductors are stacked in layers: transistors at the bottom, metal interconnect layers of copper or aluminum above them — the BEOL. The finest lines are in the lowest metal layer, for example N5's M1 at roughly 28-30 nanometers center-to-center; the higher you go, the wider they get, up to 1.5 or even 3 microns at the top. This structure is shared across nodes, so many process steps are the same or similar across N16, N10, N7, N6, N5, N4 and N3. The critical transistor layers differ, sure, but the layers above the transistors may be completely identical. This is also why old DUV machines are still used in large numbers. Over the past decade, TSMC has used this layer overlap to keep fabs from being stranded.

9:15

N10, N7 and N5 are really one capacity pool

In 2015, co-CEO Mark Liu said on an earnings call that 20nm and 16nm had 95% tool overlap, so a customer wanting to upgrade could convert 20nm capacity to 16nm. In 2018, C.C. Wei said N10, N7 and N7+ had 90% overlap — N7+ being a customized version with EUV added, made for Huawei. CFO Laura Ho added that N7-to-N5 overlap also exceeded 90%. This means N10, N7 and N5 can be treated as one giant capacity pool, so old fabs need not sit idle as customers move up. Conversion isn't free, but it can save billions of dollars in capital expenditure.

10:15

The claim that N7 can't convert to N3 expired within a year

The AI era needs a lot of N3 capacity. N5 and N3 have 90% tool overlap and both sit in Tainan's Fab 18, with a giant wafer bridge between them, making conversion easy. In 2024 TSMC began converting N5 to N3, at the cost of a bite out of gross margin. On the Q1 2024 earnings call, C.C. Wei was asked whether N7 could similarly be converted to N3; he said it was much harder, because N5/N3 are in the same fab while N7 is about 100 miles away in Taichung; he also said N7 demand would come back. By early 2025 he had changed his tune: for future AI products (namely Vera Rubin), they had to convert N5, but N5 demand was also strong, so they had to pick the timing; they had figured out a way to have N7 capacity support N5.

13:17

The foundation of the AI ecosystem is a fleet of low-tech trucks

After wafers leave TSMC's front-end fabs, they must be shipped to advanced packaging plants in Chiayi or Miaoli for CoWoS and InFO. How do you transport these $20,000 wafers? In semi-custom converted trucks carrying dozens of FOUPs, running on Taiwan's freeways like ordinary logistics vehicles. The ESG website even specifically works to improve truck drivers' labor conditions. This time TSMC has expanded the trucking service on a large scale: the Tainan fab does only the critical layers for N3 or N5, then loads the unfinished wafers onto trucks and drives 100 miles to Taichung, where underutilized N7/N6 fabs handle the finishing. The Tainan fab can thus devote itself fully to critical work. The world's largest infrastructure, the foundation of the AI ecosystem, turns out to rest on a fleet of low-tech trucks.

Glossary

cross-node utilization
Treating the capacity of multiple logic nodes as a unified pool, meeting new demand by converting old fabs and shipping between them.
gate-all-around (GAA)
A new transistor structure in which the gate wraps around the channel on all four sides, used at nodes such as N2.
back end of the line (BEOL)
The stage of building metal interconnect layers above the transistors, used to carry signals and power.
CoWoS
TSMC's 2.5D advanced packaging, placing multiple chips on a silicon interposer to interconnect them.
FOUP
The standard wafer carrier, protecting wafers inside the fab and in transit. The transcript misspells it as FOOP.
