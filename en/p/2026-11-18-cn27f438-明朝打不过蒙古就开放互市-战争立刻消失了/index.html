The Ming opened trade when military victory failed—and war vanished

After decades of fighting pirates and Mongols to exhaustion, opening trade under Longqing ended the conflicts, revealing that trade isn't zero-sum—instead, rules often emerge from the contact surface between exhausted opponents.

Three historical periods show how rules emerge from contact—Ming border trade, Shanghai's opening, and modern globalization. The common thread—that order grows from conflict surfaces—suits anyone studying institutional change.

2:32

Yangyang backpacks on African market stalls reveal China's scale

Shi Zhan saw backpacks with the Yangyang (喜羊羊) cartoon character on a roadside stall in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania—cheaply made yet shipped thousands of miles from Yiwu or Dongguan because Chinese scale made it cheaper than local production. This moment crystallized his understanding of China's sheer size and launched his decades-long inquiry: how did this scale form, and can it endure?

— Shi Zhan
8:09

Open trade in military stalemate and you end conflict

For 40 years the Jiajing era endured waves from south (pirates) and north (Mongols). The dynasty fought itself to exhaustion without pacifying either threat. The Mongols' actual demand was straightforward: permission to trade. Jiajing refused. His successor Longqing pivoted: he lifted the maritime ban in the southeast (centered in Zhangzhou) and opened Ming-Mongol trade. War stopped.

— Shi Zhan
13:09

Trade is positive-sum, war is zero-sum

Conflict between farming and pastoral societies at borders is fundamentally a clash of different peoples. War tends toward stalemate—neither side wins, both bleed out. That's zero-sum or worse. Trade is a plus game: both gain. This wasn't moral idealism but fiscal reality: endless warfare bankrupted the state, forcing pragmatism.

— Shi Zhan
14:26

Stranger societies run on contracts, not on personal trust

Fei Xiaotong described rural China as ordered by kinship and locality—a "differential order" where relationships trump formal rules. Shanghai, a port city of migrants (Ningbo bankers, Jiangbei natives, Subei natives), had no shared informal defaults. Each group's rules contradicted the others'. It switched to impersonal rules: contracts, deeds, documents. These address transactions, not persons. They scale faster and wider—why Shanghai entered modernity earlier.

— Liu Qing
18:13

New treaty port tariffs prevented dynasty collapse during rebellion

Every dynasty collapsed when rebels seized the fiscal heartland. The Qing alone escaped: the Taiping Rebellion conquered the Yangtze valley and the empire's financial core. Yet the Qing survived. Why? Shanghai's opening brought a fiscal source history had never seen: tariffs on seaborne trade, plus inland transit taxes (likin). The latter also indirectly funded Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang's armies.

— Shi Zhan
22:18

Shanghai once rejected outsiders with fierce local pride

Liu Qing arrived as a university student in 1978. Speaking anything but fluent Shanghainese meant different treatment from bus conductors and bookstore clerks in daily transactions. Even buying a seven-fen ticket required the accent. He traces this to the 1960s-70s when Shanghai only exported people (to broadcast stations and the countryside) while blocking incomers, breeding intense localism.

— Liu Qing
25:20

Order and freedom contradict; conflict changes the rules

People need both: rules provide stability, belonging, community. But rules also constrain freedom, novelty, risk. These wants clash. So people constantly break rules to claim freedom; failure drives retreat to order. How do rules change? Friction: new things press against old rules. This friction is China's engine of institutional change because geographic and cultural diversity means constant collision and renewal.

— Liu Qing
28:21

Shanghai proved Musk's impossible dream could actually work

Musk is not a manager carving a niche in an existing market. He imagined a totally new path for humanity, with EVs as a step. Model S launched in 2018 but couldn't scale. By 2019 Shanghai's Gigafactory rose in 11 months and began production. Shanghai soon accounted for over half global Tesla output and even greater profit share. Only then did capital markets believe his vision was real. His valuation took off.

— Shi Zhan
