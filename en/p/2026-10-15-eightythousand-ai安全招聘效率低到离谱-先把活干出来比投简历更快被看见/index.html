AI safety has a hiring efficiency crisis: public work outpaces résumé submissions

Because the talent market is inefficient and hard to screen, public writing, projects, and networking get you in front of AI safety recruiters faster than mass applications.

Having an informed viewpoint matters more than becoming an expert first

Building foundational AI safety literacy matters more than deep expertise. The target: hold a 30-minute conversation at an AI safety conference and convince someone you understand the core problems and a few solution paths. You need your own views on AI's progress speed, the most urgent problems, and which institutional theories make sense—you don't need certainty, but you need a reasoned position. Applicants who only repeat correct answers without showing their thinking tend to get screened out early.

Small cheap experiments reveal your fit better than career planning

The article images three precarious ladders leaning on each other, captioned: ‘It's fine if your career looks unstable for a while.’Instead of debating which path suits you—technical, policy, or communications—test with weekend experiments: write a blog post analyzing a think tank report, build a reusable data dashboard, organize a local meetup. Boredom or exhaustion from an experiment is signal too. Expand your options first; experiments and outside feedback will narrow them down.

— Matt Beard
5:49

The field grew 30× in 8 years; you're probably undershooting your chances

AI safety has more incubators, fellowships, and job listings than any other domain. The ecosystem grew approximately 3000% in 8 years, powered by problem urgency and a sprawling talent-development infrastructure. Yet the author, who advises in the field, has watched countless people undersell themselves. Apply for jobs even if you're uncertain you'll land one; the interview and test process will show you exactly where you stand.

— Matt Beard
7:26

One warm referral saves more time than a perfect application

Screening thousands of résumés is brutally costly, and even interviews and tests don't guarantee confidence in fit—so a referral from someone credible saves organizations enormous first-screen time. Even a weak signal like ‘we chatted at an event’signals seriousness and community engagement. But it's not required, the author stresses: many people get offers through cold applications, and most hiring processes include anonymous assessments to independently evaluate candidates.

— Matt Beard
10:44

Moving to a hub city will teach you more than remote preparation

People who successfully moved into AI safety told the author that relocating to where things actually happen—San Francisco, London, Washington—was their most important decision. The embedded knowledge and professional culture in a local community can't be replaced by articles or podcasts. If moving isn't feasible, attend in-person conferences like Effective Altruism Global and treat conversations as research, not social obligations.

— Matt Beard
12:20

Public work gets recruiter attention much faster than private experience

In interviews with approximately 40 AI safety organizations, respondents stressed that public writing, visible projects, and shipped work matter more than accumulated quiet experience—recruiters can't see inside your head, but they notice details like ‘she published a response to our paper.’ Public work also accelerates feedback: releasing early for criticism saves months of wasted effort, and even harsh feedback reveals whether you're missing skills or just need clearer communication.

— Matt Beard
15:35

Your side project can become your job if it solves real needs

The author describes a real case: Luca noticed AI safety lacked a talent networking platform, so he built one with login, opportunity matching, and recruiter support. Later, when 80,000 Hours was building a similar job board, someone suggested talking to Luca. After a few rounds of evaluation, they hired him full-time. The lesson: don't wait for a job offer to start doing the work you want. Even part-time side work gets you noticed faster.

— Matt Beard
17:01

Your impostor syndrome probably stems from using the wrong comparison group

The author suggests three tests: Are you comparing yourself to people with 3× your experience or 5 years your senior? Is your reference group only high-profile leaders, not the broader ‘competent people at the frontier’? Have you genuinely tested your learning potential—invested hours, tried methods, gathered feedback? He also warns that burnout is real, and consistent daily practice beats sporadic bursts of effort.

— Matt Beard
