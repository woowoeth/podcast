First direct observation of a star swallowing its companion: merger, not survival

Astronomers captured a giant star in the Andromeda galaxy slowly brightening for seven years then suddenly erupting to a million times the sun's brightness; evidence shows the companion star could not escape but was dragged into the core and merged, not the ‘shedding its shell, the binary survives’ scenario envisioned by common theory.

A star seven times the sun's mass swallowed a smaller companion

A research team observed in the Andromeda galaxy 2.5 million light-years away a giant star with mass seven times the sun swallowing a companion star with just 0.7 times the sun's mass. This is the first time anyone has truly ‘caught’ direct evidence of one star swallowing another, rather than theoretical inference. Previously, the astronomical community could only guess at this process through theoretical modeling; there had never been an observed case.

3:02

The moment a companion star drilled into a giant star's outer shell

As the giant star neared the end of its life and began expanding, its outermost gases were drawn by the companion star's gravity, transferring material toward it; this process rapidly accelerates, eventually trapping the companion star completely within the expanding shell of the giant star, with both stars sharing a single ‘common envelope’ orbiting each other. The host described this as the ‘intrastellar’ moment, and Karambelkar agreed: one star is truly inside another. Astronomers care about this process because the many binary systems in the universe with extremely short orbits and close proximity likely all originated from this kind of common envelope evolution.

— Karambelkar
5:03

Seven years of slow brightening, then sudden tenfold surge

As early as 2023, the team noticed this star slowly brightening in the infrared band, but its fluctuation pattern didn't look like typical behavior for a giant star, so in 2024 they published a paper proposing the hypothesis ‘this might be a companion-star-swallowing event.’ Then in October 2025, the system suddenly erupted, with brightness surging tenfold in a matter of weeks; the data from this eruption confirmed their earlier conjecture. The event was automatically designated AT-2025-ABAO, and the team simply called it ‘the Bow.’

— Karambelkar
6:04

Friction generates light: seven years of brightening from stars spiraling together

The slow brightening beginning in 2018 lasted about seven years, with brightness increasing about threefold. The team attributed this to the companion star, after drilling into the envelope, dissipating energy through ‘dynamical friction,’ with that energy visible as light. As the two stars orbited ever closer, the companion star eventually crashed directly into the dense core of the giant star, releasing enormous energy, as if ‘a bomb exploded inside the star’ — the brightness reached a million times the sun's in a single month during the eruption.

— Karambelkar
7:08

Data more supports merger than surviving as separate stars

Common envelope evolution theoretically has two possible outcomes: if the companion star can shed the envelope, friction disappears, and the two stars become a surviving binary with an extremely close orbit, rapid mutual circulation; if it cannot shed the envelope, the companion star will be dragged into the core throughout, eventually merging and being destroyed. The team interprets the latter as more likely, but emphasizes they cannot completely rule out the other possibility — only after the eruption's aftermath subsides and continued observation can they confirm whether the interior is a surviving binary or a single star.

— Karambelkar
8:08

First direct measurement of the efficiency parameter determining stars' fate

Whether the envelope can be shed depends on an ‘efficiency parameter’ that could previously only be estimated theoretically — high efficiency leaves a surviving binary, low efficiency leads to merger. This event provides the first direct measurement, and the team hopes to use it to judge whether other binary systems are more likely to merge or survive; however, with only one case so far, they still need to observe more similar events to verify whether this efficiency value is universal.

— Karambelkar
14:16

Bank robbers caught 60% of the time, hackers 0.05%

Crime in the physical world is far easier to catch than in the digital world: the highest clearance rate for the FBI's crime types is bank robbery, with about a 60% chance of being caught within 18 months; in contrast, the FBI doesn't even publish its cybercrime clearance rate, with the best estimate available being only about 0.05% of cybercrimes actually solved. This huge gap is the team's starting point for wanting to use quantum physics to ‘anchor’ digital communication to physical location.

19:31

Quantum entanglement solved the seemingly impossible photon-loss problem

Early quantum positioning protocols required the transmitted photons to suffer almost no loss, extremely difficult to achieve over long-distance fiber transmission. The team switched to using entangled photon pairs, drawing on the Bell test approach proposed by John Bell in 1964 — separating entangled particles far enough apart and measuring fast enough so that one measurement cannot possibly influence the other — and adapted it into a protocol tolerant of photon loss. The lab can now tolerate losing up to one-third of photons and still complete positioning verification.

— Krista Schalm
21:32

Principle verification experiments achieve 4 to 5 times better precision than classical methods

This positioning approach combining quantum no-cloning and entanglement achieved in principle verification experiments positioning precision four to five times better than any equivalent classical method. Currently limited by electronic device speed, the positioning range is roughly 30 to 50 meters, enough to confirm the signal truly comes from a certain building; the team believes that by optimizing the classical signal processing step, they could reduce positioning precision to room-size, allowing them to distinguish which department in a bank or which data center machine room the signal comes from.

— Krista Schalm
