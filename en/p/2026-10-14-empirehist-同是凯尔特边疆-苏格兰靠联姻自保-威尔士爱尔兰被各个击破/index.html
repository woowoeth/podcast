Celtic frontiers: Scotland saved by dynastic marriage, Wales and Ireland broken one by one

Normans married only with polities that had a single royal house to negotiate with—Scotland thus kept its autonomy; Wales and Ireland were dozens of mutually warring small kingdoms with no unified counterpart, so they fell one by one to military conquest.

7:14

Barbarity redefined: from heathenry to economic backwardness

William of Malmesbury, writing in the 1120s, reframed 'barbarians' from 'heathens' to 'peoples failing to meet economic and social standards.' By the late 11th century, England had abundant silver coinage, cities like London with tens of thousands, proto-universities, and Romanesque cathedrals, while Scotland, Ireland, and Wales had no mints of their own. Atop this economic standard came moral accusations—that they were lazy and insufficiently pious—which rationalized English seizure of their lands. This became the embryonic logic of what historians called ‘the First English Empire’, the template for expansion.

14:30

An exile's bloodline redraws the map of marriage and statecraft

Edgar Ætheling, with the strongest hereditary claim to the English throne, fled north after his post-Conquest rebellion failed. His sister Margaret married Scottish King Malcolm III—a union that bound the Scottish crown to the deposed Anglo-Saxon line. Their children had theoretical claims on England too, which is why William himself marched into Scotland's heartland and signed the Treaty of Abernethy. Margaret then began anglicizing the Scottish court, naming her sons in the English fashion.

— Mark Morris
20:43

David I remakes Scotland through Norman court training and imported expertise

David was Malcolm and Margaret's eighth son, unlikely ever to inherit—but his older brothers died childless, and he unexpectedly ascended in 1124. Raised in Henry I's Anglo-Norman court, he even fought for Henry in Normandy, returning to Scotland with a roll call of familiar surnames—Stuart, Balliol, Fraser. He founded more monasteries than any contemporary European ruler, established new burghs like Roxburgh and Edinburgh to attract merchants, seized Cumbria and discovered silver mines, allowing Scotland to strike its own coins for the first time.

— Mark Morris
32:11

Wales had no unified kingdom the Normans thought worth negotiating with

Scotland and Wales differed fundamentally: Scotland possessed a core kingdom the Normans could treat as ‘rustic cousins’ and marry into. Wales was a patchwork of rival petty kingdoms, none that the Normans considered a worthy diplomatic or matrimonial partner. The Normans therefore declared them adversaries, seized their lands by force, and drove them toward the sea. This explains why the Welsh March became studded with an extraordinary density of Norman castles—not as negotiating chips but as pure military outposts.

— Mark Morris
33:12

Castles alone could not hold the land; conquest required settler colonialism

Garrison strongholds and cavalry terror could not make the Welsh submit to manorial arrangements. True conquest was rooted in importing English peasants to clear land, establish new demesnes, and do the grinding labor of settlement. Lords lured immigrants with offers like ‘rent-free for ten years’ or ‘nominal pepper rents’—this was not occupation but thoroughgoing colonization, transporting population, language, and dominion wholesale. Historian Rees Davies called it the ‘second wave of Anglicization’, following the Anglo-Saxon migrations of the fifth to seventh centuries.

— Mark Morris
37:21

Strongbow lands at Dublin in 1170 and changes Ireland's trajectory

Deposed King of Leinster Diarmait mac Murchada fled to Henry II's court seeking aid. Henry was too pressed to send troops but permitted recruitment on English soil. The Fitzgerald clan moved first; Richard de Clare (nicknamed Strongbow), flush with ambition if thin on resources, landed with thousands in August 1170 and swiftly took Dublin. It was easier than anyone expected. He married Diarmait's daughter and was promised the Leinster succession. Dublin—that Viking commercial hub enriched by slave trading—became England's bridgehead into the Irish interior.

— Mark Morris
39:35

Henry II fears his subordinates will crown themselves kings across the Irish Sea

Henry II was appalled. Strongbow and his ilk had no interest in remaining mere English subjects; they wanted to cross the border and reign as kings, and refused to return and restore Diarmait. In 1171–72, Henry led an army across himself, forcibly imposing his authority over Ireland. But he withdrew early, leaving behind a half-conquered land. Hugh de Lacy, who succeeded him—described as short, hairy, muscular, and scarred—was given the lordship of Meath and became the hard enforcer of the conquest.

— Mark Morris
40:35

Gerald's Irish geography: conquest justified through racial slander and invented atrocities

When some English clergy protested—‘this is slaughter of Christians, do not do this in my name’—Gerald of Wales took up the pen in its defense. In his ‘Topography of Ireland’ he painted the Irish as filthy, depraved, living like beasts—‘utterly barbarous’. He fabricated lurid tales like a coronation rite involving bestiality with a horse. This was William of Malmesbury's ‘economic barbarian’ thesis, half a century on, upgraded into naked racial pseudoscience—the justification for seizing land, enslavement, and pushing the Irish into mountains and marshes.
