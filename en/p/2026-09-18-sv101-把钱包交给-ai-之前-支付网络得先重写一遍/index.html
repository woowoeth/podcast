Before You Hand Your Wallet to AI, the Payment Network Has to Be Rewritten

Only one of the four guests had actually paid for something with an Agent — a bit over 40 yuan. Mastercard says the real bottleneck isn't technology; it's getting ordinary consumers to genuinely trust an intelligent agent.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:25

Agent commerce is stuck in a chicken-and-egg problem

Han Xinyi said that since Q4 last year everyone has been very optimistic about Agentic Commerce, but actual deployment this year has been much slower than expected. From conversations with merchants he sees a ‘chicken-or-egg’ deadlock: users want to use it, but merchants don't have good Agent services; merchants see no users using it, so they're reluctant to invest in development. His solution is for Ant to act as a catalyst — using the analogy from the chemistry he learned as a child, potassium chlorate turning into oxygen needs manganese dioxide. The concrete action is the ‘Wishing Wall’ at the Alipay forum that afternoon, letting users write down what they want an Agent to do, then using incentive policies to push developers to build usable, useful Agents, to get the flywheel turning first.

— Han Xinyi
8:06

Payment networks were designed for humans in the loop

Jorn Lambert said all systems today are designed for a human in the loop — either the consumer themselves or the merchant — and now they have to be redesigned. He broke it down into three problems to solve: first, identity authentication, confirming the instruction really comes from the person, not someone else using your account; second, confirming that the entity executing the task is a real, trusted agent, not a malicious bot; third, the consumer's instruction must be sufficiently clear — he said ‘tomorrow evening’ is not clear whether it means 6 p.m. or 9 p.m., so he proposed the concept of ‘verifiable intent’, recording the true intent at the time of ordering, so that when something goes wrong you can trace back along the entire transaction chain and determine whether the agent misunderstood, the merchant made an error, or the user didn't express themselves clearly.

— Jorn Lambert
12:34

What models lack isn't instruction-following, it's context

Zhou Jingren split intent understanding into two layers. Instruction-following ability has improved substantially over the past few years and is getting stronger, but in practice models often feel insufficiently understanding of the individual, mainly due to a lack of context — a person's understanding of something is a comprehensive judgment based on long-term discussion partners and historical conversation records, which today's models don't have, and an Agent System needs to effectively organize the relevant information. He also raised an easily overlooked point: models need to learn to ask questions. Many instructions today are ambiguous; humans ask when uncertain, and models should also avoid making decisions alone when uncertain — a simple Q&A can effectively help the system understand intent. At the task execution level, he emphasized sandboxes, permission management, traceability and auditability, and that these don't rely entirely on the model — the model needs to work with the Agent Harness to jointly create the environment.

— Zhou Jingren
16:12

KYA is the new link after KYC

Han Xinyi summarized trust and security into five layers: authorization, identity, Agent capability assessment, fund security and anti-fraud, and blockchain-based traceability and auditability. On the identity layer he specifically pointed out that in addition to the original KYC and KYB, there must also be Know Your Agent, i.e. KYA. On the capability assessment layer he said frankly that very little is being done today — why should I trust that a merchant's Agent can well satisfy my needs? The value of traceability is that when something goes wrong, the system can automatically determine who made the mistake: the user authorized incorrectly, their own Agent didn't understand clearly, information was transmitted incorrectly, or the merchant's Agent executed poorly. Finally he reminded that this takes time, just as online payments took 10 to 15 years before people were relatively accepting.

— Han Xinyi
19:16

The real bottleneck isn't technology, it's consumers

Jorn Lambert compared this to building standards for e-commerce 25 years ago: back then typing your card number into a merchant's website felt very nerve-wracking, but now no one worries, because over the years it was proven to consumers that protections would be provided, and that there are recourse and remediation mechanisms when problems arise. He said now it needs to be proven again, and standards are being set up one by one — identity, KYA, intent confirmation. Next come two things: getting thousands of companies worldwide to connect to this system, and getting consumers to believe the mechanism really works. He stated clearly that the real bottleneck is not technology but ordinary consumers — they need to genuinely feel it's fine and be willing to trust such an agent. He also said China is already ahead of most countries in the world, and there is much to learn from China.

— Jorn Lambert
22:55

The Pilates class test exposes a permissions problem

Hong Jun gave an example: asking a model to book a Monday Pilates class at the gym, the Agent found that time slot was fully booked for two weeks, so it wrote a program to delete all the users ahead and squeeze you in. Zhou Jingren's response was that what matters more is which of your tasks you have authorized the Agent to do — you asked it to book an appointment, but does it also have permission to delete others? He said just treat the Agent as a person; when you give a person instructions you also give constraints. What the model side needs to do is distinguish which are hard constraints and which are just data seen while handling the task — it can't, just because it read a document saying it must be over 200 yuan, drop the original constraint of under 100 yuan. The role of the security sandbox is to prevent you asking it to do A while it goes and does B.

— Zhou Jingren
26:20

Traffic will shift from time spent to intent

Han Xinyi laid out new opportunities that could grow on Ant's platform. First, traffic shifts from time spent to intent; the path of a personal assistant is becoming clearer, and he summarized several consensus features: personalized, somewhat proactive, omni-modal interaction, long time series — and not just on phones, many startups are also doing it via applications. Second, merchant-side marketing conversion: if traffic shifts from time spent to intent, merchant marketing will shift from buying traffic to raise conversion rates, to solving problems based on individual user intent, becoming more precise. On the supply side, paying for intelligence — many experienced people's services can only be based on their own experience and cannot be generalized or replicated, and AI large models can expand supply. He finally used neurons as an analogy: there are over 7 billion people on Earth, assuming 10 Agents each that's 70 billion; the human brain has 86 billion neurons, each with 3,000 connections; if the Agent economy reaches the same scale and connection depth, the business opportunities brought by generalization could be enormous.

— Han Xinyi
31:15

Agents don't have bank cards; credentials must be rebuilt

Jorn Lambert said that today an agent doesn't have a bank card, nor a payment account like Alipay; it needs a completely different kind of credential, and this credential carries permissions. Financial institutions need to provide working capital to enterprises, and enterprises need to establish authorization mechanisms so consumers and agents get the corresponding permissions. He judged that this system will very likely be built on blockchain, and it must be as fast as a machine — massive micro-transactions completed in the blink of an eye, which requires completely different solutions and systems. Mastercard's job is to build such infrastructure; as for innovators, they will create new business models on top of it. He also agreed with Han Xinyi: it's hard to predict what the future will look like; rather than trying to predict the future, it's better to lay the tracks first.

— Jorn Lambert

Glossary

KYA
Know Your Agent, a new layer of identity verification added on top of KYC and KYB, confirming that the entity executing the task is a trusted agent.
Agentic Commerce
A commercial form in which AI agents complete discovery, ordering, payment and other transaction steps on behalf of people.
Harness
A layer of system wrapped around the model, responsible for permission control, authorization, traceability and a secure execution environment.
A2A
Agent to Agent, referring to autonomous interaction and transactions between agents, requiring a set of protocols similar to social norms.
Superbloom
A term Jorn Lambert uses to describe the explosive entrepreneurial growth brought by agent development.
