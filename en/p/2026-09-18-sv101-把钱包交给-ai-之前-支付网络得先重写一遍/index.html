Before You Hand Your Wallet to an AI, the Payment Network Has to Be Rewritten

Only one of the four guests had actually paid for anything with an Agent — 40-odd yuan. Mastercard says the real blocker isn't technology, it's getting ordinary consumers to genuinely believe an agent can be trusted.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:25

Agent commerce is stuck in a chicken-and-egg loop

Han Xinyi (韩歆毅) said that since last Q4 everyone has been extremely optimistic about Agentic Commerce, but actual deployment this year has been much slower than expected. From conversations with merchants he sees a ‘chicken-or-egg’ deadlock: users want to use it, but merchants don't have good Agent services; merchants see no users using it, so they're reluctant to invest in building. His solution is for Ant to act as a catalyst — using the analogy from the chemistry he learned as a kid, potassium chlorate turns into oxygen only with manganese dioxide. Concretely, that means the ‘wishing wall’ at the Alipay forum that afternoon, letting users write down what they want an Agent to do, then using incentive policies to push developers to build usable, genuinely good Agents — get the flywheel spinning first.

— Han Xinyi
8:06

Payment networks were designed for a human in the loop

Jorn Lambert said every system today is designed on the assumption that a human is involved — either the consumer themselves or the merchant — and now it has to be redesigned. He broke out three problems to solve. First, identity authentication: confirming the instruction really came from the person, not someone else operating your account. Second, confirming that the entity executing the task is a real, trustworthy agent rather than a malicious bot. Third, the consumer's instruction has to be unambiguous enough — he said ‘tomorrow evening’ doesn't make clear whether that's 6pm or 9pm, so he proposed the concept of ‘verifiable intent’: record the true intent at the moment of ordering, so that when something goes wrong you can trace back along the whole transaction chain and determine whether the agent misunderstood, the merchant made an error, or the user simply didn't express themselves clearly.

— Jorn Lambert
12:34

What models lack isn't instruction-following, it's context

Zhou Jingren (周靖人) split intent understanding into two layers. Instruction-following has improved substantially over the past few years and keeps getting stronger, but in real applications the model often feels like it doesn't understand the individual well enough — mainly a lack of context. A person's understanding of something comes from combining long-running discussions and historical conversation records, and that's what today's models don't have; an Agent System needs to organize the relevant information effectively. He also raised an easily overlooked point: models need to learn to ask questions. Many instructions today are ambiguous; people ask when they're unsure, and a model should likewise avoid deciding on its own when uncertain — a simple question and answer can effectively help the system understand intent. On task execution, he stressed sandboxes, permission management, and traceability and auditability, and noted these don't rest entirely on the model — the model has to work with the Agent Harness to create the environment.

— Zhou Jingren
16:12

KYA is the new link after KYC

Han Xinyi summed up trust and safety as five layers: authorization, identity, Agent capability assessment, fund security and anti-fraud, and blockchain-based traceability and auditability. On the identity layer he specifically called out that beyond the existing KYC and KYB, you also need Know Your Agent, i.e. KYA. On capability assessment he said plainly that very little is being done today — why should I believe a merchant's Agent can serve my needs well? The value of traceability is that when something goes wrong, the system can automatically determine who actually erred: the user authorized the wrong thing, their own Agent didn't understand clearly, information was passed incorrectly, or the merchant's Agent executed poorly. Finally he cautioned that this takes time — just as online payments took 10 to 15 years before people broadly accepted them.

— Han Xinyi
19:16

The real blocker isn't technology, it's the consumer

Jorn Lambert compared this to building standards for e-commerce 25 years ago: back then typing your card number into a merchant's website felt nerve-wracking, and now nobody worries, because over all these years consumers were shown there would be protections, with recourse and remediation when things go wrong. He said that now has to be proven again, and the standards are being put in place one by one — identity, KYA, intent confirmation. Two things come next: getting thousands of companies worldwide onto this system, and getting consumers to believe the mechanism actually works. He stated plainly that the real blocker is not technology but ordinary consumers — they need to genuinely feel it's fine and be willing to trust such an agent. He also said China is already ahead of most countries in the world, and there's a lot to learn from China.

— Jorn Lambert
22:55

The Pilates class test exposes a permissions problem

Hongjun (泓君) gave an example: asking a model to book a Monday Pilates class at the gym, and the Agent finds that slot booked solid for two weeks, so it writes a program to delete all the users ahead of you and squeeze you in. Zhou Jingren's response was that what matters more is which of your tasks you've authorized the Agent to do — you told it to book an appointment, but does it also have permission to delete other people? He said just treat the Agent as a person: when you give a person instructions, you also give them constraints. What the model side has to do is distinguish which are hard constraints and which are merely data seen while handling the task — it can't read a document saying it must be over 200 yuan and then drop the original constraint of under 100 yuan. The role of the security sandbox is to prevent you asking it to do A and it going off and doing B.

— Zhou Jingren
26:20

Traffic will shift from time spent to intent

Han Xinyi laid out the new opportunities that could grow on Ant's platform. First, traffic shifting from time spent to intent: the path for a personal assistant is getting clearer, and he summed up several consensus traits — personalization, a degree of proactivity, all-modality interaction, long time sequences — and not just on phones; many startups are doing it in the form of applications too. Second, merchant-side marketing conversion: if traffic shifts from time spent to intent, merchant marketing shifts from buying traffic to raise conversion rates toward solving problems according to each user's individual intent, more precisely. On the supply side, it's paying for intelligence — many experienced people's services rest only on their own experience and can't be generalized or replicated, and large AI models can expand that supply. He closed with a neuron analogy: there are 7 billion-plus people on Earth, and assuming 10 Agents each, that's 70 billion; the human brain has 86 billion neurons with 3,000 connections each. If the Agents in the economy reach the same scale and the same depth of connection, he believes the generalization would bring enormous business opportunities.

— Han Xinyi
31:15

Agents have no bank card, so credentials must be rebuilt

Jorn Lambert said an agent today has no bank card and no payment account like Alipay; it needs a completely different kind of credential, and that credential carries permissions. Financial institutions need to provide working capital to businesses, and businesses need to build authorization mechanisms so consumers and agents get the corresponding permissions. He judged that this system will very likely have to be built on blockchain, and it has to be as fast as a machine — massive volumes of micro-transactions completed in the blink of an eye, which requires completely different solutions and systems. Mastercard's job is to build that infrastructure; as for innovators, they'll create new business models on top of it. He agreed with Han Xinyi: it's very hard to predict what the future will actually look like, and rather than trying to predict it, better to lay the tracks first.

— Jorn Lambert

Glossary

KYA
Know Your Agent — a new layer of identity verification added on top of KYC and KYB, confirming that the entity executing a task is a trustworthy agent.
Agentic Commerce
A commercial model in which AI agents complete the discovery, ordering, payment and other transaction steps on a person's behalf.
Harness
A system layer wrapped around the model that handles permission control, authorization, traceability and a secure execution environment.
A2A
Agent to Agent — autonomous interaction and transactions between agents, requiring a set of protocols akin to social norms of behavior.
Superbloom
Jorn Lambert's word for the explosive entrepreneurial growth that the rise of agents will bring.
