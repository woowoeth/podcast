A Glass of Water Has No Final Truth: Quantum Mechanics Forces Us to Give Up the Single Viewpoint

Physicist Carlo Rovelli says the deepest lesson of quantum mechanics is not a new particle, but that the Enlightenment dream of a single objective reality independent of the observer does not hold — reality is many viewpoints that enrich one another but never converge.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:06

A glass of water has no final version

Rovelli opens with a glass of water the BBC provided: you look from one side, I look from the other, you wear glasses, I see a different colour. This glass of water can be a physical object (mass, velocity, position), it can be temperature and pressure, it can be the chemistry of H2O plus floating bits, it can be the global economy of being bottled in Italy and shipped to England, or it can simply be "we want to drink it." His claim is that all these perspectives are real, none exhausts another, none contradicts another, and there is no "ultimate glass of water." This is anti-Platonic — he does not accept a distinction between a "level of appearances" and a "fundamental level."

— Carlo Rovelli
6:13

We still have not digested quantum mechanics

Rovelli says what he is describing is not borrowed from philosophy but grown from inside physics. Physicists ran into quantum phenomena; quantum mechanics is a huge revolution, but "we have not yet digested quantum mechanics" — the newspaper stories about experiments that "confirm what I learned in a textbook in the 1970s" show precisely that we still do not truly believe it. Take quantum mechanics seriously and what it says is this: the Enlightenment dream of "describing reality completely, determinately, clearly, independently of an observer" does not hold. The observer here need not be a human; anything that interacts with something counts.

— Carlo Rovelli
8:19

The deepest level of reality is not more objective

Rovelli turns it around: the reality at the most basic level physics can reach is in fact not essentially different from the reality Lavinia deals with in poetry, or that James's characters deal with in economics — both are realities that allow different views, not a reality that is "absolutely objective underneath." So quantum physics is not that hard; it simply has not been digested. The concrete physics is of course very complex, but poetry is complex and economics is complex too.

— Carlo Rovelli
10:25

Scientists have always used metaphors

Rovelli says the words Lavinia uses could be lifted line by line into his book. What is an electron? "It's a grain, a grain of sand, but it isn't really a grain of sand." Why is an electric field called a field? Because it is "a bit like a field in the countryside." We think through metaphor, by taking old concepts apart and piecing together a new concept from a bit of each. The goal is not to arrive at a final, perfect description of reality — a century ago we thought we could, but it does not work — but to gain a better understanding, a better way of navigating reality. He adds a crucial qualification: admitting that everything has a perspective does not mean all perspectives are equivalent, nor that there is no better and worse; it is only that the hope of "arriving at the final correct story about reality" is empty — not unreachable, but meaningless.

— Carlo Rovelli
12:27

Seeking an ultimate morality is itself the error

Rovelli turns from "complexity" to "value": if you ask "what is the ultimate definition of good and evil, what is a universal ethics," he thinks this is itself a morally and ethically mistaken approach. Our morals and values are powerful, important and indispensable not because they are outside us but because they are inside us, and therefore equally perspectival. This passage connects directly to James Graham's play — whose very first line is "what is the good life."

— Carlo Rovelli
15:30

Don't be afraid to learn a jargon

Lavinia Greenlaw argues for embracing jargon: not everyone has to learn it, but if you are genuinely interested in something, don't be afraid to learn its language — a mechanic has to learn the names of parts, a surgeon has to learn the names of parts, and a poet has to learn them too. It lets you see more clearly, and it lets you see where something has gone wrong. She admits jargon can be exclusionary, but "that depends on the context in which you use it," and "you can perform difficulty, and poets and poems are probably very good at that, but that is not what difficulty means." She gives the example of poems on the Underground: before everyone looked at their phones, people would read a poem first and only then realise they were reading a poem — that kind of engagement is far more direct and confident than sitting down and being told "now you are going to read a poem."

— Lavinia Greenlaw
22:55

Friction is treated as a problem to be solved

James Graham says modern culture has fetishised "efficiency" and strips out every layer of difficulty as friction. He gives a concrete image: emailing himself a script to save it, a prompt pops up — "this is a long document, shall I summarise it for you?" — and his reaction is "how rude, I want you to sit inside it and read it." He connects this back to Bloomsbury and Keynes: meaning is discovered precisely in the friction, in the difficulty, in the process. He also says the collapse of reading is despairing, because reading demands personal investment, and today that investment is treated as a problem to be solved rather than something to be celebrated.

— James Graham
26:05

War refuses to acknowledge complexity

Rovelli uses International Peace Day on 21 September, and remarks by the UN Secretary-General and the Pope, to connect the argument about perspectives to war: war is to a large extent driven by greed and power games, but it always wraps itself in a hypocritical narrative — there is only one truth, only one good, we are the good ones, they are the bad ones, we tell the truth, they tell lies and propaganda. So war is built on a total refusal to be prepared to accept complexity, perspectives, and worldviews different from our own. The host Adam Rutherford immediately offers a counterexample: we also always need simplicity, and if you embraced difficulty and acted scholarly all day you might get nothing done and enjoy nothing. Lavinia's response pulls the topic back to rhetoric: language therefore matters more than ever, and poetry and poetics can teach people how to argue.

— Carlo Rovelli
32:29

Buying paintings for twenty thousand pounds in wartime

James Graham tells the most gripping piece of history in his play: in the First World War Britain had just gone from being the world's creditor to a debtor, and Keynes, as a junior adviser at the Treasury, asked Treasury officials and the Permanent Secretary for £20,000 to go to Paris to buy paintings, because Edgar Degas's collection was coming up for auction. At the time all the money was flowing to armaments and the war. No one knows what he said at that meeting, so the playwright gets to write his words for him. Graham says the "absurdity" of the case is precisely its power: if there was any money left over, what should be done is to buy a few paintings and put them in the National Gallery, because in the future they are both an asset on the government's balance sheet and an asset that lets the nation have beautiful things to look at after the war — "if peace is not about creating things that make people happy in the future, then what is it?"

— James Graham
37:37

Two men on a chapel roof watching for incendiaries

The opposition between Keynes and Hayek is written by Graham as the core of the play: Hayek represents the Adam Smith tradition of the "invisible hand" — let it be, it will self-correct, and intervening with imperfect human hands is dangerous and reckless; Keynes represents the interventionist mixed economy of the postwar consensus from 1945 to 1979. The two did meet, and on one occasion during the Second World War both were on the roof of King's College Chapel, Cambridge, as fire watchers, entirely by chance, watching the Luftwaffe fly over looking for incendiaries. No one knows what they said that night, and Graham says this is a gift to a playwright, so he wrote their dialogue: Keynes says "Hayek, without imagination, how do we dream a new world," and Hayek replies "You dream away, Mr Keynes, I like to stay awake."

— James Graham

Glossary

anapaestic decameter
A metre of two unstressed syllables followed by one stressed syllable, repeated five times; Lavinia jokes when giving an example that she may have got it wrong.
iambic pentameter
The basic metre of English poetry: one light syllable followed by one heavy syllable, repeated five times.
Bloomsbury Group
A group of artists, writers and philosophers in early twentieth-century Cambridge and London; Keynes was a member.
friction
In product and writing contexts, the resistance involved in using or understanding something; James Graham objects to treating it uniformly as a problem to be eliminated.
