The Dollar Stands Alone: Yen, Euro, Yuan, and Gold All Challenged and Failed

After two decades advising central banks on asset allocation, Morgan's fixed income chief says for the first time he cannot identify a currency to replace the dollar—yen, euro, yuan, and gold have all been tried and failed.

6:20

Fixed income rewards skill more than stock picking does

He believes that beating benchmarks through active stock picking over the long term is difficult, but fixed income is different—because bond returns can be calculated precisely through maturity yield and cash flows, unlike stocks which have dozens of competing valuation methods. Portfolio returns that simply exceed the benchmark have a high probability of outperforming long-term—which is why he expects his team to nearly always beat the benchmark, rather than make binary bets. Early in his career he did equity research; he switched entirely to fixed income in the early 1990s, drawn precisely by this calculability.

— Bob Michele
12:31

Strategic delevering, not luck, saved Morgan in 2008

During his two-week gap between leaving Schroders and joining Morgan Asset Management in 2008, Lehman collapsed, and Bear Stearns and Washington Mutual failed in succession—yet Morgan was the one picking up bargain assets. He credits this to Jamie Dimon forcing subprime-derivative positions off the balance sheet years earlier, preventing the bank from making speculative bets. This experience taught him: every bond has different collateral, repayment priority, and covenant terms; research cannot be outsourced.

— Bob Michele
16:39

The dollar has won the reserve currency competition

He has spent years meeting with central bank officials around the world, and historically someone always proposed switching to a different reserve currency: in the late 1980s it was the yen (when the Japanese were buying Rockefeller Center), when the euro was created there was hope it would rival the dollar, after 2009 it was the yuan (with China driving global demand through debt accumulation), and most recently it has been gold. Yet every candidate has eventually exited the stage—the euro lacks fiscal union backing, and gold cannot truly help central banks manage inflation. Now he cannot think of a second candidate.

— Bob Michele
23:52

Today's rates are not high—they're finally normal

He rejects the phrase 'higher for longer' to describe the current environment, arguing instead that this is simply a return to the long-term neutral rate the Fed first calculated in its 2012 dot plot. The low rates of the past 25 years were actually an anomaly, which he attributes to oversupply of housing (which should have retained value) by the baby boom generation, triggering the crisis. Now that rates have real pricing, it is actually good for clients—investment-grade bond yields are now above 6%.

— Bob Michele
34:07

AI companies that don't expand capacity face BlackBerry's fate

Jamie Dimon and Mary Erdos have been pushing the team to prioritize AI for three years, calling it bigger than the internet bubble or even the industrial revolution. The problem hyperscalers face now is demand far exceeding capacity—either use a clean balance sheet to borrow and expand production quickly, or build gradually on internal cash flow, but move slowly and you risk becoming the next BlackBerry or Nokia, with competitors seizing the market first. Morgan operates both asset management and banking, so it participates across the full chain from credit facilities to equity issuance for these companies.

— Bob Michele
42:21

Debt service matters more than total debt burden

Japan's debt is close to 300% of GDP, China's close to 200%, America's just over 100%—but he says these aggregate figures don't reveal where the 'structural ceiling' is. The more meaningful metric is debt service as a share of GDP—the US historical high is near 5%, currently just over 3%, and he considers this still quite reasonable. His rule of thumb: once this ratio approaches 10%, the national finances are truly in trouble.

— Bob Michele
46:31

Emerging market local-currency bonds offer genuine hidden value

He was taught early in his career that emerging markets borrow and then default, but now it is completely different—the young interns from emerging-market central banks he mentored back then have returned home and now head their own central banks. Local-currency bond markets now have complete yield curves and no longer depend on cross-border capital flows—local pension funds and insurers anchor the long end, wealth-management platforms anchor the middle and short end. In his portfolio, emerging-market local-currency debt is held nearly 90% by local investors, and in 2021 these central banks even raised rates before developed markets did.

— Bob Michele
58:51

The 1991 cohort will remake demand and policy for decades

He sees demographic structure as the main story for the next twenty years: the generation born around 1991 is now 35 years old and is replacing the generation born in 1959 as the dominant cohort earning, spending, and saving money—this will bring structural growth and inflation pressure. Meanwhile, people like him from the baby boom generation are living longer and will not clear their assets upon retirement as past models assumed, but will continue holding longer. These two forces combined will reshape demand structures and policy responses ahead.

— Bob Michele

Bonds issued by US local governments, whose interest is usually exempt from federal tax
Stablecoin
Crypto asset whose value is pegged to fiat currency like the US dollar
