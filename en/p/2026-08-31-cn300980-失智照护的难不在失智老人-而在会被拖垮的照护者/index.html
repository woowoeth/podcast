The hard part of dementia care isn't the patient — it's the caregiver who gets ground down

The hard part of dementia care isn't the illness itself, but the caregiver worn down day after day — care turns a wife into a nurse and pushes a millennial to quit. Love is the starting point, not the support, and the system hasn't caught these families yet.

The care gap ends up pressing back down on every family

China has 323 million people over 60, 23% of the population; 35 million are disabled elders, but there are only about 500,000 eldercare workers. The supply gap hasn't been absorbed by the market or institutions, and the pressure mostly falls back on families and individuals. Alzheimer's is not a distant term: reports say China's patient count is nearly 30% of the world's, and the host estimates that on average one in every 20 Chinese families is affected — for the first generation of only children, this may be a reality their parents are approaching.

9:08

Dementia has no standard symptoms, and the movies can't be trusted

After interviewing 34 families, the misunderstanding Tang Yong most wants to correct is this: there is no uniform image of a person with dementia. Society's imagination of dementia mostly comes from films, neighbors or hearsay — the professor's incontinence in Still Alice is just one form. He has met a tailor who, after dementia set in, still noticed stains on a visitor's clothes and taught people to remove them with orange peel; and a man disabled by a car accident who, when he could only hum Liuyang River, had a look of total concentration. Dementia itself covers Alzheimer's, vascular dementia and other causes, and each person's life marks linger in different ways — caregiving can't be performed from a script.

— Tang Yong
23:47

The dementia village ideal is paid for by state nursing insurance

Most people treat the Dutch dementia town Hogeweyk as the ideal answer: elders here stroll the supermarket and visit the café like residents, while staff play shopkeepers to keep up a normal atmosphere. But the details the host adds expose how hard it is to replicate — the 23 homes built in 2009 cost more than 18 million euros, of which 17.8 million was borne by the government, and residents' fees are covered by long-term care-related systems. What Tang Yong saw in a low-income American nursing home was the opposite: elevators had to be locked to keep dementia patients from wandering off. Same disease, and the difference isn't philosophy but the state's payment system.

33:11

Love isn't enough, and sacrifice will grind down the caregiver too

The host sums up the caregiver's predicament precisely: many people swing back and forth between ‘I love my mom and dad so much’ and ‘I'm about to be crushed by my mom and dad’. Tang Yong says care is not one-way help but a relationship; when a spouse has to learn to bathe, feed and handle emotions, the equal partnership is overlaid by a nurse-patient relationship. Long-term sacrifice is not a noble devotion to be romanticized: a daughter he interviewed in Guangzhou twenty years ago, caring for a disabled father, divorced and fell into severe depression; the book also includes a millennial caregiver forced to quit because of caregiving pressure. Love is the starting point, but without outside resources and community support, the caregiver becomes a patient too.

— Tang Yong
46:39

The more you see, the less ground you have on eldercare

After years of researching elder care, Tang Yong has no clear plan for his own parents' old age. He is a first-generation only child, his parents are both 75 this year, and the more cases he sees the less ground he has; his research assistant even said after every interview that the more families she saw, the less she knew how she would arrange things for her parents. Families with multiple children have friction too: who does more, who pays less, or someone's rough caregiving gives an elder bedsores; only-child families lack people to share the load, and some who brought their parents to Shenzhen find rent, medical bills and nanny fees stacking up faster than pensions rise. An ideal model cannot be designed by a single child alone.

— Tang Yong
50:14

The pain of dementia care is losing something every day

The biggest difference between dementia care and disability care is that the caregiver must repeatedly accept loss. While volunteering in Seattle, Tang Yong observed an elder who could at first paint blue lines on wet watercolor paper, but weeks later only dared to praise others and not put brush to paper; another elder's husband reported new losses every week: his wife could not recognize the grandchild their daughter had just had, and did not know where this child had come from. This day-after-day, irreversible downward slide drains a caregiver more than physical disability. The length of care is not the repetition of hardship but a small goodbye faced every day.

— Tang Yong
1:00:47

Long-term care insurance is here, but it can't catch every elder

Long-term care insurance has been piloted in Qingdao and Shanghai since 2016, and by around 2025 it has spread to about 90 cities, with the official pace being universal coverage within the next two or three years. But Tang Yong cautions that long-term care insurance doesn't hand money to every elder; through unified assessment it mainly covers the moderately and severely disabled, paying for home nursing, meal delivery and other services — the mildly impaired may not qualify. The hardest part is mindset: putting parents in a nursing home in China is often equated with unfiliality, and elders, like the grandfather in the interviews, feel that after a month there their hair is nearly falling out. Tang Yong's judgment is that as younger generations age, the share who actively choose institutional care will rise, but the institutional gap won't disappear on its own.

— Tang Yong

Glossary

Hogeweyk
A closed community designed for severely dementia-impaired elders, where staff play residents to maintain a normal living atmosphere.
The Soul of Care
By Arthur Kleinman; argues that care is not one-way help but a relationship.
informal care
Unpaid care provided by spouses, children and other family members, as distinct from hired nannies and care workers.
long-term care insurance
Social insurance that pays for nursing services for disabled and dementia-impaired elders, not a cash subsidy.
wet-on-wet watercolor
Painting on wet watercolor paper so colors bleed naturally, used as a non-pharmacological intervention for elders with dementia.
