The Hard Part of Dementia Care Isn't the Dementia Patient—It's the Caregiver Who Gets Dragged Down

The difficulty of dementia care lies not in the patient's condition itself, but in the caregivers who are worn down day after day—care turns wives into nurses and forces post-90s to quit their jobs. Love is the starting point but not the support; the system hasn't caught up with these families.

0:00

The care resource gap ultimately falls back on every family

China's population aged 60 and above is 323 million, accounting for 23% of the total population; there are 35 million disabled elderly, but only about 500,000 eldercare workers. The supply gap is not absorbed by the market or institutions; the pressure mostly falls back on families and individuals. Alzheimer's is not a distant term: reports say China's patient numbers account for nearly 30% of the global total, and the host estimates that on average, one in every 20 Chinese families is affected—for the first generation of only children, this may be the reality their parents are approaching.

9:08

Dementia has no standard symptoms; movies are unreliable

After interviewing 34 families, Tang Yong most wants to correct the misconception that dementia patients have a uniform image. Society's imagination of dementia mostly comes from movies, neighbors, or hearsay; the professor's incontinence in 'Still Alice' is just one type. He has seen an elderly man who, having been a tailor, still noticed stains on visitors' clothes and taught them to remove them with orange peel; and an elderly woman who, disabled after a car accident, could only hum 'Liuyang River' with focused expression. Dementia itself covers multiple causes—Alzheimer's, vascular dementia, and others—and each person's life imprints remain in different ways, so care cannot follow a script.

— Tang Yong
23:47

The dementia village ideal depends on state nursing insurance

Most people see the Dutch dementia town Hogeweyk as the ideal answer: residents shop at the supermarket and visit cafes like ordinary residents, while staff pose as shopkeepers to maintain a normal atmosphere. But the host adds details that expose how hard it is to replicate: the 23 houses built in 2009 cost over 18 million euros, of which 17.8 million was borne by the government, and residents' costs are also covered by long-term care-related systems. In contrast, what Tang Yong saw in a low-income U.S. nursing home was the opposite: elevators had to be locked to prevent dementia patients from wandering off. The same disease differs not in philosophy but in the national payment system.

33:11

Love isn't enough; sacrifice drags down caregivers

The host captures the caregiver's predicament well: many oscillate between 'I love my parents very much' and 'I'm about to be dragged down by them.' Tang Yong says care is not one-way help but a relationship; when a spouse must learn to bathe, feed, and manage emotions, the original equal partnership is overlaid by a nurse-patient relationship. Long-term sacrifice is not a beautifiable devotion: twenty years ago in Guangzhou, he interviewed a daughter who, after caring for her disabled father, divorced and fell into severe depression; the book also features a post-90s caregiver forced to quit their job due to caregiving pressure. Love is the starting point, but without external resources and community support, caregivers themselves become patients.

— Tang Yong
46:39

The more you see, the less confident you feel about eldercare

Having studied eldercare for years, Tang Yong himself has no clear plan for his own parents' care. He is a first-generation only child; his parents are both 75 this year, but the more cases he sees, the less certain he feels. His research assistant even said after each interview that the more families she saw, the less she knew how to arrange care for her own parents. Multi-child families also have friction: who does more, who pays less, or someone's rough caregiving causes bedsores. Only-child families lack shared manpower; one person moved their parents to Shenzhen, where rent, medical costs, and nanny fees stacked up faster than retirement wages rose. An ideal model cannot be designed by a single child independently.

— Tang Yong
50:14

The pain of dementia care is losing every day

The biggest difference between dementia care and disability care is that caregivers must repeatedly accept loss. While volunteering in Seattle, Tang Yong observed an elderly man who initially could paint blue lines on wet watercolor paper, but weeks later could only praise others and dared not put brush to paper. Another woman's husband reported new losses every week: his wife couldn't recognize their daughter's newborn grandson and didn't know where this child came from. This daily, irreversible downward slide drains caregivers more than physical disability. The long duration of care is not repetitive hardship but facing a small farewell every day.

— Tang Yong
1:00:47

Long-term care insurance has arrived but doesn't cover all elderly

Long-term care insurance was piloted in Qingdao and Shanghai in 2016, and by around 2025 had expanded to about 90 cities; the official pace is to move toward universal coverage in the next two to three years. But Tang Yong reminds that long-term care insurance is not giving money to every elderly person; through unified assessment, it mainly covers those with moderate to severe disability, paying for home care, meal delivery, and other services; those with mild conditions may not qualify. The hardest part is also cultural: sending parents to a nursing home is often equated with filial impiety in China, and elderly people, like the grandfather in the interview, feel their hair would fall out within a month. Tang Yong's judgment is that as younger generations age, the proportion actively choosing institutional care will rise, but the institutional gap will not disappear automatically.

— Tang Yong

Glossary

Hogeweyk
A closed community in the Netherlands designed for people with severe dementia, where staff pose as residents to maintain a normal living atmosphere.
The Soul of Care
A book by Arthur Kleinman that argues care is not one-way help but a relationship.
informal care
Unpaid care provided by family members such as spouses and children, as opposed to paid caregivers.
long-term care insurance
A social insurance that pays for care services for disabled or demented elderly, not a cash subsidy.
wet-on-wet watercolor
Painting on wet watercolor paper so colors blend naturally, used as a non-drug intervention for dementia patients.
