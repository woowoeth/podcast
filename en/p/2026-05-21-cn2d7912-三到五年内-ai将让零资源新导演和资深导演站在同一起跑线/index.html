In three to five years, AI puts debut and veteran directors on equal footing

Zheng Zhi observes that film production has always meant an all-or-nothing gap between those with resources and those without—until AI drove live-action costs down exponentially. In the next three to five years, he predicts, a resourceless young talent and an acclaimed director will stand on equal ground; from then on, it will matter only whether you have something worth shooting.

He stumbled into directing only after vetoing every other candidate

While writing *The Forest Within the Forest*, Zheng announced it would be his last screenplay—he planned to focus on novels instead. Over two years of pre-production, he rejected every director the producers suggested. Finally, someone asked if he simply didn't trust anyone else with his vision, and he reluctantly took the chair himself. He'd never shot even a short film before, yet went straight into a star-studded feature with Yu Hewei and Gao Yuanyuan. Everyone who saw the finished film was struck by its sophistication.

— Zheng Zhi
20:19

His most vivid memory from filming had nothing to do with the camera

Early in production, Yu Hewei flipped his electric bike while filming an oncoming-traffic scene and was hospitalized. The crew steeled for the worst—they might not be able to shoot that sequence. Yu sent a reassuring message saying he'd return soon. The team flew to Hainan to wait anyway, and one night the three creators got trapped in pitch darkness at the base of Wuzhi Mountains nature reserve. They spent nearly ten minutes watching fireflies in absolute black. That moment—not a single frame of footage—remains Zheng's most indelible memory from the entire shoot.

— Zheng Zhi
21:19

The hardest lesson a first-time director learns is how much to cut

The rough cut ran nearly four hours—originally more of a micro-historical epic. But theaters and the market wouldn't accept the length. Cutting from four hours to two and a half went smoothly; after that, every cut meant scraping away two or three minutes at a time. The final cut landed just under two hours. Zheng admits this reflects raw inexperience: you can write a hundred scenes, but a debut director has no instinct for how long each should play. This math—deciding what to cut—proved harder than the shooting itself.

— Zheng Zhi
45:32

AI fundamentally changes who can afford to direct a feature film

Zheng argues that film budgets have always created a binary divide: you have resources or you don't. But once AI drives production costs down exponentially, the coming three to five years will see a penniless young talent and an established veteran standing on level ground. Then the playing field shifts from 'who can mobilize what resources' to 'who actually has something worth shooting.' This is technological equity, not mere cost reduction.

1:07:49

The age of art forms that unite an entire nation is over

Zheng's generation of Northeast writers—called the ‘三杰’, or three peaks of the Northeast renaissance—would have achieved vastly different fame if born two decades earlier, during the avant-garde literary boom. Luo Yonghao ran several deep dives on this using AI, and every major model reached the same conclusion: mass art forms will likely never emerge again. Audiences will only fragment further across niche interests. It's not a bad thing, but it does mean the experience of a whole country debating a single work may be permanently gone.

— Luo Yonghao
1:40:01

His heavy use of dialect was a calculated marketing move, not authenticity

Zheng admits his early work leaned heavily on Northeast dialect—a deliberate stylistic hook, the kind successful writers of Liu Zhenyun's and Wang Shuo's generation also wielded when starting out. He gives a telling example: he was the editor of the Hong Kong edition of Moyan's *Red Sorghum*, published after the Nobel Prize win. The edition contains at least fifty typos—all because Shandong dialect words failed to convert from simplified to traditional Chinese, and even a Shandong-native copyeditor couldn't decipher them. His new novel does the opposite: he's nearly abandoned dialect entirely.

— Zheng Zhi
1:56:42

He got published through a crush his roommate had on the same girl

At 19, while cramming for the college entrance exam, Zheng finished his first novel. A Hong Kong roommate—who'd grown close to him over a mutual crush on the same woman—took the manuscript to Beijing to show his father. The father gave it a score of 85 and recommended he submit it. Zheng printed it out, walked into Writers' Publishing House, and pitched himself to an acquisitions editor for nearly an hour straight. The editor didn't refuse on the spot. Months later came an email: the manuscript had ‘a chance’ of being published. Zheng had expected the classic story of dozens of rejections before acceptance, but it was ‘too smooth from the start’—it got published outright.

— Zheng Zhi
2:24:21

A single thriller novel solved a financial crisis he thought might end his career

His first three serious literary works sold worse with each release. The third earned only 9,000+ yuan in royalties over two years. Zheng began to doubt the writing path was sustainable. He made a deliberate shift: ‘I just want to write a story people actually want to read.’ He wrote a mystery novel called *Swallow Whole*, serialized it online first, and ultimately sold 200,000 copies—his best-selling book ever. The royalties directly solved his financial crisis. ‘It saved me,’ he said, surprised even at himself.

— Zheng Zhi
