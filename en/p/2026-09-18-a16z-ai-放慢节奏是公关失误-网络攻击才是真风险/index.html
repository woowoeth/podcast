Pacing Is a PR Failure; Cyberattacks Are the Real Risk

Frontier labs packaging safety as pacing pleases no one; the genuinely urgent curve is CVE weaponization collapsing from years to minutes, while enterprises still have no agents, only faster search.

0:00

Pacing is a PR failure, not a safety strategy

Ali argues that frontier labs packaging "safety" as "pacing" is a textbook PR failure. Pacing is orthogonal to safety — you can build weapons slowly, and that's no different from building weapons; and these companies have been running full speed and buying more compute, so nobody believes pacing. The result pleases no one: doomers don't think it's a pause, and policy people don't think you know how to do it. What they should be saying, he argues, is "secure the frontier," not "pace the frontier."

— Ali Ghodsi
12:24

RSI only counts if it meets four conditions at once

Ali gives four criteria for judging whether recursive self-improvement is happening, and all must hold simultaneously: the next generation of models requires fewer resources (GPUs), takes less training time, is smarter/more accurate, and these three things can repeat over and over. If any one fails, the system self-limits — for instance, with constant resources it hits a hardware wall. He stresses that "software writing software" is already happening today, with over 90% of software at Databricks written by AI, but that is not the same as superintelligence.

— Ali Ghodsi
15:24

The cost curve for frontier training runs the other way

Sarah points out that the compute bar for training frontier models keeps rising — from 100 million to 1 billion, and now roughly 5 to 10 billion. Ali adds: there are only one or two such training runs a year, and they completely violate those four criteria — they need more resources, more people, are more fragile, and require building data centers, networking and fault tolerance. They've blown up several times, and failure means burning enormous sums. So the reality is "slower, more fragile, harder," not "faster, cheaper, smarter."

— Ali Ghodsi / Sarah Wang
24:40

Weaponization time for a vulnerability went from years to hours

Ali gives this curve: in 2018 and 2019, a CVE took roughly two to three years from disclosure to weaponization; by 2022 it was down to eight or nine months; from 2022 to now it has compressed to basically hours, almost no time at all. The reason is that data and AI are merging with the cybersecurity market — companies run large numbers of agents internally, generating massive logs and traces, and the volume of data that needs analyzing is orders of magnitude larger than a year or two ago.

— Ali Ghodsi
32:44

Say AI will destroy the world, then ask you for IPO allocation

Ali concedes two things can be true at once: some people really are scared by AI risk, and some people really do want regulation to "pace us" — because slowing down benefits them. People can always reconcile self-interest and fear into a harmonious whole in their own heads, he says. The industry has long had this marketing play: the moment a new model is trained, shout about how terrifyingly good it is, and the world's attention arrives. But he also gives the other side: the time from CVE to actual weaponized exploit went from years to minutes in three or four years, and cyberattacks are real. So "scary" is both a genuine risk and excellent marketing.

— Ali Ghodsi
39:50

Enterprises have no agents, only faster search

Ali polled the room: how many people are managing hundreds or thousands of agents coordinating and negotiating with each other? Almost no hands went up. Most enterprises use Microsoft Copilot, which is essentially a chatbot — "a very, very glorified, efficient Google search." Coding is the only scenario that has truly landed, and even there the ROI is debatable. He thinks the reason isn't that models aren't smart enough, but that models lack the organization's internal context: they haven't sat in every meeting, don't know what's in everyone's head, don't know the processes. Every organization has a few employees who know everything, and when you tap them on the shoulder you think, "what if he quits?" Pour that context into today's frontier models and you get enormous productivity — and that doesn't require a smarter model.

— Ali Ghodsi
47:01

Ontology is that employee who's been there five years

Ali explains ontology with a concrete picture: two people equally smart, equally hardworking, with identical educational backgrounds — one on his first day, one who's been there five years. The difference is that the latter knows how the organization runs, who the people are, how things get done — "don't look at the org chart, don't ask that guy, he can't get anything done." That's ontology: tacit knowledge scattered across everyone's heads. He contrasts it with the state of agents today: the agentic loop looks up resources one at a time, like Google crawling ten sites live on every search, taking ten minutes, then handing you ten blue links — expensive, slow and bad. Google's answer was the index, and AI needs that index computed offline too, only harder — because of permission constraints, since data I can access isn't necessarily data you can access, and the object types go far beyond web pages.

— Ali Ghodsi
56:08

Same model, different harness, nearly 2x the cost

Databricks' answer is a full cost-governance stack: Unity Gateway provides token capacity for OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, Grok and open-source models, sets budget caps and alerts by person and by group, does cost forecasting analytics, and adds intelligent routing to switch to cheaper models when nearing budget or when the question is simple. Ali gives a concrete number: the same model, the same version, just changing the harness, can differ in actual cost by nearly 2x. Their in-house harness is called Omnigent, which can multiplex across different harnesses. The result: token usage keeps climbing, but AI costs are roughly flat.

— Ali Ghodsi

Glossary

pacing
A framing frontier labs use to package safety issues, arguing for deliberately slowing the pace of AI development.
RSI
Recursive self-improvement: AI that can improve the next generation of AI, forming a self-accelerating loop.
CVE
Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures: the numbering system that publicly records software security flaws.
ontology
The tacit knowledge structure inside an organization about people, processes and permissions.
harness
The software layer that wraps a model and handles tool orchestration and context.
branching
Lightly spinning up multiple branches on the same database so agents can experiment in parallel.
