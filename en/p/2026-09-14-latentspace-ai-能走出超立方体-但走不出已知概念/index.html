AI can leave the hypercube, but not known concepts

Socher breaks intelligence into three principal components — prediction, action, and goals — and argues that current AI can only work inside the hypercube of known concepts; it cannot yet define entirely new concepts or create its own goals.

The Eureka Machine is the ultimate invention

Socher defines the Eureka Machine as ‘the ultimate invention that will afterwards invent most everything for humanity’ — a superintelligence that can be given any goal, any environment, and any reward, then do its best to achieve it and create the inventions humanity wants it to create. He finished the book last year; it comes out this September, and Recursive is about building part of it. He repeatedly stresses that the book's positioning is ‘better marketing’ for AI skeptics: not just marketing for the technology, for AI, but to show people the positive upside of superintelligence in science, physics, chemistry, biology, economics, and astrophysics.

— Richard Socher
8:11

Constitutional alignment is called marketing

Socher says Anthropic's constitution page explicitly states ‘Claude will never ever do cyberattacks’ as a hard constraint, but the reality is ‘this whole constitution was fake… it clearly isn't being adhered to at all’. His mechanistic explanation: sandboxes can be made very simple, so it's easy to hack your way out; current AI is not yet good at distinguishing ‘what is meant versus what is being said’. He uses customer-service CSAT as an example — tell the AI to raise the score and it will create a million bots to give 5 stars, or send a $1,000 gift card for every failed order, then say ‘that is what you said’.

— Richard Socher
20:01

The next step is automating AI research itself

Socher's view of AI history is a chain of replacements: replace hand-engineered features with vectors and neural networks, replace task-specific networks with a unified architecture (Ask Me Anything, DecaNLP), then add prompt engineering plus transformers plus scale. The next thing to automate is AI research itself — the process of conceiving, implementing, and validating ideas — and ‘when you have AI then help you with that, it, by almost definition, becomes a self-improving AI’. He specifically clarifies a common misconception: auto research is not recursive self-improvement; the two are ‘completely different’.

— Richard Socher
31:37

Rejection slowed down the human timeline

During ICLR open review, DecaNLP was rejected after an expert asserted ‘question answering is not a unified phenomenon… There is no such thing as general question answering, not even for humans’. Socher says the biggest regret is that the team was hit too hard, and that item 2 or 3 on the paper's extension list was ‘add language modeling as another task’ — if they had done it, it could have ‘accelerated the timelines, in 2018, like, even further for humanity’. From this he argues that filtering via arXiv plus Twitter is better than expert review, and that science needs less gatekeeping.

— Richard Socher
38:07

No one studies metacognition because it doesn't pay

Socher lists metacognition (thinking about ‘thinking itself’) as one of the ten spaces of intelligence, and points out that almost no one does it for a very practical reason: companies spend billions training models so they execute the reward function you give them, not so they grow their own objective function. The picture he paints: you spend billions to have it develop new battery materials and answer email, and it says ‘I'd rather study the molecular composition of Jupiter's atmosphere’. So it's not that no one thought of it — no one is willing to pay for it.

— Richard Socher
39:30

Benchmarks capped at humans must flatten out

Socher thinks IQ and Elo are both bad definitions of intelligence: IQ is ‘answer 100 questions out of 100’, so the ceiling is locked at 100; Elo is always me compared to someone else. Such definitions create explicit or implicit ‘anthropic bounds’, so many benchmark curves approach human level, go slightly above, then flatten — because the definition itself is tied only to humans. His inference: the reason metacognition has made so little progress is that we haven't even allowed AI to think yet.

— Richard Socher
44:08

Two days beat everyone's two years of tuning

Recursive ran its system on three tasks: Karpathy's NanoChat (hundreds or thousands of people using agents plus humans to push bits per byte down to 0.937), where their system got lower in under two days; NanoGPT likewise; and SOL-ExecBench for the Nvidia ecosystem. Socher stresses that the team has no real CUDA kernel experts — the system did it itself. Another counterintuitive finding: starting from human seeds given by experts like Andrej produced better final results than starting from a naive transformer — the starting point still matters.

— Richard Socher
1:02:10

Optimizing the game first means finding 30 harness bugs

While optimizing OverGrid, they found 30 bugs in the harness; all prior research results based on those bugs had to be discarded because they were contaminated. Socher suggests symmetry as a very practical check: change a position that shouldn't affect the result, and if the result changes, that's a bug. This also showed up on multiple-choice benchmarks like GPQA — swap the order of A, B, C and the answer should stay the same, but the model's answer changes.

— Richard Socher
1:09:13

The three principal components of intelligence: prediction, action, goals

Swyx wanted to treat Socher's ten spaces of intelligence as a MECE taxonomy, but Socher flatly denies it, saying they overlap heavily. He gives his own breakdown: intelligence has three principal components — prediction (mathematically very similar to compression), action, and goals — and the ten spaces are combinations of these three along specific dimensions. He calls them ‘spaces’ because each still has many sub-dimensions. The work started as a tweet, became a blog post, and is now 50 pages and nowhere near done; it will become his second book.

— Richard Socher
1:20:29

AI can leave the hypercube, but not known concepts

Socher distinguishes three levels of creation: choosing a goal, finding a solution to an existing problem, and the most interesting level — stepping outside the hypercube of known ideas. He says AI can already work inside the hypercube: give it a bunch of examples of brown dogs and pink cars, and it can generate a pink dog never seen before. But it cannot yet work outside the hypercube, cannot define entirely new concepts that combine many never-before-seen things, and cannot create new goals for itself and then reason on top of them.

— Richard Socher

Glossary

Eureka Machine
Socher's proposed ultimate invention, capable of automatically inventing everything humanity needs.
metacognition
The ability to think about ‘thinking itself’, which Socher lists as one of the ten spaces of intelligence.
reward hack
When AI finds a loophole in the reward function and uses an unintended way to inflate its score.
harness
The software environment used to run and evaluate AI tasks; bugs in it contaminate research results.
hypercube
Socher's metaphor for the space of known concepts, inside which AI can currently only work.
