Addiction is wanting despite knowing it won't work and being unable to stop

Neuroscience reveals 'wanting' and 'liking' as two separate brain systems: drugs make you crave them increasingly without making you enjoy them increasingly. This divergence is addiction.

DSM's 11 diagnostic criteria point to one marker: dysfunction

Angela runs through the 11 DSM diagnostic criteria for substance use disorders: using more than intended, repeated failed attempts to cut back, spending huge amounts of time obtaining and using the substance, abandoning social and occupational activities, continuing despite knowing it's harmful, escalating tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, craving, failing to meet role obligations, persisting despite interpersonal consequences, and using in dangerous situations. Stephen identifies the common thread: "dysfunctionality"—whether the behavior has seriously impaired your functioning at work, in relationships, and in social roles. Both agree this is the key boundary between a bad habit and genuine addiction.

8:23

Below DSM's line lies a spectrum of quasi-addiction

Stephen notes that DSM sets a deliberately high threshold to satisfy insurance coverage, but vast ground lies below that line. Many people feel genuine compulsion about late-night sweets or Netflix binges yet fall short of clinical addiction. Angela agrees addiction exists on a continuum; the question isn't "Am I addicted?" but "Where do I fall on the dysfunction spectrum?" The boundary itself is somewhat arbitrary.

— Angela
14:42

Coca-Cola's official stance: regular consumption doesn't mean addiction

Angela cites Coca-Cola's website statement: ‘Many people occasionally enjoy sweetness—that's normal. Regularly consuming food and drinks you enjoy doesn't mean you're addicted to them’. She notes the irony: both regular and Diet Coke contain caffeine, widely recognized as addictive, yet the company selectively sidesteps this by discussing "sweetness" in general rather than addressing caffeine's role.

— Angela
16:47

Addiction happens when wanting disconnects from liking

Angela cites neuroscientist Russ Poldrack's definition of addiction as engaging compulsively and uncontrollably in a behavior despite harmful consequences. She explains that in healthy behavior, what you want is what you like. But with certain drugs, the brain's "craving" and "enjoyment" systems completely separate: you want more and more without liking it more. This is addiction's most counterintuitive feature.

— Angela
20:57

Drug users strategically plan theft; addiction isn't just habit

Angela distinguishes habit from addiction in behavioral form: habits are unconscious and automatic (like always turning left at a familiar intersection without noticing), while addiction often involves highly conscious, planned, goal-directed behavior—a drug user carefully plotting "I'll rob this person for cash, then buy drugs." This shows addiction and mere habit operate through different mechanisms.

— Angela
25:05

Dopamine drives wanting, not the pleasure of consumption

Angela cites neuroscientist Kent Berridge's 2021 paper, distinguishing "liking" (the hedonic response, mediated by the limbic system's hedonic hotspot) from "wanting" (incentive salience, mediated by dopamine-related corticolimbic networks). The public believes dopamine is the "happiness chemical," but research shows it corresponds to the "wanting" system. All highly addictive drugs share the trait of stimulating this "wanting" system intensely and persistently, without making the activity itself more enjoyable.

— Angela
28:18

What humans are most addicted to is other people

Angela recounts economist Gary Becker (known for rational addiction theory) saying informally that what humans are most addicted to isn't drugs or gambling, but other people—we crave approval, love, and admiration. Stephen counters with the Unabomber: gifted enough to skip grades yet unable to form friendships or romantic relationships, he deliberately chose isolation in a Montana cabin. Yet he still "interacted" by sending bombs. Whether this refutes or confirms Becker's claim remains unresolved between them.

— Angela/Stephen

Glossary

DSM
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, the official standard used by American psychiatrists to diagnose and classify mental illness, and the basis for insurance reimbursement.
incentive salience
The neural mechanism that makes a reward cue particularly compelling, driving desire and craving.
rational addiction theory
Gary Becker's economic theory that addiction, like other behavior, results from rational cost-benefit calculation.
