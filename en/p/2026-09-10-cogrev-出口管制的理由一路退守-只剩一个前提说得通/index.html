8:56

What the China discourse lacks is a target state

Nathan says the missing piece in America's discussion of China is a ‘target state’: everyone jumps straight to ‘we have to win’, but nobody answers what winning looks like, what losing would mean, or whether there is an achievable condition for victory. His proposed goal is Pax Machina, or better, Pax Robotica — the two countries and the world sharing the prosperity and peace of the AI era, robots going into factories, into fields, into homes, while avoiding the AI version of the sword of Damocles. He says explicitly that this is not about winning, it is about arriving together. A competition with no target state leaves nothing but escalation itself.

— Nathan Labenz
18:56

China isn't rising, it's returning to historical normal

To the claim that ‘China can't innovate’, Nathan applies one word: cope, and says it is isomorphic to cope in the AI field — conceding that AI has gotten this far while insisting it won't reason, won't discover new knowledge, won't change paradigms. His judgment is that China's return is the long-run historical norm, and that the period he and Dario grew up in — China down, America the sole superpower — was the anomaly. Any effort to slow China's progress can therefore work at most in the short term, and China will be a first-tier player in almost every major human domain. This is not a prediction, it is, in his view, something already locked in by historical structure.

— Nathan Labenz
28:44

On purchasing power, China is half again as big as the US

In dollar-denominated GDP, the US is about 30 trillion and China about 20 trillion, so the US is 50% bigger; on purchasing power parity, China is about 45 trillion and the US still 30 trillion, so China is 50% bigger the other way. Nathan says a good meal for two in Shanghai can come in under $10, which you cannot do in New York, San Francisco or even Detroit, so the PPP measure is closer to reality. A more concrete picture: a roughly 45-minute pedicure costs about $12, and the young employee in her twenties at the shop pulls out an iPhone 17 — the iPhone 17 is not cheaper in China than in the US, and high-end Xiaomi and Huawei phones also approach a thousand dollars. His explanation is the one-child structure concentrating the wealth of four grandparents plus two parents onto a single person.

— Nathan Labenz
1:25:10

The chip ban hits a century of humiliation

China sees itself as a top-tier civilisation that spent most of history at the technological frontier, and missed it once and was invaded, forced to take opium, with the European powers and Japan as the perpetrators — and someone who was 20 when the Cultural Revolution ended in 1976 is still alive today. The core lesson is that you cannot fall behind at the technological frontier: ‘we are never going back.’ Huawei is the tech champion under this framework: the first to genuinely reach the top of a global frontier industry, only to be blocked by the US pressuring allies over espionage risk, when Snowden has already proven that the US listens to everyone. The chip ban is the same script replayed: AI is barely off the ground, there isn't even an agent that can reliably book a flight, and the sale is banned first. Nathan says this is not Xi Jinping's view alone; it has seeped into educated society. He also concedes that American companies' grievance over stolen IP is entirely legitimate.

— Nathan
1:35:23

China doesn't talk about an AI race, it talks about public goods

Nathan did not hear a single person the whole trip say China is ahead or China will win, and almost nobody even framed AI as a race; what he heard was prosperity, material abundance, how to care for an aging population. In a mall he asked a car-selling company about autonomous driving and the person volunteered that ‘but Tesla is better.’ At WAIC, the first line of Xi Jinping's speech was that this field of AI began at Dartmouth College in the US — he could have left it out, and careful observers said this was an olive branch; another line was that we should jointly oppose ‘over-extending the concept of national security in the AI field.’ The hardest signal China has put out so far is open weights: take them, run them for free, fine-tune them however you like, and once they're out you can't take them back. Nathan says this is both a sincere wish and a strategic position, quite credible now, but entirely capable of changing later.

— Nathan
2:11:27

The rationale for export controls keeps retreating

Nathan thinks export controls do have an effect, but the effect is mainly in the future — the large-scale expansion of chip capacity hasn't landed yet. What he really objects to is the drift in the policy's rationale: originally to prevent military applications, then retreated to stopping frontier models, now retreated to limiting how many AI agents China can run; this sounds like an attempt to suppress the Chinese public, to keep Chinese companies and individuals from using the tools Americans use themselves. The only self-consistent premise for this policy is a belief that we are in a short race toward godlike superintelligence and whoever gets there first takes all — which is basically Anthropic's belief, so Dario advocating these policies is not self-contradictory: he genuinely believes recursive self-improvement kicks off in '27 or '28. Nathan concedes he can follow the logic, but still argues for slowing the frontier together instead.

— Nathan
2:47:07

The most dangerous part of decoupling is not understanding each other

He asked a Chinese interviewee: if decoupling continues and the two sides end up on different branches of the technology tree, no longer understanding what the other has, would that worry you? The answer was ‘it's not time yet’; when would it be time? ‘When China's chip ecosystem is good enough that we can do our own thing entirely.’ From this he derives the core risk: a hardware fork will drag along a hardware-algorithm co-design fork, and add to that China stopping open weights and stopping publishing in English, and it may not take long to reach ‘we don't know the shape, the architecture, the capabilities of each other's AI.’ At that point both sides would genuinely be right to panic, and genuinely right to race.

— Nathan Labenz
3:07:25

Replace the arms race with a disease gold-medal table

He first lays down a rule: don't train LLMs to maximise money-making on the open internet, and don't train AI to accomplish military objective x by any means necessary — that is training by hand the takeover we fear most. The alternative is a ‘disease-cure gold-medal table’: both China and the US love medal tables, and Olympic-style competition is a positive-sum game. The table could be most kinds of cancer cured, most diseases cured, highest energy-density battery, lowest annual traffic deaths. He says this takes very little capital to catalyse and can be offered unilaterally as a global public good. The landing point: he doesn't care whether the cancer cure comes from China or the US, he just wants cancer cured.

— Nathan Labenz

Glossary

Pax Robotica
Nathan's proposed target state: China, the US and the world sharing the prosperity of the AI era, robots in factories and homes, rather than suppressing one another.
eternal 1991
Dario's phrase for American complacency after winning the Cold War; Nathan flips it to say the Chinese government is in its own 1991.
cope
A psychological defence that accepts the fact but rejects its implications; Nathan uses it for the claim that ‘China can't innovate.’
confidential computing
Data is computed on inside a protected environment and only the conclusion is returned; even the operator cannot see the raw data.
formal methods
Proving mathematically that software behaviour matches its specification; Nathan suggests using it as a condition for lifting the Huawei ban.
system three thinking
Nathan's coinage: transcending circumstance, dreaming, insight, breaking out of existing paradigms — the residual advantage leaders have over AI.
