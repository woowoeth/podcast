Polish film history was actually written by film festivals: never reaching Cannes means never having made a film

A researcher noted that when David Bordwell wrote the history of Romanian cinema, he didn't write about it at all—not because the films were bad, but because those films were never sent to Western film festivals where he could see them.

9:17

Art films were Eastern European countries' foreign communications budget

After the Cold War, especially in the new century, Eastern European countries' film funds faced a choice: fund commercial films, and only domestic audiences would watch; fund art films, and they could be seen by foreign scholars, critics, and international audiences, creating international circulation. Yin Xiang points out that this logic led these countries' film management institutions to consistently favor artistic works, funding many art films rather than commercial ones.

— Yin Xiang
15:12

International film festivals determined who got written into film history

Yin Xiang says that how Western scholars wrote Cold War-era film history depended heavily on what they could see at international film festivals—basically, whatever the film festivals showed, they wrote about. He gives an example: David Bordwell, a top film historian, didn't write a single word about Romanian cinema, for a simple reason: those films were never sent to Western film festivals, so Bordwell had no way to see them.

— Yin Xiang
28:31

Polish film style changed overnight when Stalin died in 1953

Yin Xiang found through research that before Stalin's death in 1953, Polish films were standard "land reform films": you could tell the landlord villains as soon as they appeared on screen, the visual language was completely stylized, comprehensible without subtitles. The moment Stalin died, the style changed dramatically. Kawalerowitz's subsequent works were described as "French Left Bank style, entire film with grand psychological structure"—this kind of turn would have been absolutely impossible before.

— Yin Xiang
34:07

Making a warrior from an exile government the protagonist was risky at the time

The protagonist Maciek in Ashes and Diamonds was a member of the London exile Polish government's Polish Home Army, essentially a "villain" by the official political stance of the time. Yet Wajda built the entire narrative structure of the film around this character's perspective, rather than around the secretary character in the original novel. The guest believes this choice itself carried the national emotional structure Wajda never laid down in his life—the split between European Poland and Poland seized by the Soviet Union.

— Cheng Yanliang
49:26

Man of Iron's political influence far exceeded its artistic evaluation

Yin Xiang frankly said he didn't particularly like Man of Iron, because structurally it's almost a sequel to Man of Marble, and "negative propaganda is also a form of propaganda." But both acknowledged that this film's real-world impact might be the largest of all Polish films—there's a saying that it helped Lech Wałęsa, the real person (who also appeared in the film), go international, eventually leading to a Nobel Prize and the presidency, though this causal connection is considered exaggerated.

— Yin Xiang
58:29

Man of Marble exposed propaganda by comparing source material with the finished film

In Man of Marble, a student director gets the original raw materials of documentary footage used to create model workers, and discovers they're completely different from the finished film: shots of exhausted workers, muddy ground and other images were all edited out, leaving only beautiful images of engineering feats paired with music. The guest believes this is exactly where Wajda was most brilliant—not telling you who the bad guy is, but directly showing audiences how political propaganda discourse is manufactured through editing.

— Yin Xiang
1:02:40

The lie about the Katyń massacre lasted over forty years

In spring 1940, the Soviet NKVD executed approximately 22,000 Polish officers and intellectuals in Katyń Forest, including Wajda's father. After the Nazis discovered the mass grave in 1943 and first reported it publicly, the Soviet Union turned around and insisted it was the Nazis' doing. This narrative was maintained with unified messaging throughout the Eastern Bloc during the entire Cold War, until the Gorbachev era when the Soviet Union finally admitted it was the NKVD's deed, approved by Stalin himself.

1:12:06

Wajda made national epics, Kieślowski made high-concept films

Yin Xiang distinguishes the two Polish directors in one sentence: Wajda dealt with national issues and big subjects—audiences immediately knew he was telling Polish history; Kieślowski made entirely "high-concept" films, like The Double Life of Véronique, which on the surface is a romantic film about two girls' mysterious connection, but actually also alludes to Eastern European upheaval, yet wraps it extremely lightly—the threshold is far lower than Wajda's films which require first taking a history lesson.

— Yin Xiang
