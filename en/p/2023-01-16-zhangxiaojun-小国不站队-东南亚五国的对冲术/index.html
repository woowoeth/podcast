Small States Don't Pick Sides: The Hedging Playbook of Five Southeast Asian Nations

Myanmar, Laos, Cambodia, Brunei and Timor-Leste rarely fall in line with one side. Instead, they rely on one power for agriculture and another for defence, using multi-directional diplomacy to hedge between great powers.

Small states rarely fall in line; they all hedge

Huang Yuxiang cites the framework from his teacher's book, ‘client or hedge’: traditional theory holds that a small state within a great power's sphere of influence must obey unconditionally, but states today are far shrewder and rarely fall in line. What they practise is multi-directional diplomacy — for instance, depending a bit more on one country for agriculture and a bit more on another for defence, so that viewed through traditional client-state theory they look deeply contradictory. He gives Bhutan as an example: it is in effect an Indian protectorate, yet it has also established diplomatic relations with the UK and tried to establish them with China. However small a state is, it wants to retain a degree of autonomy.

— Huang Yuxiang
13:13

Myanmar is a state that has not finished being built

Since the 2021 military coup, Myanmar has been in a long civil war with no sign of ending. The immediate trigger was the house arrest and detention of Aung San Suu Kyi, which broke its earlier hedging strategy of maintaining economic cooperation and communication with both China and the US. The local historian U Thant Myint-U has a book called “Myanmar: The Unfinished Nation”, arguing that Myanmar is a state that has not finished being built — although it has a territorial map, in reality it is a geographical term. The dominant Bamar ethnic group is only 50% to 60% of the population; the north around Kokang has long been autonomous and beyond control; and the warlords in Shan State and Wa State command forty to fifty thousand troops each. The army's combat effectiveness is poor and corruption is severe, reflecting a country that does not know where it is heading.

— Huang Yuxiang
16:16

Northern Myanmar's chaos is three countries' bandits squeezed together

Huang Yuxiang cites Han Enze, a professor at Xi Yang University, in “Strong Neighbours at the Side: The Contest for the China-Thailand-Myanmar Borderlands”: the Southeast Asian highlands, including Yunnan, Guizhou and southern Sichuan, all the way to northern Myanmar and northwestern Thailand, form a single geographical unit above 3,000 metres in altitude, where people do not need to migrate; the Burmese and the Thai both came down from Yunnan along the rivers. There is a ‘shower effect’ here — if we assume bandits are a constant, then if you cannot make it in Yunnan you run to Myanmar, and if you cannot make it in Thailand you also run to Myanmar. The better China controls the Yunnan border, the more bandits run to northern Myanmar. So northern Myanmar bears the bandits of three countries with the strength of one, and is of course very hard to govern.

— Huang Yuxiang
27:23

Laos's value is visible only through the lens of land routes

Laos is the only landlocked country in Southeast Asia and has long been a client state of Vietnam, a transitional geographical unit with little independence. From a sea-power perspective it does not matter, because it has no coast; but if you want to build a fast corridor or a high-speed railway, running a rail line from China to Thailand's outlet to the sea, you must pass through Laos. Its territory is all mountains, and the flat land around the capital Vientiane is essentially on the Thai plain. It naturally lacks resources and can only rely on connections to collect tolls, like Afghanistan. It is now hedging, seeking cooperation with Vietnam and Thailand, and there is the Greater Mekong economic cooperation framework integrating the Mekong basin economy; the World Bank says this is the last place on earth to be developed.

— Huang Yuxiang
38:29

Cambodia's killing was meaningless killing

The previous episode concluded that the more economically developed countries in Southeast Asia were all built on bloody killing, yet Cambodia killed so many people and its economy still did not develop well. Huang Yuxiang's explanation is: that was meaningless killing. Indonesia is a country of a thousand islands, and to achieve economic integration it had to deal with people who held different views, and killing was the fastest method; but Cambodia itself has a long history, and through the experience of the Khmer Empire and colonisation it was already a state and did not need such a construction process, yet in pursuit of an ideal it slaughtered millions of its own people. The result was that the people were utterly disgusted with the regime, and Vietnam's troops occupied Cambodia very smoothly.

— Huang Yuxiang
46:35

Brunei lives on oil but never developed a second industry

Brunei was once a very large kingdom, relatively strong from the eighth to the eighteenth century, called Boni in Chinese records, and in the sixteenth century it controlled the whole of North Borneo and the southern Philippines; today its area is only just over five thousand square kilometres. Its economic success comes entirely from the development of oil and natural gas; its luck was that when it was invaded it retained a piece of territory with relatively abundant oil and gas, close to the sea and easy to develop. The problem is that all the oil money went into welfare and it did not develop much industry of its own. Only in recent years has it begun to think about diversification, and it has only just started; no one knows how it will turn out.

— Huang Yuxiang
50:37

Timor-Leste cannot even afford to fly

Timor-Leste has an area of just over ten thousand square kilometres, half of Taiwan, with mountains covering 90% of it; its geology is granite and limestone, making agriculture basically impossible. The capital covers just over one hundred square kilometres, and the population is basically all in the capital. It was colonised by Portugal, and its value within the Portuguese Empire was as a place to exile convicts. Its natural conditions are extremely poor, and it suffered long, harsh Indonesian rule; it has been independent for only 20 years. You can tell from the flights: you cannot fly there directly from China, you must go via Singapore on Timor Air, and Timor Air belongs under Singapore Airlines and seems to have ceased operations after the pandemic, so you can only fly from Australia. It cannot even afford cargo, it does not even have an airline.

— Huang Yuxiang
57:41

Japan used economic reparations to penetrate Southeast Asia

The US is simpler: its traditional ally is Thailand, as the front line containing Vietnam. Japan is more complicated: after the war it had to pay economic reparations, and through reparations it penetrated local economies. Reparations were not food but aid, like the Marshall Plan — I give you aid, but you must buy my goods. Japan has always been a player in Asian regional integration, and that only changed somewhat after China launched the Belt and Road. Japan's territory is very small, so it must expand politically and economically outward in order to have generous welfare and an economic foundation, which is why it has always promoted the Asian Monetary Fund and the Chiang Mai Initiative, funded half by China and half by Japan. The US pivot to Asia, Japan's arc of freedom and prosperity, and China's Belt and Road all intersect in Southeast Asia.

— Huang Yuxiang

Glossary

hedging
A diplomatic strategy in which small states bet on multiple sides among great powers to reduce the risk of dependence on any one.
multi-directional
Depending on different great powers in different domains such as agriculture and defence, rather than falling in line with one side.
reconciliation
The core goal of transitional justice: allowing a society to move on after reckoning with its history.
The Art of Not Being Governed
A study of the anarchist history of the Southeast Asian highlands, explaining how mountain peoples evaded central empires.
