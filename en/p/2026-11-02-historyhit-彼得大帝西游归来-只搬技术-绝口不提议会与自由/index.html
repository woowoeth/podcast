Peter the Great Returns: The Ships He Took, the Parliaments He Left

He visited post-Glorious Revolution England and the Dutch Republic, but brought home only shipbuilding technique, shaving ordinances, and gun-fort blueprints—parliament and political freedom stayed in Western Europe.

2:00

The embassy demolished England's most famous hedge garden

Peter's mission lodged at John Evelyn's Deptford estate, site of a 400-foot holly hedge—9 feet high, 5 feet thick—that Samuel Pepys called ‘one of the finest things I have ever seen in my life’. Visitors discovered wheelbarrows and treated them as toys, pushing each other through the hedge until it was leveled. They used portraits as targets and chopped furniture for firewood. Historians call it a rock band in a resort—and the 6-foot-8, 25-year-old tsar led the charge.

10:21

A ten-year-old boy watched relatives impaled on palace pikes

In 1682, the death of Tsar Feodor III triggered succession chaos. The Streltsi, hereditary musketeers, rebelled against young Peter's claim. The ten-year-old hid behind his mother while relatives were thrown from the Kremlin walls onto Streltsi pikes below. A regency agreement—Peter and his half-brother Ivan V with half-sister Sophia as regent—ended the immediate crisis. The trauma left him permanently unwilling to tolerate any rival's threat to his life.

15:35

The embassy called itself alliance-building; it was really a fact-finding mission

Officially, Peter sought an anti-Ottoman coalition among Northern European powers. The guest stresses that what he actually wanted was to learn what the West could teach. This was not worship of the West but transaction: ships that sail, cannons that fire, fortresses that hold. Over roughly 18 months (1697–1698), he concentrated on Holland and England, with stops in German principalities and Vienna. He wanted knowledge, ideas, and talent moved back to Russia, not Western institutions copied.

— Daniel Beer
24:52

He ordered beards shaved, then forbade any talk of parliaments

Back home, Peter launched a cultural revolution: mandatory shaving for nobles (beards showed God's image in Orthodox theology—a raw religious nerve), women in public society, simplified alphabet for secular printing, Julian calendar and January 1 as New Year. But his hosts had been post-Glorious Revolution England and a Dutch republic. He came home indifferent to political freedom, constitutionalism. This was not model-copying. It was pure selection: a few frigates, a few beards—but absolutely no parliament.

— Daniel Beer
28:12

He lost catastrophically at Narva, then won because his opponent looked away

In 1700, opening battle of the Great Northern War: the Russian army was annihilated by King Charles XII of Sweden. All artillery and supplies captured. Total humiliation. Peter's fortune was that Charles XII turned to the Polish battlefield to the west. That window let Peter rebuild his army and start a navy. The war lasted 1700–1721 and became the shadow across his entire reign—most of his reforms were rushed through to serve it.

— Daniel Beer
30:18

Saint Petersburg was built by conscripting thousands into a swamp

In 1703, Peter built Saint Petersburg at the mouth of the Neva to project power into the Baltic. It began as a military fortress and became Russia's new capital (formally, 1710). It rose because Peter could summon thousands of forced laborers to dig canals and build palaces in a mosquito-infested swamp, and order his own nobles to move there. The guest emphasizes: not a city grown from voluntary settlement and commerce, but the direct projection of one man's autocratic will.

— Daniel Beer
41:42

The tsar who beat his own son to death with a whip

Peter and his eldest son Alexei had an irreconcilable break. Alexei fled to Habsburg protection. Peter saw betrayal and feared that nobles opposed to his reforms would rally to Alexei as a banner. He lured Alexei back to Saint Petersburg, charged him with treason, and beat him with a knout—a raw leather whip that kills in a few blows. Alexei died from torture in 1718. The guest sees the tangle: real succession politics and the 1682 Streltsi wound. Peter increasingly saw anyone pulling Russia in a different direction as a traitor.

— Daniel Beer
45:49

The nobles spoke French; the peasants' life barely changed except they got poorer

Peter left an autocratic state perfected: the poll tax bound peasants to the land because landowners and officials now profited from keeping them there. Serfdom hardened. Church and nobility—potential checks on tsarist power—became state appendages. The nobility, meanwhile, Westernized: by the 1750s, they spoke French. Peasant life stayed nearly unchanged except taxes climbed and military service stretched longer. Alexander Herzen later called this ‘two Russias’. In the 19th century, Peter's legacy was always contested—some called him the man who turned Russia westward, others the man who tore indigenous Russian culture apart. That argument has never ended.

— Daniel Beer
