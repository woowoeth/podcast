Running a company like training a model: too much SFT and the team loses its creativity

Tim tells Yang Zhilin every day: manage with RL, not SFT. SFT is telling people directly what to do — too much of it and they lose their agency; RL only gives a reward, but if the reward is badly defined the whole team will go hack it.

6:06

This snow mountain may have no summit

Yang Zhilin uses The Beginning of Infinity to explain AI R&D. The book says two things should be carved in stone: problems are inevitable, but problems are soluble. Before the Enlightenment society was static — thunder in the sky was the Thunder God, snow in winter meant some deity was in a bad mood, and bad explanations filled the gaps; after the Enlightenment society became dynamic, new knowledge is constantly created, and every problem solved produces new problems, because the boundary of knowledge keeps expanding. So this snow mountain may have no end, and ‘I hope it never ends’ — that way it is an infinite mountain. He also says that after climbing for a while it isn't necessarily you doing the climbing: you might use K2 for data processing, model analysis, even training, treating the model as an amplifier.

— Yang Zhilin
12:14

Reasoning is a brain in a vat; an agent has to reach out

A strong-thinking reasoning model is a ‘brain in a vat’: imagine a fish tank with a brain placed inside it, with no connection to the outside world, thinking away inside its own head, able to solve a problem without any interaction with the outside. The agentic RL paradigm is the opposite — think while acting, now using search, now a browser, now writing code, solving the problem over multiple turns, where the next action depends on the external feedback from the interaction. Both point to test time scaling, but they scale different dimensions: one scales the thinking tokens within each turn, the other scales the number of turns. Yang Zhilin thinks Cloud is precisely a bet on the agent route — its reasoning performance isn't very high, but its performance on agents is.

— Yang Zhilin
28:24

Muon makes one copy of data do the work of two

K2's base model is a bet on token efficiency. Yang Zhilin says high-quality data grows very slowly, multimodal data can't raise the intelligence of text itself, and high-quality data is close to a constant, so you have to make every piece of data more valuable — training faster doesn't itself raise the intelligence ceiling, because there are still only so many tokens. An optimizer like Adam has been in use for 10 years and treats matrix parameters as independent elements; Muon considers the dependency between parameters, and in early compute-optimal experiments it gave a 2x gain: learning one copy of data equals someone else learning two with Adam, so 30T high-quality tokens is equivalent to 60T. Muon was proposed by Keller, and his team did a lot of optimization on top of it, using it to train a language model at scale for the first time — the price was running into max logit explosion, a problem that can't be reproduced in small-scale experiments.

— Yang Zhilin
33:31

What agents lack most isn't capability, it's generalization

Yang Zhilin thinks the biggest challenge for agentic models right now is generalization. The limitation of RL is that both the training tasks and the evaluation metrics are single points: train on swe-bench and the swe-bench score goes up, but a higher metric doesn't mean better generalization. What he worries about is the model overfitting to certain tools, certain environments, certain specific tasks — those tasks can be good observations, but you don't want to overfit to them. This problem is more serious in agent training than in dialogue models. And there aren't many benchmarks agents can use right now; the scores seen on those benchmarks often aren't a reflection of capability and are one-sided. He thinks evaluation as a whole is still the bottleneck, and an important reason holding agent models back from becoming more general.

— Yang Zhilin
45:38

Use L4 technology to solve an L3 problem

ChatGPT's L1 to L5 are Chat, Reasoning, Agent, Innovation, Organization, but Yang Zhilin thinks they aren't necessarily sequential. The reason: today's agents aren't general enough, so you have to go the other way and use technology from the Innovation layer to solve an L3 problem — use AI to train AI, to align AI, letting the model take part in more of the training process rather than just optimizing a few single-point tasks. If you only manually define some tasks and then fit that task, performance on other unseen tasks may be poor; if you only grind the scores on those few tasks, users won't feel as good in more OOD scenarios. He also says a key part of Innovation is when a model can participate in the R&D of its own next generation — he hopes K2 can take part in developing K3.

— Yang Zhilin
53:44

Open source can help you serve, but you can't change the base model

How many open-source and closed-source players will be left in the end? Yang Zhilin judges the market will gradually concentrate and converge: a few hundred at the start, down to a few dozen, and the last few may be a fairly stable number. A year ago he said open source would lag closed source, on the grounds that open source is centralized and contributions aren't validated by compute, while closed source has talent concentration and market consolidation. His position has changed: once a model is out, the community really can contribute, there's a lot you can do on the inference side, and you can get more people to serve for you for free; but to make the base model itself better, only the original maker can do it. Still, doing agentic post-training on top of an open-source model may create new opportunities — for example, a startup that wants to build a legal agent can take K2 and train a specialized agent under its own tool set.

— Yang Zhilin
1:05:54

The data flywheel lost to compute scaling

Why haven't AI products formed a data flywheel? Yang Zhilin's first reason is that compute-based scaling is too powerful. RL scales much more efficiently than pre-training, because it is on-policy training with gradients, so directly scaling compute and scaling flops brings very large gains, which makes gains from other paths look small. The second is that a data flywheel depends on feedback from the external environment, and that feedback can't be too noisy — large models are relatively sensitive to noise when learning, unlike recommender systems. He also doesn't think user data is entirely useless: you need a certain number of users to know the distribution of overall demand, what works well and what doesn't, and then abstract that into evaluations to optimize the model; and now there's a new watershed, where professional users of agents are themselves producing commercial value.

— Yang Zhilin
1:25:07

Manage the team with RL, not SFT

This is what Tim tells Yang Zhilin every day: managing a team should use RL, not SFT. SFT is telling people directly ‘this is how you should do it’; RL gives a reward from above, and more often it only shows up in the objective. He is practicing this too, and it seems to have some effect; the core is grasping the balance between SFT and RL — RL as the main thing, with a portion of SFT to keep things from flying too far and to prevent forgetting. The biggest problem with managing a team by RL is that it's easy to hack: everyone's results look good, but you haven't actually reached what you ultimately wanted; the risk of managing by SFT is that everyone loses their creativity. So his latest understanding as CEO is grasping the balance between RL, SFT and reward hacking.

— Yang Zhilin

Glossary

Muon optimizer
An optimizer that accounts for dependencies between matrix parameters and has higher token efficiency than Adam.
test time scaling
Investing more tokens or more turns at inference time to improve performance.
brain in a vat
A model form that reasons only within itself and doesn't interact with the outside world.
reward hacking
The optimized object exploits a loophole in the reward definition, so the metric looks good but the goal isn't achieved.
curriculum learning
A training arrangement that starts at an appropriate difficulty and raises it step by step.
