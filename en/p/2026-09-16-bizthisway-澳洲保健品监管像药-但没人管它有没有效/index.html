Australia Regulates Supplements Like Drugs, but Nobody Checks Whether They Work

Australia puts supplements and medicines under the same agency, which looks strict; but efficacy is neither reviewed before market entry nor tested during production — the impression of "good" was planted by marketing and cross-border policy.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:00

Australians taking pills is a tradition

The soil for Australia's supplement industry isn't the natural environment, it's four social habits: a sparse population that leads people to self-medicate, pharmacists who also diagnose and dispense, naturopathic industry organisations dating back to the 1920s, and health food stores resembling food-as-medicine shops. Stacked together, these four made Australians used to "solving health problems without going to the hospital." Black Moth (澳佳宝) started as a naturopathic clinic, and only after the second generation took over did it translate that into products the mainstream system could recognise — for example, turning "take a spoonful of fish oil a day" into standardised fish oil capsules.

12:05

The contract manufacturers are the industry's foundation

Pen Pharmaceuticals, which appeared in 1974, was Australia's first company focused on contract manufacturing of supplements, and by around 2003 it was producing roughly 70% of Australia's vitamins, minerals and herbal products. Its value lay in process know-how: tablets, compound ingredients, the rubber-and-oil seal of soft capsules, the mixing uniformity of powders, the stability of liquids — each is its own set of solutions. In the 1980s and 1990s a new generation of contract manufacturers extended service in both directions, moving from pure production (CMO) to CDMO covering formulation, ingredients, trial production, labelling, packaging, testing, and registration for export. The brand only has to hand over a concept, and the factory can turn it into a product ready for market.

15:05

Registration means nobody looks before market entry

Australia legislated in 1989, putting the TGA in charge of both medicines and supplements, and all products must obtain an AUST number. Most supplements fall into the AUSTL category: they may only use low-risk ingredients permitted by the TGA and may only make low-level health claims, and companies can get a number just by applying themselves — the TGA does no pre-market review, only post-market spot checks. By around 2010 Australia had roughly ten thousand complementary medicines, of which nearly 98% belonged to this registration system. Only prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines and a small number of high-risk supplements enter the AUSTR tier, which requires the TGA to assess safety, quality and efficacy in advance.

20:06

It looks strict, but in practice it is loose

Put the contract manufacturing and regulatory lines together and you see that GMP only guarantees the factory produces consistently to the standard the brand gives it, and that problems can be traced to a batch for recall — it is not itself responsible for whether the product works. The TGA side only handles declarations and issuing batch numbers, and efficacy is explained by the brand itself. So the claim that "Australia regulates supplements like drugs" is not wrong in itself, but it is misleading: the method of control is the same, but the degree of control over the efficacy part differs enormously. The whole system does no effective upfront oversight of a product's actual effects, and there is a risk of disconnect between the brand and the product's efficacy.

25:08

The marketing was copied from Centrum, not invented

CEO Radek Sali, who took over in 2008, did not conjure a marketing plan out of thin air. He did industry research and found that the best-selling peer was Centrum, and feedback from retailers was: Centrum didn't give the channel much profit, but it was on TV all year round with heavy advertising and had great athlete endorsements, so consumers would ask for it by name when they walked into a pharmacy. Sali wanted to replicate this at Swisse. He first invested in Australia's national sport, cricket — the men's team captain — buying only very short ad slots during the hottest summer cricket broadcasts, so that even a limited budget could appear at important moments. But the female-dominated sales channel wasn't moved; one buyer didn't watch cricket herself and didn't know the athlete. He asked his mother what shows she liked, and she said she liked a dance variety show, and the strategy shifted to spoken placements in entertainment content.

26:09

Selling health as a lifestyle

Swisse's bigger round of promotion drew on Oprah and Ellen, and signed actress Nicole Kidman for three years, pushing its reach from Australia to the United States. By 2012 Swisse had 25 brand ambassadors, had shot more than 80 TV commercials in the previous 12 months, reached annual revenue of 183 million Australian dollars, and spent forty to fifty million Australian dollars a year on marketing — reportedly at one point the highest marketing spend of any consumer brand in Australia. Its ads didn't emphasise efficacy or ingredients; they mainly presented a state of being energetic, self-disciplined and good-looking — turning health into a lifestyle consumers aspire to. In 2012 Swisse took more than 20% market share in Australian supermarket channels and more than 10% in pharmacy channels.

32:14

The growth point was found in Chinese pharmacies

Swisse's expensive push into the US market wasn't a success, and on returning to its home market in Australia it found that Swisse sold very well in pharmacies in Chinese-concentrated areas, with some stores selling at ten times the level of ordinary stores — because students and Chinese immigrants bought it in bulk to take back to China or resell online, that is, daigou. Two policy shifts came in 2015: in March the Hangzhou cross-border e-commerce comprehensive pilot zone was approved, and in June China and Australia signed a free trade agreement, which formally took effect that December. The FTA mainly affected ordinary trade; Australian official statistics put the tariff on vitamins and supplements generally at 3% to 10%, while Black Moth itself estimated an average rate of 20%, and after the agreement it was said at the time that this could fall to zero within four years. Cross-border e-commerce goes through bonded warehouses, platforms buy in bulk, logistics time and cost drop sharply, and such goods are treated in policy as for personal use, so they don't have to go through the registration and filing process for supplement imports.

35:16

The brand was bought, the impression stayed put

In September 2015, Hong Kong-listed Biostime completed the acquisition of a controlling stake in Swisse, and two years later completed a full takeover, in a deal worth about 1.7 billion Australian dollars, or 8.7 billion yuan. For Swisse the deal carried little premium; for Biostime it added a large chunk of business revenue and imagination. Now all those Swisse ads on Hunan TV variety shows are, in theory, a Chinese supplement company spending money, yet the brand can still say it is an Australian supplement brand, and consumers can still apply their old vague good impression of Australia to it — a large proportion of the acquisition's core value is deposited in brand goodwill.

Glossary

TGA / Therapeutic Goods Administration
Australia's specialised agency regulating both medicines and supplements, playing the role of the US FDA.
AUSTL / registration listing
The category most supplements use, obtained simply by the company applying itself; the TGA does no pre-market review, only post-market spot checks.
AUSTR / assessed registration
For prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines and a small number of high-risk supplements, requiring the TGA to assess safety, quality and efficacy in advance.
GMP / Good Manufacturing Practice
A common international standard at the production level, ensuring consistent production to standard and traceable recalls, but not responsible for efficacy.
CMO / CDMO / contract manufacturing / contract manufacturing with R&D
A CMO takes on only the production step; a CDMO also takes in development work such as formulation, ingredients, trial production and registration filings.
