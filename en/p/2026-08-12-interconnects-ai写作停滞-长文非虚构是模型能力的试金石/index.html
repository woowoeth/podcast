AI Writing Has Stalled: Long-Form Nonfiction Is the Touchstone of Model Capability

Models are improving slowly at long-form nonfiction writing, exposing a defect in their ability to organize knowledge. This is more alarming than progress in code and math, because it bears on whether AI can solve open scientific problems.

0:00

If it can't explain mature science, it can't solve open problems

Criticism of AI writing mostly centers on creative writing, holding that good writing requires a distinctive voice, a point of view, and deep human expression. But nonfiction writing, such as filler text, was once one of the things LLMs were genuinely useful for. The author points out that the stagnation of models in long-form nonfiction writing is an alarming signal for anyone relying on AI to autonomously solve open scientific problems. Models struggle to organize and present the most mature science in their own domain, and that should be a prerequisite for solving open problems.

1:30

Insight comes from compression, and models are adding entropy to knowledge

The author argues that current AI progress in science looks more like picking low-hanging fruit and making cross-domain connections than revolutionary insight. Organizing knowledge is a form of compression, and a necessary condition for producing insight. But LLMs add entropy in long-form nonfiction writing, and that cannot be stacked indefinitely. Models will depend on humans for guidance. Even so, the author remains optimistic about the transfer of progress in narrow scientific domains like math to broader fields.

— Nathaniel
2:30

Coding went from mediocre to superhuman, writing hasn't moved

The author just finished a textbook on RLHF, using LLMs heavily along the way for typesetting, proofreading, and figures. He had expected a nonfiction book published in 2026 to look obsolete, but the reality is that model progress in writing is far below expectations. Some models known for writing ability, such as GPT-4.5 and Kimi K2, are already old. Models have gone from mediocre to superhuman in coding and math, but writing ability has stalled, and it feels orthogonal to other skills.

— Nathaniel
3:30

Writing isn't neglected, inference-time scaling just isn't unlocked

The author argues that writing isn't neglected; it's just a big challenge with a lack of targeted training data. There are some low-hanging fruits, such as dedicated toolchains and prompts, but they are unlikely to multiply capability. Writing is a very hard task, and unfortunately we haven't yet unlocked inference-time scaling for writing. Current models perform terribly at long-form technical writing: sentences are passable, but whole chapters are disorganized, vaguely worded, and often contain conceptual errors.

— Nathaniel
4:30

Models can check every sentence, but can't string a chapter together

The author observes that GPT models excel at finding typos and detail problems, while Claude models have better taste as editors, better understand the mental model of the task, and offer more interesting suggestions. These examples show that models are good at checking the content of each unit, but bad at connecting components, which leads to compounding errors. In math and code, RLVR (rule-based verification reinforcement learning) magically reduces these errors, but there is no counterpart in writing yet.

— Nathaniel
5:42

With an expert gatekeeper it isn't cheating, but it's a slippery slope

The author shares that while writing the book, AI models contributed less than 1% of the sentences explaining technical concepts, because the author, as an expert, endorsed those sentences. During editing, he used Claude Code to navigate editorial comments, distinguish simple typos from complex problems, and request suggestions. The author believes that if an expert can gatekeep strictly, using AI output isn't cheating, but it is a slippery slope, and he ultimately accepted some AI suggestions during a full review of the second draft.

— Nathaniel
7:30

The effort writing saves is future intuition traded away

The author stresses that using AI for nonfiction writing weakens the growth of understanding, and that understanding (intuition, taste, instinct) is more valuable in the future. If you aren't an expert, you can't spot AI's flaws. The author wrote the book to give back to the community, so even with AI, finishing is a win. He admits that with more human effort there would be more learning and a better product, but the deciding factor was the fear that the book would be obsolete by publication.

— Nathaniel
9:00

Early organizing needs insight, and AI is far off

The author argues that AI models are good tools for converting knowledge into different forms, suited to producing filler material or background content. But the early organizing stage of nonfiction writing requires insight, which LLMs are far from replacing. The author hopes more experts will use AI to write books and share more knowledge, but for now AI only saves 10-20% of the effort, and won't become the majority in the short term. Social pressure makes some people think writing books is meaningless, but high-quality educational content is always scarce.

— Nathaniel
10:30

Bad prose means the data-center genius story doesn't hold up

The author concludes that models perform well in two situations: verifiable domains, and small edits with lots of context, such as finding bugs or solving specific math problems. But they perform poorly at open-ended prose generation. Long-form writing will be cracked before creative writing, but this is a strong signal that models cannot express all their knowledge in underspecified problems. This limits their ability to become a "data-center genius" solving major scientific problems.

— Nathaniel

Glossary

RLVR
A reinforcement learning method that verifies rewards through rules, reducing compounding errors.
RLHF
A training method that uses human feedback to optimize model behavior.
inference-time scaling
A technique that increases computation at inference time to improve model performance.
