He Guangzhi: Being good is easier than being bad, because deception is too hard

Choosing to be good isn't instinct but mathematics, He Guangzhi argues: good deeds almost always repay, but being bad demands perfect deception—an impossibly high bar.

1:01

Champion confidence was built on quicksand and desperate self-hypnosis

He Guangzhi admits the ‘seemingly sure to win’ energy from round one was manufactured—he'd only stockpiled two pieces of material all year and was terrified. Last season, by contrast, he had two years of accumulated work and still had two full routines left unperformed when the competition ended. This season was pure theater. ‘I kept performing confidence until everyone believed it, and then I believed it myself.’ The self-assured competitor on stage and the creator's actual inventory of material were two completely different stories.

— He Guangzhi
19:18

A single favor became the bit that won the championship

His finals-round piece ‘Chang'an's Lychee’ came from a grudging favor—showing up for a former colleague who jumped to a new film production, because he feared refusing would make them look bad in front of their new employer. The movie's story moved him. He jotted notes on the spot; the material tested perfectly. That night he told himself ‘your kindness just saved you once again.’ Then he confessed the calculation buried inside: he spent months wrestling with whether his good deed was pure kindness or just waiting for a payoff. Luo Yonghao told him this confusion has a name: the motivation dilemma of altruistic behavior.

— He Guangzhi
26:20

The working-class comedy lane wasn't planned, it just became one

He Guangzhi stresses he didn't map out the ‘poor’ material in advance—it emerged when he started writing ‘real situations I was embarrassed to admit, like my Spotify playlist or my ride-share driver's.’ The outside world labeled it a lane; he just followed the material where it went. He calls these jokes ‘life jokes’ drawn from his first thirty years lived as a struggling person. Art, he argues, usually works backward: the artist creates unconsciously, then theorists build the framework around it. The advantage: even if he becomes a billionaire, recounting how poor he used to be won't ring false. The past never changes, and audiences don't resent rich people talking about where they came from.

— He Guangzhi
44:23

The stand-up critics who don't work the room are being left behind

He Guangzhi has noticed the rotating roster of ‘stand-up commentators’ reviewing the show have steadily lost touch with newer audiences—they don't face the live crowd and can't read why it suddenly roared. The game between performer and audience has shifted too: an actor can now pinpoint ‘routine six plus one callback and the crowd will erupt.’ Audiences clock the patterns just as fast. Both sides are playing with their cards face-up, but ‘the rules for laying cards keep changing.’ Even He can't articulate what the new rules are. It points to a dynamic where both sides know each other's playbook better than ever, yet creating genuine surprise keeps getting harder.

— He Guangzhi
1:07:42

Some comedians literally cannot survive without this stage

He Guangzhi has grown attentive to the ‘light-up’ segment at the end because he knows some young performers in this circle are genuinely broke before they make it—stand-up is their only income and lifeline. Unlike actors who juggle other work, this is all they have. Before the finals, he negotiated with the producer: if only one light would stay on, he'd rather not be filmed at all, so the camera could stay with ‘people who can't afford to lose.’ He's haunted by the thought of ‘losing by one vote,’ keeping someone awake at night. Some young people could build other lives without this platform, he admits, but some people: the world simply won't give them an equal shot without it.

— He Guangzhi
1:25:50

Doing good is simpler than doing evil because evil demands flawless execution

He Guangzhi says he chose to be good not from innate virtue but because he ‘decoded how the world runs.’ Across thirty years, he's watched good deeds almost always pay returns—the pattern never broke. Being bad, by contrast, requires: perfect logic, skill at lying and imposing a false front, and a truly rotten core. One crack in the facade and you collapse instantly. Good people only need to show one authentic spark and they can live off it for life. His counterintuitive conclusion: the world is actually harsher on bad people. The barrier to entry for evil is too steep; most would-be villains simply lack the competence to pull it off.

— He Guangzhi
1:56:23

Success comes from staying close to opportunity while writing clean material, not at distance

He Guangzhi remembered the choice between Beijing or Shanghai this way: Beijing had the creative energy but nowhere to perform; Shanghai had the production companies. His reasoning: to break through, you have to get close to the actual machinery that makes you visible. Then stay there and write the most honest material you can—not flee to some remote sanctuary to craft ‘dirty’ work away from temptation. He took everything he'd saved (about 10,000 yuan) and rode a green-car coach for fourteen hours to Shanghai, renting a share room so narrow he could touch both walls from his bed. When he hit his head on the ceiling, his first thought was ‘someday I'll be rich enough to buy this whole building.’

— He Guangzhi
3:00:11

Faking poverty on stage got exposed once he wasn't actually poor anymore

By season five of the stand-up competition, He Guangzhi's real life had improved, but his creative range hadn't expanded to match—so he kept grinding out material about being broke. It didn't work. The audience felt it. He felt it. What had been truth curdled into performance, and both he and the crowd sensed the inauthenticity ‘until I got called out on stage.’ His survival strategy across that five-year rough patch was ‘write for a year, rest a year,’ coasting on a handful of genuinely strong moments. He only confirmed he could graduate from the poverty lane once a commercial gig produced a workplace joke (using a deck of cards as a metaphor for office hierarchy) that resonated widely. That gave him the confidence to bring entirely new material to the finals.

— He Guangzhi
