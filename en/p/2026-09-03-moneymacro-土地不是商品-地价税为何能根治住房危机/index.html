Land Is Not a Commodity: Why a Land Value Tax Would Cure the Housing Crisis

The housing shortage is only a symptom; the real disease is land monopoly. A land value tax shifts the burden off buildings and onto land, forcing hoarders to build or sell, and returning location value to society instead of fattening landlords.

Land Value TaxHousing CrisisHenry GeorgeUrban EconomicsPolicy Experiments
The land value tax is an overlooked lever that economists across the left and right agree on. Lars Doucet turns an abstract tax design into a power struggle, using Houston parking lots, South Korea's appraisal system, and Norway's resource dividends as evidence.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:05

A land tax is not a property tax, it is a location tax

Lars builds his case on an analogy: the land value tax shares an origin with the ordinary property tax, but it splits ‘building value’ from ‘land value’. A building tax punishes you for constructing, maintaining, and renovating; a land tax only punishes you for occupying a good location and letting it sit idle. Land value, he says, comes from the labour of the people around you — your ability to earn a living, go to school, and make friends the moment you walk out the door depends entirely on your neighbours and the whole city. The landlord lies back and sucks that collective value into his own pocket, and that is the paradox of Progress and Poverty: the ballpoint pen fell from $270 to 11 cents, and land never does.

— Lars
0:13

The builders are right, but not right enough

The host asks: Oakland relaxed density and rents fell, so do we still need a land tax? Lars concedes that ‘housing shortage’ is the main cause, but points out that YIMBYs never explain one counterexample: Houston has no zoning, yet its downtown still holds a giant surface parking lot worth $500 million. Why? Because the property tax bundles land and building together, so the more you build the more tax you pay, while holding and waiting for appreciation is cheaper. Developers therefore have no incentive to develop, only an incentive to lobby for rezoning and then keep holding. Without land tax reform, he says, you end up in a stalemate: ‘you changed the zoning, but I just won't sell.’

— Lars
0:23

The objections are powered by imagination

Lars sorts the opposition into three camps and takes them apart one by one. How do you appraise land separately from buildings? He says 14,000 assessment jurisdictions worldwide already do something like it: South Korea manually reviews 500,000 sample parcels a year and produces nationwide land values in 5 months; Norway trained a national automated valuation model on the state property data platform Finn.no with a core team of just 3 people. Think there will be too many protests? He says the real source of protest is ‘my land looks the same as my neighbour's, so why am I charged more’ — under a land tax, you and your neighbour are assessed on the same location basis, which actually makes it easier. The excuse for not doing it is that nobody has measured it.

— Lars
0:35

The little old lady is not an argument against it

The sharpest criticism is ‘the transition is unfair’ — on the day a land tax is announced, existing landowners instantly bear the capitalized value of all future tax liability, which amounts to expropriation in one stroke. Lars concedes this is a Gordon Tullock-style transition trap, then offers the guardrail: the move is never a one-step nationwide 100% land tax, but a gradual switchover by school district or county; run revenue-neutral simulations and most single-family homes actually see their tax bill fall. As for the elderly afraid of being driven out of the old family home, he says Texas already has a tax deferral system: you owe it, and it is deducted with interest from the estate after death.

— Lars
0:41

Britain's failure was a capacity problem

The most commonly cited counterexample online is Britain's 1910s ‘People's Budget’: the valuation took 6 years and was overturned by shopkeepers' protests and the courts. Lars's response is blunt: the much-shared article is simply ‘deriving a universal law from one sample’, and it simultaneously claims that ‘a pure land tax has never succeeded’ and that ‘land cannot be valued’ — even though South Korea has been appraising nationwide land values parcel by parcel for decades. That only proves the British state machine was weak, he says, not that a land tax is theoretically unworkable. His tone hardens: if you want to be a serious critic, go find counterexamples, instead of passing off lazy ignorance as a verdict.

— Lars
0:45

Norway achieved Georgism through resource dividends

Lars points to a fact that makes Norway a ‘success story’ today: the entire Norwegian resource management regime is Georgist in structure — natural resources such as oil are treated as the property of all the people, and extractors pay extremely high resource taxes, while production and investment themselves are not punished, so entrepreneurship is encouraged and the natural bounty flows into the treasury to fund universal welfare. Norway's Georgism is almost perfect, except that it is not on land value. This ‘Norwegian paradox’ leads him to assert: the land tax is not the enemy of the welfare state, it can be its foundation.

— Lars
0:51

The automobile opened a second frontier

Lars explains why the land question that gripped every city 100 years ago later vanished: the frontier is itself a mechanism of ‘free land’ — as long as you can still expand into the wilderness, the poor do not have to face the squeeze of ‘high wages plus high rent’. America's first frontier was westward expansion, and when it closed Henry George appeared; in the 20th century the automobile turned the suburbs into a ‘second frontier’ that lasted another 80 years, until it closed again around 2006-2008. He says people now call this a ‘second Gilded Age’, but it is just a script from over a hundred years ago with a new cast, and post-pandemic remote work, as a ‘third frontier’, held out for roughly 5 minutes before capital digested it.

— Lars
0:53

Don't win online, do the politicians' homework

The closing answer is very practical: Lars says the most effective way to create change he has learned in the past 5 years is not arguing with keyboard warriors, but taking every objection a state senator or city council member might raise, running the data simulations and preparing the answers in advance, and putting them directly on their desks — ‘doing politicians' homework’, he says, is an absurd but extraordinarily effective strategy. Several states have already passed bills allowing land tax reform. His call to action: go measure, don't guess.

— Lars

Glossary

Land Value Tax (LVT)
An annual tax levied on the value of land rather than total property value, which does not punish building improvements.
Georgism
The 19th-century movement launched by Henry George: land is common wealth, and land rent should be captured by society.
YIMBY
The movement that advocates loosening land-use zoning rules and allowing more housing to be built.
Mass Appraisal
Using a unified model and sample parcels to value properties across an entire jurisdiction automatically, rather than assessing each one on site.
Single Tax
The Georgist ideal: tax only land value and abolish all other taxes on labour and capital.
Natural Dividend
The high rents extracted from natural resources such as oil, paid out to all citizens.
