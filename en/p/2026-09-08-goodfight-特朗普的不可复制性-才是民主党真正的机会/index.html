Trump's Irreplicability Is the Democrats' Real Opening

Trump's staying power comes from decades as a television celebrity, and no one else can copy it; if Democrats treat the lessons of the Trump era as universal laws, they will repeat 2024 in 2028.

0:00

Trump isn't a celebrity, he's a reality show in the flesh

Longwell's core argument: Trump's unique advantage isn't being a ‘celebrity’ — it's that for decades he has appeared before the public in the same identity: in Page Six, writing The Art of the Deal, hosting a TV show, keeping up relationships with radio hosts. She says voters in focus groups don't see him as a failed businessman but as ‘a rich developer, did TV, owned a football team.’ That gives him a natural advantage in the American public psyche that no one else can replicate. The Republican Party then ceded itself entirely to him, no longer having its own ideas or candidates, becoming a personality cult. Longwell thinks this is precisely the opening: he is exiting, and if Democrats or the broader pro-democracy coalition can unite, they can dominate the Republican Party over the next three or four years.

— Sarah Longwell
2:30

Harris's problem wasn't policy, it was that nobody knew who she was

Longwell says Harris absolutely should not run for president again, but not because she dislikes Harris — she thinks Harris outperformed expectations and was ‘done dirty’ by Biden's team, which never elevated her and never let her distance herself from Biden. Mounk counters that Harris was then the Democratic leader and presidential candidate and could absolutely have picked one thing to disagree on. Longwell concedes this was part of the failure: Harris didn't have a strong enough sense of who she was, so when Biden's team said ‘no distance,’ she complied. Re-listening to post-election focus groups, the clearest voice was ‘I don't know who she is, I don't know what she stands for, I don't trust her.’ This isn't fixable with more practice or minor policy tweaks. Trump knows what his positions are — he loves tariffs, he has clear feelings about immigration.

— Sarah Longwell
8:00

Democrats have a ‘looks good on paper’ problem

Longwell says Democrats are always checking boxes: this person is a minority, this person has a beautiful résumé, but they never ask ‘can this person actually appeal to ordinary people? Do they talk in a way ordinary people understand?’ She says too many Democrats are more at home in the teachers' lounge than on the shop floor, whereas Trump is at home on the shop floor because he's always been around construction workers. Mounk adds that Democrats call themselves the party of the working class yet are too far from the working class — they don't even know what working-class voters consider working class. He gives the example of Senate candidates this cycle being packaged as super working class when they aren't. Longwell says the fix isn't swapping candidates but rethinking from the top down who the party is and how it communicates. She'd give politicians a ‘crazy’ piece of advice: put your hand on the other person's shoulder and ask ‘What do you believe? Just tell me.’

— Sarah Longwell
14:30

Republicans are mercenaries, Democrats are an issue-advocacy network

Longwell says she has been an operative on both sides, which is a rare vantage point. Republicans are pure mercenaries, doing business in politics, with business interests tied to candidates' political interests, so when Trump came in and redefined the Republican Party they didn't much mind. Democrats, by contrast, have a vast network of nonprofits pushing issue advocacy — not lining up to support candidates but having their own agendas and trying to pull candidates over to agree with them. After coming to the Democratic side, Longwell had two thoughts: first, genuine sympathy for how hard the Democrats' coalition is to manage, whereas the Republican coalition is homogeneous and easy to understand; second, that the young people on the Democratic side are all trying to unionize the nonprofits, or demanding Fridays off. Her response: ‘The authoritarians are at the door — you can't work from home, you have to put on pants and come to the office.’

— Sarah Longwell
22:00

Democrats need to build, not tear down

Longwell's book has a section called ‘Build Baby, Build,’ and the slogan is plastered all over her office. She says this may be DNA from her Republican background: when Republicans were shut out by academia and the mainstream media, they complained, but they also went and built a competing ecosystem. Democrats now have to do the same. The institutional advantages Democrats once had, such as the mainstream media, are gone, because the right-wing media ecosystem is now extremely strong and Republicans — not just voters but politicians — can live inside it. Meanwhile the mainstream media is still doing that objective reporting that treats both parties as the same, without a corresponding center-left to left media ecosystem. Worse, the people actually building new media are often the far left, because they are clearer about their own message, whereas ‘I want to tell you why pluralism and liberal democracy matter’ won't move ordinary Americans.

— Sarah Longwell
28:00

Higher turnout means Trump wins more

Longwell explicitly rejects the ‘we just need to turn out our own people’ line. She says anyone who actually looked at the data after 2024 will tell you: if turnout were higher, Trump would have won more. Democrats have a fantasy that there are more voters on their side and that if they just grab them they'll turn out — that's wrong. What really decides the outcome is low-information voters — people who don't follow the news much, who are just raising kids and getting by, who tune in before the election and then choose between two not-very-appealing people. Their logic: I don't much like Trump, but the economy was decent before the pandemic last time, and I've barely seen Harris, I don't know what she stands for, so I'll go with him — at least I know him better. Longwell says the structural advantage in the midterms is on the Democrats' side, because the midterm electorate is completely different from the presidential electorate.

— Sarah Longwell
34:00

Trump's mobilization is an exception, not the GOP's new normal

Longwell says many lessons of the Trump era won't necessarily carry over — many are specific to Trump. She tells a story: after the 2024 election she was at a New York Times forum, and Kevin McCarthy was there too, and McCarthy spent the whole time saying what an incredible mobilization machine Trump is, that Republicans themselves couldn't believe it, that he gets ‘Saddam Hussein-style numbers’ in rural communities. But the question is: can Marco Rubio do that? Can JD Vance do that? Can Tucker Carlson do that? Longwell thinks that's a big question mark. Mounk adds that from an international perspective, almost every democracy has a long-term trend of white working-class voters moving right and educated voters moving left, and he uses The Simpsons as an analogy: when the show was created, Homer was obviously a Democrat and Ned Flanders was obviously a Republican, but today Ned Flanders might be a Democrat, Homer would vote for Trump without hesitation, and there are more people in America like Homer than like Ned.

— Sarah Longwell
40:00

Swing voters want three things: wallet, safety, stop talking identity

Longwell says the average American voter is a 55-year-old white working-class man without a college degree, and you should think about how to appeal to that person. Swing voters want three things: first, a better material life — gas, groceries, childcare, upward mobility, health care; spend 70% of your time talking about this, because it's what they care about most. She has listened for eight years, through hundreds of focus groups, and every time she asks how the country is doing, the answer is the economy, the cost of living, rent, housing, health care. Second, a sense of safety — people can't feel compassion when they don't feel safe. Trump says immigrants bring drugs, crime, rapists; he knows what he's doing. Republicans love to talk about the border and crime, and Democrats giving up those issues is a mistake; border security is a threshold issue for voters. Third, identity politics — Black, Hispanic, and gay voters all say ‘don't put me in your box, don't tell me how to vote because of how I look,’ and this troubles swing voters.

— Sarah Longwell
