Mercedes-Benz China team uses data to win decision-making power from German HQ

The era of Tier 1 black-box delivery is over; OEMs want to operate the data themselves. The China team didn't seize decision-making power — they earned it through commitment, delivery, and trust.

A R&D VP who spent 18 years at Delphi and joined Mercedes-Benz three years ago explains the mechanism by which OEMs are reclaiming design authority from Tier 1 suppliers, and how the China team is feeding innovation back to the global organization.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

13:14

Tier 1 goes from black box to white box

Wang Xin explains the root cause of OEMs reclaiming design authority very clearly: cars have gone from mechanical products to data-driven products. Cars collect data on the road, and that data helps intelligent driving assistance iterate continuously. Previously, Tier 1 delivered a black box to the OEM, and the OEM had no idea what was inside. Now the OEM says, I want to operate all the data inside myself, so my customers can get new features faster. As a result, Tier 1 increasingly delivers white boxes and gradually exits the design domain, becoming a production domain — manufacturing and cost control can still be done well, but design authority returns to the OEM, so iteration speed and project progress can be controlled.

— Wang Xin
15:15

Tier 1 was originally spun off from OEMs

Many people assume that OEM dependence on Tier 1 is a natural industry structure. Wang Xin says no. Before the 1990s, today's Tier 1 suppliers all came out of OEMs — Delphi and Visteon were originally divisions of Ford and GM. OEMs found that holding all divisions in-house made design costs too high; you need more production line cost iteration to amortize, and it wasn't worth it. So around 2000, Ford and GM successively spun off these divisions to form large component companies. In other words, OEMs doing everything themselves had happened before; the business logic is that they split off when costs become uneconomical. Now they're taking it back, not for cost reasons, but for user-centric response speed — the cooperative relationship between Tier 1 and OEMs is too slow.

— Wang Xin
27:25

Decision-making power isn't seized, it's earned through delivery

When asked whether the China team's decision-making power with headquarters was fought for, Wang Xin gave a counterintuitive answer: No, it's that we committed, we delivered, and everyone built trust — in English, we commit, we deliver, we trust. He emphasized that many things are built on trust; without trust, you can't move forward. When working with headquarters, they always commit to what they can do, never over-commit, but pursue it with ambition, and ultimately deliver on time and with quality, and the trust relationship is established. This explains why the China team can take the lead — not by winning arguments, but by delivering again and again.

— Wang Xin
31:27

Mercedes-Benz is the only automaker using QNX for its intelligent driving OS

Wang Xin says Mercedes-Benz should be the only automaker in China using QNX rather than Linux for its intelligent driving assistance operating system. QNX is closed and has a very high safety level — the instrument cluster behind it must be QNX, because the safety level must at least reach ASIL-B. Intelligent driving must also basically reach ASIL-B, equivalent to what the medical industry calls a medical device level. Many competitors say they are Safety Linux, but it's still Linux. Linux is open, shared by everyone, and everyone can contribute; there will be different understandings of functional safety, and it won't be as demanding as QNX. For Mercedes-Benz, safety comes first, so even though it's very difficult, they chose QNX.

— Wang Xin
34:29

Battles in Germany are mild, in the US they're fierce

Wang Xin has worked at American companies, and the contrast is clear: American companies are more direct, they argue things out in meetings based on facts, hoping to solve many problems at once, so the process is very intense and sometimes the atmosphere is hard to control. Battles in German companies are still relatively mild, everyone compromises, but throughout the collaboration they still want to speak with data and facts — everyone is an engineer, and putting data and facts in front of them makes communication more efficient. He also mentioned disagreements over UI design: interfaces that Chinese colleagues are used to, when brought to Germany, German colleagues say they can't find the function they want, there are too many buttons; what Germany provides, Chinese colleagues say requires many steps to find a function, basically no one would use it.

— Wang Xin
1:01:59

Switching from rule-based to end-to-end took seven or eight months of agony

Wang Xin says this transition was quite agonizing because it's a mindset change. At first everyone did rule-based, and it actually worked okay, but gradually they found that end-to-end brings more benefits, giving unlimited potential and perception improvements that rule-based can't provide. But you have to overturn previous accumulation, and redeploying to the car shows obvious regression. This process is agonizing; you have to watch it slowly improve through daily training accumulation, and then one day it's suddenly much better than rule-based. This transition was led by the China team, mutually motivating with partners. The tug-of-war lasted about seven or eight months, and ultimately it was settled by massive testing and data, by performance, and they moved forward unswervingly.

— Wang Xin
1:03:00

CLA has neither map nor lidar, except for Tesla

Wang Xin says the CLA is basically the only one with neither map nor lidar, besides Tesla — not a single lidar, all vision. There are several reasons: first, if training is good enough, adding lidar doesn't make much sense and increases cost; second, adding more sensors doesn't necessarily increase credibility, sometimes it adds more noise, and you have to constantly judge whether to trust the data supplemented by lidar or by perception. This decision had battles, but always with data and facts — for example, presenting that 20,000 km, 30,000 km with and without lidar are the same or even better. But moving toward L3, they haven't given up lidar, because it's a good redundancy.

— Wang Xin
1:11:06

A car must run 5 million kilometers before it rolls off the line

Wang Xin gave the specific scale of Mercedes-Benz safety validation: a new car must have at least nearly 5 million kilometers of testing before it rolls off the line. A single function must have accumulated over 150,000 kilometers of durability testing, and it must be tested in extreme cold, extreme heat, and extreme humidity in various places, in both China and Europe, passing different laws and regulations. In addition, they do a lot of simulation testing and real vehicle testing. Basically, a car is allowed about 150 crash tests; real vehicle crashes must be done; virtual tests must also be done about 15,000 times. His logic: each number may just be a number to us, even 0.1%, 0.2%, but for many people's families, that is a family. You can't just see it as a number.

— Wang Xin

Glossary

Tier 1
Component system suppliers that supply directly to OEMs, such as Delphi and Visteon.
QNX
A closed real-time operating system with high safety levels, commonly used in instrument clusters and intelligent driving.
Rule-based
An intelligent driving approach that uses manually coded rules for planning and decision-making; long-tail scenarios are hard to exhaustively cover.
End-to-end
A large-model approach that goes directly from perception to planning and control, replacing rule-based algorithms.
SOA
Service-Oriented Architecture: breaking vehicle functions into callable services to facilitate software iteration.
V2X
Vehicle-to-everything communication between vehicles and vehicles, vehicles and infrastructure; still in an early stage.
