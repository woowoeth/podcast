Hexi Corridor is not a trade route, but the geographic switch for China's unification

Han Dynasty occupied the Hexi Corridor to isolate Qiang and Xiongnu, preventing grassland and plateau nomads from pincer attacks on Chang'an; after Yuan Dynasty penetrated Tibet through the Hexi Corridor, Tibetan Buddhism became the spiritual legitimacy for Mongol and Qing rule over grassland and plateau.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:01

When farmers and herders meet daily, mutual understanding comes naturally

Eastern farming and herding are separated by latitude—the two populations live far apart and rarely meet, making mutual understanding difficult. The Hexi Corridor reverses this with elevation-based boundaries: herders on mid-slopes, farmers in valleys, the two groups meeting daily and understanding each other. Shi Zhang notes that most of the Mongols' favored ‘色目人’ (semu people) came from these mid-elevation oasis areas, and precisely because they understood both farming and herding, Mongol rulers of the Central Plains needed them as administrators.

11:17

Han occupation of Hexi was to avoid encirclement from both sides

In Western Han times, Chang'an faced Xiongnu grassland nomads to the north and Qiang highland nomads to the southwest. If they coordinated, they could form a crescent siege around Chang'an. Han strategy was called 'isolate Qiang and Xiongnu'—and the only way to truly cut them apart was to control the Hexi Corridor, severing all contact between grassland and plateau.

— Shi Zhan
14:20

Han walls cling to river valleys as a three-dimensional defense

The Hexi four prefectures form a narrow passage with no defensive depth; Xiongnu charges could be devastating, so Han built walls along snowmelt rivers like the Black River, connected by watchtowers. The walls came in three lines: the central line hugged the river valley for water access, while northern and southern lines ran on higher mountain slopes. Using the logic 'whatever my eyes can see is my territory,' they provided mutual cover, creating a layered defense system.

19:23

Dragon temples in Hexi ask one thing: please don't send rain

In the east, drought calls for rain prayers to the Dragon King, but Hexi is inverted—farmland depends entirely on snowmelt from mountains; with summer sun steady, water flows reliably and rain is unwelcome. Real danger comes from downpours triggering flash floods that destroy carefully built irrigation channels, forcing costly repairs. Though both regions housed Han Chinese, climate difference flipped their prayer's substance entirely.

— Shi Zhan
23:24

China's mother of grottoes is neither Yungang nor Longmen

Northern Liang's imperial family commissioned major imperial grottoes at Tianti Mountain. When Northern Wei conquered Northern Liang in 439 CE, they conscripted the craftsmen bodily to Datong, who built Yungang. Decades later, when Northern Wei moved its capital to Luoyang, their apprentices went on to build Longmen. Some craftsmen escaped the draft and fled to Dunhuang, where the grottoes eventually became scaled and magnificent. Tianti Mountain earned the title 'mother of Chinese grottoes.'

— Shi Zhan
29:29

Kumarajiva spent 17 years stranded in Wuwei and half-built Buddhist Chinese

Former Qin commander Lü Guang was sent to bring High Monk Kumarajiva back from Kucha, but Former Qin collapsed midway. Lü Guang established a breakaway Later Liang state, trapping Kumarajiva in Wuwei for 17 years, neither able to advance nor retreat. In exile, he lived as equal alongside Central Plains literati who had also fled to the Hexi Corridor, developing methods to fuse Buddhism and Confucianism. After reaching Chang'an, his large-scale sutra translations created everyday phrases: ‘大千世界’(cosmos of a thousand worlds), ‘粉身碎骨’(body shattered and bones ground), ‘过去现在未来’(past, present, future), ‘心田’(field of the heart), ‘爱河’(river of love)—all Kumarajiva's neologisms.

— Shi Zhan
44:37

Mongols detoured through Tibet only because they couldn't cross the Yangtze

Mongols avoided naval warfare; frontal crossing of the Yangtze against Song was too difficult, so they wheeled in a strategic arc, going high through the plateau to strike Dali directly. After seizing Tibet, they realized 'the goal isn't conquest but governance'—three years of study convinced them that controlling the head of Tibetan Buddhism could command the entire plateau. In 1247, Sakya Pandita (萨迦班智达) and Mongol prince Kouduan (阔端) met at Liangzhou for a covenant, exchanging political submission for religious sanction, bringing the snowy plateau under direct imperial rule for the first time.

— Shi Zhan
53:44

After losing the Central Plains, Mongols rekindled Tibetan Buddhism through sworn kinship

When Ming drove Mongols back to the grasslands, the plateau-grassland connection severed and Mongols reverted partly to shamanism. In 1578, Altan Khan (俺答汗)—a skilled warrior without royal bloodline who couldn't become khagan—met rising religious leader Sönam Gyatso (索南嘉措) at Kumbum Monastery in Qinghai. They mutually recognized each other as reincarnations of Kublai Khan and Phakpa Lama, using religion to re-establish dynastic legitimacy. Later, when Manchus prepared to enter China, they built the Manchu-Mongol alliance using precisely this formula: 'shared faith in Tibetan Buddhism' as the spiritual bond.
