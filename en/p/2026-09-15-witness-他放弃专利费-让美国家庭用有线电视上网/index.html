He Gave Up the Patent Royalties and Got American Homes Online Through Cable TV

In 1991, an Iranian-born engineer used the coaxial cable already in place to move data both ways in and out of the home, then donated the technology royalty-free to the telecom industry to become a global standard — he could have made billions from it.

The idea everyone called him crazy for

In 1991, Ruzbe Yassini and his team used the coaxial cable already laid across America for cable TV to move data both ways in and out of homes, connecting millions of American households to the internet for the first time. He recalls the reaction from bankers, the cable TV industry and every investor: you're crazy, nobody will solve these problems; and even if you do, nobody will care — who wants to connect data at home? The absurdity of that judgment is that they were betting homes didn't need data, while Yassini was betting the infrastructure was already there.

— Ruzbe Yassini
3:08

Two jobs pieced together the technical path

Yassini's technical instinct didn't come from nowhere. From 1981 to 1986 at General Electric he learned how television systems and cable TV systems worked, how audio and video entertainment worked; around 1987 at a high-speed data company called Proteant he learned how high-speed data worked. His judgment was: put high-speed data onto cable TV infrastructure. In the 1980s many people assumed a whole new set of wires had to be laid, and the dream was fiber to the home — fiber itself was fine, the problem was cost. At the time about 56 million American households were already connected to cable TV; digging trenches again, pulling cable, hiring people, the cost was extraordinary.

— Ruzbe Yassini
4:10

The hardest part was making data travel back

Coaxial cable had until then only been used to send cable TV programming one way into the home; the hard part was sending data back the other way. Yassini told the founder of the company he was at, and the reaction was: if anyone wanted to do this, they'd have done it already; and besides, who cares about homes being connected? Who uses a computer and network at home? Rejected, he didn't stop — he first got the system running at Rock Island Arsenal, an army base. But the real question was: could this thing run not just in the closed network of a government building, but in homes across thousands of villages, towns and cities?

— Ruzbe Yassini
5:11

The first field test failed completely

In 1990 he founded his own company and found real homes to test on. A local commercial and residential cable TV company in the small Massachusetts town of Stowe was willing to give them a channel to try. The first trial found that residential cable TV lines were so noisy they couldn't even find their own signal. He says they didn't call it signal-to-noise ratio, they called it noise-to-signal ratio, the situation was very horrible, and it failed badly on residential cable. This was the most dangerous moment of the whole technical path: running in the lab and on the base didn't mean it could run in real homes.

— Ruzbe Yassini
6:11

The pigeon flew

After solving the problems one by one, they ran a second test: sending a file from a residential address to the company office, and every time it was the same image of a pigeon in flight. Once the file transfer started, everything ran perfectly over the old residential cable, and the team called him excitedly, shouting that the pigeon was now flying over that network infrastructure. That image is the most concrete success signal in the whole episode — not a metric being hit, but a pigeon flying from a home to the office.

— Ruzbe Yassini
7:12

He chose royalty-free over billions

Once the technology worked, he didn't go make billions — he licensed the technology to the telecom industry royalty-free. He says people often ask him: don't entrepreneurs usually make money first and then donate a little to become famous? Why did he do it the other way around? His explanation: he comes from a region where most of the problems since the invention of oil have come from that middle stretch of land, and he always thought that if there were a telecommunications system that could do healthcare, education and other good things, it could reduce dependence on oil, both protecting the planet from pollution and giving the people of that region freedom, letting them enjoy everything a democratic society brings. So he believes donating the technology royalty-free and making it a global standard was the cornerstone of his vision at the time.

— Ruzbe Yassini
8:17

From standard to home modem

After the royalty-free licensing made the technology a standard, Yassini went on to build a mass-market cable modem for home use. By 1995 his modem held a considerable share of the American market; just one year later his company was acquired for tens of millions of dollars. The whole thing started with 13 people plus 7 advisors — 20 people pushing a vision into execution, and execution into a tool usable in the home.

— Ruzbe Yassini

Glossary

coax cable
The copper cable used for cable TV, originally carrying only one-way television signals.
signal-to-noise ratio
The ratio of signal strength to noise strength; when residential lines are noisy the signal gets drowned out.
royalty-free
Users don't have to pay the patent holder for each product.
