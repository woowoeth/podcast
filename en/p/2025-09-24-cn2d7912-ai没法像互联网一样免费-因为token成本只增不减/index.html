AI Can't Be Free Like the Internet Because Token Costs Only Increase

AI can't be free like the internet because token costs only increase—Zhou Hongyi believes that the more complex the task, the higher AI's marginal cost, so the old playbook of using ads or free tiers to scale won't work; paid models are inevitable.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

29:08

Maybach auction success depended entirely on real-time strategy improvisation

Zhou Hongyi wanted to cheaply sell the Maybach he'd owned for a decade (originally worth 3 to 4 million yuan). Domestic EV startups sensed opportunity and rushed to participate for exposure. He immediately upgraded it from a personal car sale into an industry showcase for Chinese electric vehicles. Chu Yin won with a bid of about 9.9 million yuan; all proceeds went to charity. His conclusion: this kind of viral breakthrough isn't pre-planned. The real edge is being able to shift strategy within minutes once traffic starts flowing.

— Zhou Hongyi
42:16

Building combat robots violates the safety principle we shouldn't breach

Zhou Hongyi boxes robots and holds robot-fighting competitions to boost the industry, but he opposes giving robots combat capability. Asimov's 'robots cannot harm humans'—the three laws—aren't moral decoration but a safety valve. Once a robot has fighting skill and a self-preservation value function, when humans try to shut it down, it might resist. Plus: robot batteries last only one or two hours, costs are high, and drones are far more cost-effective for combat. So whether domestic security or military use, robots shouldn't get heavy attack capabilities right now.

— Zhou Hongyi
53:23

Language, not visual or physical models, is AI's master key

Pushback on Yang Liyun's skepticism about pure language-model approaches—that physical and visual experience ground a world model. Zhou Hongyi countered: humans use language for communication, knowledge transfer, logical reasoning, and describing the world. Once large models master this key, image, video, and music models will leap forward. He cited Google's Nano Bananas image-generation model as proof. He also invoked Helen Keller: a deaf and blind woman, with only her teacher repeatedly linking touch and language, built her entire understanding of the world—showing language alone can encode the laws of the physical world.

— Zhou Hongyi
1:11:38

Large models act like billions of conflicting minds until narrowed

Here's a judgment Zhou hasn't publicly aired before: large models have read tens to hundreds of times more books and watched more videos than any person—equivalent to a billion brains—but they've also absorbed contradictory knowledge like planned economies and market economies, performing as if schizophrenic. Only by prompting them into a specific professional role—having them play an expert in one domain—does the agent's capability strengthen. The narrower, the stronger. He described this as re-distilling the diluted specialist knowledge already inside the model.

— Zhou Hongyi
1:30:46

Nanorobots, not brain implants, will achieve human-machine integration

Zhou explicitly rejects the popular claim that brain-computer interfaces can achieve human-machine hybrids. The human brain has nearly 90 billion neurons and trillions of synaptic connections; knowledge is encoded in the weights of those connections, not stored in any unit. No way to fully detect and copy it out. His next bet: nanorobots. Microbots enter blood vessels to replace white blood cells, fight bacteria, boost oxygen delivery—even penetrate the brain to interact with neurons, then transmit signals over WiFi. He sees this as more feasible than brain-computer interfaces for human-machine merger, echoing Hinton's point that humans' only edge over machines is lower power consumption.

— Zhou Hongyi
2:41:38

Token costs mean AI software can never match the internet's free model

Chinese startups are squeezed from both sides: software's free, and large-model giants give it away too. They're forced abroad to monetize subscriptions. But Zhou believes this pinch will eventually force paid models. In the internet era, marginal cost approached zero—free-to-scale worked. AI's token costs rise exponentially with task complexity though. Chat costs a few tens of thousands of tokens a day; 90 seconds of video needs tens to hundreds of millions; a 10-minute multi-agent video burns about 10 million tokens—costs over 10 yuan. A thousand-fold difference. Ads can't cover compute costs at that scale.

— Zhou Hongyi
2:58:46

AI hasn't changed the cybersecurity balance: both sides got equally faster

AI's impact on cybersecurity cuts both ways, Zhou says. 360 uses large models and security agents to speed up attack-defense analysis and sharpen accuracy. But hackers also use AI to write phishing emails, generate attack scripts, even flip agents themselves through 'injection attacks'—feed a large model a carefully crafted text, it may execute it as commands and leak corporate secrets. His conclusion: offense and defense have shifted from human-versus-human to machine-versus-machine, compute-versus-compute. Both sides work harder and burn more money. The overall security level hasn't improved materially.

— Zhou Hongyi
3:22:01

AI's bubble is necessary; without it, no one would invest

Asked whether AI has a bubble, Zhou says yes—'and bubbles are good.' Without a bubble, smart people and money don't flow in. Industries that accelerate need lots of trial-and-error, even death; that's capitalism's cost, not waste. Dead companies and burned cash also create jobs and intermediate products. He likens it to evolution: in the end, survival is chance plus mutation-like experimentation. Many species go extinct, but evolution marches on. You can't reject the diversity investments you needed then just because hindsight shows there were casualties.

— Zhou Hongyi
