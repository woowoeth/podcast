Jobs 1997: Computers Should Remove Hard Drives, Networks Are Faster

He stored his entire home directory on a NeXT server for seven years without losing data or backing up the computer. Once gigabit ethernet arrives, he argued, PCs should strip all moving parts except keyboard and mouse.

AppleJobsOperating SystemsOrganizational RestructuringNetwork ComputingDeveloper Ecosystem
Jobs' first appearance answering developer questions as a consultant after Apple acquired NeXT. Covers organizational restructuring, OpenDoc's cancellation, and rethinking clone licensing—information-dense but conversational and lengthy.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:26

Focus means constantly saying no, offending people, and enduring bad press

Jobs acknowledged that killing OpenDoc disappointed some developers, but said Apple's past problem was poor engineering management: teams pulling in eighteen different directions doing things that 'made sense microscopically but not macroscopically.' He argued that focus isn't deciding what to do, but constantly saying no, offending people, and accepting bad press—but only this way does the sum exceed the parts.

— Steve Jobs
8:28

Media naturally lag in perceiving company changes by eighteen months or longer

One developer complained about a Wall Street Journal reporter shorting Apple while writing negative pieces. Jobs responded that media's understanding of companies naturally lags, like how people see you as you were eighteen months ago even after you've grown. The solution isn't correcting media, he said, but continuing to make good products and communicating directly with customers and developers; by year-end the situation will naturally look different.

— Steve Jobs
14:43

With gigabit ethernet, server access becomes faster than local disk storage

Jobs described his workflow in the NeXT era: all personal data stored in a server home directory, nothing on the local drive. Log into any machine and retrieve all your files. Someone backed up the server daily with redundant drives and power supplies. As gigabit ethernet arrives, he argued, server access will be faster than local disk access, so PCs should eliminate all moving parts except keyboard and mouse.

— Steve Jobs
18:46

Vertical integration lets Apple solve complex problems five to ten years early

Critics have long said Apple's approach of doing everything in-house—hardware, software, user experience, marketing—is a fatal flaw. Jobs countered that this is exactly Apple's unique advantage: the PC industry spent five years just getting plug-and-play standards pushed, with Intel, Microsoft, and Compaq fighting each other. Apple controls design end-to-end and has the opportunity to solve genuinely complex problems five to ten years ahead of the industry.

— Steve Jobs
22:47

Platform improvements multiply developer productivity by five to ten times over

Jobs cited Lighthouse as an example: 18 developers using NeXT's development environment produced five applications, each best-in-class, including Concurrence (which he considered the strongest presentation software across all platforms) and Quantrix (a spreadsheet descended from Improv). His conclusion: new platforms can boost developer productivity five to tenfold. Three people in a garage can ship a product in six to nine months—something he says he hasn't seen in the industry in ten to twelve years.

— Steve Jobs
34:03

Clone makers should design hardware freely and pay tiered software license fees

Asked about the future of Mac-compatible machines, Jobs said he supported broad licensing but needed to change how it works. Forcing clone makers to copy Apple's hardware design was 'stupid'; they should be free to design hardware as they wish. Software licensing fees should be tiered by volume; otherwise clones only target high-margin high-end models, and $10-per-copy software fees eat away the profit Apple depends on from low-end machines to maintain the ecosystem.

— Steve Jobs
47:22

Television advertising wastes money; print campaigns and PR prove value more efficiently

Asked when morale-boosting television ads would appear, Jobs flatly said Apple shouldn't be on TV this year—that's spending big money to tell the world 'we're fine.' What they should do instead is put money into print advertising and PR, so journalists write 'Apple is back' on the front page rather than Apple spending money to prove it on pages seven through fourteen. He emphasized that marketing's first priority is actually profitability: losses in a quarter will wash away all the positive noise anyway.

— Steve Jobs
1:01:32

Organizations cannot successfully manage three operating systems without destroying quality

When asked about Newton's future, Jobs said his opinion didn't matter much, but most companies can't even do one operating system well, and few can manage two. Apple will find it very challenging managing just Mac OS and Rhapsody for the next few years, and can't imagine successfully managing three simultaneously. This has nothing to do with Newton's quality, but is purely an organizational capacity issue.

— Steve Jobs
1:06:35

Apple's problem is execution and basic management discipline, not communication

Describing his role as a consultant to Gil Amelio, Jobs said his main effort over the past few months has been helping dismantle the company's siloed business units and rebuild as a flat functional organization: Avie Tevanian overseeing software, John Rubinstein overseeing hardware, Guerino De Luca overseeing marketing, Fred Anderson as CFO. Apple's problem was never 'not saying enough,' he said, but lacked execution and basic management discipline. These people were establishing that foundation.

— Steve Jobs

Glossary

OpenDoc
Apple's 1990s document component architecture, killed by Jobs for being incompatible and poorly engineered.
Rhapsody
Apple's next-generation operating system built on NeXTSTEP after acquiring NeXT, later evolved into Mac OS X.
NFS
Network File System—a protocol allowing computers to store files on remote servers while transparently accessing them like local disk.
CHRP
Common Hardware Reference Platform—Apple's universal hardware reference standard for PowerPC-compatible machines at that time.
