School Isn't the End of Play — Free Play Becomes More Precious

Once a child enters full-time school, independent, unstructured play isn't replaced — it becomes scarcer and more important. It's the core way children digest the stimulation of school and develop creativity.

This episode responds to a mother's anxiety about full-day kindergarten. Janet uses Stuart Brown's definition of play and a list of capacities to argue that play can coexist with school. Suited to parents of preschoolers or young children.

5:05

Full-time school doesn't mean the end of play

Many parents worry that once a child enters a full-time program, play time gets squeezed out. Janet responds directly: no matter what kind of school a child attends, play doesn't end — it becomes more precious. She points out that from kindergarten age onward, children are fully capable of adapting to a structured environment, and can do it well. The key isn't the type of school, but whether parents preserve independent, unstructured play time for the child after school. She even suggests that the heads of after-school care programs should also set aside plenty of free play time, so children get a therapeutic release at the end of the day.

— Janet Lansbury
6:11

Letting go is a parent's lifelong work

Janet quotes Magda Gerber, noting that the hardest part of parental love is finding the balance between "holding tight" and "letting go." She sees this as the emotional work of parenting: trusting that the child can move on to the next stage. From infancy — allowing the child to move freely, to choose what to look at and touch — to handing the child over to others to care for, to school, to driving, to college, every step is a letting go. She stresses that children need us to let go with confidence, but that doesn't mean parents can't have feelings. This mother's struggle is normal and healthy.

— Janet Lansbury
11:20

After-school programs shouldn't fill up free play

Janet observes that even when a child doesn't need after-school care, parents often feel they must schedule all kinds of enrichment classes — and none of these are unstructured play. She calls on the heads of after-school programs to set aside at least plenty of free play time, not all classes or guided learning. She cites Stuart Brown's book "Play," noting that play is "an engaging, seemingly purposeless activity that provides enjoyment and suspends one's sense of self and of time." She believes the capacity to play is essential not only to happiness, but also to sustaining social relationships and becoming a creative, innovative person.

— Janet Lansbury
13:28

After school: downtime first, then homework, then screens

Janet proposes a concrete arrangement: after school is mainly downtime — not screen time, but at least an hour or two of free time, letting the child do things that look like "doing nothing," or sink into their own hobbies, or even just space out and process emotions. She suggests that as the child grows older and has homework, give free time first, then homework, and after homework is done, screen time can be given if the child wants it. She stresses not using a threatening tone of "you can only do that after you finish this," but calmly presenting it as "this is just how things work."

— Janet Lansbury
15:32

Homework is between the child and the teacher

Janet advises parents not to turn homework into their own task. She sees homework as the work between the child and the teacher; parents can help and be present to encourage, but shouldn't take on the responsibility of "getting the child to finish homework." She has seen too many parents stuck in this position, where homework becomes the parent's homework. If the child truly can't finish it, she suggests letting the teacher know, and letting the teacher and child adjust it, keeping the responsibility between the child and the teacher. She also thinks kindergarten assigns homework too early.

— Janet Lansbury
17:40

Play looks like goofing off, but it's shaping the brain

Janet quotes a passage from her new book "Baby Person": play is studied, discussed and recommended with enormous enthusiasm by child educators and psychologists, and for good reason. Through this intrinsically interesting activity, children expand their minds and abilities in wondrous ways. Even when play looks like mere goofing off, it is working magic, activating cognitive, motor, social, creative and learning skills such as critical thinking, problem-solving and focus. Play is also a natural therapeutic tool, helping to unload stress, digest experiences and process emotions.

— Janet Lansbury
20:48

The list of capacities free play teaches children

Janet shares a list of what children learn in self-directed play: making choices, devising strategies, understanding how things move, making and carrying out plans, solving problems creatively, learning the properties of objects and materials, tolerating frustration, resolving conflicts with objects and other children, taking initiative, focusing and persisting, meeting challenges with spontaneous and fresh responses, abstract thinking and hypothetical reasoning, self-motivation, being internally directed, cause and consequence, satisfaction in one's own effort, perseverance, processing emotions, being with oneself, trusting one's own judgment, self-reward, concentration, autonomy, love of learning, understanding, following intuition, curiosity, dreaming, imagining, understanding the world. She tells this mother that the child can still get all of this and more in the precious gaps of time.

— Janet Lansbury

Glossary

unstructured play
Play in which the child decides what to play and how, with no adult-set goals or rules.
downtime
Time after school with no scheduled activities and no screens, letting the child relax freely or space out.
self-directed play
Play the child initiates and leads, with adults not intervening in its direction.
