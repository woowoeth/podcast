Traditional CAPE Valuation Is Miscalculated: The Market Isn't As Expensive As It Looks

Traditional CAPE weights components by earnings rather than market value, underweighting high-priced, low-earnings tech stocks; using market-value-weighted component CAPE instead shows the US market's current overvaluation as less severe than official CAPE suggests, and predictive accuracy improves by 20–25%.

Ten-year expected returns depend on current valuations, not historical averages

Ben Marshall notes that for forecasting 100-year returns, using the historical 10% annual average over the past 100 years is a reasonable starting point; but most people care about the next 10 years, where historical averages fall short because research shows ten-year expected returns are conditional on current valuations: high valuations imply low expected returns, and vice versa. Ben Felix adds that US historical returns also cannot represent global expectations, since American actual performance has significantly exceeded other markets.

11:12

The three-factor model is about 30% more accurate than naive forecasting

Marshall's team tested four combinations—dividends alone, dividends plus growth, valuation alone, and dividends plus growth plus valuation—and found that the three-factor model using CAPE as the valuation input was most consistent. By out-of-sample R-squared and mean absolute error, it outperformed naive forecasts (assuming the future equals the historical average) by about 30%. While not perfect, this improvement is substantial compared to other published forecasting models.

13:08

Current CAPE approaches 40, more than double the historical average

As of the recording date (November 26), Shiller CAPE stands at approximately 40–41, versus a historical average of around 18, meaning current valuations far exceed historical levels. The three-factor model also incorporates dividend yield (currently at historic lows) and earnings growth assumptions. Marshall believes the market's current pricing reflects very robust expectations for future earnings growth, whether this materializes remains to be seen.

14:34

Ten-year investors should reduce stock allocations; thirty- to forty-year investors can ignore valuations

The model is valid only for ten-year forecasts and offers no predictive value for next month, next year, or even the next three years. For ten-year investors, high valuations may require reducing stock allocations; for thirty- to forty-year investors, they can ignore current overvaluation because a long enough holding period dilutes near-term valuation effects. Ben Felix notes that PWL uses a weighted expected return estimate combining 25% of the CAPE reciprocal with 75% of the historical average.

— Ben Marshall
19:25

CAPE's fatal flaw: using earnings-weighted ratios to forecast a market-value-weighted index

Traditional CAPE has two overlooked mismatches. First, the index's current constituents' prices are compared against ten years ago's entirely different constituents' reported historical earnings—for example, Tesla replaced Apartment Investment in the S&P 500 in December 2020, so when calculating 2021 CAPE, the numerator included Tesla's price but the denominator mixed in the real estate company's historical earnings. Second, more counterintuitively, traditional CAPE weights by earnings scale, not market value, meaning an earnings-weighted ratio forecasts a market-value-weighted index return, introducing inherent bias. Research also found this problem affects only ratios where the denominator is not price or market value (like CAPE), not metrics like dividend yield or price-to-book, where the denominator is already price-based.

— Ben Marshall
24:53

Reweighting each stock's CAPE by market value boosts predictive power by 20–25%

The solution is to calculate CAPE for each stock individually, then aggregate using market-value weights rather than earnings weights to obtain component CAPE, aligning the weighting method perfectly with the S&P 500's own market-value-weighted approach. By out-of-sample R-squared, this change boosts predictive accuracy by 20–25%; measured by certainty equivalent return, the economic value for asset allocation also shows investors genuinely benefit. In 1999, when tech valuations soared, traditional CAPE's earnings weighting suppressed high-price, low-earnings tech weights, while component CAPE assigned them far greater weight—the gap was especially pronounced in that period.

— Ben Marshall
36:40

Both versions identify overvaluation, but traditional and component CAPE score it differently

Currently traditional CAPE is about 1.8 times the historical average (80% above), while component CAPE is about 1.6 times (60% above)—both signal overvaluation, but component CAPE's degree is notably more modest. Marshall sees this as one reason component CAPE has forecasted better recently: valuations stayed high over the past decade, but the market performed well overall, making traditional CAPE appear too pessimistic, while component CAPE's optimistic bias actually tracked reality more closely.

— Ben Marshall
45:37

The permanent portfolio's historical outperformance was largely underwritten by gold price appreciation

Marshall acknowledges his paper did not conduct deep analysis but speculates that Harry Browne's permanent portfolio (25% each in stocks, bonds, gold, and cash) outperformed traditional stock-bond portfolios from 1972 to 1991 largely due to gold's extraordinary performance during that period (after the US left the gold standard and Americans regained the freedom to own gold), rather than inherent superiority of the portfolio structure itself—since relative to traditional portfolios, the permanent portfolio's main distinction is gold overweight. Ben Felix later adds he ran out-of-sample tests from 2012 onward to verify this.

— Ben Marshall

Glossary

CAPE ratio (cyclically adjusted PE ratio)
A valuation metric calculated by dividing an index's current price by its average earnings per share over the past 10 years.
component CAPE ratio
An improved CAPE that calculates the ratio for each stock individually, then aggregates using market-value weighting.
out-of-sample R-squared
A measure of how much a model's forecasts improve relative to naive predictions based on historical averages.
certainty equivalent return
The risk-adjusted return that would make an investor equally satisfied compared to holding a risky investment.
Buffett indicator
A valuation metric calculated by dividing total market capitalization by GDP.
permanent portfolio
An asset allocation strategy with equal 25% allocations to stocks, bonds, gold, and cash.
