Odysseus's wife recognised her beggar husband early — she just didn't say so

The hosts argue Penelope has already recognised her disguised husband: her uncharacteristic laugh, her reading of her son's sneeze as a death omen for the suitors, and her talk of proper bride-gifts are all signals — and Odysseus gets them.

A low-density book-by-book close read, but the debate over whether Penelope has already recognised her husband, and the setup of putting everyone in one house as a moral test, are useful to anyone writing revenge and the return to power.

3:27

The one recognition that needs no deception

Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, comes into town and passes the old dog Argos, lying in mule dung with no one to care for it. The dog wags its tail, its ears droop, and Odysseus weeps but cannot go over and hold it — the goatherd Melanthius is right there, and any exposure would wreck the revenge plan. The hosts say this passage is special because the book is layered with deception everywhere: he deceives his mother, he deceives his most loyal slave woman, whereas with Argos there is only pure perception, and it cuts straight across, no testing required. One student wrote a paper arguing Argos is Ithaca itself: a place neglected, slowly rotting, with no one stepping in to fix it; the suitors are the dung, and both Argos and Telemachus lie in the dung without becoming dung.

7:29

Has she actually recognised her husband or not

One host calls this the book's biggest hermeneutic question, one he has worn everyone out with in his honors seminar: by the end of Book 18, has Penelope recognised Odysseus? The other host's answer: if you don't ask, I'd say no, the text reads as if not; but once you ask, I think yes. The evidence: a fugitive prophet brings an oracle saying he is already on Ithaca; Telemachus sneezes, she laughs and says it is a death omen for the suitors — before this she was either crying or about to soak the bed with tears, never this happy. The counter-evidence comes from the same person: Telemachus earlier would not bring her before Odysseus, because over the years many people had come with good news, all of them lying. Their landing point is that she is already blinking at her husband in Greek Morse code.

19:36

This character test doesn't decide who dies

Athena has Odysseus disguised as a beggar begging among the suitors so as to see who is good and who is bad — but the text says in the very next line that she had no intention of saving anyone in the slaughter that follows. So the character test in this house carries no survival payoff; even the consistently friendly Amphinomus dies all the same. The hosts say this is a reminder: the point of the test is not to decide who lives. They attribute the test's motive to Athena — what she wants is to draw Odysseus's blood up, to put him back into a killing state, not to sift out who deserves pardon.

24:39

Begging in your own house is the hardest self-control

One line gets repeated throughout the disguise: a beggar cannot have shame. Odysseus has to behave in the most shameless way, and the hosts think this is harder than taking the stool to the back or enduring Melanthius's abuse — a once-great king now begging in his own house, from these people, for food that is his own. The humiliation is close to the limit here, but the more accurate word is that it humbles him: he is pressed down to the lowest point, in some sense he brought it on himself, and by accepting it and owning it he is rebuilding his personhood out of it. There is another layer: as king he was always courteous to beggars, and now that he is one, perhaps he is almost relieved — at last he knows what their lives are like.

29:41

You can win, but win too well and you're exposed

The other farce is Irus — a fat drunk who runs errands for the suitors and is nicknamed Iris for it — coming to fight Odysseus over begging turf, with a goat's stomach stuffed with fat and blood as the prize. The hosts say Odysseus is walking a tightrope: he cannot be beaten by this drunk, but he also cannot hit too hard, because once other suitors step in to help he would have to beat them all too, and his identity would be exposed on the spot. So his trick is to make them swear that no one will lift a hand for Irus. The hosts compare it to Clark Kent fighting an ordinary person: you can't show your real strength. Another joke: the moment Irus shows fear, Antinous turns on him instantly, saying he'll ship him off to some king who cuts off people's noses and ears.

31:42

The one actually haunted is Odysseus himself

After winning, Odysseus sits down beside Amphinomus and gives that speech: of all creatures that live and breathe and crawl on the earth, man is the weakest; our mood depends on what Zeus sends each day. Then he warns him: do not turn from the right way, do not disrespect the wife of a man who will soon be home. Amphinomus understands, paces back and forth in the house, his heart disturbed — and the hosts say this is where they start reading these books as a ghost story: Odysseus comes back to town like High Plains Drifter, letting the suitors reveal their true faces and dig their own graves. But he adds that maybe he is the ghost, and he himself seems haunted by something too. The narrator's close is cold: the good man gets no exemption, Amphinomus still dies on a spear.

35:42

Penelope starts playing her own hand

Athena first gives Penelope a makeover, making her more beautiful and raising desire in the suitors. One host says he sometimes leans toward reading it as metaphor: a person who for so long could only weave the shroud and unravel it, unravel it and weave it, keeping the clock stopped, decides to act for the first time, and that excitement is itself the light on her face. The other won't give up the literal sense: the storyteller most likely really believed a god did this. Right after, Penelope tells the suitors that her husband made her promise to remarry once her son grew a beard, and the date has come; but a respectable family courting should bring fat sheep and fat cattle and good gifts, not eat up another man's goods for free. Odysseus is pleased to hear it, because her mouth is saying pretty things while her mind is calculating something else — and the hosts wonder how he can be so sure it is a trick when he hasn't tested her yet. Their answer is domestic: a husband just knows when his wife means what she says.

41:48

The god wants Odysseus to ache inside

After the slave woman Melantho taunts him and Eurymachus leads the mockery, the text says Athena keeps the suitors provoking him, because she wants the pain to sink deep into Odysseus's heart. The hosts ask why: one intuition is that she wants to stir up righteous anger; the other is that it is both — she enjoys a good fight, and Odysseus has not been in a warrior's mindset for a long time. This passage also makes them notice that the gods may not actually need to intervene: these men have been drinking all day and have seen so many strange things, they can go that mad on their own, no god required to teach them to do vile things. And in these books Athena is very active yet never appears as she once did, so even the hosts can't say for sure whether Odysseus and Telemachus know she is pulling strings.
