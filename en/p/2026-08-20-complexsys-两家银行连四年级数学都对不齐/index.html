Banks Reconcile by Phone, Not by System: One Bad Trade Can Cost Hundreds of Millions

When it comes to who owes whom how much, institutions often rely not on automated reconciliation systems but on phone calls, favors, and the tacit understanding that they'll still be doing business together tomorrow.

6:01

The biggest cost of a large order is being seen by the market

Put a large order to "sell 1 million shares of Microsoft" directly out there and the market immediately reads the signal and quotes you in the wrong direction. Ways to avoid this include: splitting the order into dozens of trades worked out slowly over the day, handing portions to five or six brokers to each post a piece, or even having an investment bank hold it directly — so the market sees the bank as the seller rather than the real one, and the bank then breaks the order up itself and works it out over the course of a day. Slicing has its tells too: if a trade of the same size at the same price shows up every five minutes, the other side can immediately guess there is more behind it and front-run the position.

19:37

One unread error message cleared 20 million shares

A client instructed a sale of 20 million shares of a highly volatile instrument, agreeing that 5 million would be executed manually in one block by a senior trader and the remaining 15 million would be worked into the market at market prices in slices by a junior trader. When the senior trader clicked to execute, the system threw an error because the other side had already started posting orders and the inventory had gone from 20 million to 12 million; the trader responsible for the remainder did not notice the error and assumed the whole order would go out normally. In the end all 20 million shares were liquidated in one block at the market close, and the team was called back over the weekend to hedge the exposure with futures.

The exchange saw the fat-finger order and still wouldn't stop it

A Japanese brokerage meant to sell 1 share of a stock at about 6,000 yen per share, but the order actually went out as 600,000 shares at 1 yen — the quantity and the price had been transposed. When the order reached the exchange, staff could see it was plainly an input error, but concluded they had no authority to unilaterally cancel a bank's order, so they let it through. The brokerage lost an amount in the hundreds of millions of dollars, while an American institution known for its aggressive trading bought heavily at that price and made a fortune. Regulators were later furious at the exchange staff's acquiescence in "not daring to stop it."

34:29

What killed Knight wasn't a bug, it was that no one had the authority to call stop

Knight Capital's technology failure sent orders flooding out of control within minutes, losing far more than the company's capital. The post-mortem found that the hardest problem was not the technical error itself but that no one was clearly empowered to halt trading — because the firm had agreements with some clients to stay online, in some cases as their exclusive trading venue, which in turn gave those clients an incentive during the crisis to inject capital to keep it alive rather than let it shut down. The rest of the industry was barely affected, because everyone else's risk exposure was designed on the assumption that a counterparty could fail.

39:16

In the last few minutes of reconciliation, one side carries the gap naked

Two institutions have to confirm by a fixed time each day (in the episode's example, 5:15 p.m., 5:30 at the latest) whether the day's net amount matches, after which one side wires the difference over Fedwire. If the person responsible happens to be on vacation with no backup, the firm makes emergency calls all the way up from the operations desk — first confirming whether the other side accepts the number, then confirming whether the money can actually arrive — because during those few dozen minutes, one side has to unilaterally carry the risk of that money. Ordinarily it is just an embarrassing day; if it happens on a Friday when the market is in broad panic about the banking system, the mood is completely different.

44:26

Finding the real positions meant using Yahoo Finance

A code bug in an algorithmic trading system mismatched the ticker symbols in execution reports — a Microsoft fill was labelled as Apple — and no one knew which records had been swapped. The team pulled every fill of the day and went to Yahoo Finance to check each stock's actual trading range that day, manually checking trade by trade: "this fill was at $47, but Apple never traded below 90 that day, so this must be a different stock," piecing together the correct positions from an external quote site. The same firm also had a position that had been open for four or five years and could not be closed, because the counterparty had gone bankrupt or the product had long since stopped trading.

— Alex
48:54

What saved Goldman in 2008 was a single position database

During the 2008 crisis, Goldman Sachs had a single, centralized position database, so a few keystrokes told it whether it was net long or net short a given instrument, and when the crisis hit it could quickly reverse, close out and free up capital. Many institutions assembled over the years through mergers had cash, options, equities and Treasuries sitting in separate systems, making a global position picture very hard to obtain. At one firm the central position server actually went down, and it was only because trading desks kept their own private copies of positions and reconciled them against each other that the firm was not completely blind; that outage took 27 hours to resolve.

1:01:03

Quotes depend not on the order but on who is placing it

Retail orders at Schwab or Robinhood almost certainly do not carry information that can move the market — a daughter heading to college means a withdrawal, a 401(k) buys on payday — and that kind of flow can be quoted very loosely. But once genuinely informed money is mixed in, quotes have to be cautious; if the voice on the phone is Goldman Sachs, you quote especially carefully, because "this is Goldman, and if you give it money it will take it." This is also the hidden yardstick by which prime brokers decide whom to extend credit to and how much: the same seemingly ordinary flow can be smart money or dumb money behind it, and the treatment is completely different.

Glossary

MPID
An institution code stamped on quotes and trades to identify which firm is trading
Reg NMS / NBBO
The U.S. rule requiring brokers to execute customer orders at the best prevailing price across the market
Prime broker
An intermediary that provides hedge funds and other clients with trading access, settlement and credit leverage
Golden record
The position/holdings database recognized internally as the single authoritative source
Payment for order flow
The practice of market makers paying brokers for the right to handle their retail orders
Busting a trade
When an exchange or counterparty agrees to treat a clearly erroneous fill as if it never happened
