Computing power is what to verify, not human behavior

Breaking down AI development inputs reveals three categories: staff, algorithms and data, and computing power. Milton selected computing power as the focus because it's uniquely specialized for AI, requires thousands of chips, and currently must be physically concentrated in one location—making it far simpler to verify than monitoring employee actions or auditing code. This is why computing power would be the first and most easily verifiable component if the US and China negotiated an AI slowdown agreement.

Inference re-execution lets GPUs prove they only run predictions

Amodo's prototype places a network splitter at a data center's edge, copying traffic to verification servers that re-run identical input tokens and compare outputs (using the DiFR algorithm to handle model randomness). If all network traffic can be explained by this re-execution, the compute is provably running only inference; unexplained network signals reveal a hidden training job. This deterministic replay technique makes it nearly impossible to disguise what a GPU is actually doing without getting caught.

— Tom Milton
15:55

Power consumption signatures expose model architecture and size

The episode demonstrates wrapping an inductor around a GPU power supply, connecting it to an oscilloscope, and running nanoGPT training. The power curve clearly shows forward pass, backward pass, and idle periods—and the small spikes between layers even reveal the model has 12 layers. These ‘side channels,’ traditionally exploited by attackers to steal secrets, can be repurposed as surveillance tools to see what compute is doing. That said, achieving GPU-level precision across an entire data center is extremely difficult; the best you can do is capture signatures that can be partially obfuscated, but only at the cost of efficiency.

— Tom Milton
27:58

Both sides must test verification systems in each other's data centers

Before signing the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty, the US and USSR didn't rely on faith. They ran a joint verification experiment: Soviet scientists went to the US, American scientists went to the USSR, and each tested their own seismic monitoring equipment on the other's ground. Milton argues AI verification needs the same path. Before any agreement takes effect, each party's red teams should attempt to break—or verify—the other side's verification systems in real data centers or test environments. This is how you build confidence that the system actually works.

— Tom Milton
39:37

Confidential inspection results let verification work without exposing trade secrets

Under the Chemical Weapons Convention, inspectors bring mass spectrometers into chemical plants, but the instruments run in ‘confidential mode’—they output only pass or fail, not the full chemical spectrum, safeguarding corporate trade secrets. Milton hopes AI verification follows the same path: proving compliance with international rules without disclosing model weights or user data. But he emphasizes this isn't a perfect parallel. AI moves faster than the arms-control systems that took decades to negotiate, and some verification techniques may not transfer as cleanly.

— Tom Milton

Starting today, a skilled researcher could rank among the world's top five by year-end

Milton's assessment: if major labs have truly committed only one or two people to this work, a genuinely talented AI safety researcher beginning to focus on compute verification now could easily be among the five most consequential people globally doing this by year's end. He frames it as extraordinarily consequential: if you're a safety or security engineer at a lab, the nearest path to getting credit for ‘putting AI on the right track’ and eventual recognition might be developing the verification infrastructure that could underpin an international agreement.

1:03:28

Even the experts admit verification might be optional, not necessary

Pressed on whether verification technology is truly mandatory, Milton's answer is measured. He doesn't pursue a theoretically perfect, formally-proved ‘cheat-proof’ system—international coordination has always mixed trust, espionage, and eyeballs, much like arms-control treaties that count bombers with satellites. He positions verification as an ‘option’. A year ago almost nobody mentioned it; today researchers and governments are calling for it, so it increasingly looks like it will be deployed. But he admits nobody can definitively prove it's necessary.

— Tom Milton
1:44:14

Losing AI access for one day exposed Britain's core vulnerability

Milton describes three days of using Claude to design hardware circuits in one continuous session—until a Saturday morning when he typed ‘continue’ and got an error: the US government had enacted export controls on Fable, cutting off his access. This visceral jolt made him understand Britain's precarity: during compute scarcity, the US will likely prioritize domestic supply, potentially locking out countries like Britain that rely on cutting-edge foreign models unless they build sovereign compute infrastructure with sufficient security classification. This poses a real threat to company productivity and national research capability.

Glossary

A cryptographic technique proving a claim without revealing underlying data like model weights
