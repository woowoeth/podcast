Believing He Could, So Never Preparing: How the Stunt King Nearly Died in a 4,000-Foot Canyon

Stunt rider Knievel treated "positive attitude" as a methodology: no practice runs, no angle calculations, no risk assessment before a jump. Psychology professor Oettingen's experiments show that optimistic fantasy doesn't fuel success — it paralyzes. The Snake River failure was sealed before ignition.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:19

When the countdown began, even he no longer believed

On September 8, 1974, in front of live cameras, Evel Knievel told a reporter to his face that he wished he weren't there and didn't have to do this. True to his style, he operated on positive visualization and full throttle — no calculating the ramp angle, no practice runs, no rehearsal. But the Snake River Canyon is about 4,000 feet wide, and his previous motorcycle jump record was only 140 feet. As the countdown began, only one thought was left in his head: pressing the button might be the last thing he ever did.

6:28

The stunt king's creed was taught to him by an insurance company

As a young man he made a living selling insurance, once claiming he sold 110 policies in a day, with clients including the staff and patients of a mental hospital; he believed he deserved a vice presidency, and when his boss W. Clement Stone refused him to his face, he quit on the spot. Stone was the co-author of Napoleon Hill's "positive mental attitude" doctrine. The later Knievel lived on this stuff — his future "never prepare, just believe" approach to jumping traces back to this encounter in the insurance business.

10:36

The success guru's biography doesn't survive scrutiny

Napoleon Hill is regarded as the father of modern self-help publishing, wrote Think and Grow Rich, and interviewed Ford and Rockefeller. But the evidence the show presents points to him being a fraud: no record of him serving as an adviser to Wilson; the claim that Carnegie mentored him in writing the book can't be found in the archives; his interview notes "happened" to be lost in a fire. Think and Grow Rich was actually his thousand-page The Law of Success compressed into 230 pages and rewritten as "13 steps to riches" — yet it sold 100 million copies.

15:46

His first famous jump was improvised mid-air

After leaving the insurance company, to open up sales for Honda motorcycles, he jumped 40 feet in public without ever having attempted a test jump; the landing zone was a group of mountain lions and a cage full of rattlesnakes. Mid-air he realized he didn't have enough speed, but improvised by lifting the front wheel — the whole maneuver was written as he flew. He landed on the snake cage, man and bike stayed upright, and the snakes instead crawled into the audience — a comical scene. It became the first "victory" of his life, and from then on he was hooked on jumping.

24:04

The more you fantasize about success, the less you clear the obstacles

NYU psychology professor Gabriella Oettingen's experiments directly conflict with positive thinking: students who fantasized about getting good grades were more likely to fail; patients who fantasized about recovering smoothly from hip surgery stayed bedridden longer. Her explanation is that optimistic fantasy lets people "pre-spend" the feeling of achievement, so they don't bother clearing the real obstacles; imagining the obstacles is what triggers action. The show calls this a counterintuitive conclusion: thinking through the bad outcome first is precisely the way to make the good outcome happen.

31:22

Both prototypes sank, and the third carried a person directly

The engineering for the Snake River Canyon jump was almost never validated: during an unmanned X-1 test flight it spiraled upward and plunged head-first into the Snake River; the modified X-2 prototype also went out of control and ended up on the riverbed alongside the X-1. Because of budget shortfalls, engineers are said to have made a valve out of a dog food can. With only weeks left before the land lease expired, there was no next Skycycle available. So the only option was to put Knievel into the never-flight-tested X-2 for the actual launch.

34:33

His life hung on a single 45-pound grip

The X-2 was registered as an aircraft by Idaho authorities, and its main controls were just two things: foot pedals for the tail fin, and a handle requiring 45 pounds of pressure to hold — hold it and the parachute doesn't open; let go or pass out and the chute deploys immediately. He had never flown a plane, never practiced in the cockpit, not even G-force training — he just wore a stars-and-stripes jumpsuit. When asked if he feared passing out mid-flight, he said no, he would stay conscious. The show's narration points out: that kind of determination is no substitute for a plan.

36:41

Snake River wasn't one failure — it was the start of the decline

Everything went out of control immediately after ignition. Knievel felt his "whole field of vision disappear," perhaps briefly blacking out; the chute deployed early, the craft decelerated violently, and finally grazed the canyon wall and fell into the Snake River, not far from shore — he survived. Engineer Truax's conclusion: you didn't hold the handle. Knievel blamed the parachute design, and harsher fans said he deliberately deployed the chute early and chickened out. Afterward his public performances gradually fell apart — alcoholism, hitting a business partner with a baseball bat, jail — the Snake River jump was the beginning of his long decline.

Glossary

Positive Mental Attitude
The success philosophy Hill and Stone sold: think only of the good, and misfortune won't find you.
Manifesting
Repeatedly visualizing a wish in your mind and believing it will come true on its own; the show says Hill's method can be seen as a precursor to the manifesting trend.
Control Optimists
People who believe good outcomes come only from actively seeking opportunities and doing real work; the opposite of wishful optimists.
X2 Skycycle
The steam-powered rocket vehicle Knievel had custom-built to jump the Snake River Canyon, registered as an aircraft but never test-flown.
