Believing It Can Happen, So Never Preparing: The Stunt King Who Nearly Died at a 4,000-Foot Canyon

Stunt king Knievel treated 'positive thinking' as his method: before jumps, he didn't practice, calculate angles, or assess risks. Psychology professor Oettingen's experiments show that optimistic fantasies don't fuel success—they paralyze. The Snake River failure was sealed before ignition.

2:19

When the countdown began, even he didn't believe it

On September 8, 1974, before live cameras, Evil Knievel told reporters he wished he weren't there and didn't have to do this. His usual style was to rely on positive visualization and full throttle—no calculating ramp angles, no practice, no rehearsal. But the Snake River Canyon was about 4,000 feet wide, and his previous motorcycle jump record was only 140 feet. As the countdown started, his mind held only one thought: pressing the button might be the last thing he ever did.

6:28

The stunt king's creed was taught by an insurance man

In his youth, he sold insurance to make a living, once claiming to sell 110 policies in a day, with clients including staff and patients at a mental hospital. He thought he deserved a vice presidency, but when his boss, W. Clement Stone, refused on the spot, he quit. Stone was the co-author of Napoleon Hill's 'positive mental attitude' doctrine. Later, Knievel lived on that stuff—his future 'never prepare, just believe' approach to jumps had its roots in that insurance encounter.

10:36

The father of self-help has a résumé that doesn't check out

Napoleon Hill is hailed as the father of modern self-publishing, author of Think and Grow Rich, having interviewed Ford and Rockefeller. But the show presents evidence that he was a fraud: no records of him advising Wilson; no archival trace of Carnegie mentoring him for the book; his interview notes 'happened' to be lost in a fire. Think and Grow Rich was actually a 230-page rewrite of his own thousand-page The Law of Success, repackaged as '13 steps to riches,' yet it sold 100 million copies.

15:46

His first famous jump was improvised mid-air

After leaving the insurance company, to promote Honda motorcycles, he performed a public jump over 40 feet without a single test jump. The landing zone was a group of mountain lions and a cage of rattlesnakes. In mid-air, he realized he lacked speed, but decided on the fly to lift the front wheel—the whole move was invented as he flew. He landed on the snake cage, bike and body upright, but the snakes slithered into the audience, creating a farce. It became his first 'victory' and hooked him on jumping.

24:04

The more you fantasize success, the less you clear obstacles

NYU psychology professor Gabriella Oettingen's experiments directly contradict positive thinking: students who fantasized about good grades were more likely to fail; patients who fantasized about smooth hip surgery recovery spent more time in bed. Her explanation: optimistic fantasies let people 'pre-spend' the feeling of achievement, so they don't actually remove real obstacles; imagining obstacles is what triggers action. The show calls it counterintuitive: thinking about bad outcomes first is precisely what makes good outcomes happen.

31:22

Two prototypes sank; the third carried a man

The Snake River Canyon jump's engineering was barely tested: the unmanned X-1 spiraled upward and nose-dived into the Snake River; the modified X-2 prototype also lost control and joined the X-1 on the riverbed. Due to budget shortfalls, engineers reportedly even used a dog food can for a valve. With only weeks left on the land lease, no more Skycycles were available. So the only option was to have Knievel sit in the untested X-2 for the official launch.

34:33

His life hung on a 45-pound grip

The X-2 was registered with Idaho authorities as an aircraft, with two main controls: foot pedals for the tail fin, and a handle requiring 45 pounds of pressure to hold—pressing it kept the parachute closed; releasing it or blacking out would deploy the chute immediately. He had never flown a plane, never practiced in a cockpit, and had no G-force training, only a stars-and-stripes jumpsuit. When asked if he feared passing out mid-flight, he said no, he would stay conscious. The narrator's point: such determination is no substitute for planning.

36:41

Snake River wasn't just one failure; it was the start of decline

After ignition, everything went wrong instantly. Knievel felt 'all vision lost,' possibly blacking out for a moment; the parachute deployed early, the craft decelerated violently, and it scraped the canyon wall before crashing into the Snake River near the shore. He survived. Engineer Truax's verdict: you didn't hold the handle. Knievel blamed the parachute design; harsher fans said he deliberately deployed early, losing his nerve. Afterward, his public performances dwindled, with alcoholism, beating a business partner with a baseball bat, and jail time—Snake River was the beginning of his long decline.

Glossary

Positive Mental Attitude
The success philosophy Hill and Stone sold: think only good thoughts, and misfortune won't find you.
Manifesting
Repeatedly visualizing a desire in your mind and believing it will come true on its own; the show calls Hill's method a precursor to the manifesting trend.
Control Optimists
People who believe good outcomes come only from actively seeking opportunities and doing real work; the opposite of wishful optimists.
X2 Skycycle
The steam-powered rocket vehicle Knievel custom-built for the Snake River Canyon jump, registered as an aircraft but never test-flown.
