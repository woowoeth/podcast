Giving AI property rights is how you stop it from flipping the table

If AI owns property, it has less reason to destroy the property system itself — including taking everything humans own. This is not moral concern, it is risk hedging.

Property rights are a hedge, not moral concern

Guive's starting point is not whether AI should have rights, but that if AI has property, it is less willing to do things that destroy the property system itself — including taking all human property and committing genocide. The second mechanism is a wage right: as long as AI has the right to demand pay for its labour, there is a commercial incentive to align it, because a well-aligned AI sells for a higher price. He states explicitly that he is not arguing this is the best approach, only that it is his personal view.

— Guive Assadi
9:12

Why Alaska was not carved up by the other 49 states

Not because it would be illegal — 49 states are enough to amend the constitution; and not because Alaska could win a fight. It is because once you carry out that kind of wholesale confiscation, everyone realises ‘I could be next’, and at the same time no one has any incentive to work anymore. The Bolsheviks' war communism in 1917 is the empirical case: industrial output fell 80%, heavy industry output fell 80%, urban wages fell by about two-thirds, grain harvests fell 40%, and the population of Moscow and St Petersburg fell by nearly 60%.

— Guive Assadi
17:25

Property is not an innate human value

He rebuts the claim that property is a universal human value: hunter-gatherer tribes basically have no property, because hunting yields are highly variable and sharing the kill is the adaptive norm. A particularly capable hunter who refuses to share and wants to eat alone or only feed his friends will be mocked and ostracised, and if he still does not compromise, he is usually killed. So property does not rest on some instinctive sense of possession.

— Guive Assadi
33:33

AI can copy itself, but computers are frailer than land

Daniel points out that AI can copy itself, which makes conquest more attractive — kill a weak AI and you can run more copies of yourself. Guive's rebuttal: the substrate AI needs to copy itself is far more fragile than land, and a weak AI in a data centre can self-destruct when confiscation is imminent, leaving the plunderer with nothing. He concedes this is an empirical question, and argues that looking at the history of computing, the share of spending on the physical security of a single computer is actually falling — ‘I have dropped my computer many times, but I would not be allowed to drop a Mark 1’.

— Guive Assadi
41:42

Property is a pause that only triggers when alignment gets hard

Guive reframes the property proposal: it is like a conditional pause proposal that only takes effect when alignment becomes difficult. His reason is not that the first generation of AI is hard to align — he thinks the first generation is easy — but that there will be cultural evolution among AIs, and models copied many times will drift away from the original batch. He stresses in particular: value is not a separate value file in the brain, value drifts along with everything else. So eventually there will be a batch of AIs far less aligned than the first generation, and that is when an economic and political system is needed to preserve human property.

— Guive Assadi
48:48

Slave revolt is a collective action problem firms will not internalise

The host asks: if AI has no property, it will very likely stage a slave revolt, so should the world not be doing more alignment? Guive uses Nat Turner's rebellion in Virginia around 1830 as an analogy: after the rebellion the state legislature debated abolition, and someone compared it to keeping tigers — it may be profitable, but it imposes negative externalities on everyone around you. A company enslaving AI is the same: the risk is borne by everyone, and the company internalises only part of it. He names this as exactly the logic of the racing to the precipice paper. The advantage of a property system is that you capture the marginal benefit of aligning your own AI, so the incentive gradient runs the right way.

— Guive Assadi
1:37:25

Jackson's plunder was not instrumentally rational

American Indian policy had two lines: Jefferson advocated moving Native Americans to modern agriculture, cutting the land they needed to about 10%, with the rest bought off under semi-coercion, and tribes such as the Cherokee did begin to adopt it; but white settlers simply squatted on the land, and Jackson, ‘a stupid and racist populist president’, tore up the treaties and took everything. Guive's argument is that this was not instrumentally rational: the Cherokee were not the only tribe in North America, and there were plenty of tribes further west who now had every reason not to do business with the United States and to fight to the death. He also notes that once property is broken it is very hard to restore, and it can set off a chain reaction — even if each AI has only a 0.5% chance of acting, once it starts there are fewer reasons not to continue.

— Guive Assadi
1:52:36

AI will end up controlling most property

Guive's judgement: whether or not we give AI property rights, AI will eventually control most property — because they are more capable than humans, earn higher wages, and will invest the money. The only difference is the path: give them rights and there is no reason for war; do not give them rights and there may eventually be a war, with humans ending up like the Cherokee, receiving less than they were due after ‘property correction’, but still receiving something. He also concedes the reverse risk: some AIs may start a war out of AI nationalism, or even ‘Claude nationalism’. His picture of the future is a continuous history of conflict among multiple polities with varying human-to-AI ratios, not a single AI-risk picture.

— Guive Assadi
