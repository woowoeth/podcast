He, not Boudicca, was truly Rome's spark for invasion

The host notes that most people thinking of British resistance to Rome think of Boudicca, but Miles points out that Caratacus was actually earlier and resisted longer. Moreover, he likely sparked Rome's interest by continuously expanding his power in the pre-Roman period, squeezing neighboring kingdoms and forcing deposed rulers to seek aid from Rome—inadvertently prompting Claudius's invasion decision. Yet his story has long been overshadowed by Boudicca's fame.

17:28

When the father died, fraternal conflict ignited the crisis

After Cunobelinus died, a succession vacuum opened: by custom the heir needed Rome's approval to claim the title Rex, but Rome remained silent. The eldest son Togodumnus seemed Rome's preferred legitimate heir, while his brother Caratacus appeared an ambitious usurper unwilling to accept subordination. Civil war erupted between them, plunging southern Britain into chaos—one of Rome's direct justifications for invasion.

— Miles Russell
19:35

Claudius needed a war to establish military credentials

Claudius had been excluded from power for years due to physical or mental defects, and became emperor only by accident when Caligula was assassinated—with no military achievements to his name. Caesar twice set foot in Britain but never conquered it, yet was deified; if Claudius could truly subdue Britain, he would surpass Caesar himself. Britain's internal chaos at this moment gave him a near-zero-risk opportunity to gild his resume.

— Miles Russell
34:24

The Thames battle was actually fraternal combat

Cassius Dio records that Roman forces sent ‘Celts’ to cross the river first; scholars typically interpret this as Gallic auxiliary troops recruited by Rome. But Miles argues it actually refers to pro-Roman Britons—specifically Togodumnus's forces, who knew the river and found the crossing point for the Romans. In other words, the one who helped Rome cross the Thames and broke Caratacus's defenses from behind was his own brother.

— Miles Russell
48:45

Scorched-earth guerrilla war: not victory but exhaustion

After withdrawing to Wales, Caratacus convinced the local Silures tribe to abandon pitched battle, instead burning their own crops and villages and ambushing Roman supply lines. This was no heroic strategy, but the only way to make the legions pay. His real goal was to raise the cost of occupation until Rome deemed it not worth the effort, forcing Claudius or his successor to abandon Britain voluntarily.

— Miles Russell
54:56

The Welsh battle: fortified walls, but retreat when losing

In this final battle, Caratacus built successive stone walls on a hillside, funneling Roman forces into ever-narrower channels—tactics reminiscent of Teutoburg Forest. But when the Romans formed testudo, withstood the barrage, and began advancing uphill, he ordered a full retreat rather than fight to the death—a decision that seemed cowardly to tribal warriors who prized personal valor. He escaped; his wife and daughters were captured.

— Miles Russell
1:00:07

The Brigantes queen handed him over to Rome

Caratacus sought to open a third front among the Brigantes tribe in Yorkshire, but Queen Cartimandua was already Rome's ally, with Roman trade goods flowing freely into her territory. For her tribe's survival, she chose to hand Caratacus over directly to the Roman governor. Miles argues she shouldn't be dismissed as a traitor—even Boudicca later was initially Rome's ally; each tribe prioritized its own survival over any pan-British identity.

— Miles Russell
1:03:15

One speech transformed him from condemned to honored guest

By custom, captured barbarian leaders were either strangled after the triumph or thrown into the arena—Vercingetorix was the cautionary tale. But Claudius orchestrated the entire ceremony: Caratacus was led before the crowd and delivered a speech, asking the Romans why they envied people living in mud huts, and arguing that his death served no one, while clemency would show Claudius surpassing Caesar himself. He was freed on the spot, struck of his chains, though confined to Rome for life.

Glossary

Testudo
A Roman defensive formation where soldiers locked their shields to repel projectiles and arrows.
Rex
A title appearing on Iron Age British coins, denoting a ruler officially recognized by Rome.
Triumph
A Roman military parade held after a general's victory, where captured enemies were often executed at its conclusion.
