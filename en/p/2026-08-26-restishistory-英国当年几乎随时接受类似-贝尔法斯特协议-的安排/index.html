Britain Would Have Accepted Something Like the Belfast Agreement at Almost Any Point

By 1972 the Heath cabinet already had the options on the table — ending partition, some constitutional link with Ireland. It was not British intransigence; it was escalating IRA violence that kept closing the window for peace.

This episode places Bloody Sunday inside the full political spectrum that ran from withdrawal to negotiation, correcting the simplified story of deliberate British repression — the key to understanding the Northern Ireland conflict.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:29

Even Ireland's own government did not want Britain to withdraw

The first option facing the Heath government was withdrawal. But the large majority of people in Northern Ireland wanted to remain in the United Kingdom, and a withdrawal would have left the Protestant community feeling betrayed — risking civil war and sending a flood of Protestant refugees into mainland Britain. In 1975 the Irish government went so far as to privately approach Kissinger, asking the United States to press Britain not to withdraw. Even Ireland feared it could not absorb the consequences if Britain simply walked away.

18:37

The commander knew in advance that unarmed people would be shot

In December 1971 Ford, the newly appointed commander of British forces in Northern Ireland, took charge in Derry. He warned in writing that if troops went into the no-go areas to clear them, so-called unarmed rioters and even teenagers would inevitably be shot — the risk of casualties was high. He knew what would follow and still argued for the hard line. That set the command-level groundwork for Bloody Sunday.

29:57

Most of the dead were shot in the back — this was not a firefight

On the day of the march, the Paras were authorised to enter the no-go area to arrest rioters, but the orders were ignored. They charged into the Bogside and fired more than 100 rounds into the crowd, killing 13 people, six of them only 17 years old, most of them shot in the back. What the command level authorised as "punitive violence" became indiscriminate shooting on the ground.

44:01

The claim that Britain obstructed the peace process is simply wrong

In February 1972 the Heath cabinet discussed the future of Northern Ireland with every option on the table: redrawing the border, letting Catholic areas join the Republic, holding a referendum on ending partition, or keeping Northern Ireland nominally within the United Kingdom while giving it some constitutional link to Ireland. The hosts stress that at any point after 1970 Britain would have accepted something along the lines of the Belfast Agreement — which refutes the common claim that Britain stubbornly obstructed the peace process.

47:23

The IRA's bombs killed Catholics first

The Provisional IRA attacked the Abercorn restaurant in central Belfast. The restaurant was Catholic-owned, though the bar upstairs was popular with soldiers. The explosion killed two Catholic women in their twenties and injured 140 people. One doctor, after treating the wounded through the night, was told that one of the dead was his daughter Janet.

— Dominic
51:43

One bomb pushed London into issuing an ultimatum

On 20 March the IRA detonated a bomb on Donegall Street in central Belfast, killing seven people, including two policemen and three refuse collectors. The warning call came too late, and it named the wrong street. Two days later Faulkner was summoned to London, where Heath delivered an ultimatum: either accept that Britain would take over law and order, abolish internment, hold a border referendum and bring moderate nationalists into the government — or Stormont would be shut down and direct rule imposed.

— Dominic
1:04:48

The IRA sat down to talk because it believed it was winning

Through the spring and summer of 1972 the IRA thought victory was within reach and was willing to negotiate. On 13 June, at a press conference in Derry, the IRA offered to declare a ceasefire if Britain agreed to talks. Whitelaw privately accepted two conditions: special category status for IRA prisoners, and the release of an interned 23-year-old named Gerry Adams. British officials then met IRA representatives in secret and agreed a temporary ceasefire — a sign that both sides believed at the time that a deal was possible.

— Tom
1:21:13

The church told a grieving minister to stop talking about peace

Reverend Parker founded Witness for Peace in memory of his son, holding ceremonies outside Belfast City Hall and the GPO in Dublin and planting a white cross for each person killed. But his bishop disliked his calls for peace and told him to stop talking about it, saying it would alienate parishioners, and confined his peace work to his days off. In 1975 the Parkers emigrated permanently to Canada.

Glossary

Direct Rule
The British government taking over Northern Ireland's devolved powers, temporarily or long-term, and governing it directly from London.
Special Category Status
Treating prisoners as combatants rather than ordinary criminals; the IRA read it as political recognition.
Provisional IRA
The hardline faction that split from the IRA in 1970 and was the main armed force during the Bloody Sunday period.
