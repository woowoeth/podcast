Britain Was Almost Always Ready to Accept Something Like the Belfast Agreement

By 1972, Heath's cabinet had options on the table—ending partition, a constitutional link with Ireland. It wasn't British intransigence; it was IRA violence that kept closing the window for peace.

This episode places Bloody Sunday within the full political spectrum from withdrawal to negotiation, correcting the simplified narrative of deliberate British repression. It's a key to understanding the Northern Ireland conflict.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:29

Even Ireland didn't want Britain to withdraw

The first option facing Heath's government was withdrawal. But the vast majority of Northern Irish wanted to remain in the UK; withdrawal would make the Protestant community feel betrayed, risking civil war and a flood of Protestant refugees into mainland Britain. In 1975, the Irish government even privately contacted Kissinger to ask the US to pressure Britain not to withdraw—even Ireland feared it couldn't cope if Britain washed its hands.

18:37

Commanders knew beforehand they'd kill unarmed people

In December 1971, the new British army commander in Northern Ireland, Ford, took charge in Derry. In a letter, he warned that if they went into the no-go areas to clear them, the so-called unarmed hooligans, even teenagers, would inevitably be shot—casualties would be high. He knew the consequences, yet still argued for toughness. This set the command-level stage for Bloody Sunday.

29:57

Most were shot in the back—this wasn't a firefight

On the day of the march, the Parachute Regiment was authorized to enter the no-go area to arrest rioters, but the order was ignored. They charged into Bogside, firing over 100 rounds into the crowd, killing 13 people, six of whom were just 17 years old; most were shot in the back. The command's authorization of 'punitive violence' turned into indiscriminate killing at the ground level.

44:01

British intransigence blocking peace is a misreading

In February 1972, Heath's cabinet discussed Northern Ireland's future with all options on the table: redrawing the border, letting Catholic areas join the Republic, a referendum on ending partition, or keeping Northern Ireland nominally in the UK but with some constitutional link to Ireland. The host stresses that Britain after 1970 was almost always ready to accept something like the Belfast Agreement—this rebuts the common claim that British stubbornness blocked the peace process.

47:23

IRA bombs killed Catholics first

The Provisional IRA attacked the Abercorn Restaurant in central Belfast, which was owned by Catholics, but the upstairs bar was popular with soldiers. The blast killed two Catholic women in their twenties and wounded 140. A doctor spent the night treating the wounded, only to be told that one of the dead was his daughter, Jeanette.

— Dominic
51:43

One bomb forced London's ultimatum

On March 20, the IRA detonated a bomb on Donegall Street in central Belfast, killing seven people, including two policemen and three cleaners. The warning call came too late and gave the wrong street name. Two days later, Faulkner was summoned to London, where Heath issued an ultimatum: either accept Britain taking over law and order, ending internment, a border referendum, and inviting moderate nationalists into the executive; or close the Northern Ireland parliament and impose direct rule.

— Dominic
1:04:48

The IRA sat down because it thought it was winning

In spring and summer 1972, the IRA believed victory was at hand and was willing to negotiate. On June 13, at a press conference in Derry, the IRA proposed a ceasefire if Britain agreed to talks. Whitelaw privately agreed to two conditions: granting IRA prisoners special category status and releasing 23-year-old Gerry Adams from detention. British officials then met secretly with IRA representatives and reached a temporary ceasefire—showing both sides thought a deal was possible.

— Tom
1:21:13

The church told a priest to stop talking about peace

Reverend Parker founded 'Witness for Peace' in memory of his son, holding ceremonies outside Belfast City Hall and the GPO in Dublin, planting a white cross for each victim. But his bishop disliked his calls for peace, telling him to stop talking about peace because it alienated parishioners, and restricted his peace activities to his days off. In 1975, the Parkers emigrated permanently to Canada.

Glossary

Direct Rule
The British government temporarily or permanently takes over Northern Ireland's devolved powers, governing directly from London.
Special Category Status
Treatment of prisoners as military rather than ordinary criminals; the IRA saw it as political recognition.
Provisional IRA
The hardline faction that split from the IRA in 1970; the main armed force during Bloody Sunday.
