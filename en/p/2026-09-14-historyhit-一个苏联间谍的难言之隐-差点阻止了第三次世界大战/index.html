A Soviet Spy's Secret Shame Nearly Stopped World War III

In 1981, a senior KGB officer in Tehran walked into MI6 and offered to spy — not for money or ideology, but because he believed the West could cure his erectile dysfunction. The intelligence he handed over led the US and Britain to give the Soviets' coup plan to the Iranians.

0:00

A spy story that has never been told

Ben Macintyre's new book Redwood tells a Cold War story that had never been made public before: in 1981, a senior KGB officer in Tehran, Vladimir Kuzichkin, walked into MI6 and offered to spy, becoming a double agent codenamed "Redwood." The intelligence he provided revealed that the Soviets were planning a coup through the Iranian communist Tudeh Party to overthrow Khomeini's government, after which the Red Army would cross into Iran from Afghanistan, Azerbaijan and Turkmenistan. The Pentagon's response plan was a nuclear strike — because conventional forces were not enough to stop a full Soviet invasion. What makes the story so strange is that Kuzichkin's motive for defecting was intensely personal: he suffered from erectile dysfunction and believed Western medicine could cure him.

3:01

1981 came closer to nuclear war than the Cuban Missile Crisis

Ben Macintyre points out that 1981 was one of the closest moments of nuclear confrontation in the Cold War, second only to the Cuban Missile Crisis. After the Iranian Revolution, every faction — nationalists, liberals, communists and Islamists — was fighting for power, and Tehran became a "seething cauldron of spies." The CIA, MI6 and the KGB all poured in at once. The Soviets had just invaded Afghanistan and were making good progress, while the US was desperate to re-establish a foothold in the Middle East. If the Soviets took Iran, they could control warm-water ports and oil fields and win the Cold War. So the Pentagon was already drawing up nuclear response plans, including using portable nuclear weapons to blow up mountain ranges and block Soviet armored columns.

— Ben McIntyre
8:18

The CIA was blind in Tehran; MI6 broke the rules and kept a man there

After the Iran hostage crisis, the CIA was thrown out of Tehran, had no human intelligence in Iran at all, and knew nothing about a possible Soviet coup. The only service still capable of operating was MI6, but Britain's condition for maintaining a diplomatic mission in 1981 was "no spying of any kind." MI6 nevertheless sent Ian McCready in under commercial counselor cover — a huge gamble by the British government. McCready's instructions were "do nothing, recruit no one, keep a low profile" — because if he were caught, the Iranians would take the entire embassy hostage. He could only communicate with London using a World War II-era cipher machine called RockX, which was extremely cumbersome to operate and of which only two existed in the world.

— Ben McIntyre
12:29

His motive for defecting was impotence, not money or ideology

Kuzichkin was a senior officer at the KGB residency in Tehran, in charge of illegal spy programs across the entire Middle East, and the only defector who understood how the illegal system worked. He walked into MI6 on his own and offered to spy for the West. The intelligence world has an acronym, MICE — money, ideology, coercion, ego — but Kuzichkin fit none of the categories. He was partly driven by alienation from the regime, but the root cause was this: he suffered from erectile dysfunction and believed the West had a drug that could cure it. He had just married, loved his wife deeply, and could not reveal the secret to his KGB colleagues or to Soviet doctors. McCready did not mock him or exploit him; he took him with extreme seriousness, even sending MI6 headquarters an unprecedented telex: "Help. What do I do with a spy who has erectile dysfunction?"

— Ben McIntyre
21:52

He photographed every KGB file in the Middle East

For nearly a year, McCready ran Kuzichkin with old-fashioned tradecraft: dead drops, brush contacts, a miniature camera. He gave Kuzichkin a Minox miniature camera to carry into the office every day and photograph every document he could get his hands on. In the end the Redwood archive filled four truckloads. MI6 gave him the codename "Redwood" — because he was six foot four, and because they could not resist the pun: Red for communism, Wood for his "problem." Kuzichkin's core intelligence was this: the Soviets were planning a coup through the Tudeh Party, the money and weapons were already in place, the Tudeh had a secret military underground, and Kuzichkin himself was the coordinator between the KGB and the Tudeh.

— Ben McIntyre
29:05

The US and Britain handed the coup plan to the Iranians

After Kuzichkin escaped to Britain, MI6 debated internally whether to use the intelligence. Chris Cohn, who ran the operations directorate and later became chief of MI6, argued it had to be used. He said that if they did not use it, intelligence about a Tudeh communist coup was meaningless. So MI6 sent Redwood to Islamabad, Pakistan, under diplomatic cover, to meet Iranian intelligence. The meeting lasted three days, Kuzichkin laid everything out, and MI6 handed the Iranians all four truckloads of intelligence. It was also an attempt to open a secret channel to Iran at a time when Tehran's relations with the West were dire. It was deeply illegal, and if it had come out it would have been a huge scandal — the British and American governments acting to protect the Islamic Revolution.

— Ben McIntyre
31:05

Iran used the intelligence to execute eight thousand communists

Once the Revolutionary Guard had the intelligence, within two months it arrested eight thousand Iranian communists, about half of whom were executed. This became a cornerstone of Iran's modern theocratic state. From that moment on, Khomeini tolerated no opposition at all; everyone was purged, including people already in prison, and it turned into a massacre. It also reinforced two convictions of the Islamic Revolution: first, that all opposition must be destroyed, because fifth columns were everywhere; second, that whatever happened, the British were behind it. MI6 and the CIA inadvertently consolidated the Iranian regime and created a monster — almost an exact replay of Afghanistan, where money poured in to Islamic guerrillas fighting the Soviets and ultimately produced al-Qaeda, ISIS and 9/11.

— Ben McIntyre
38:11

History is often driven by perceptions, not facts

Ben Macintyre stresses that it cannot be known whether the Soviets would actually have carried out the coup, but everyone involved believed they would. McCready believed it, Kuzichkin believed it, and MI6, the CIA, the KGB and the Tudeh themselves all believed it. That was enough — nuclear war can break out on the basis of perception rather than fact, and the Cuban Missile Crisis was the same. Would Khrushchev really have used those weapons? We do not know, but everyone believed he might. The story was never told because everyone involved did not want it told: the Iranians did not, because it meant the West had outsmarted them again; the KGB did not, because it was a humiliation — they had failed to spot the defector, 18 Soviet diplomats were expelled from Iran as a result, the entire Tudeh operation was dismantled, and agent networks across the Middle East and even in Canada were exposed.

Glossary

MICE
The classic acronym for the four motives behind spy recruitment: Money, Ideology, Coercion, Ego.
RockX
A British cipher communications device used in World War II, cumbersome to operate, of which only two existed worldwide.
Minox
A miniature camera commonly used by spies during the Cold War to photograph documents.
Tudeh Party
The Iranian communist party, still tolerated by Khomeini's government in 1981 before being purged on a massive scale.
brush contact
A spycraft technique in which two people exchange intelligence or items while quickly passing each other in a public place.
dead drop
A secret location used by spies to pass intelligence, where the two sides never appear at the same time.
