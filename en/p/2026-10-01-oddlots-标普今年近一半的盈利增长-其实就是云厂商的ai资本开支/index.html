Half the S&P's Earnings Gains This Year Are Just Cloud Giants' AI Spending

Goldman Sachs calculates that roughly half of the S&P 500's earnings growth this year comes directly from cloud giants' AI capital spending; the market is betting not on today's sales, but on whether this money converts to revenue in three years.

4:01

Breadth collapse mirrors the internet bust, but does not reliably predict tops

The S&P 500 is only 0.5% from all-time highs, yet just 51.2% of its constituents trade above their 200-day moving average—the last time the market was this close to records with so few stocks participating was the day after the internet bubble burst. But Luke counters with precedent: in 1998, breadth was equally dire, yet the S&P went on to gain for two more years. Breadth alone does not determine tops.

— Luke Kawa
8:07

Consumer stocks are now the proxy for betting against AI exuberance

Nike crashed from $180 in 2021 to $35; McDonald's, Walmart, and Costco face similar valuation compression. Luke's theory: these stocks became proxies for shorting software companies expected to be disrupted by AI. Walmart's steady monthly throughput resembles subscription ARR; Costco's membership fee is essentially recurring revenue. When AI stocks surge, the market needs something to punish.

— Luke Kawa
9:08

Half of S&P earnings growth this year is cloud capex itself, not its monetization

Goldman Sachs estimates that roughly half of the S&P's earnings growth this year comes directly from cloud giants' AI capex spending. Valuations now hinge not on next year's sales expectations but on three-year-ahead sales. Economist Hanno Lustig goes further: to fully justify this spending, a handful of companies must eventually capture 9% of GDP in revenue—both Luke and Joe call this absurd. Nvidia has begun separately listing sovereign AI and robotics revenue streams in its earnings to argue the addressable market is broader than cloud capex alone.

— Luke Kawa
15:17

Rising bond yields reflect surging growth, not uncontrolled inflation

Luke attributes the 10-year Treasury yield breaking 5% primarily to strong global growth pushing real rates higher, not inflation expectations spiraling. Breakeven inflation rates remain stable. This surprises him: the Fed has failed to keep inflation near its target for years, so you'd expect markets to discount central-bank credibility. Yet breakeven rates show no such skepticism.

— Luke Kawa
20:18

Markets have moved too fast for shorts to build effective hedges

Luke sums up last year as TINA (There Is No Alternative); this year, it's prices and earnings rising too fast to hedge effectively. AI is compressing the timeline of economic events; add AI agents trading while humans sleep, and the market operates at an unprecedented pace. Robinhood retail net-buy data show peaks in June (around Micron earnings) and October last year (around Palantir, bitcoin, quantum compute hype), with September recovering to 89% of June's peak.

— Luke Kawa
29:30

Negative free cash flow at giants demands increasingly creative financing tactics

Luke argues that once cloud giants' free cash flow turns negative, two paths remain: slow AI buildout, or get creative with financing. The latter is happening. He links this to Minsky moments: as long as the debt looks safe (cloud balance sheets, federal deficits), valuations hold. But that's exactly how "safe" tips into "unsafe."

— Luke Kawa
32:00

This AI expansion is built on debt, not on AI-generated cash

Luke borrows from Wall Street Journal's Greg Ip: past internet giants funded expansion with their own cash machines—Facebook's ads, Google's search. This time is different. Oracle has no pure cash cow; it must borrow. Credit default swaps keep rising. Nvidia even steps in to boost credit for peers, trying to make all AI borrowers look equally safe.

— Luke Kawa
36:39

AI has replaced real estate as the driver of economic cycles

Luke notes that wealth gains from equities now exceed those from real estate, a watershed moment. Real estate defined the business cycle because HELOCs and wealth effects flowed into consumption; the same logic now applies to AI—capex surges and wealth-effect surges emanate from here. He calls this abnormal: ultimately, downstream consumers must buy or the cycle breaks.

— Luke Kawa

Glossary

TINA (There Is No Alternative)
Investment stance that stocks are the only viable asset class with acceptable returns
Breadth / Market breadth
Metric measuring how many individual stocks participate in an overall market rise
Negative FCF (Free cash flow)
Operating cash flow minus capex is negative; growth relies on external financing
CDS (Credit default swap)
Derivative contract that prices the risk of corporate bond default
Minsky moment
Sudden reversal of debt-fueled boom, triggering a wave of selling and credit unwinding
Credit wrap
Strong-credit party guarantees debt of weaker party to reduce perceived default risk
