AI Designing Chips Is Real; Verification Is Still the Number One Bottleneck

AI can already write RTL autonomously, but the real choke point in the entire chip pipeline is verification: it eats over half the development time, yet AI cannot answer "what should be tested." OpenAI's twenty-person team can shorten the front end, but the back end still has to be left to humans.

The author uses what he saw firsthand and the words of frontline engineers to pull "AI builds chips" out of clickbait: which parts are prototypes, which can go into production, and where the moat actually is.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:10

K3 has only a front end, no back end

The source material for "AI tapes out in 48 hours" is a small inference accelerator called Nano KPU that Kimi announced: 238,000 parameters, about 1.46 million standard cells, 0.277MB SRAM, claimed to close timing at 100 MHz within 4 square millimeters with decode throughput of 8,700 tokens/s. But the host looked closely: this batch of RTL only had functional verification, with no cell placement, routing, physical verification, or sign-off; the so-called "timing closure" relies on estimates, 100 MHz is not impressive in a 45nm open-source library where a human using the same library can hit 20-450 MHz, and commercial processes can reach 1-3 GHz. K3's goal is not to build a good chip but to prove an agent can autonomously reach functional completeness.

6:10

AI writing RTL is not news at all

Many people think AI writing chips only started with K3, but back in 2020 someone at NYU used GPT-2 to generate beginner-level RTL, with reports citing 94.8% accuracy; the now-defunct eFabless ran an AI design competition, and a design Will Salcedo generated with GPT-4 actually went to tapeout, one of the earliest LLM-designed tapeouts the host knows of. "AI writes RTL" is not itself the innovation; what actually changed is that an agent can run unattended from start to functional completeness — the autonomy of the flow has been forged.

8:12

The agent's future is being strapped to the toolchain

While fully autonomous design is still nowhere in sight, the real product form is wrapping an LLM around the EDA toolchain: the LLM directs deterministic EDA tools through interfaces like MCP and APIs, just as Claude Code lets a model call computer tools. The long-term goal of Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens is to open up their own stacks and hand data feedback and control to agents that can run for hours or even overnight. Siemens played a demo at Semicon Taiwan: having an agent drop hundreds of standard cells one by one into a simulator to measure load, speed, and power for later designs to look up. Redoing this tedious work for every process node change is exactly the kind of thing to hand to AI before leaving the office.

10:15

Even the best results can't do without a human in the loop

The post by former xAI engineer Christina Lee is, in the host's eyes, the most convincing breakdown. Her meta-harness has an inner and outer layer: the inner layer has the model call EDA tools to write RTL, synthesize, place and route, and verify; the outer layer has the model review what was right and wrong in the previous round, propose improvements, and run again. The first stage only did a MAC multiply-accumulate array, iterating over multiple rounds to 900 MHz to 1 GHz; the second stage connected the MAC to SRAM and other modules, and complexity immediately rose. Note that the human always holds the steering wheel: the so-called goal strings were likely provided as reference by the outer model, but the filtering and final confirmation power rests with the human. AI is not the boss, it is the co-pilot.

13:17

What's truly scary about Jalapeno is the 20-person team

OpenAI's Jalapeno demo had many people fixating on "only 9 months from RTL to tapeout." The host reminds us: Synopsys had previously said on an unrelated occasion that using their tools without AI also takes about 9 to 12 months; the chip itself is not complex, and the starting point is unclear — the architecture concept only began in October 2024. What really stands out is the team size: only 20 to 30 people stood up when the talk ended. And this group's token consumption ranked near the top of OpenAI's internal leaderboard year-round, with RTL still being changed the day before freeze. If such a small team can pull it off, future chip roles will need an order of magnitude fewer people, which may be exactly why they don't emphasize it in the slides.

16:20

Verification is the real bottleneck in AI chip design

The whole industry talks up the front-end RTL while glossing over the back end: OpenAI's launch slides dwell a lot on spec and RTL, and the host guesses the back end was probably handled by partner Broadcom, thanked only once on slide 34; Lee also handed the back end to Yosys/OpenLane. The mechanistic asymmetry is this: PPA (power/performance/area) optimization is a closed loop — generate, verify, measure PPA — and AI has a clear reward signal; verification does not, because a test passing or failing cannot tell AI how far it is from full coverage, and "what should be tested" still can only come from humans. A chip bug cannot be changed once taped out, so seeing a defect the first time you lay eyes on the TSMC silicon is the most crushing thing. This explains why verification can eat 50 to 70% of development time, and echoes Shibo Chen's line that "AI does the design, humans do the verification" is what gives people confidence.

17:22

The front-end wrapper's biggest enemy is OpenAI

A large number of startups have sprung up in Silicon Valley wrapping frontier models with proprietary knowledge to write RTL. The risk the host sees is this: Anthropic and OpenAI themselves both own the models and are building chips, and the moment they decide to open up the models used for chips, the front-end wrapper's differentiation is gone. So he prefers to look at teams moving toward the back end and verification, where end-to-end is where the moat is. How fast is this field moving? Just as he was writing, Architect Labs announced that the Redwood chip was produced and verified entirely by AI, with the spec written only by two architects; even TSMC's 3D IC talk used "agentic AI." The host worries his own judgment will be overturned by new news in a week or two.

Glossary

EDA / Electronic Design Automation
The software tools needed to design chips, dominated by a handful of vendors such as Synopsys and Cadence.
RTL / Register Transfer Level
The hardware language layer that describes data flow and logic in Verilog/VHDL and the like; it is the chip's "code."
PDK / Process Design Kit
A confidential process parameter package maintained jointly by foundries and EDA vendors, tightly protected under NDA.
PPA / Power, Performance, Area
The three major metrics for evaluating chip design quality, directly usable for closed-loop optimization.
tapeout
Handing layer upon layer of verified design data to the foundry to manufacture a real chip.
