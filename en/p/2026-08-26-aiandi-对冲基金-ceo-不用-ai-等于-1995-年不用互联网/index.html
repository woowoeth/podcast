AI Isn't a Personal Tool — It's an Organizational Change Leaders Must Model Themselves

AI isn't a personal tool but an organizational change leaders must personally model. Walleye has everyone using AI daily and recording every meeting, building first-mover advantage through operating leverage and a data lake.

6:42

The turning point was one person's demo, not a strategy manifesto

In March 2023, a TMT analyst used the then-newly-emerged GPT-3 to build a workflow that made him far more efficient as an analyst — possibly even able to replace himself. Will was initially quite skeptical, because this wasn't the typical approach; but after seeing the demo he was convinced ‘this is the direction’. Walleye immediately launched an internal AI project, first trying agent-assisted analysis in fundamental stock picking. This example shows that the real turning point isn't a grand manifesto, but a person who understands the business applying a new tool to their own role, and the demonstration effect that produces.

— Will
14:19

Not using AI is like refusing the internet in 1995

Will sent the whole company an email titled ‘AI at Walleye’, opening with ‘I wrote this email with ChatGPT, you should too, and be proud of it’; ‘not using these tools is like refusing to use the internet in 1995 because it wasn't perfect’. He directly defines AI not as cheating but as a magic potion that makes everyone 20% smarter. The email also stipulated: everyone who writes, researches, analyzes, builds decks, handles data, or works with their thinking must use AI every day; managers must first become proficient themselves, then push their teams, because this is part of their job.

— Will
18:58

Using AI as cheating only holds in academia

Will observed many people secretly using ChatGPT to write emails, then pretending they polished it themselves — he considers this foolish self-deception. He quotes Jim Collins: AI is about ‘building clocks, not telling time’: it frees people from mechanical writing so you can think about the next level of the task. He explicitly distinguishes academia from business: using AI is cheating in academia, but in the business world results are what matter. In the future, even if you don't manage any humans, you'll manage a batch of AI employees, and that itself is a skill. So the point isn't shame, it's operating leverage.

— Will
26:04

AI outsources rhetoric, not thinking

The host worried that LLM ghostwriting would make thinking shallower. Will says the key is distinguishing ‘concept’ from ‘language syntax’: in the past, a lot of writing time went into decorations like avoiding ending on a preposition or making sentences sound smarter, and these are exactly what AI can replace. His approach is to first type out the key points and logic himself, making clear ‘what concept I want to convey’, then let the model expand it in his voice. A task that takes 15 minutes now used to take four or five hours. The time saved isn't for clocking off early, but for thinking more deeply, because AI won't think for you — it just outsources the rhetoric.

— Will
35:57

A hedge fund must become a collective, not a group of people

Walleye's long-term goal is to become a ‘collective’: pour all the firm's information (emails, Slack, call recordings, reports) into a data lake, then use AI to extract insights. The most mature case is the internal product Current: it structures analyst notes, sell-side reports, PDFs and earnings call transcripts, letting every PM process all the text in real time during earnings season. Will says more than 50 external institutions have already applied to join the beta test. What machines can do now isn't just summarization — they read, listen to voice, and do second- and third-order processing, which is replacing humans' information synthesis work.

— Will
39:35

World-class companies will still need tens of thousands of employees — mostly machines

Will uses the upheaval between the American Civil War and World War I as an analogy: railroads and the transatlantic cable transformed the world completely within a few decades, and many people went from obsolete skills to being displaced within their own lifetimes — and this round will be faster. His judgment about the future: world-class companies will still need tens of thousands of employees, but a large portion of those employees will be machines, and the number of humans will be far smaller. He remains cautiously optimistic: just as cowboys were eventually subsumed by barbed wire and the ‘civilization’ brought by railroads, technological inflection points always meet resistance from the old order, but what leaders need to do isn't defend, it's lead the embrace of change.

— Will
49:11

Human intuition is also a neural network, just unexplainable

Will points out that quant investing has been at ‘full speed’ since at least the early 1990s: models search for statistical structure in stock prices and information, and much of that structure humans can no longer understand, just like the nonlinear relationships in LLMs. But he still believes human investors will retain an edge in new situations where ‘the law of large numbers doesn't apply’, because machines haven't seen all precedents, and humans are better at handling ambiguous situations. He goes further, comparing human intuition to a neural network: both are high-dimensional judgments based on massive experience, impossible to explain precisely but trustworthy. AI's role isn't to replace intuition, but to act like a coach pointing out ‘you didn't notice these two moves are actually connected’.

— Will
57:52

Never keeping a journal was never laziness — it was too time-consuming

Will journals with AI: each day he covers three sections — family, work, health — says a few key points first, and AI fills it out in his voice, all in under a minute. The reason he started: in finance he could look back at daily P&L, but couldn't remember what he was thinking at the time, so he began recording. He thinks most people never journal simply because it used to take too much time, and AI brings the time cost down to near zero. This is also his ‘if you can't measure it, you can't manage it’ principle in practice: he logs every workout and body metric, watching long-term trends. His two most important first principles are ‘the power of incentives’ and ‘intellectual honesty’ — the former for understanding the motivations of people and organizations, the latter for facing real decisions.

— Will
