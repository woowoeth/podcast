ECT Is Not Torture: 70% Respond, 60% Remit

ECT brings about response in roughly 70% of patients and wipes out symptoms in 60%; it is not the electrocution of One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest but the oldest somatic treatment to survive the slaughterhouse, the asylum and the anti-psychiatry movement.

Historical narratives tend to two extremes: barbaric torture or miracle cure. This episode puts efficacy, abuse, media amplification and patient testimony side by side, so you can see where the stigma comes from and why it is not the whole picture today.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:03

The heaviest side effect is not memory loss, it is stigma

The episode opens with the nurse Elan telling her own story: depressed since childhood, she chose ECT after medication failed. She says that after three treatments, colour seemed turned up in saturation and the world became approachable again. The side effects are real: for days after treatment she could not summon nouns, got lost in familiar neighbourhoods, lost words mid-conversation; remote memories from before treatment were still there, but felt like a room that had been rearranged. She states plainly that the heaviest side effect is the stigma around ECT — she worried that sharing her experience would change how people saw her, and she believes that stigma is exactly why doctors did not recommend ECT to her sooner.

7:53

Fear of ECT is not innate, it was taught by film and television

A global survey of film and TV published in 2016 found that 81% of movies and 72% of television shows portrayed ECT inaccurately and negatively. In the most famous example, One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, Jack Nicholson is strapped down and shocked while fully conscious, in a scene that borders on torture. A few positive depictions exist, such as Homeland and Call the Midwife, but they are rare. The hosts' judgment: public fear of ECT did not come from nowhere — it was a media stigma reinforced over decades, and it makes people unwilling even to consider ECT.

13:07

ECT is the most effective intervention psychiatry has

ECT treats severe mental illness such as treatment-resistant depression by passing electric current through the brain to induce a seizure; today it must be given under anaesthesia and muscle relaxation, with strict informed consent. Two figures are routinely overlooked: about 70% of people respond and about 60% achieve clinical remission, making it one of the most effective interventions in psychiatry. Set against the patient feedback the show saw when it covered SSRIs, it is especially effective in treatment-resistant cases where antidepressants alone have failed.

17:09

Electricity was only the tool; the target was always the seizure

The shock therapies that appeared in the 1920s had nothing to do with electricity at first: insulin coma therapy put patients into hypoglycaemic coma and convulsions, and Metrosol chemical shock therapy used a drug to induce seizures. Doctors persisted down this road because of an observation at the time that epilepsy and schizophrenia seemed rarely to coexist, and because some catatonic patients improved after a spontaneous seizure — so they treated the seizure itself as the key therapeutic ingredient. Cerletti later wanted a safer, more tolerable way to induce one, which is what turned him to electricity. Electricity was only the tool; the target was the seizure.

22:14

ECT's technical starting point was a pig in a slaughterhouse

Cerletti's starting point was the slaughterhouse: workers would shock pigs to stun them, and some pigs seized after the shock. In April 1938, a man on the streets of Rome, incoherent in speech and brought to hospital by police, became the first human subject. There was no anaesthesia and no muscle relaxant, and the first two shocks failed to induce a seizure. After the third, at a higher dose, a grand mal seizure appeared; the patient briefly stopped breathing and turned cyanotic, and only after forty-five seconds drew a long breath and returned to normal. Cerletti later reported 36 cases: 11 complete remissions, 20 improvements, 5 unchanged.

31:26

It spread so fast because there was no informed consent

By 1941, only three years after the first human trial, 42% of psychiatric hospitals in the United States had an ECT machine. Several reasons explain the speed: it was seen as an improved version of existing shock therapies and the barrier to entry was low; doctors had run out of options for chronic, severely ill patients; ECT was not the only therapy but often part of a recovery path alongside psychotherapy; early patient reports were broadly positive; and at the time there was no informed consent and no patient protections requiring an extended trial period. That last point is precisely what sowed the seeds of later general distrust of psychiatry.

36:34

Using ECT as punishment was not a movie invention

The negative history is not a movie invention: some institutions used ECT to treat homosexuality or hysteria; a 1940s account admits that the amnesia and disorientation caused by shock could quiet patients and keep them from disrupting the ward. This kind of abuse happened mostly in institutions with fewer resources and more marginalised patients — it was not what every hospital did, but when it was exposed it caused enormous public outrage. The anti-psychiatry movement then went on to call mental illness itself a form of social control. The hosts do not excuse these acts; they only remind listeners that at the time there were no effective drugs, wards were severely overcrowded, and doctors and families alike were mired in despair.

42:45

Anti-psychiatry ended up forcing ECT to produce its evidence

The anti-psychiatry movement drove ECT use down sharply in the 1960s and 70s, but it also forced the treatment to submit to stricter scrutiny and randomised controlled trials. The result is that it is the only major somatic treatment to survive from before psychopharmacology and still stand up to testing. After the 1980s, public accounts by Dick Cavett, Carrie Fisher and the psychologist Norman Endler brought the positive narrative back. Memory loss remains a genuine controversy: studies say long-term effects are rare, but some patients believe their experience was not adequately captured by the measurement tools. The experience cannot be reduced to one star or five stars — it should be neither mythologised nor stigmatised.

Glossary

electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)
Passing electric current through the head of an anaesthetised patient to induce a seizure, used for severe mental illness such as treatment-resistant depression.
somatic therapy
Treatment that acts on the body to change mental state, as distinct from psychotherapy; ECT belongs to this category.
insulin coma therapy
An early shock therapy that gave patients large doses of insulin to induce hypoglycaemic coma and convulsions; dangerous and potentially fatal.
Metrosol shock therapy
A chemical shock therapy that used the drug Metrosol to induce seizures; a poor experience with slow recovery, later replaced by ECT.
anti-psychiatry movement
A social current that emerged in the 1960s and 70s, arguing that mental illness is not real and that psychiatry is a form of social control.
