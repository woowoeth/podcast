The Illuminati existed but accomplished nothing: the government that banned it created the conspiracy-theory myth

After the Bavarian government banned the Illuminati and published all seized documents, this internally fractious and historically ineffectual society paradoxically became the template for conspiracy theories for two centuries.

The Illuminati's birth moment is exactly documented: May 1, 1776

The Illuminati's origins are precisely traceable: May 1, 1776, when Adam Weishaupt, a law professor at the University of Ingolstadt in Bavaria, assembled four of his most trusted students into a secret society. With no publishing culture, no representative politics, and no Paris-style salons in the region, this secret society became Weishaupt's only tool to spread Enlightenment ideas: recruit members, instill Enlightenment values in them, then let them gradually rise to positions of state power to transform their country from within.

— Michael Taylor
18:50

Thousands joined, but the Illuminati changed almost nothing

By the mid-1780s, the Illuminati had recruited 2,000–2,500 prominent figures across German territories: rulers of Brunswick and Weimar were members, Goethe himself was a member, Schiller was invited but refused, and there is evidence Mozart either knew of or was approached by the society. Yet Taylor stresses that Weishaupt never expected rapid results—he understood that Enlightenment seeds might take decades or generations to germinate. This group was willing to fight a long game with no visible endpoint, not to seize power immediately.

— Michael Taylor
22:56

The Illuminati were revealed through carelessness, not espionage

The turning point came from pure accident: Father Lanz was struck by lightning while walking in Regensburg. During the official autopsy, authorities found Illuminati membership lists, code names, and recruitment instructions sewn into his clothes—the first hard documentary evidence. Later, deputy Zwack was forced from Munich and, in desperation, hid a stack of Illuminati documents in his wife's underwear drawer; Bavarian authorities discovered them there. Both of history's most damning revelations leaked because the members stored them incompetently.

— Michael Taylor
28:09

Publishing seized documents made the Illuminati more powerful, not less

In 1787, the Bavarian government formally banned the Illuminati and publicly released all seized documents, intending to prove it was a dangerous subversive force. The plan catastrophically backfired: the Illuminati became the most exhaustively documented, most famous secret society in history—even the Freemasons and Jesuits have never had their records published so completely. Taylor calls this the ultimate ‘Streisand effect’—the government's suppression action succeeded as marketing better than the founder's own efforts could have.

— Michael Taylor
29:14

Same conspiracy theory, invented by strangers who never coordinated

A few years after the French Revolution began, conservative priest Barruel and Scottish physicist John Robison, without knowing each other, arrived at nearly identical theories: the Illuminati, French philosophers, and Frederick the Great had conspired together to destroy church and monarchy. American Federalists embraced this account; Washington, Adams, and John Jay all read and believed it, seeing Jefferson's Republicanism as a Trojan horse for Jacobin conspiracy.

— Michael Taylor
37:29

An Irish uprising was misread as proof of the conspiracy

In 1798, the United Irishmen launched an anti-British rebellion using cellular structures resembling the Jacobins and Illuminati. France sent naval forces and planned to land 6,000 militia in support—weather prevented the invasion. Taylor stresses that no evidence shows the Irish rebels actually knew of or were influenced by the Illuminati. Yet to British observers of the moment, the uprising seemed proof enough of a larger international conspiracy.

— Michael Taylor
41:32

A forgery grafted onto Illuminati theory created antisemitic conspiracy

In the early 20th century, Russian authorities, aiming to justify pogroms in Ukraine, forged the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, claiming Jewish elders plotted to rule the world through economic leverage. This forgery surfaced around 1905 and was translated into English between 1919 and 1920. Nesta Webster, a historian who claimed to be a reincarnated 18th-century French aristocrat, merged Illuminati conspiracy theory with this forgery to explain the French Revolution, the Russian Revolution, and even British imperial decline. Churchill at the time even published articles endorsing this framework.

— Michael Taylor
44:36

The best refutation of conspiracy thinking is just how badly conspiracies fail

Taylor's conclusion: the strongest argument against ‘true conspiracies’ is human incompetence. Illuminati members were perpetually quarreling and accomplished nothing. In the same secret letter debating the foundations of civil society, they bickered over whether to split an extra florin for supper. Apply this today: Beyoncé's hand signals, Davos forums, Soros and Hillary are all labeled with the same ‘controlling the world’ narrative, yet these supposedly all-powerful conspiracy figures repeatedly failed to achieve the outcomes attributed to their plots.

— Michael Taylor
