Rock Chronicle Lost Him Several Ferraris, Yet He Says It's Not a Failure

The 1994 Hong Kong Red Stadium concert is the pilgrimage site of Chinese rock. No one was staging a pilgrimage-level show anymore, so he staged one himself. After signing the artists and fixing the dates, he still didn't know which city it would land in. He lost several Ferraris' worth of money, and when the industry asked why, he said that as long as he could cross off his wish list, it wasn't a failure.

This episode isn't a post-mortem of a success story; it lays bare how a mega-show drives its founder to the brink, loses money, and forces him to tough it out. The latter half offers sharp takes on the sudden cooling of the festival market and AI songwriting.

The Chronicle was forced into being, not planned

The starting point of the Rock Chronicle was a fan who wanted to see a pilgrimage-level show no one was staging anymore, so he decided to do it himself. When it came to execution, he signed the artists first and fixed the dates, then went looking for a city and venue, with only about two months left before the show. A friend bluntly told him to be prepared to lose 10 million yuan. He had no confidence in how fans would receive it, but he believed he wasn't alone—having done hundreds of shows over more than twenty years, the only thing he'd never been sure about was the box office. Many things in his life worked this way: fix the goal first, then force yourself to find the path.

The moment tickets go on sale decides a show's fate

Lao Ma says a concert isn't like software or a film: after launch, you can still turn things around with iterations and word of mouth. From ticket sales to the show is only a month, and how tickets sell in that month is basically set in stone, rarely reversible. So he believes the first 80% of a project is determined by the initial planning, and all the overtime and extra effort afterward can at most push the score from 80 to 85 or 90. To keep the content from shrinking, he kept adding elements, eventually making it two days with 12 acts, each nearly the scale of a solo concert—adding one act means a team of ten to twenty more people and costs.

56:20

Poor ticket sales, but he won't pay to fill seats

The second stop in Yancheng was colder than the first, with many empty seats. Some advised him to use technical means to fill the venue, saying 'it'll be lively and no one will know.' He flatly refused: inviting people who don't love rock to fill seats would affect the mood of those who truly love the music, making it the most failed product. He said that as the main investor, the loss only hurt himself, so there was no need to put on a show. He also explained that the two lineups seemed repetitive, but after deduplication, only about 27% overlapped; the loss was due to the May Day slot being unfriendly to middle-aged fans, the broader downturn, and travel diversion, but all service aspects had been iterated.

1:28:25

Each edition costs the team several members

This is a project with extremely high emotional cost: no reverse gear, no room for error. After the first day, he reviewed and then smashed up his office; looking back, he admits that was out of control. Every time he runs a Chronicle, several team members leave—those who don't understand leave, those who do stay. He attributes his anxiety outburst to 'loss of control': at the time he only thought about responsibility for the event, not whether the salaries employees received could bear such pressure. During the first edition, there were five or six hurdles that made him feel it really couldn't go on, including last-minute demands from artists and various noise from partners and the industry.

— Ma Zhiyong
1:41:13

The Chronicle, a big book, shouldn't have a sequel

After the show, many cities invited him to replicate the IP, promising to bear the box office risk, but he refused all of them. Reason one: the rock market can't support a tour of 'ten stops and eight stops.' Reason two: replication would kill uniqueness. He compares the Chronicle to a book with an end—at most 3.0, likely only 2.0. He says he never expected to make money from it, nor does he need a success to prove anything; if someone in the future can carry the IP, the trademark and related assets are in his hands, and he can hand them over.

— Ma Zhiyong
2:37:20

A 3 million yuan fee can't bring back 300,000 in ticket sales

Facing runaway artist prices, he created a 'de-artist' new product, the Laifu Island Lifestyle Festival, and cited an inverted account: some artists quote 3 million yuan but can only drive 300,000 in ticket sales—promoters really can't swallow that. So he shifted the project's focus from the band lineup to the experience itself: water fights, Disney-style parades, islander stages, affordable drinks, capping the scale at 5,000 to 8,000 people, with 8,000 as the limit, never chasing tens of thousands. He says it can be a small-but-beautiful, zero-negative-review, sell-out-in-seconds, repeatable lifestyle brand, complementing the non-replicable IP of the Chronicle.

— Ma Zhiyong
2:42:33

From 700 festivals down to a hundred, the market is due for a shakeout

He offers a brutal industry curve: before the pandemic, China had about 100 to 180 music festivals a year; at the peak, it surged to over 700, several times more. With more festivals, lineups become copy-paste, and audiences get fatigued—'even good things, if you eat lobster every day, you'll throw up.' He predicts continued contraction: last year already halved, and this year about 50% of festivals are canceled or postponed on top of that. Only two types will survive: localized local scenes, and promoters with strong IP and reputation who truly care about the fan experience.

— Ma Zhiyong
3:09:44

AI can write every style, but not my tears

Lao Ma says he hosted a global AI songwriting competition last year. Technically, you can ask AI to write in any style, but he hasn't heard a single song that moved him to tears or made his hair stand on end. He believes traditional creation is writing a song to move many people, while AI, to truly move someone, must enter a person's memory, experiences, and hobby database to craft a work unique to them. He himself has started using AI as a creative assistant: having it first gather corporate culture, slogans, and materials, then he organizes them into lyrics—but he won't let AI produce the final product directly, because that isn't creation.
