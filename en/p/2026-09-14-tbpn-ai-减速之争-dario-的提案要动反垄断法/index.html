The AI Slowdown Debate: Dario's Proposal Would Rewrite Antitrust Law

Dario is calling for a Sherman Act exemption for frontier labs so Anthropic and OpenAI can legally coordinate their pace — a safety argument and also the thing he most wants commercially.

4:30

The slowdown is not a new topic; it started in July

Dario's "We must pace the frontier" did not come out of nowhere. On July 28 there was first an open letter on "pacing the frontier," signed by people from OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepMind, Meta and others; the same day Sam Altman said publicly that "we may have to slow down AI development"; two days later OpenAI told Axios that the wording of that petition was shaped with OpenAI's involvement, and that Altman had discussed pacing with White House officials. On August 18 OpenAI published a post saying it had indeed slowed scaling and paused a large RL training run; on August 31 an official Anthropic article explicitly discussed pacing the frontier and called for a coordination mechanism; on Saturday, September 12, at 7 a.m., Dario's article landed.

6:30

Of the three proposals, the second is the real problem

Dario's three-step plan: one, let independent third-party evaluators into AI companies to verify safety practices — he names METER, but people have moved back and forth between METER and OpenAI and Anthropic, so its independence is heavily questioned; two, frontier labs and democratic governments should jointly establish AI safety standards with government help; three, the US and other democracies should try to coordinate with authoritarian governments. The second is the core of the dispute: Dean Ball notes that the Sherman Antitrust Act has very clear rules on coordinated corporate behavior, and if a few companies get together and say "for safety we should do less," that is the same class of problem as airlines colluding to cut flights and push fares up. So what Dario wants is not a consensus statement but a legal exemption.

— Speaker 2
9:30

Sacks's retort: you don't need anyone's approval

David Sacks's response on X was the sharpest side of this round: by any reasonable metric — market share, revenue growth, model capability — you are the frontier, you two have a duopoly on frontier intelligence, and you claim your lead is widening. If the unreleased models really are so terrifying that you think you should slow down, I support you deciding that responsibly, but stop pretending you need anyone's permission, stop pretending antitrust law has to be suspended so you can form a cartel, stop pretending you need a regulatory approval process that sits above product liability, and stop pretending METER is independent. The worry on the other side is regulatory capture: if safety rules become extremely onerous, small teams like Instinct simply cannot build a breakout consumer product.

— Speaker 2
14:00

Trump says the only guardrail AI needs is a smart president

Over the weekend Trump fired off posts on social media: he said the only controller guardrails AI needs is a strong, smart, high-IQ president, and that the US has that "in spades"; he said this administration has already stopped AI people from doing bad things, for instance Dario "now pretending to be a perfect little angel"; he said he already has enormous criminal and regulatory power over these companies, that there is a sick conspiracy against AI and data centers, and that the only one happy about it is China. Then he posted again: when in business history has a leader of an industry called for regulation that, if strongly enforced, would drive them into bankruptcy? He also mentioned that Xi Jinping announced China will not stand in AI's way, and that Google is going to build a big plant in Finland because US approvals are too hard.

— Speaker 1
17:30

Gavin Baker: the only new fact is embedded evaluators

Gavin Baker's weekend summary separated noise from fact: proposals were flying everywhere in 24 hours, and the only tangible new fact is that OpenAI and Anthropic will accept embedded third-party evaluators from unknown institutions, with Dario offering METER as one possibility. He thinks third-party evaluation is smart, because model outputs have no Section 230-style liability shield, and showing duty of care will matter in future litigation — back in the day, plenty of internet companies might have gone bankrupt without Section 230. His conclusion is that this has minimal impact on investing, but for anyone who wants a longer, smoother cycle, constraints are a good thing. He also notes that Dario's proposal this time is far milder than his earlier call for an "FAA for AI"; now people are talking about a FINRA for AI.

— Speaker 2
20:00

A slowdown could compress margins, so it may not be 4D chess

Rune offered a counterintuitive reading: if you really pace the frontier, that compresses margins, because others catch up and the market becomes more oligopolistic. Right now OpenAI and Anthropic are a duopoly at the frontier, but if you hold the pace down, MSL, DeepMind and Grok all catch up to the same level, and you face four, five, six, seven genuinely capable competitors. Karazian says August AI spending fell slightly, mainly because of price cuts rather than people using less — OpenAI is discounting, Fable's adoption is weak, and everyone is asking "are we sure we want to use a million-dollar model to check the weather." So a slowdown does not necessarily fall in the "good for business" quadrant, and therefore is not necessarily the kind of 4D chess that needs a cynical reading.

— Speaker 2
22:30

What really pressures prices is not Chinese open-source models

An easily overlooked judgment: what puts pricing pressure on frontier labs is not Chinese open-source models but the dynamics of the duopoly itself. The reason is that although enterprise customers can download DeepSeek's weights, they are unwilling to take on scaling, uptime and reliability risk — they would rather do business with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or a service running on AWS or Azure. There are cheap options in the API market too, but they are less mature than the frontier labs, and it is not a question of intelligence level but of "if I plug it into my business, will it reliably give me the right results, does it have a high-quality SLA, will the service go down." That explains why the price war is happening among the leaders rather than being punched through from below by open source.

— Speaker 2
23:30

The PBC structure lets them say "deal with it"

Sam Altman said in a Fortune interview that if something is bad for our business, we have told investors for ten years that this is more like a nonprofit, it is a public benefit corporation, and in many ways it is not as purely aligned with shareholder interests as other companies. So both Sam and Dario are quite confident they can tell investors: we are doing something that will slow revenue growth, compress margins, maybe even lower the company's market value, but we think it is right, so deal with it — and you cannot sue us, because you invested in a PBC. But the show also concedes that almost no one gives them that trust, and everyone reads it as a cynical move to send them toward a ten-trillion-dollar market cap.

Glossary

Sherman Antitrust Act
The core US antitrust law prohibiting companies from colluding to restrain competition.
regulatory capture
When regulation is shaped by industry giants, which instead raises the barrier to entry for small companies.
Section 230
The liability shield in US law that spares internet platforms from responsibility for user content.
PBC
public benefit corporation, a structure that lets a company weigh goals beyond shareholder interests.
FINRA for AI
A model of AI oversight led by the industry rather than the government, following the financial industry's self-regulatory organizations.
