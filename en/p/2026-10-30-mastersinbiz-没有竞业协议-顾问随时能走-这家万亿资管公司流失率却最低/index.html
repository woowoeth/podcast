Without non-competes, advisors can leave anytime, yet this trillion-dollar wealth manager has the industry's lowest churn

12:20

Robo-advisors didn't actually eliminate human advisors

In the mid-2010s, the wealth-management industry feared robo-advisors would eliminate jobs for human advisors. Steinmeier recalls genuinely fearing this during his time at UBS. Instead, the price and performance transparency robo-advisors introduced actually raised client expectations for all advisors—the combination of technology and people proved better than either alone. LPL's example: by using robotic tools to drive account-opening costs from thousands of dollars down to a few, LPL could afford to eliminate account minimums.

— Rich Steinmeier
29:40

Acquisitions aim to avoid homogenization, not just scale

LPL acquired Commonwealth for $2.7 billion, adding to earlier deals like Atria, rapidly scaling up. But Steinmeier stressed the real risk isn't affordability—it's whether acquired companies get homogenized afterward. Backend functions like account setup, account transfers, and core technology can consolidate, but acquired firms' response patterns to advisors and service experience must be deliberately preserved and isolated. He summarizes this approach as ‘growing while feeling small’.

— Rich Steinmeier
35:48

Despite its scale, LPL functions as a small company

Steinmeier uses ‘small company that's big’ to describe LPL's current state: as scale grew, so did processes, discipline, and structure, but he doesn't want the company to lose its human touch. He says this is the real strategy behind recent acquisitions—how to gain scale and investment capacity without letting the organization homogenize into a sprawling machine.

— Rich Steinmeier
37:50

Advisors spend 70% of time on processes, not clients

LPL measured advisor time at the office: only 30% goes to actual client advice, the remaining 70% to process work—meeting prep, plan updates, tax optimization. Steinmeier says AI investment's core goal is compressing that 70%: starting with tools for meeting notes and note-taking, then expanding to account automation and portfolio construction. This ratio is LPL's baseline for measuring AI value, and why it chose to build its own technology stack rather than patch together third-party tools.

— Rich Steinmeier
39:52

AI deployment splits into three layers: advisors, company, engineers

LPL splits AI applications into three categories: first, advisor-office workflows—auto-populating account forms, submitting, then human review; second, internal company workflows like compliance checks and fund transfers (representing 80% of that 70% process time); third, making ~3,000 internal developers more efficient at code. Separate investment tracks because automation depth and risk tolerance differ completely across categories—one toolkit can't address all three.

— Rich Steinmeier
42:59

Without non-competes, LPL helps departing advisors take their clients

LPL's advisors are all independent contractors—no non-compete clauses in their contracts, free to move their book anytime. Counterintuitively, when advisors do leave, LPL doesn't try to retain their clients like some firms do; instead it helps them transfer client data along. Steinmeier says this forces LPL to re-earn advisor trust every day rather than rely on contract lock-in. The result: industry churn runs ~5%, LPL's sits at 1.5%.

— Rich Steinmeier
47:02

Portfolio allocation shifts from 60-40 to 40-30-30 structure

LPL's research team observed traditional 60-40 stock-bond portfolios evolving toward 40% stocks, 30% bonds, 30% alternatives (private equity, private credit, uncorrelated assets). Steinmeier supports lowering the bar to access alternatives—even pushing legislation to let 401(k) plans hold them—but admits the industry hasn't reached consensus on where to set the asset-size minimum.

— Rich Steinmeier
52:06

In ten years, advisor time shifts to 70% with clients, 30% on process

Steinmeier predicts the next decade will flip today's 30%-70% ratio: advisors spend 70% on client service, 30% on backend. More controversially, he foresees only a handful of peer wealth platforms surviving—those building their own technology, holding their own clearing capability, operating their own advisor workstations and investment tools, and sharing unified data with AI agents. Platforms merely stitching together third-party tools will be eliminated.

— Rich Steinmeier

Glossary

RIA (Registered Investment Adviser)
An independent wealth management firm fiduciarily required to act in the client's interest—structurally different from traditional brokerages.
Wire house
Full-service brokerage system (like Merrill Lynch or Morgan Stanley) where advisors are employees, not independent.
Non-compete clause
Contract restriction preventing departing employees from serving competitors or taking client relationships.
Transition loan
Forgivable loan offered to advisors switching firms; debt forgiveness vests over years.
ERISA (Employee Retirement Income Security Act)
U.S. federal law governing employer-sponsored retirement plans like 401(k)s.
40-30-30 portfolio
Asset allocation model with 40% equities, 30% fixed income, 30% alternatives (private equity, credit, uncorrelated assets).
