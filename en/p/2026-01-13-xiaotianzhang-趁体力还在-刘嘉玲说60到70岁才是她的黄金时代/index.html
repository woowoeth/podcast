While the energy lasts: Carina Lau says her golden years are 60 to 70

She says after 70, her body won't sustain travel and social obligations; 60 to 70 is instead her most emotionally stable decade—the time to visit Egypt, Jerusalem, and other longtime wishes while she can.

At seventeen, she negotiated her way back in with her own counter-offer

In her first audition for the wireless training program, Lau lasted only two sentences of Cantonese before being sent away—one of 3,000 candidates. Two weeks later, the head instructor's secretary called her back. They said her Cantonese was weak and suggested she try again next year. She refused to leave. Instead, she proposed a deal on the spot: test her every three months, and they could let her go anytime if she didn't pass. She'd suggested these terms herself, and the training program accepted—even assigning extra tutors for language, dance, and music. She credits her own drive to improve for changing their minds.

You owe no explanations to people who just want the drama

When she was young, media distortions and out-of-context reporting hurt. Now she doesn't care what anyone says. She answers to herself, not to gossips. The shift came from learning which friends lift you up and which ones drag you down—she chooses people with wisdom, reads books on inner life, and lets those things help her process outside judgment. Other people's noise: let them have it.

26:16

Life that blooms from hardship shines more brightly

Hiking a 3,000-meter peak in Bhutan, Lau stopped for water during a day of rain and sun. There, on a tree past the season when rhododendrons still bloom at lower elevations, one vivid flower had opened. Rain fell on its petals, then on her face. She and her friend wept on the spot. That blossom survived harsh conditions and bloomed anyway—that kind of life force, born from hardship, must be far stronger than anything that grew in a greenhouse.

You can feel bad, but don't let it camp out

She doesn't deny pain exists or that she gets stuck in it. But she doesn't let those feelings stay long. The trick: force yourself to move, go outside, switch your mood on purpose. Even grief at the scale of losing a parent—the worst—will lift after three months. Time heals everything. Staying unhappy too long only hurts you, and that's not worth it.

42:22

An agent's credibility evaporates the moment she profits

When Hollywood approached Tony Leung about Shang-Chi, Lau didn't know Marvel herself. She asked young people why it was so big at the box office, then watched Marvel films and decided he should do it. He was skeptical—didn't see himself in that kind of movie. She had him call the director directly; once he heard the pitch, he chose to take the role. It opened him to a whole new audience of teenagers and twentysomethings. She made a point of taking no commission on this deal. Once you take money, she says, any advice you give loses its weight.

— Liu Jialing
58:36

When an actor practices at home, that's his work, not a mood

Tony Leung spends half a year to nine months preparing a role. Once he's in character, he starts speaking in that character's voice at home without telling her, practicing his walk. She didn't get it at first—she'd wonder if she'd married a gangster for life. Eventually she understood: this is how he works. He gets anxious if he feels he hasn't stepped into the character fully each day. After wrapping, he needs time to step back out, or he gets mentally disoriented.

— Liu Jialing
1:02:37

In Hong Kong's golden age, no one was a star yet

Looking back at works like Days of Being Wild, she notes everyone was in their twenties—directors barely thirty—with no hierarchy of untouchable stars. The director could assemble anyone, shoot however, endlessly, and nobody complained. Everyone was just making art together. That's why the eight decades-ending to nine decades-ending period became Hong Kong cinema's golden era. Films after 2000 were bigger, more commercial; the whole feeling changed.

— Liu Jialing
1:10:40

Sixty to seventy is when life actually gets golden

She describes herself as faking maturity at twenty, messing up at thirty, overconfident at forty. From fifty to sixty, she'd stabilized emotionally. But sixty to seventy—that's the golden decade. No more dreams she has to chase. Her body still carries her through travel, drinks, meals. After seventy, the body can't keep up. So now is the time to go to Egypt, Jerusalem, Bodh Gaya in India—places she's always wanted to see. Her mindset has shifted from ‘living in the moment’ to ‘living each moment well.’
