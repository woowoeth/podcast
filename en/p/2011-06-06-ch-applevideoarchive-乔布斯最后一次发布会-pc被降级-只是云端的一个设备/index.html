Steve Jobs' Last Keynote: The PC Demoted to One Device Among Many

Jobs announced: the PC is no longer the digital hub for photos, music, and files—the cloud takes that role. iCloud lets all devices sync automatically; users don't even need to open sync software.

AppleiCloudWWDCSteve JobsMac App StoreiOS
This is Jobs' last public WWDC keynote. iCloud's strategic pivot, Lion's pricing strategy, and Mac App Store's channel data remain, in hindsight, the foundation of Apple's ecosystem moat.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:06

Mac Grew 28% While Industry Shrank 1%

Phil Schiller cited All Things D's assessment ‘The Mac is kicking ass’ to frame last quarter's figures: the PC market shrank 1% year-over-year, while Mac grew 28%. He stressed this was no accident. For five straight years, Mac's quarterly growth outpaced the entire PC industry. The driver: notebook lines like MacBook Air, which now account for nearly 75% of Mac shipments.

— Phil Schiller
16:29

Mac App Store Became America's #1 PC Software Channel in Six Months

Six months after launch, Mac App Store became America's top PC software sales channel, surpassing Best Buy, Walmart, and Office Depot. Schiller cited developer examples: Autodesk's Sketchbook Pro gained a million new Mac users after launch; Feral Interactive's game revenue doubled; small-team Pixelmator's revenue quadrupled, earning $1 million in its first 20 days on the store. Direct evidence that the app-store model disrupted legacy CD and mail-order software channels.

— Phil Schiller
38:04

iOS Reached 44% Market Share, Making It the Leading Mobile OS

Scott Forstall announced iOS devices had surpassed 200 million cumulative units sold, with 44% market share—the leading mobile OS. More striking: the category's growth velocity. The all-new iPad sold over 25 million units within 14 months of launch, faster than iPhone or iPod reached in equivalent timeframes. The takeaway: tablets aren't a transitional category, but a standalone new growth market.

— Scott Forstall
43:31

Notification Center Turns Interrupting Popups Into Persistent Lists

Forstall identified two critical flaws in existing notification design: notifications pop up as modal dialogs, interrupting play mid-game; messages shown on the lock screen vanish once you unlock, with no way to review them later. The new Notification Center solves both. Notifications arrive as a banner sliding down from the top—non-intrusive and self-dismissing. Pull down from the top anytime to reveal a persistent notification list. Lock-screen notifications swipe directly to their app.

— Scott Forstall
1:20:14

iCloud Solves the Device-Syncing Problem Driving Users Crazy

Jobs recalled his judgment from a decade earlier: the PC as the digital hub, with photos, video, and music flowing to other devices from the computer. But that model had broken down in recent years—iPhones and iPads now capture photos, store songs, and shoot video themselves. The result: buy a song, sync it to Mac, then to another device; shoot photos that need to sync back to Mac. His exact words: ‘keeping these devices in sync is driving us crazy.’ This is the problem iCloud solves.

— Steve Jobs
1:21:15

PC Downgraded From Hub to Mere Device Among Many

Jobs laid out the strategic shift plainly: now that all devices are networked, move the digital hub to the cloud. Mac and PC drop to equal footing with iPhone, iPad, and iPod touch—‘one device’ among many. He explicitly rejected the notion of cloud as ‘just a disk in the sky.’ It's not files dragged up and down by hand, but content auto-uploaded, auto-stored, then auto-pushed to all your devices, deeply integrated with each app. Users learn nothing new.

— Steve Jobs
1:23:21

MobileMe Tanked; Core Apps Become Free

Jobs self-mockingly acknowledged MobileMe was ‘not our finest hour,’ but stressed the team learned from it. The three core MobileMe apps—Contacts, Calendar, Mail—were completely rewritten as iCloud apps. Changes on any device push automatically to the cloud and sync to others; Calendar adds family sharing. The service, originally $99/year, turned completely free starting that day. All three apps, free.

— Steve Jobs
1:54:32

iTunes Match Undercuts Amazon and Google on Pricing

For music users ripped themselves, not purchased from iTunes, iTunes Match skips the full-library upload. The software scans against iTunes' 18 million-song catalog, matching in minutes rather than weeks of uploading. Matched songs get free upgrades to 256kbps AAC. Priced at $24.99/year, Jobs compared on stage: 5000 songs cost $50 storage on Amazon, Google hadn't published pricing yet.

— Steve Jobs
