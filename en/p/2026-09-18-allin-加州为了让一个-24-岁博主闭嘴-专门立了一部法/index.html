California Passed a Law Just to Shut Up a 24-Year-Old Blogger

California's counterattack on an investigator isn't denial, it's legislation: the moment he uploads footage he's captured, he's fined $4,000 first, then pays the other side's legal fees. The person who introduced the law is the wife of the state attorney general.

0:08

$15 billion of high-speed rail leading to nowhere

The first number Nick Shirley's new film gives is $15 billion, 18 years, and the number of towns California high-speed rail connects so far is zero. When it was approved in 2008, it promised to connect Los Angeles and San Francisco; that same year China started building its own high-speed rail, and today it has connected more than 30,000 miles and covers the entire country, while California's segment connects nothing. He calls it the ‘high-speed rail to nowhere’—not a figure of speech: walk along the route and all you see is concrete and rebar. To finish it, another $100 to $200 billion is needed.

— Nick Shirley
1:09

Cost went from $33 billion to $236 billion

The next part of the same ledger: the original estimate was $33 billion, jumped to $126 billion, and the latest estimate is $236 billion. By that number, it would become the third most expensive infrastructure project in world history—first is the 1950s interstate highway system, second is the International Space Station. Interviewees say the difference is: you can drive I-5 or I-80 across the country, you can go to space, but ‘we will never ride California high-speed rail.’ The name he gives this segment is ‘the most expensive piece of concrete known to man,’ while people in the Central Valley call it a modern Stonehenge. Newsom himself admitted in a privately recorded 2016 clip that he did not believe the project would be built in our lifetimes.

3:09

A third of the money goes to delay compensation

A third of the $15 billion goes to ‘change order delays’—because the builders don't know where the infrastructure is or how to build it. Contractors therefore got compensation rights: $170,000 per day, $5 million per month, simply because they couldn't start work. This February, California just settled a delay settlement of over $500 million, the largest delay settlement. Meanwhile, California still hasn't bought high-speed rail trains; Nick's judgment is that it's because they know there will never be a train on the tracks. Another calculation he makes: at current ridership, buying one-way tickets directly, $15 billion would subsidize free one-way tickets from San Francisco to Los Angeles for 150 years; at $236 billion, it would subsidize 300 years.

9:11

The state senator says it's happening

State Senator Cortezi just pushed through $1 billion a year for 20 consecutive years. Nick asked him face to face: not an inch of track has been laid in California, how do you explain that? He said ‘it's happening,’ and asked the reporter whether he had been there. Pressed that Newsom had privately said it was impossible, his answer was ‘maybe he did, maybe he didn't.’ Asked whether the high-speed rail authority had ever been held accountable for delays and overruns, he said ‘we're trying to hold them accountable.’ His justification wasn't transportation but that this is the largest economic development project in the country, and no other project in the country can compare. That same afternoon at 4:09 PM, Nick walked into the California High-Speed Rail Authority office—no one was there, even though they don't get off until 5.

12:11

California passed a law named after him

California passed AB2624, which legislators call the ‘Stop Nick Shirley Act’: as long as an ‘immigration service provider’—including nonprofits and medical institutions—sends him a demand letter, he cannot upload the video he filmed; uploading first incurs a $4,000 fine, then court, and if he loses he also has to pay the other side's legal fees. He says the law only appeared after his exposé, and he is suing California on the grounds that it is unconstitutional. Co-sponsor Chura took $80 million in government funding to do immigration advocacy; the person who introduced the bill is Mia Bont, wife of the state attorney general, whose job is precisely to prosecute fraud. On the very day this episode was recorded, the California DOJ charged 12 people in a $10 million fraud case, in the San Diego daycare he had filmed.

— Nick Shirley
21:17

They hate him because he didn't pick a side

Jason's mechanism is: these people were idealistic once too, they wanted to bravely expose things, but they got captured, politicized, picked a side, so a person who doesn't pick a side is a threat to them. The way they criticize him isn't by saying he filmed it wrong, but that his fact-checking isn't rigorous enough. Jason's rebuttal is that none of that matters: what matters is he found real fraud, put people on camera, and then those people were charged, and he's the only one doing it. He says this used to be the job of 60 Minutes and Frontline, and they forgot how. Nick adds: no one in the mainstream media ever came to ask him how he did it, they all came to investigate him.

— Jason Calacanis
22:17

One video made the governor drop his re-election bid

The scale of the Minnesota daycare episode even he didn't expect: after the video was released, there were disturbances and protests locally; he says politicians encouraged people to take to the streets because with ICE present, the crowd could cover up the fraud. The result was Tim Walz dropping his re-election bid; the head of a money-laundering center who took $4 million tried to fly to London on a one-way ticket, was arrested for daycare fraud, and has already pleaded guilty; he says the same area also broke the largest autism fraud case in history at the same time. He attributes this output to rhythm rather than inspiration: for two and a half years he posted one video a week, and tipsters would proactively tell him where to go.

— Nick Shirley
27:17

The next honeypot is welfare fraud

What he's going to dig into next is welfare fraud, which in his words is ‘absolutely insane’: in Portland, Oregon, a strip club can swipe SNAP cards; adult day care, hospice, anywhere that takes government money is full of fraud. The cost falls on personal safety: he now has to have security, because people really want to kill him. What he thinks about repeatedly before sleep is—will a fraudster, realizing they're finished, just shoot, so the video never gets out. The antidote proposed on the show is to have viewers pay for him to have researchers and security, on the grounds that Vice President J.D. Vance said 10% to 20% of tax revenue is wasted.

— Nick Shirley

Glossary

immigration support service providers
Entities defined as funded subjects under California law, including nonprofits and medical institutions, broadly referring to institutions that use tax money to serve immigrants.
AB2624 / Stop Nick Shirley Act
A bill passed in California targeting undercover filming: once a filmed institution sends a letter, the video may not be uploaded, and violators face fines and legal fees.
change order delays
Schedule delays caused by the route location and construction plan being undecided; one third of California high-speed rail costs go here.
SNAP
US federal food assistance, distributed via a card, which can be used for purchases.
