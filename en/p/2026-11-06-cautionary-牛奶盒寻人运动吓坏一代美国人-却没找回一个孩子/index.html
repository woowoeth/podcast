The first time imagining centuries ahead, humans didn't know what mattered

The Century Safe was sealed in 1876 and scheduled to open in 1976, but its planners confused importance with curiosity—inside were public symbols like congressional photos, prohibition pamphlets, and paid signatures, nothing that mattered. Historian Jill Lepore said nothing in the box was useful. Roman Mars noted this was humanity's first serious attempt to imagine its traces surviving a century, yet it traded what truly held value—personal records now so scarce that an archived email may be worth more than most published works—for what looked like big history.

— Roman Mars
16:58

The ‘dangerous stranger’ label masks more common threats to children

The milk carton campaign began with two boys' disappearances; dairies printed photos on packaging, using milk's daily purchase and updateable images to spread the word. Cases were classified as dangerous stranger abduction, a frame that fixed public attention on strangers. But the actual harms children face—poverty, alcoholic households, abuse by people close to them—occur dozens to hundreds of times more often, yet were systematically ignored because they lack drama.

— Roman Mars
19:00

Halloween poisoned candy never happened but became perfect cover

There is no documented case of a stranger poisoning Halloween candy; the real Halloween danger is children being hit by cars after dark. The one ‘poisoned candy death’ was a father who poisoned his own son for insurance money—he felt safe doing it because the public assumed strangers poison candy, so no one would suspect him. It's the episode's sharpest point: a false threat, repeatedly amplified, ends up sheltering the people actually doing harm.

— Tim Harford
22:06

The milk carton campaign terrified a generation but found zero children

The campaign was brief but frightened Mars's entire generation. Yet traced back, not a single child was found because of a milk carton. The only related case came from a random traffic stop. It didn't change outcomes—it changed the order of fears in people's minds. Strangers' faces became fear number one, while poverty and family violence were pushed behind.

— Roman Mars
27:12

The time clock was built to defend workers, then sold to their bosses

Willard Bundy was an engineer fascinated by clock mechanics who invented the first time clock so workers could prove their hours hadn't been stolen. That version didn't sell. His brother Harold flipped the pitch: don't tell workers it protects them, tell factory owners workers are lazy, stealing time—buy this clock to manage them. A tool's meaning depends on who buys it first, not the inventor's intent.

— Roman Mars
30:17

Taylorism flattened workers into a boiler and a pump

Frederick Taylor timed every movement on the factory floor with a stopwatch: handling a tool counted as pause time, speaking counted as pause time. He forced out the theoretical minimum, then demanded workers maintain it all day. A factory owner of the era described workers as "movable power plants"—the stomach is a boiler, the heart a pump. The description is accurate, but it strips out what actually matters: belonging, will, trust.

— Roman Mars
31:17

Even Lenin couldn't resist copying Taylorism's blueprint

Before the revolution, Lenin condemned Taylorism as a terrible, oppressive tool of capitalist class war. After the revolution succeeded, when the Soviets had to actually manage factories, he introduced Taylorism. The reversal exposes its true nature: not a capitalism-specific disease, but something any power structure wanting to reduce complex humans to an efficiency number inevitably reaches for.

— Roman Mars
32:21

Screenshot every five minutes: Taylorism's digital-age upgrade

Remote monitoring software auto-screenshots every few minutes and tracks activity. Harford mentions an earlier episode about a female manager whose system photographed her screen every 5 minutes. Real work went unmeasured: bathroom breaks, walks to think, pen and paper, mentoring someone on a call. Taylorism faded because happiness and trust were hard to measure. Now managers believe they have enough data—and they're making the same mistake with better tools.

— Tim Harford

Glossary

Century Safe
A time capsule sealed in 1876 and planned to open in 1976, intended to preserve what seemed important about American civilization for a century.
Bundy clock
The first employee time clock, invented by Willard Bundy to let workers prove accurate hours, later repurposed by employers to track worker time.
Taylorism
A management method using stopwatches to time every worker's movement, reducing each task to its theoretical minimum and demanding workers maintain that pace.
