Smallpox Wasn't Eradicated by Mass Vaccination — It Was Eradicated by Ringing Every Case

In 1977 the world was down to one smallpox case, in Somalia: a hospital cook named Ma'o Malin, who hadn't been vaccinated because he was afraid of the vaccine. Ring vaccination ran its last lap on him, and the world reached zero.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:08

Smallpox could be eradicated because of its weaknesses

Smallpox had several "easy to eradicate" properties other diseases lack: symptoms were easy to identify, unlike influenza, which blurs together with a pile of respiratory infections; it spread only person to person and infected only humans, with no animal reservoir hiding the virus — yellow fever can't be eradicated because it has other primate hosts and mosquito transmission; recovery gave lifelong immunity; and outbreaks moved relatively slowly, so once a patient was infectious they were usually too sick to get out of bed, and cases tended to cluster in the same household and neighbouring blocks, meaning fast action after detection could cut transmission. Most critically, a vaccine existed that could block transmission. Remove any one of these conditions and a global eradication campaign is out of the question.

— Tracy V. Wilson
12:18

The Soviets proposed eradicating smallpox first, and were shot down twice

In 1953 the World Health Assembly did not consider smallpox a candidate for global eradication at all, and it was not a priority. Five years later Soviet Deputy Health Minister Viktor Zhdanov proposed a ten-year eradication plan and promised Soviet vaccine donations, and it still did not pass — WHO's energies were entirely on its malaria programme. Only when the Soviet proposal was put forward again in 1959 did it pass, and even then only as a voluntary global vaccination campaign: smallpox-endemic countries were asked to vaccinate 80% of their populations, and non-endemic countries were to supply money and vaccine. At that point official reports had smallpox present in 63 countries, with 77,555 cases — a figure later shown to be wildly low.

— Tracy V. Wilson
13:18

The official numbers were hundreds of times below the real ones

Those 63 countries were only WHO member states, and some smallpox-endemic countries were not members at all, including China, with a population of more than 660 million. Even within the 63, reporting was a mess: countries without public health infrastructure did not track cases at all, and where infrastructure existed, sometimes only infections that reached a hospital were reported. In 1959 the countries where smallpox was genuinely still circulating held more than half the world's population, and the annual case count was more likely 50 million than 77,000. Under-reporting was so severe that there was no way to judge whether national vaccination campaigns were working, which made it even harder to win funding and support from countries that had already eliminated smallpox.

— Tracy V. Wilson
15:22

Using doses given as the metric, India reported 140%

Most early national campaigns measured success by "how many shots were given," so vaccinators picked the easiest targets first — vaccinating every student in a school, say — while out-of-school children in remote areas went unvaccinated. When records were unclear, vaccinators would return to the same school and vaccinate everyone again, even though successful vaccination usually leaves an identifiable scar. India at one point reported a 140% vaccination rate, because so many people had been vaccinated repeatedly. This metric-driven approach persisted for about five years, until Karel Rashka of Czechoslovakia became director of WHO's Division of Communicable Diseases in 1964, WHO created a dedicated smallpox eradication unit in 1965, and that same year US President Johnson announced American support for the global eradication programme.

— Tracy V. Wilson
18:27

The bifurcated needle pushed vaccination down to 10 cents a person

In July 1965 Benjamin A. Rubin's bifurcated needle was patented. When it was dipped into a vaccine vial, a fixed dose of liquid was caught between its two prongs; the prongs' design made the needle enter the skin at the correct angle and depth, and the two-pronged structure meant fewer presses were needed per vaccination. The result: a single vial could vaccinate four times as many people, someone with no medical experience could learn to use it in about 15 minutes, and the needle itself was cheap. It was originally designed to be disposable, but at WHO's request it was switched to a boilable steel alloy, so vaccinators dropped used needles into a container and boiled the whole batch at the end of the day. Add research showing that swabbing with alcohol before vaccination did not reduce bacteria, and that complication and secondary infection rates were the same with or without it, and most programmes — especially in poor regions — simply dropped that step. Together this brought the cost of smallpox vaccination down to about 10 cents per person.

— Tracy V. Wilson
21:30

The ten-year target was copied from the moon shot, and WHO itself didn't believe it

The intensified eradication programme launched on 1 January 1967 had the goal of eradicating smallpox worldwide within ten years, finding and isolating the last naturally occurring case before 31 December 1976. The initial budget was $2.5 million, which sounded like more than before, but spread across 31 smallpox-endemic countries plus the neighbours that needed protecting, it came to about $50,000 per country. Donald A. Henderson, head of the CDC's smallpox eradication programme, became the programme's chief medical officer. By his account the ten-year target was fairly arbitrary, inspired by Kennedy's "moon in a decade" declaration; he also said WHO was pessimistic about the prospects of success, and that Director-General Marcolina Kandao, who was Brazilian, thought smallpox could not be eradicated in the Amazon basin alone, let alone anywhere else. Henderson also wrote that WHO wanted an American at the helm so that if it failed, the United States would share part of the blame rather than it falling entirely on WHO.

— Tracy V. Wilson
26:39

Only 30% of vaccine samples met standard; some contained no virus at all

In 1967 WHO asked vaccine manufacturers to submit samples, and only 30% met quality and potency standards. Some submitted samples contained no vaccinia virus at all, and others were contaminated with bacteria. This pushed WHO to set stricter standards and to continuously monitor whether vaccines actually met them. At the same time, war and unrest made vaccination hard to push forward in some endemic areas: the Nigerian civil war broke out in 1967, making health workers and patients more endangered and causing vaccine shortages. But it was precisely that shortage that forced a strategic turn — with not enough vaccine for everyone, health officials concentrated on vaccinating the household members and close contacts of smallpox patients. This proved an effective way to block transmission, and Nigeria's last smallpox case was reported in May 1970.

— Tracy V. Wilson
29:42

The ring method: wrap every case in a circle of the vaccinated

By 1973 smallpox was down to five countries: Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Pakistan and Ethiopia. But these countries bordered one another and held more than 700 million people, and years of mass vaccination had failed to eliminate the disease. India alone had 120,000 health workers on the programme. So the strategy shifted to replicating what had actually worked in Nigeria in the late 1960s: isolate every smallpox patient, use extensive contact tracing to find everyone who might have been exposed, and vaccinate their immediate families and surrounding communities. This was the "ring method" — building a circle of vaccinated people around every active case so the disease could not get out. Surveillance and tracing were the crux, because health workers could only draw a ring around known cases. With this method, Pakistan reported its last case in October 1974, Nepal in April 1975, India in May 1975, and Bangladesh in October 1975.

— Tracy V. Wilson
31:47

The last case was a hospital cook who was afraid of the vaccine

In 1977 Somalia reported 300 smallpox cases, and the last one was Ali Ma'o Malin, a cook at Merca hospital. On 12 October 1977, a vehicle carrying two smallpox patients to an isolation camp stopped at the hospital to ask directions, and Malin got in and rode along for less than 15 minutes, only to show the driver where the head of the smallpox surveillance team lived. The two patients were a six-year-old and a one-and-a-half-year-old, brother and sister, and when the authorities traced contacts they missed Malin. He worked in a hospital but had never been vaccinated against smallpox, and in a later interview he said he had been afraid of the vaccine. He began feeling unwell on 22 October, and doctors initially thought it was malaria; a rash appeared on 26 October and was first diagnosed as chickenpox; on 29 October he began to suspect smallpox himself; on 30 October someone reported it to health officials, and the same day it was confirmed in the laboratory.

— Tracy V. Wilson
33:50

For one man, 161 contacts traced and an entire hospital sealed

The operation to keep Malin's single case from sparking a new outbreak shows exactly how the machinery worked. Contact tracing found 161 people who might have been exposed, the farthest 120 kilometres away, including hospital staff, family and friends, many of whom had visited him while he was ill. The authorities identified 33 face-to-face contacts who had no evidence of prior smallpox infection and had not been vaccinated in the past three years; 12 of them had neither evidence of infection nor evidence of vaccination. All of these people, and all known and possible contacts and their families, were vaccinated. Over an 18-day surveillance period, health workers made multiple house visits to each potentially exposed person, with the number and frequency depending on exposure and risk — someone with long exposure and no vaccination would be visited many times, while someone vaccinated with brief contact might be checked only twice. In the end, none of the 161 contacts fell ill.

— Tracy V. Wilson
35:53

The last natural case missed the ten-year target by less than a month

Ali Ma'o Malin became the last person to be naturally infected with smallpox, 10 years, 9 months and 26 days after the intensified eradication programme began — even though that ten-year target had been arbitrary to begin with, it still came up just short. After recovering he joined the polio eradication effort, working as a vaccinator, organising house-to-house campaigns, and telling the story of how he had skipped vaccination out of fear and ended up the last smallpox patient. Somalia declared itself polio-free in 2007, polio reappeared in 2013, and Malin threw himself back into vaccination work, then suddenly fell ill and died on 22 July 2013, with a diagnosis of malaria. But the last person to die of smallpox was infected after Malin: Janet Parker, a medical photographer at the University of Birmingham Medical School in the UK, in 1978.

— Tracy V. Wilson
38:00

The lab leak: the last person to die of smallpox

Janet Parker was infected with smallpox through her work at the University of Birmingham Medical School. The microbiology laboratory was one floor below her darkroom, and also below the office where she made phone calls. Exactly how she came into contact with the laboratory's smallpox virus is unclear: the leading theory at the time was shared ventilation ducts, but later research considered that unlikely. She had been vaccinated against smallpox, but vaccine protection lasts about ten years, and her last vaccination was in 1966. The strain she was exposed to was also unusually lethal. Her mother, Hilda Whitcomb, was infected while caring for her but recovered; her father, Frederick, died of an apparent heart attack while in isolation, and Hilda missed both her husband's and her daughter's funerals because of her own illness. Health workers isolated hundreds of potentially exposed people, and at least 500 were vaccinated to stop the outbreak spreading. The laboratory had already been scheduled to close, as WHO was reducing the number of facilities holding virus samples. It had previously been inspected and cleared to keep operating, but lacked basic safety measures — no changing facilities, no dedicated shower, no airlock. It had also had another smallpox case in 1966, also involving a medical photographer.

— Tracy V. Wilson
39:00

The lab director killed himself days before the patient died

The laboratory's director, Dr Henry Bedsen, had been trying to finish as much work as possible before the lab closed. He blamed himself for Parker's illness and killed himself days before Parker died; Parker died on 11 September 1978. Meanwhile health officials were still closely monitoring other countries to confirm smallpox was no longer spreading. On 26 October 1979 a ceremony in Nairobi declared smallpox eradicated from the Horn of Africa — two years after Ali Ma'o Malin first developed his rash. On 9 December 1979 the Global Commission for the Certification of Smallpox Eradication declared smallpox eradicated. On 8 May 1980 the World Health Organization confirmed the disease's eradication.

— Tracy V. Wilson
40:02

Smallpox was not eradicated by everyone cooperating

A claim circulates on social media that this worked because everyone did their part, implying or stating outright that everyone at the time accepted vaccination. That is not true. Resistance and hesitancy were present throughout, especially in childhood vaccination campaigns — many parents were unwilling to do something that might harm or sicken their children, and vaccination methods evolved over the years, so some parents had had fairly traumatic vaccination experiences themselves and did not want to repeat them on their children. Anti-vaccine activists such as Lily Lote, who worked for the National Anti-Vaccination League and edited its journal, kept distributing material against vaccines, and against smallpox vaccine in particular, as the eradication campaign advanced. On top of that, early vaccines were grown in the skin of live animals, drawing opposition from animal welfare and anti-vivisection campaigners; after methods advanced to tissue culture, distrust of science turned into opposition to that method instead.

— Tracy V. Wilson
41:03

Why the vaccine was taken to be smallpox itself

In places like India, smallpox vaccination had been a routine part of public health services since 1947, yet outbreaks continued for decades afterward. That led people to claim the vaccine was worthless, or even that the vaccine was what caused smallpox. Countries stopped vaccinating after eliminating smallpox within their own borders, which in some people's eyes only reinforced that impression — they stopped vaccinating and smallpox went away, so it must have been the vaccine's fault. There was religious opposition too: different peoples and cultures understood smallpox as the will of God, or treated existing smallpox prevention methods as religiously meaningful. And the countries that eliminated smallpox last were largely countries long colonised and exploited by European powers, so suspicion of a public health campaign led mainly by the West is entirely understandable. Global smallpox eradication was therefore accomplished amid hesitancy and resistance, not in their absence.

— Tracy V. Wilson

Glossary

variolation
Deliberately introducing material from a smallpox patient's pustules or scabs into another person to prevent smallpox, at the risk of causing severe smallpox.
vaccinia virus
Another orthopoxvirus that replaced cowpox in smallpox vaccine production from the 19th century onward, of unknown origin.
bifurcated needle
A two-pronged needle that picks up a fixed dose of vaccine when dipped into a vial; patented in 1965, it sharply cut vaccination costs.
ring method
Isolating every smallpox case and vaccinating its contacts and surrounding community, forming a ring of immune people that blocks transmission.
variola major / minor
The two strains that cause smallpox; variola major is more lethal, killing up to one in three of those infected.
