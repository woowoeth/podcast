The Milky Way's Outer Disk Has Slowed Down, and Both Dark Matter and MOND May Be Wrong

Gaia's measured rotation curve for the Milky Way's outskirts looks like a Keplerian decline, while both dark matter and MOND predict it should be flat — both camps may be waiting on the wrong curve.

2:02

The evidence for dark matter is not "it spins too fast"

Vera Rubin's generation's discovery is often simplified into "galaxies spin too fast," but Brian Keating stresses that the real key is the radial motion pattern: the luminosity in galaxy outskirts has long since faded, yet the gravitational influence does not fade with it, meaning that at large radii there is gravitational mass that visible matter cannot supply. The flat rotation curve is only the visible manifestation of this mismatch; what it marks is "where the missing gravity lives." The standard explanation wraps a larger dark matter halo outside the visible disk; the halo emits almost no light, yet keeps contributing enclosed mass as radius increases, so the disk-plus-halo curve can stay flat.

— Brian Keating
3:05

We are guessing the shape of the pitch from inside the stands

Measuring the Milky Way has one innate disadvantage: we are inside it. Keating's analogy is trying to infer the shape of an entire football pitch from inside the stands. Gaia gives extremely precise stellar positions and motions, but it has no button labeled "true Milky Way rotation curve." To get a curve you must first choose a proxy, correct distances, choose a model, correct for asymmetric drift, and assume the system is in equilibrium and symmetric. The instrument supplies data; the pipeline tells us what those data points mean — the two must be looked at together, and you cannot cite only the latter's conclusion.

— Brian Keating
4:24

Asymmetric drift happens to be largest exactly where it matters most

Stars do not travel on perfect circular orbits; they oscillate radially and vertically, so a stellar population's mean azimuthal velocity is lower than the circular velocity of the gravitational field — that difference is called asymmetric drift. To recover circular velocity from observed velocities, you must model the random motions. The trouble is that this correction matters most precisely in the sparse outer regions — and the outer regions are exactly where the enclosed mass is greatest and the controversy is fiercest. In other words, the place that most needs an accurate correction is the place where the correction itself is least reliable.

— Brian Keating
5:04

What a slope of negative 0.47 means

The analysis's inference is: between 19.5 and 26.5 kiloparsecs from the center, velocity falls by about 30 kilometers per second, and the fitted outer slope is negative 0.47 plus or minus 0.15. That number sounds close to negative 0.5, i.e. 1 over the square root of radius — which is exactly a Keplerian decline. If circular velocity really falls by this law, velocity squared falls as 1 over radius, and substituting into the circular velocity equation makes the enclosed equivalent spherical mass approximately constant: the farther out you go, the less extra gravitational mass is inferred. That is very awkward for an extended dark matter halo that should keep increasing its enclosed mass. But Keating cautions that "equivalent spherical" is a crucial phrase: the Milky Way has a disk, gas, a bulge, a warp and a three-dimensional halo, and turning one curve into a mass requires a great many geometric assumptions.

— Brian Keating
6:10

260 billion solar masses is fitted, not measured

This analysis estimates the Milky Way's total mass at about 2.6 times 10 to the 11th solar masses, i.e. 260 billion solar masses, including stars, gas and dust. That is lower than many independent estimates — the latter approach one trillion solar masses. The problem lies in where this number comes from: Gaia's rotation curve covers only about 9 to 27 kiloparsecs, while the Milky Way's halo may extend to around 200 kiloparsecs. Using less than 30 kiloparsecs of data to give a total halo mass means most of the mass comes from extending the fitted model, not from the part stars directly trace. Extrapolation is unavoidable, but observation and extrapolation are not synonyms.

— Brian Keating
8:04

There is no radius in MOND's formula

MOND holds that below a certain characteristic acceleration, the effective dynamics deviate from Newtonian relations: acceleration is approximately the square root of Newtonian acceleration times the MOND scale A0. In the low-acceleration limit, velocity to the fourth power equals Newton's constant times baryonic mass times A0 — there is no radius in this equation. Precisely because of this, MOND naturally gives asymptotically flat rotation curves for isolated galaxies, which is its most attractive feature. But Keating says this also becomes its vulnerability: if the Milky Way's outer curve really is a Keplerian decline, MOND likewise expects a flatter result and has not found one. Dark matter and MOND reach flat curves through completely different physics, and now may be challenged by the same data at the same time.

— Brian Keating
9:15

Flaws lead to new laws

Keating says he often tells students: flaws lead to new laws. But a Keplerian decline does not immediately falsify all versions of MOND. MOND is nonlinear, and an external gravitational field can affect a galaxy's internal dynamics, thereby changing the Milky Way's outer behavior — the external field effect alone can produce a decline. So the defensible conclusion is not "Gaia killed MOND," but rather: a robust Keplerian curve would create tension with the predictions for isolated galaxies, requiring the external field or other modifications to do substantial quantitative work. The test of a scientific claim is whether a theory can fit the measured curve with independent and reasonable parameters, not whether it can tell a pretty story after the fact.

— Brian Keating
10:19

The Milky Way is not a quiet laboratory

Distance errors change both a star's inferred position and its tangential velocity; the asymmetric drift correction depends on tracer density and velocity dispersion; selection effects change which stars enter the sample; the warp breaks simple disk geometry; the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy and the Large Magellanic Cloud also drive non-circular motion, and are themselves external gravitational mass. These tracer stars become sparse at large distances, and one must also question whether they are in equilibrium. In the Gaia analysis, neglected dynamical terms and systematic error budgets grow at the outermost radii. Keating stresses that none of this proves the decline is fake; it is a beautiful result, but it means that smooth curve may be masking a messy galaxy.

— Brian Keating
12:26

All three explanations are still alive

Keating gives three possibilities that remain viable. First, the decline is real but mild: the Milky Way's halo is lighter or more concentrated than some older models, and both dark matter and MOND can adjust parameters and survive. Second, the decline is real but exaggerated: affected by systematic errors and non-equilibrium, which are implicitly assumed away in the models — this is the least glamorous answer, so scientists must take it seriously. Third, the outer disk perturbation is large enough that the reconstructed curve simply cannot be assumed to be equilibrium circular motion. One pattern, three physical stories, and the existing evidence cannot uniquely constrain or choose among them.

— Brian Keating
13:14

What would end this debate

Keating lists the tools that could break the deadlock: dynamically colder young stars, Cepheids, stellar streams that probe farther out, and globular cluster satellites that allow tests at larger radii — the 1920s Shapley debate used similar methods to determine the size of the Milky Way. Future Gaia data releases will improve astrometric precision, radio surveys will provide different tracers such as gas, and better theoretical models can explicitly incorporate the warp, the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy and Large Magellanic Cloud effects. If different methods and different systematic errors all recover the same declining curve, then one must listen — it could go from 3 sigma to many sigma.

— Brian Keating

Glossary

asymmetric drift
The difference by which a stellar population's mean azimuthal velocity falls below the circular velocity of the gravitational field because of non-circular stellar motions.
MOND
A theory that modifies Newtonian gravity at low accelerations, explaining galaxy rotation curves without dark matter particles.
external field effect
A nonlinear feature of MOND: when a galaxy sits in an external gravitational field, its internal dynamics are altered.
kiloparsec
A distance unit of about 3260 light-years, used to describe galaxy-scale distances.
Cepheids
Dynamically colder young variable stars that can serve as tracers for measuring the Milky Way's outer motions.
