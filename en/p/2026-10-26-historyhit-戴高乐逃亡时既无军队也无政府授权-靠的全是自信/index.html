2:04

He fled France with no army, no mandate, and almost no followers

When he boarded a British plane to escape France on June 17, 1940, his rank was only brigadier general—the lowest grade in the French army—with no official government authorization, no money, and almost no followers. Yet he was convinced he had to make the French believe that France had lost a battle, not the war. The next day he broadcast what would later be called the ‘Appeal of 18 June’. The episode emphasizes that almost no one heard this broadcast at the time, and those who did rarely knew who de Gaulle was—the historical significance later attributed to this moment far exceeded its actual impact on the day it happened.

12:48

A military reformer who believed soldiers must obey civilians

During his captivity in World War I, de Gaulle lectured fellow prisoners on ‘the divisions within the enemy’, arguing that Germany would lose the war because its military leadership had seized too much power and civilians had lost control over strategy. From then on, he insisted that no matter how technical a military question was, the final decision must rest with politicians, not generals. Ironically, his life's most dramatic moments came when he, as a military man, challenged the military command structure itself—whether considering the replacement of the 73-year-old top commander Weygand in 1940, or using his 1961 televised speech as president to crush the coup attempt by Algerian generals. In each case, he stood on the principle that political authority must trump military authority.

18:07

The Germans invented blitzkrieg, not de Gaulle

Between the wars, de Gaulle wrote a book promoting mobile tank warfare, titled ‘Towards a Professional Army’, which was highly controversial at the time because conscription had always been seen as the symbol of republican defense, and advocating for a professional military sounded like authoritarianism. But the episode clarifies that while British theorist Liddell Hart later claimed to have invented blitzkrieg, the reality is that the Germans themselves invented it and put it into practice—which is why they won in 1939 and 1940. De Gaulle's writing remained at a relatively abstract theoretical level; it was the Germans who actually combined tanks, air support, and communications into a war-winning system. He did not.

32:49

On June 18 his broadcast came from nowhere: no government, no rank, no legitimacy

After de Gaulle's BBC broadcast, many people assumed he had established a French government-in-exile. He had not. Britain never recognized him as representing an exile government; Churchill's statement was only ‘I recognize you as the leader of Free Frenchmen wherever they may be’—a phrase that could mean almost anything. Britain even maintained diplomatic relations with the Vichy government; South Africa and Canada, as Commonwealth members, kept ambassadors in Vichy until 1942 despite having declared war on Germany. The military forces de Gaulle could actually command at that moment probably numbered only in the thousands. The British initially even assumed he would be nothing more than a brigade commander under British command.

— Richard Vinen
47:17

Before D-Day, Churchill treated him like a servant, not a partner

When the Allies prepared for the Normandy invasion, they kept de Gaulle in the dark until the last moment, then refused to allow him to broadcast to the French people as the representative of a French government. De Gaulle saw this as a profound humiliation; decades later in the 1960s he was still saying that when Churchill summoned him, it felt like a country gentleman summoning his estate manager. Consequently, French forces' participation in Normandy was symbolic rather than militarily significant, and de Gaulle was almost unwilling to commit any troops to the invasion. What actually changed his situation was the overwhelming welcome he received when he landed on French soil—the first time he truly met the French people face to face. That showed the Americans and British that he really could inspire French passion.

— Richard Vinen
1:01:43

He pushed for Algerian independence from racism, not principle

When de Gaulle returned to power in 1958, he himself hadn't yet decided what to do about Algeria. He told crowds in Algiers ‘I understand you’, and both settlers and the military took this as support for keeping France in Algeria. But he was deliberately ambiguous. The episode is blunt: de Gaulle was not a natural anti-colonialist, but a racist. He didn't believe France could genuinely integrate with an Algeria that would bring a large Muslim population; he advocated complete separation for that reason. He said he was ‘a man of the nineteenth century, born in the age of oil lamps and sailing ships’, then immediately added that policy untethered from reality has no value—and the reality was that France was no longer an empire. This decision later triggered military mutiny and multiple assassination attempts against him.

— Richard Vinen
1:05:59

Assassination attempts didn't destroy him; they cemented his grip on power

Extremists opposed to Algerian independence made multiple attempts on de Gaulle's life. The most famous came when his Citroën took roughly 200 machine-gun bullets; the driver kept the car moving despite the blown-out tires and got de Gaulle to safety. De Gaulle got out, brushed the glass from his sleeve, and said ‘they can't shoot straight’. The episode argues that this near-supernatural calm didn't weaken him—it reinforced his mythology, which he then used to push through constitutional reform. Claiming his life hung by a thread, he seized the moment to introduce direct presidential election in 1962. In 1965, at age 75, he became the only person in French history to be elected president by popular vote.

— Richard Vinen
1:13:19

He resigned at the peak of power; France forgave him only after he died

After the May 1968 upheaval, de Gaulle's party won a sweeping victory in parliamentary elections. But the episode points out that de Gaulle disliked being ‘an elderly figure people felt reassured by’, so he found a minor referendum question, declared that if it failed he would resign immediately, and when it did fail, he issued one brief statement and departed. He returned to Paris only once after that, and died little more than a year later. When he died, he was not a figure beloved by all France. Mitterrand and others had long defined themselves by opposing him. What finally transformed his image into something beyond party politics and widely respected was France's ability, after his death, to close the book on Vichy and Algeria—historical burdens that could finally be put to rest.

— Richard Vinen

Glossary

Appeal of 18 June
De Gaulle's radio address broadcast from the BBC on June 18, 1940, calling on the French to continue resisting Germany.
Fifth Republic
The system of government de Gaulle established in 1958 with a new constitution, in which the president holds real power; still in use today.
RPF (Rassemblement du Peuple Français)
The political movement de Gaulle founded in 1947 to oppose the Fourth Republic's constitution.
Free French
The resistance forces de Gaulle organized in London, representing those who refused to surrender to Germany.
