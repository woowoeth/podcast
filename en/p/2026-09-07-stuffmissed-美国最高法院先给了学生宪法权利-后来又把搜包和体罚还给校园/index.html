The Supreme Court First Gave Students Constitutional Rights, Then Shrunk Them: Lockers, Paddles, and the Schoolhouse Gate

From refusing to salute the flag to searching a purse, six cases trace the arc of public school students' constitutional rights; the Court declared that 'schools are not a totalitarian enclave' while also upholding corporal punishment and reasonable-suspicion searches, narrowing the boundary of rights.

Flag Salute Case: Students' Rights Enter the Schoolhouse

Can students really claim First Amendment rights during class? This case actually started with religion: Jehovah's Witnesses families believed saluting the flag violated the commandments against other gods and idolatry, so they refused to salute. In 1940 the Supreme Court ruled against them, and World War II led more districts to require daily salutes and pledges. West Virginia expelled students who refused to recite the pledge and labeled them delinquents; parents could be fined or jailed. In 1943, the Court reversed 6-3. Justice Jackson wrote on Flag Day: no official can define orthodoxy in politics, nationalism, religion, or other opinions; students need not confess words that contradict their hearts or make empty gestures. This was the first time the Court established that students have some First Amendment rights inside school.

11:21

Official Prayer Ruled Unconstitutional

During the Cold War, America cast itself as a God-fearing nation; in 1954 'Under God' was added to the Pledge, and more public schools began reading scripture or praying before class. In 1951, New York's Board of Regents asked religious leaders to compose a short, non-denominational prayer, leaving adoption to local districts. Districts that used it allowed students to opt out, but parents of ten students sued anyway. In 1962, Engel v. Vitale struck it down 6-1. The key was not coercion but that the prayer was government-written, itself constituting an establishment of religion. Justice Black quoted Madison: 'It is proper to take alarm at the first experiment on our liberties'; the same power that could establish Christianity to the exclusion of other religions could easily establish one sect of Christianity. The decision sparked fierce backlash; the plaintiff families faced ridicule and harassment.

— Tracy V. Wilson
18:31

Armbands Are Constitutional Expression

In December 1965, Des Moines middle and high school students planned to wear black armbands to protest US involvement in Vietnam. The school announced that refusing to remove them would lead to suspension, yet some students—including four siblings—wore them and were suspended. In 1969, the Supreme Court ruled for the students 7-2. The issue was not ordinary dress code but a quiet, passive expression close to pure speech, and it did not disrupt class. Without evidence of actual or materially substantial interference with schoolwork, school officials could not suppress student opinion. The opinion declared: 'It can hardly be argued that either students or teachers shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate.' This 'substantial disruption' test became the benchmark for campus speech. Justice Black dissented, predicting students would defy all rules; the hosts note that this slippery-slope argument sounds quite absurd.

24:40

School-Sponsored Newspaper Can Be Censored

In 1983, Hazelwood East High School's student newspaper, The Spectrum, had two articles that made principal Reynolds uneasy: one on teen pregnancy used pseudonyms but might still identify the interviewee, and a divorce story named and criticized a student's parent. Reynolds decided to delete the entire two pages rather than just the articles, citing lack of time to edit and the need to publish before semester's end. In 1988, the Supreme Court ruled for the school 5-3. The key distinction was between 'tolerating student speech' and 'promoting student speech': the paper was funded by the school, readers would see it as bearing the school's imprimatur, and the articles were indeed immature journalistically. The Court held that schools may regulate content they sponsor. This drew the first clear boundary around Tinker's rights.

29:46

Corporal Punishment Not Cruel and Unusual

Host Tracy suddenly recalled that many teachers kept wooden paddles on their desks when she was a child, and research shows corporal punishment has not disappeared in America. In the 1977 case Ingram v. Wright, the school's paddle was less than two feet long, three to four inches wide, and half an inch thick, with one to five strokes as the norm; but Ingram was hit more than 20 times in a single incident and required medical attention. The Court did not dwell on whether this particular case was excessive; it ruled that school corporal punishment does not violate the Constitution. The Eighth Amendment protects convicted persons; prisoners and schoolchildren are separated by 'the harsh fact of criminal conviction and incarceration.' Schools are open institutions with teachers and peers as witnesses, and students go home at the end of the day. According to NEA statistics, corporal punishment remains legal in public schools in 17 states and is actually used in 14; boys, Black students, and students with disabilities are paddled at significantly higher rates.

— Tracy V. Wilson
33:52

Purse Search Needs Only Reasonable Suspicion

In 1980, a 14-year-old New Jersey student, TLO, was reported by a teacher for smoking in the restroom. Vice Principal Choplik searched her purse, found cigarettes and rolling papers, then continued searching and found what appeared to be marijuana and evidence of drug dealing. TLO was convicted and sentenced to a year's probation. In 1985, the Supreme Court upheld the search 6-3. The Court acknowledged that the Fourth Amendment applies to school officials and that students have a legitimate expectation of privacy, but it must be balanced against the school's need to maintain a learning environment. Therefore, school searches need not meet the probable cause standard required for police; a lower threshold of reasonable suspicion suffices. The teacher's report gave the vice principal reason to suspect she had cigarettes, and the purse was the obvious place; seeing rolling papers naturally extended suspicion to marijuana, which was also reasonable.

Glossary

Substantial Disruption Test
The threshold from Tinker for disciplining student speech: actual material disruption or evidence that it would substantially interfere with schoolwork.
Establishment Clause
The First Amendment clause prohibiting government establishment or endorsement of religion; originally applied only to the federal government, later extended to states via the Fourteenth Amendment.
reasonable suspicion
A lower evidentiary standard than probable cause, applied to school officials' searches of students and their belongings.
probable cause
The higher evidentiary standard required for police arrests or searches; contrasted in this episode with reasonable suspicion for school searches.
corporal punishment
Punishment involving physical pain; in this episode, paddling students on the buttocks with a wooden board.
closed-circuit recipients
People who can only passively receive what the state chooses to indoctrinate, without the ability to express their own views.
