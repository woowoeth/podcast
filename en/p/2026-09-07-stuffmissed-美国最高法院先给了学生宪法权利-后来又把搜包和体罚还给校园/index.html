Stuff You Missed in History Class 2026-09-07

The Supreme Court Gave Students Constitutional Rights, Then Handed Searches and Paddlings Back to the Schools

From refusing to salute the flag to having a purse searched, six cases gradually drew the line of American public school students' constitutional rights; the Supreme Court laid down that "schools are not totalitarian enclaves" while accepting that corporal punishment and reasonable-suspicion searches do not violate the Constitution, and the boundary of those rights narrowed as a result.

The flag-salute case: student rights enter the schoolhouse first

Can students really invoke the First Amendment in class? This case actually begins with religion: Jehovah's Witnesses families held that saluting the flag violated the commandments against having other gods and against graven images, and refused to pledge or salute the flag. In 1940 the Supreme Court had ruled against them, and World War II pushed more school districts to require students to salute and recite the pledge daily. West Virginia expelled students who refused the pledge, labeled them delinquents, and their parents could be fined or even jailed. In 1943 the Supreme Court overturned the earlier ruling 6-3, and Justice Jackson wrote the opinion on Flag Day: orthodoxy in politics, nationalism, religion or other matters of opinion is not to be defined by any official; students need not utter words they do not believe, nor make empty gestures. This was the first time the Court established that students hold some First Amendment rights inside school.

11:21

An official prayer text is held unconstitutional

In the Cold War the United States cast itself as a God-fearing nation, added "Under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954, and more public schools began reading scripture or praying before class. In 1951 the New York State Board of Regents asked religious leaders to co-write a short, non-denominational prayer, with each district deciding whether to adopt it. Districts that used the prayer let students opt out, but the parents of 10 students still sued. In 1962 Engel v. Vitale held it unconstitutional 6-1; the key was not whether it was compulsory but that the prayer was written by the government, which itself constituted establishing religion. Justice Black quoted Madison: it is proper to take alarm at the first experiment on our liberties; the same authority that could establish Christianity to the exclusion of other religions could easily establish one Christian sect. The ruling provoked fierce backlash, and the plaintiff families were mocked and harassed.

18:31

An armband is constitutional expression

In December 1965, middle and high school students in Des Moines planned to wear black armbands to class to protest American involvement in the Vietnam War. School officials announced in advance that refusal to remove them would mean suspension, and some students — including four siblings — wore the armbands anyway and were suspended. In 1969 the Supreme Court ruled for the students 7-2. The issue was that this was not ordinary dress or grooming but quiet, passive expression close to pure speech, and it did not disrupt class. Absent evidence of actual disruption or a demonstrable likelihood of substantial interference with teaching, school officials may not suppress student opinion. The ruling said: public schools may not be totalitarian enclaves, and students and teachers do not shed their constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate. This "substantial disruption" standard later became the baseline for free speech on campus. Justice Black dissented that students would from then on resist almost every school rule, and the host says on the show that this slippery-slope argument sounds rather absurd.

24:40

A school-funded newspaper can be cut

In 1983 two pieces in the Hazelwood East High School student newspaper The Spectrum troubled principal Reynolds: a story on teen pregnancy that used pseudonyms but might still allow the interviewees to be identified, and a divorce story that named and criticized a student's parent. Reynolds decided to cut both full pages rather than just remove the articles, on the grounds that there was no time to revise and the paper had to come out before the semester ended. In 1988 the Supreme Court ruled for the school 5-3. The key distinction is between "tolerating student speech" and "promoting student speech": this newspaper was funded and published by the school, readers would take its contents as endorsed by the school, and the two pieces were indeed immature by journalistic ethics. The Court held that school officials may exercise control over what they promote. The rights established in Tinker were thus given their first clear boundary.

— Tracy V. Wilson
29:46

Corporal punishment is not an Eighth Amendment matter

Host Tracy suddenly recalls that many teachers kept wooden paddles on their desks when she was a child, and on looking it up finds corporal punishment has still not died out in the United States. In the 1977 case Ingram v. Wright, the school's paddle was under two feet long, three or four inches wide and half an inch thick, with one to five swats in principle; but Ingram was struck more than 20 times in a single incident and needed medical attention. The Supreme Court did not dwell on whether that instance was excessive but held that school corporal punishment does not violate the Constitution. The reasoning: the Eighth Amendment protects the convicted, and prisoners are separated from schoolchildren by "the harsh facts of criminal conviction and incarceration"; schools are open places, with teachers and classmates as witnesses, and you can go home after school. According to NEA figures, corporal punishment remains legal in public schools in 17 states and is actually practiced in 14, with boys, Black students and students with disabilities struck at significantly higher rates.

— Tracy V. Wilson
33:52

A searched bag needs only reasonable suspicion

In 1980 in New Jersey, a 14-year-old student known as TLO was reported by a teacher for smoking in the restroom. Vice principal Choplik searched her purse, saw cigarettes and rolling papers, kept digging, and found what appeared to be marijuana and evidence of drug dealing; TLO was ultimately convicted and given a year's probation. In 1985 the Supreme Court upheld the search 6-3. The ruling acknowledged that the Fourth Amendment binds school officials and that students have a privacy expectation, but that it must be balanced against the school's need to maintain a learning environment. So school searches need not meet the probable cause police require; the lower threshold of reasonable suspicion suffices. The teacher's report that TLO was smoking gave the vice principal reason to suspect she had cigarettes, and a purse is exactly where they would be; seeing rolling papers naturally extended the suspicion to marijuana, which was equally reasonable.

Glossary

Substantial Disruption Test
The threshold established in Tinker for school punishment of student speech: there must be actual serious disruption or evidence that teaching would be disrupted.
Establishment Clause
The First Amendment clause barring government from establishing or supporting religion; it originally bound only the federal government and was later applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.
reasonable suspicion
An evidentiary threshold lower than probable cause, the standard applied when school officials search a student's belongings.
probable cause
The higher evidentiary standard police need to arrest or search, used in this episode to contrast with reasonable suspicion for searches in schools.
corporal punishment
Discipline that inflicts physical pain, in this episode the practice of striking students on the buttocks with a wooden paddle.
closed-circuit recipients
People who can only receive in one direction what the state chooses to instill and cannot express opinions of their own.
