Moving Trees Uphill: The Bet and the Cost of Saving Forests

In high-elevation burn scars, seedlings moved up from lower ground survive at 80% to 90%; but seed banks are limited, and staking precious genetic material on unproven large-scale planting is itself a gamble.

When the big trees die, the next generation has no seed

Where high-intensity fire has passed through, large areas are left with no living trees. Many conifers depend on the seed bank in cones on living trees to regenerate the next generation; with no living trees, natural regeneration has nothing to work from. Scientists are seeing natural regeneration fall short of expectations more and more often in high-severity burn areas in the interior. Layer climate change on top and the seedlings themselves are sensitive; managers report that planted seedlings don't survive the way they used to. The low-elevation Ponderosa pine belt is already at its ecological edge, with little natural regeneration and poor survival of replanting, and some worry these places will degrade into grassland or shrubland.

6:12

Assisted migration means moving seed somewhere warmer

The core worry is this: replant with local seed sources and the trees may be adapted to the past climate, not today's or the future's. Hence assisted migration — taking seed from somewhere slightly warmer (downslope or from a lower latitude) and planting it at higher elevation. In this burn scar, researchers planted 11,000 seedlings of 7 conifer species, putting high-elevation species down low and low-elevation species up high, with seed sources drawn from multiple elevation bands, to see how each performed.

— Katie Langan
9:21

Trees live for centuries, so which climate do you aim for

A tree can live for centuries, and the climate when it germinates will not be the climate when it is 50 or 100 years old, so there is no simple answer to ‘which climate should we aim for’. British Columbia is relatively far ahead: in 2023 it implemented a planting regime that roughly plants at the quarter point of the so-called species rotation — that is, discounted to the point in the forestry cycle when the plan is to come back and harvest, essentially a hedge, putting the tree at its optimal fit at about a quarter of its life cycle. Others can make different choices, but this really is hard.

— Katie Langan
12:24

High-elevation sites see 80% to 90% survival

The high-elevation site is about eight kilometres from the ignition point, and seedlings planted in 2022 have already grown to around half a metre, doing very well. The only poor performer is the low-elevation pinyon pine. Camille Stevens-Rumman says every high-elevation site looks like this, with survival across all species planted in 2022 at 80% to 90%. Ponderosa pine, Douglas fir and pinyon pine don't naturally grow at that elevation, but a good number of the Ponderosa and Douglas fir have so far survived there. The conclusion only goes as far as ‘seedlings can survive’; the long term remains unknown.

— Camille Stevens-Rumman
13:28

Seed is a scarce resource, not something to scatter

Maren Chambers says she is on the conservative side here: committing precious biological resources to large-scale implementation when we don't yet know whether it works strikes her as irresponsible, so she favours small-scale trials first. Seed collection itself is extremely labour-intensive — sending people out to stands that have seed, trucking it back to nurseries to grow seedlings, then returning the seedlings to the forest — and every step of that pipeline faces a capacity problem; the United States currently can't keep up. Enormous effort went into selecting multiple seed sources per species, with workers handling seedlings in near-freezer conditions, all so the seedlings can get into the ground as soon as they reach the site rather than dying before they are planted.

— Maren Chambers
15:33

Short term you watch survival; long term you wait decades

The key metric is first survival: seedling roots are short in the first years, and a dry spell in the soil or a cold snap can kill them. But long-term performance matters just as much. A study launching in 2028 will plant 40,000 seedlings in Colorado and track them for decades; the study Katie visited will follow its trees for roughly 10 years, still not enough for long-lived species. Another study will space the trees further apart so their growth can be tracked as they mature and begin to reproduce.

— Katie Langan
16:36

Snow is a protective layer for seedlings, not just a water source

These Christmas-tree-shaped conifers have long branches that shade the ground and delay spring snowmelt, which benefits downstream water users by avoiding an early melt surge; but the roots also take up water, and snow sitting on the branches sublimates in winter, lost straight out of the system, so it is hard to quantify precisely. What surprised Katie is that snow is good for the seedlings themselves: scientists think high-elevation snowpack provides an insulating layer that helps seedlings ride out winter wind and cold. And as the climate changes, that layer of snow may be shrinking, making the whole equation more complicated.

— Katie Langan
18:40

Whether there should be forest here is now a human choice

At the low-elevation Ponderosa pine site, scientists look at rows of dead seedlings and start discussing almost philosophical questions: should the same genetic material be put back on this land? Is that the best investment? Or would those resources be better spent on another ecosystem? No one has an answer. Once you start intervening, you have to decide again at a very basic level: should there be forest here? Can forest survive here? And if so, who should its members be? Choices that nature used to make now fall to people.

— Katie Langan

Glossary

assisted migration
Moving seeds or seedlings from warmer areas to warmer or higher-elevation sites to match the future climate.
subalpine forest
The coniferous forest belt below the treeline and above the alpine zone.
serotinous cones
Cones whose seeds are sealed in resin, released only after fire melts the resin.
species rotation
A forestry term for the timespan over which a manager plans to come back and harvest a tree.
theory of mind
The capacity to attribute mental states — thoughts, perceptions, emotions — to others.
comparative cognition
An interdisciplinary umbrella for the study of animal minds, spanning comparative psychology, ethology and more.
