Two hundred million invested, company valuation grew to over 20 billion—she attributes this to a repeatable framework: calculate risk-return ratios precisely, then let probability work in your favor, not just luck.

6:03

Create scarcity, become leverage

In a male-dominated engineering school, she founded a speakers association, monopolizing the scarce resource of articulate, polished people. Her insight: platform determines who you can connect with, scarcity determines why they choose you, leverage multiplies how far those connections reach. She'd reuse this framework repeatedly—in building investor networks, in launching her media presence.

9:04

Market noise drove her out of equities

As a chemical-industry analyst early in her career, she updated morning and evening reports daily, graded on daily KPIs, jerked around by market swings. She's methodical by nature—she prefers to think things through and build long-term commitment to one company. Daily price noise gave her no positive feedback. The unhappy internship confirmed she belonged in venture capital, not following conventional prestige.

— Wang Jin
12:06

Signed the offer, was told not to come

She accepted her dream job, signed the three-party agreement, declined all other offers, then received a pre-start call: her future direct manager didn't like her, the firm wouldn't buy out the contract, and suggested she voluntarily withdraw. She found it absurd—"this society is truly ridiculous"—but recovered quickly, activating her internship network to land another role after hiring season officially closed, eventually into venture capital.

— Wang Jin
20:07

Halved the valuation, scored 100x return

She independently researched the OLED-replacing-LCD inflection point, discovered Jinzhida (金志达), a testing-equipment maker, and validated the domestic-substitution logic. The founder wanted 400 million valuation; she and her boss mapped his cash needs, available options, and psychological floor, closing at 200 million. The company later went public, reaching over 20 billion in valuation—roughly 100x paper return. Luck is a venture capitalist's core competency, she says, but "once it becomes repeatable, it stops being pure luck."

— Wang Jin
27:09

Used the same playbook, misjudged the person

She deployed the same domestic-substitution framework on an upstream consumables company—profits, valuation, market position all checked out, she nearly invested on autopilot. But she'd only seriously engaged with a capable #2; the actual CEO wielded far more power than she realized. When the deal failed, it became her first loss at the new firm, cementing her lesson: "choice matters far more than effort"—know when to cut.

— Wang Jin
37:14

Seventy-six pages, read aloud at altitude

She learned through industry gossip of a no-storefront company with breakthrough technology. A site visit nearly got her mistaken for a scammer; a cofounder told her off and said to come back when she'd done her homework. Two weeks of intensive diligence produced a 60,000+ character report. When two giants competed for the deal, she upgraded her plane ticket on her own dime, spent 2+ hours on the flight forcing her boss to read every single page of her 76-page memo, and landed with her boss telling the founders: "We're all in."

— Wang Jin
45:21

Silicon Valley taught her: hardware is the edge

She traveled to Silicon Valley expecting more AI anxiety, only to discover American engineers barely discussed hardware. Software efficiency means nothing divorced from physical reality—and that's precisely where China has structural advantage. She watched one portfolio company close five aerospace-engine deals in a single three-day trade show, 10–20 million per unit. Back home, she pivoted her thesis to 2C deep-tech products aimed at global markets.

— Wang Jin
1:00:29

Lower your wants—this becomes the formula

She describes herself as quick to learn but unambitious, chasing no fads, practicing long-termism—that's her boundary. She defines happiness as a ratio: what you want, divided by what you can actually achieve. Her non-negotiables are health and immediate family; everything else is upside. After a decade, she realized something more vital than health itself: vitality—the life force nourished by reciprocal relationships, extinguished by extractive ones.

— Wang Jin
