A Fed Chair Never Loses a Vote: Warsh Talks Against Forward Guidance, but His Path Is Paved for Hikes

Posen gives Warsh's Jackson Hole speech a B-, saying he talks against forward guidance but is actually paving the way for rate hikes, and predicts the Fed will hike 75-100 basis points over the next six months.

4:07

Warsh didn't say hike, but the whole speech was paving the way

Posen gives Warsh's Jackson Hole speech a B-, but thinks it deserves a B+ given the chaos of the previous two months. The fourth section systematically lists reasons inflation could persist or even rise, yet never says 'therefore we must tighten.' Posen sees this as deliberate groundwork: if no hike follows, markets will speculate it's a capitulation to Trump's pressure. He himself has been arguing for hikes for months because inflation is real. Warsh also reaffirmed the 2% core PCE target and refused to use wage inflation as a predictive indicator—all mainstream moves.

8:14

Without defining the 'right pace,' returning to target is empty talk

Posen's biggest worry is that Warsh ended his speech saying inflation needs to fall in the 'right direction' at the 'right pace,' without ever defining what the right pace is. Without a pace constraint, 'returning to target' is empty talk. From his confirmation hearing, two press conferences, to the ECB Sintra speech, Warsh has reserved the right to decide at the last minute, refusing to pre-commit to his own observation metrics. This is close to Greenspan's 1999 style, but Greenspan held it together through personal skill and control of the committee; once you err, the whole credibility collapses.

— Adam Posen
12:20

A Fed chair never loses a single vote

Posen explains Fed committee culture: unlike the ECB, which seeks consensus, the Fed has historically given the chair more power. More critically, there's an unwritten rule—only a few dissent per meeting, and the chair never loses a vote. When Volcker found he might lose, he simply announced it would be his last meeting. This culture makes the committee's ability to correct the chair very weak, so Warsh's 'reserve discretion' style is riskier than he thinks.

— Adam Posen
19:26

Yelling at the central bank isn't dangerous; making it sell Treasuries is

Posen distinguishes two types of political pressure: ordinary central bank bashing is part of the game and can even help the central bank take the blame when necessary. What's truly dangerous is when a president or Treasury secretary asks the central bank to help sell government debt—the central bank must answer, 'I'm here to sell debt for you and your successors,' and must never manipulate or cheat. Trump's threats to the Fed are far more serious than rhetoric—trying to strip regional Fed presidents of voting rights, disrupt staggered terms, change the mandate, and touch the budget directly undermines functional independence.

— Adam Posen
24:34

The most radical reform might be making the central bank talk less

Posen thinks the communications review led by Mervyn King is most likely to produce radical proposals. King himself is a founder of inflation targeting and fan charts, but since retirement he has publicly questioned central banks' forecasting ability and information supply: too much noise makes markets dependent on official guidance, creating moral hazard and bubbles. Peter Fisher similarly argues that central bank 'certainty' dulls market risk sensitivity. Both may push for less, not more, information release. Warsh's 'hall of mirrors' metaphor in his speech already admits markets aren't always right.

— Adam Posen
34:51

Economists entering AI companies lose their independent voice

Posen thinks the rush of economists to AI companies isn't unprecedented—it happened during the internet era—but this time is more troubling. Firms like Anthropic hire economists partly to design AI transition policies, and these scholars genuinely believe they're doing good. The problem is that once inside, they enter the corporate hierarchy, losing independent voice and freedom to choose topics, and their views are rationally discounted by outsiders. Posen's hope is that scholars stay at research institutes, earning low six-figure salaries, and still have public influence.

— Adam Posen
38:53

AI replacement hasn't happened yet; programmer employment is still growing

Posen says labor market data doesn't yet show AI replacement—even programmer and truck driver employment growth continues. Brynjolfsson's J-curve theory holds that firms need time to reorganize, and there will be a period of human-AI collaboration first. Garicano's messy jobs theory points out that seemingly automatable jobs actually contain a lot of tacit knowledge and interpersonal relations—truck drivers don't just drive. Large-scale replacement might take about five years, and it will first show up in reduced hiring of young people.

— Adam Posen
54:13

Rates will be 75-100 basis points higher in six months

Posen reiterates his earlier forecast of 4% year-end PCE inflation is materializing, with CPI already above 4% early in the year. The bigger issue is inflation inertia: while energy prices may cause a slight dip in the next month or two, core services inflation is sticky, with 3/6/12-month moving averages all rising. He expects the Fed to hike in September or December; if September, December will follow, and in six months the federal funds rate will be 75-100 basis points higher than now. Only then will inflation truly begin to decline, hovering in the 3.5%-4.5% range in the meantime.

— Adam Posen

Glossary

fiscal dominance
A situation where the government forces the central bank to accommodate fiscal financing, undermining monetary policy independence.
fan charts
Charts that display forecast uncertainty using fan-shaped probability intervals.
J-curve
A trajectory where technology initially causes a dip in performance before significant improvement after adjustment.
messy jobs
Jobs that seem easy to automate but actually involve tacit skills and interpersonal relations.
staggered terms
Appointments of regional Fed presidents are staggered to prevent wholesale replacement.
