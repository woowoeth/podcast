Okinawa's Turn Is Not a Defeat for Pro-China Forces, It's Young People Accepting Reality and Wanting Material Benefits

In the Okinawa gubernatorial election, the side opposing the US military base lost after nearly two decades in power. This is not a left-right shift, nor a change in attitude toward China: after decades of protest with the base not moving an inch, young voters have accepted reality and want immediate economic benefits.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:03

The approval rating holds steady because the Diet is out of session

The Diet session ended, and there was no political agenda all summer, meaning no occasion for the Prime Minister to lose points—in the show's words, "say less, err less." Takaichi is now governing entirely on X: no press conferences, no facing the media, just posting those very long tweets. So the approval rating holding steady is neither a new policy adding points nor a crisis being resolved. The real test comes in September: the Diet reconvenes, plus a dense diplomatic season from September to the end of the year—in the show's words, entering a troubled autumn.

12:10

Okinawa's turn is not a left-right struggle

Denny Tamaki's third term came to an abrupt end, and his opponent was a pure newcomer born in 1972, with no memory of the US occupation. Exit polls showed that among the issues voters cared most about, "economic revitalization" accounted for 49.5%, while "base issues" accounted for only 20.9%; the vote was 420,000 to 270,000. This is not Okinawa becoming conservative, but exhaustion after decades of protest with the base not moving an inch: after nearly two decades in power, voters felt "enough, let's try something different," and wanted immediate economic benefits. As soon as the newcomer won, the central government's Okinawa promotion budget immediately jumped by 300 billion yen and was announced publicly. Japanese Communist Party leader Tomoko Tamura cried over this, saying it amounted to indirect vote-buying. Taiwan media portrayed it as a "pro-China defeat," which is stirring the pot.

20:14

The US Treasury Secretary has become Japan's supreme overlord

Bessent has already directly spoken to the market, demanding the yen stop falling. The show says he "can basically already become the MacArthur of Japan's economic circles," a supreme overlord giving direct instructions. Why would a US Treasury Secretary watch the yen? Because a weak yen drags down US Treasuries, and the US runs entirely on debt issuance, with debt already exceeding 40 trillion dollars—unsellable Treasuries are the most fatal issue. He first wrote down on paper how much money would be needed to intervene in the exchange rate, was photographed by the media as a hint, found it couldn't hold for even a week, and then switched to explicit statements, saying he is the market maker with 6 billion dollars in hand. The Bank of Japan subsequently signaled it would not rule out consecutive rate hikes.

30:18

No political party dares oppose tax cuts

Takaichi's Achilles' heel is fiscal and exchange rate policy. She is the heir to the Abe line, and the narrative that "a certain degree of inflation is acceptable, a weak yen benefits exports" has been digested by the Japanese public for many years; but even the mind that once guided Abe's three arrows is now saying that Japan has entered an inflationary channel and can no longer pursue active fiscal policy. The trouble is that tax cuts are highly politically correct in Japanese politics—all parties are shouting for them, and if you don't follow, you get no votes. The only party that raised an objection is too insignificant. Shigeru Ishiba, Taro Kono, and Obuchi Keiko have all publicly opposed it, and Obuchi Keiko even resigned as chairman of the Policy Research Council over it. Moreover, the tax cut plan itself has been made complicated; restaurant owners complain they're being toyed with: buy this and it's reduced, buy that and it's not, with different explanations every time.

41:23

Only the LDP can defeat the LDP

Takaichi is currently stable because her public support is high, and those within the party who want to challenge her don't dare declare too early. But the more fundamental problem in Japanese politics is that the opposition parties are ineffective: the Democratic Party got a chance in 2009 but did too poorly, the public was scared, and for over a decade since then they haven't dared give the opposition any chance, always voting LDP. So no matter how the opposition splits and merges, it can't shake the structure; the show believes there will be no earth-shattering change in Japanese domestic politics. The real variable is within the LDP: Takaichi has public opinion but no faction base of her own, and must rely on the Aso faction—the two have a love-hate relationship, needing each other yet guarding against each other.

50:27

The cabinet reshuffle changes neither the soup nor the medicine

The observation point is whether the Secretary-General is replaced, because that is an indicator of the Takaichi-Aso relationship. The result was that key positions didn't move and the Secretary-General stayed on. In July and August, there were rumors that Aso was dissatisfied with Takaichi and had a three-hour secret meeting with Toshimitsu Motegi, making people think they were about to fall out; in the end, no personnel changes, which can only be interpreted as Takaichi continuing to compromise with Aso—"the attempted rebellion failed." The logic is simple: policies like active fiscal policy and whether to raise rates can't be pushed without Aso faction support, and Aso, from the Ministry of Finance, has many handles on her. Another observation point is whether Koichi Hagiuda returns to a key post—that would mean the black money party ticket issue is over for him.

1:00:32

The most promising non-mainstream candidate is Yoshimasa Hayashi

In the last leadership election, Yoshimasa Hayashi came in third; Shinjiro Koizumi threw his support to Takaichi and joined the cabinet as Defense Minister, while Hayashi himself took the Internal Affairs and Communications Minister post. He is seen as capable of challenging Takaichi: he follows the LDP's traditional dovish line, completely different from Takaichi, and can rally the anti-Takaichi party base. But to challenge the incumbent president, staying in the cabinet means being controlled and having no time to travel the countryside, and local votes are his weak point—he originally belonged to the Kishida faction, so he doesn't worry about parliamentary votes, but lacks local name recognition and trust. Makiko Tanaka's assessment of him: long in the House of Councillors, not aggressive enough in politics, not ruthless enough, hasn't dirtied his hands and feet, more like a staff member than a daimyo. So whether he accepts the next cabinet position is itself Takaichi testing him.

1:13:37

As long as Takaichi is in office, there will be no change

From mid-to-late August, Japanese lawmakers visited China one after another, and many Japanese media interpreted it as a softening of China's attitude. The leading lawmaker clarified upon return: he proposed the idea in February this year, China agreed in May, and the trip took place in August—it was not a Chinese invitation. The International Liaison Department's response was direct: as long as Japan's attitude doesn't change, there will be no change at the government level. And Takaichi cannot retract her remarks or apologize, so as long as she is in office, there can be no fundamental change. The show goes further: even if someone else takes over, if they continue with ambiguous statements, it won't be recognized either. The show cites Shigeru Ishiba's view that Japan originally had room for ambiguous statements, but it was Japan itself that spoke definitively and backed itself into a corner.
