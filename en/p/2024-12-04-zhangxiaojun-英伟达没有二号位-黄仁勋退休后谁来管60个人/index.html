Nvidia Has No Clear No. 2: Who Runs the 60 Direct Reports When Jensen Huang Retires

Nvidia's strategy wasn't planned — it was locked in after tactics like three teams, two seasons and shipping a whole cow proved themselves. But the company still has no obvious No. 2.

NV1 lost because it led on tech but wasn't compatible with the mainstream

Nvidia's first product, NV1, used 3D graphics acceleration, while the market at the time was broadly on 2D standards. They judged the market would move quickly from 2D to 3D, but the transition was slower than expected. The hottest game at the time, Doom, was still 2D, so NV1's newest graphics chip couldn't run the best-selling game. Priem also added audio to the chip, but used his own standard, incompatible with the then-popular Sound Blaster. The result was a product that won praise but not sales — customers fed back that your graphics display is pretty good, but take out the audio and I'll buy it.

— Zhou Kanglin
29:24

One contract clause saved a dying Nvidia

During the NV2 era, Nvidia signed an agreement with the Japanese game company Sega. Huang added a clause to the contract: as long as I can produce a chip prototype and it works on your motherboard, you have to pay me — rather than requiring me to mass-produce. That million dollars of revenue gave the company financial breathing room. Yajun says the contract originated from a passage in the contract at the time; without that passage, NV3 might never have come out, and the company might have died then.

— Wang Yajun
34:27

Riva 128 won the bet on redundancy and a simulator

Riva 128 was Nvidia's comeback product from the brink. The plan no longer chased its own leading-edge technology elements, but followed the technology route the market recognized, packing in the most transistors. The company had only that little bit of money left and could afford only one tape-out. To guarantee success, they did something few had done before: simulating and debugging the chip's operation on a simulator. They also left some blank space and redundancy in the chip design, and that redundancy ultimately saved it — and that decision wasn't made by Huang, it was made by architect Priem.

— Wang Yajun
54:40

Three teams, two seasons was fought out, not planned

The chip development cycle at the time was about 18 months, which would miss two PC selling seasons. Nvidia split its teams into three: while one team designed the chip, the other two supported it, compressing the development cycle to under six months. This strategy wasn't thought up from the start — it was forced out of the situation during Riva 128, when the books only had enough cash for nine months, and after buying the emulator the estimate dropped to six. After doing it, they found the company could work this way, and only then locked it in. 3DFX later tried to copy it, and found that what Nvidia did was just execution and a strict spec sheet.

— Dong Shiming
1:00:43

Shipping a whole cow: holding the low end for just over a dollar

Chips need quality testing after manufacturing, and poorer-quality products used to be scrapped. Nvidia realized it could lower the clock speed on these products and push them to market as low-end products, spending just a little to fix them — a bit over a dollar — but holding a market segment so others simply couldn't get in. This is ‘shipping a whole cow’ — one cow eaten many ways, nothing wasted. This strategy also wasn't dreamed up in the office; it came out of Huang talking with employees about cost control and discovering a special packaging cost of about a dollar or so.

— Wang Yajun
1:14:55

Wall Street didn't buy CUDA, and Huang didn't waver

While developing CUDA, the whole Wall Street market was very disapproving. At offline meetings people kept asking: are you going to spend a lot on new product development, will it hit gross margin hard — which left Huang quite depressed. Then the financial crisis hit, the stock fell terribly, but Huang never wavered, and the company kept investing in CUDA, looking for more use cases and a better architecture language. By now everyone sees Nvidia isn't just a hardware company, and the CUDA ecosystem makes it very hard for users to migrate to other chips.

— Dong Shiming
1:30:02

Priem thought the architecture was his; Huang said it was Nvidia's

Priem was an excellent architect; Riva 128 and three teams, two seasons were both built on his clever chip architecture design. But after the company reached a certain stage, he believed this is my architecture, while Huang believed it isn't your architecture, it's Nvidia's architecture — one person can't do everything. Priem was very possessive, and would delete or change the architecture without notifying colleagues. Later Huang demanded that what he deleted be restored and that next time he follow company process. Priem found that hard to accept, and combined with problems in his marriage, he ultimately chose to stop managing chip architecture and gradually left.

— Dong Shiming
1:33:03

Nvidia has no obvious No. 2

At the end the show discusses whether Nvidia has a No. 2: if Huang retires, how does the company run, who manages those 60 people. There's currently no very obvious news report or information indicating one person can take on that role, pick up Huang's baton, and keep leading the company in executing its existing systems and culture. Because the way he's chosen makes Nvidia an extension of his own personality — but if he retires, that's actually a problem.

— Zhou Kanglin
