罗永浩的十字路口 2026-02-11

Comfort breeds no creativity; the greater the conflict and difference, the better

Folk musician Zhang Weiwei looks back on a decade-plus from a small northwestern industrial town to rehearsing in Beijing basements: divorced parents, state-enterprise collapse, starving and busking in Guangzhou, band dissolution—each upheaval never crushed him, yet when life finally stabilized, he found himself truly unable to create.

2:02

Silver was built to serve mining, not discovered then developed for it

Silver is a Gansu industrial city created ‘from scratch’ for resource extraction—originally just a small county town on the Yellow River, but after Liberation populated from Shanghai, Hangzhou, Sichuan, and the Northeast to build factories and infrastructure around mineral development. Zhang Weiwei notes that locals never felt Silver was home—on New Year's Eve, the company housing was nearly empty, and staying for the holiday was seen as a sign of family trouble. This ‘manufactured city’ dynamic explains the widespread lack of belonging among mining-sector children, and the default life trajectory of inheriting your parent's job.

— Zhang Weiwei
18:14

Resist once or be bullied for the rest of your life

Luo Yonghao recalls his childhood and his friend who'd served time in jail: once you choose to endure, you're permanently classified as someone who can be pushed around; but if you resist even once—even if you get beaten worse in the moment—the other person starts to recruit you, treat you as one of them. He directly applies this to the workplace: many people give in once, and their boundaries keep getting pushed further back; stand up and take a step forward, and the boundary moves with you. This isn't motivational-speaker bravery—it's a specific social-psychological mechanism he's observed.

— Luo Yonghao
34:32

Parental crisis gave teenagers unexpected freedom to experiment

State-enterprise restructuring in the early 1990s threw Zhang Weiwei's parents into panic, leaving no bandwidth to supervise their kids—which gave a sixteen- or seventeen-year-old Zhang Weiwei enormous autonomy. After his parents divorced, he kept his household registration with his mother in Lanzhou but told his father he was with his mother and his mother he was with his father, collecting allowance from both sides. He's direct: those years were ‘like constant New Year's,’ his only regret being that this freedom window lasted less than a year or two total.

— Zhang Weiwei
58:54

Girls taught him music mattered more than any lesson could

Zhang Weiwei was forced by his father to play piano as a child and came to hate music. The real turning point came when Guolong brought four campus beauty-queens home; Zhang played ‘Für Elise’ and all four ‘their eyes went wide,’ even prompting Guolong to pick up drums to compete for their attention. From that point, he discovered his own improvisations—simple chord progressions behind pop songs, like adding accompaniment to ‘I Can't Help Falling in Love’—worked far better than trudging through Czerny études, because pop songs carry emotional weight and touch people more easily. Luo Yonghao adds: most people's introduction to music isn't respectable; ‘because of girls’ is the normal path, not the exception. True appreciation of Bach from childhood aristocratic education is rare.

— Zhang Weiwei
1:14:08

Magic Rock Trio redefined stardom more radically than Cui Jian

Zhang Weiwei argues that the emergence of the Magic Rock Trio (Dou Wei, Zhang Chu, He Yong) in 1994 was a more thorough ‘revolution’ than Cui Jian's ‘Resolution’ or Luo Dayou's ‘Comrade Lover’—previously, musicians like Cui Jian were still lumped in with 1980s singers in a single category, but the Trio for the first time made individual rock-star personas into something more than just ‘distance,’ presenting instead three completely different spiritual extremes. Zhang and Guolong and another friend even named themselves Dou Wei, Zhang Chu, and He Yong using fake names on health certificates, just to be called by those names at ticket booths.

— Zhang Weiwei
1:58:45

Hunger in Guangzhou forced them to gamble their dignity daily

In 1994, Zhang Weiwei was talked into going to Guangzhou to make his fortune by several ‘foolish friends’ who turned out to be completely unable to help; the group nearly became street people. Eventually only he and Guolong remained, surviving by busking in the underground passage beneath Times Square—because his voice was loud and his singing uninhibited, other street singers would automatically give way. When money ran out, the two played cards, betting with slaps: losing meant taking a hit to the face, converting physical pain into distraction from hunger. Finally they tricked Guolong into coming to Guangzhou with 600–700 yuan his parents gave him, claiming job opportunities were plentiful and he needed to post a deposit—only then could they finally have a real meal.

— Zhang Weiwei
2:16:51

Daily music-making sustains artists more successfully than chasing stardom

The Wild Children band was Zhang Weiwei's second spiritual shock after the Magic Rock Trio—but in the opposite direction. Instead of stage presence and intensity, they proved someone could do music as plainly as a farmer does, with no need to become a superstar. Zhang Quan and Xiaosuo lived in a basement below the Oriental Song and Dance Theater, turning on their metronome every day at two p.m. for rehearsal whether the drummer showed up or not; their life remained poor but unhurried. This state—‘gathering strength through what you do every day, not through what you acquire’—became Zhang Weiwei's spiritual anchor during his Beijing years.

— Zhang Weiwei
3:02:19

Riding others' dreams leaves you stranded when they wake

The Red River Bar era lasted less than two years before SARS in 2003 wiped it out in a single blow—one month without cash flow and a small operation's lifeline snaps. Then in fall 2004, Xiaosuo, worn down by years of heavy drinking, was diagnosed with stomach cancer and died. Zhang Quan took his dombra and walked away to Tibet and Yunnan; the Wild Children disbanded. Zhang Weiwei says the band's breakup hit him hard. In an interview, he said something many people still bring up: ‘When you're a musician, it's like flying in someone else's dream. When they wake up, you have nowhere left to fly.’ That realization drove his decision to make his own album. In late 2006, he wrote and recorded his first complete original work, ‘Rice Shop’.

— Zhang Weiwei
