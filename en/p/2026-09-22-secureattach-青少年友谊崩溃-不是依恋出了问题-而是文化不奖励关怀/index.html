Securely Attached 2026-09-22

Teen friendships are collapsing — not because attachment failed, but because the culture doesn't reward care

Forty years of tracking the same kids found that secure attachment is only a protective factor: in a culture that prizes grades and competition over care, even the most securely attached child isn't spared the adolescent crisis of connection.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:28

The attachment bond is more fundamental than food and water

Way says attachment theory used to place relationships at the base of Maslow's pyramid, sitting alongside food and shelter as equally essential. The claim now goes further: the relational bond actually matters more than food and water, because it is that relationship — someone taking care of you — that gives you access to food and water in the first place. Infants show sensitivity to who is helping whom within their first year: in experiments, when one person fails to help another, babies turn their heads away and look distressed.

— Niobe Way
8:47

Three- and four-year-olds already come equipped to care, cooperate and be curious

Way's research finds that children between three and five already have three core capacities: care (it isn't only parents caring for children — children are watching out for their parents and for each other), cooperation, and the one that has long been overlooked, curiosity. A three-year-old will ask, ‘Does your brain think the same things my brain does, because we're family?’ A four-year-old will ask her mother to her face, ‘Why do you smile when you're sad?’ These capacities are innate rather than taught, but the culture wears them down as children grow up.

— Niobe Way
26:20

The deep friendship boys want disappears in the middle of adolescence

Tracking the same group of boys over decades, Way found a clear trajectory. Between 12 and 14, boys say outright that they want ‘deep secret friendships’ — the kind where you can be vulnerable without fear of being laughed at — and they say it in language almost identical to girls', which overturns the stereotype that boys only want sports while girls want heart-to-hearts. But by mid-to-late adolescence, those same boys start to withdraw and grow distant, saying it's hard to find anyone they can trust that way anymore, and some simply stop looking. That shift from wanting it to giving up on it is what she calls the crisis of connection.

— Niobe Way
28:24

Labeling emotion as girly is what the crisis actually grows out of

Later the boys name the reason behind the crisis of connection themselves: ‘Maybe it would be better to be a girl — then you wouldn't always have to be without emotions.’ What that line exposes is a culture that equates emotion with being girly and equates not sharing your feelings with being mature. Way stresses that this is not only about boys: girls now report almost as much pressure to ‘man up’ as boys do, because the culture as a whole systematically values the hard things — reason, independence, competition — over the soft ones, emotion and connection.

— Niobe Way
31:34

Mental health is a symptom that shows up after social health breaks down

Way's central argument: mental health is not the root problem but a symptom of social health going wrong — and yet public health discourse has almost no concept of social health, only mental health and physical health. She tells the story of asking a group of ten-year-olds what predicts mental health; a fifth-grade boy blurted out ‘how other people treat you’, and another child added that if your friends treat you badly you aren't going to feel good. She marvels that a conclusion it took her nearly forty years of research to reach was something ten-year-olds arrived at on instinct. From this she argues that the only thing worth focusing on in raising a child is helping them build healthy relationships; everything else follows on its own.

— Niobe Way
37:42

Secure attachment is a protective factor, but it can't hold up against an antisocial culture

Pressed by the host on whether getting the attachment foundation right is enough, Way grants that attachment theory does have nearly a century of research behind it, that secure attachment produces better relationship quality, and that it is an important protective factor. But she immediately draws the limit: if the larger culture values money over people and treats ‘hard skills’ as worth more than ‘soft skills’, then even a securely attached child won't necessarily end up with healthy relationships — because everyone around them is scrambling to climb, nobody is staying curious about anybody else, and nobody is actually using those relational skills to connect with people. Attachment, she says, is more like the frosting on the cake than the cake itself.

— Niobe Way
48:07

Asking your kid what they're watching, without judgment, beats telling them to stop

Way offers a concrete practice: instead of leading with an attempt to pull your child off Instagram or TikTok, start by getting curious about who they follow and why. She gives her own example — her daughter kept following a makeup tutorial creator, and Way had assumed it was a waste of time until she couldn't help asking why her daughter watched her all the time. The answer: ‘I really like her voice, it calms me down.’ Once this kind of nonjudgmental curiosity starts, she says, the child feels ‘seen’ — and what follows being seen is almost always the same: the child becomes curious about you in return and starts asking about your life.

— Niobe Way
59:26

Perspective-taking is in decline, and it is what math actually rests on

Way cites a national study, which she read about in The New Yorker, showing that Americans' capacity for perspective-taking is declining. She thinks this is precisely because society treats perspective-taking as a ‘soft skill’ and learning math as a ‘hard skill’, without recognizing that perspective-taking is itself the capacity higher-order math requires — whether you are doing mathematical abstraction or, as in this very conversation, holding the host's, the listeners' and the producer's needs in mind at the same time, it is fundamentally the same ability to step outside what you already know and think from someone else's position. Her closing piece of concrete advice to parents: starting today, ask the other person ‘What's your favorite childhood memory?’ and then let them ask you back.

— Niobe Way

Glossary

social health
The quality of your relationships treated as a health dimension in its own right, standing alongside mental health and physical health
antisocial culture
A culture that fails to value humans' social nature and treats achievement and money as more important than care and connection — not a reference to antisocial personality
crisis of connection
The shift in adolescents from wanting deep friendship to actively giving up the search and drifting away from each other
relational intelligence
Distinct from emotional intelligence, which is self-directed: the cognitive and social-emotional capacities used specifically to build connection with other people
metacognition
The ability to think about your own thinking, which begins to appear around age three
