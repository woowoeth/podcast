Ten Years of Parenting Taught Me: When Your Kid Won't Listen, the Problem Isn't Your Kid

On the podcast's tenth anniversary, the host revisits ten parenting beliefs she once held as certain — from forcing her daughter to take medicine to treating an expert's framework as truth. Each one was learned first in a book, then relearned in a real conflict.

Forcing the medicine won the behavior and lost the relationship

The host recalls her three-year-old daughter refusing antibiotics, and her holding the girl down and forcing the medicine in; the daughter threw most of it back up. That night her husband Alvin stayed with their daughter in the bathroom for an hour, calmly saying the whole time, ‘I'm willing to do anything, but we're not leaving until we figure this out.’ In the end the daughter finished the whole course herself, with small sips of medicine, small sips of water, and a small piece of chocolate afterward to kill the taste. The host says the difference wasn't that her husband was more patient — it's that he wasn't solving a behavior, he was understanding what would actually help the child do this thing. What the child needed was autonomy over the process, and to feel she and her parent were on the same team, rather than being held down to do something.

9:19

The authoritative parenting framework rests on 146 white kids

The host says she accepted uncritically early on that ‘authoritative is the best parenting style,’ because every blog and study said so. It wasn't until she made the episode on corporal punishment that she found a paper co-authored by Diana Baumrind defending parents' ability to spank their children, and started digging. She found that this framework — cited more than 26,000 times and defining sixty years of parenting research — was originally based on just 146 white, middle-class preschoolers in the town where Baumrind lived. She calls this evidence of her own insufficient critical thinking, and says she now asks at the macro level ‘does this theory make sense,’ and at the micro level ‘does it fit this specific child in front of me.’

— Jen
17:41

Not punishing your kid doesn't mean being ruled by your kid

The host says she once believed that without punishments and consequences children would run wild — she even stood in the shower when her daughter was four months old thinking ‘how do I discipline her without becoming a mean mom.’ Clinical psychologist Dr. Houri Parsi admitted in the membership community that she too had treated ‘obedience’ as the goal, and that every tooth-brushing was a battle. Once, recording for the host to see, she deliberately used no threats, no punishments, no rewards, and just asked her two-year-old son ‘why don't you want to brush your teeth.’ He couldn't put it into words — he just called out for mama and buried his head in her chest. She realized what he wanted was time alone with her. She put the toilet lid down, sat down, held her son, and sang a song with him, and he brushed his own teeth, the whole thing in under five minutes.

— Jen
21:31

‘I need you to take out the trash’ is not a need

The host says looking back, she didn't understand what a ‘need’ was, and thought saying to her husband ‘I need you to take out the trash’ was a perfectly logical sentence. Before their daughter was born the two of them almost never argued — mountain biking in summer, skiing in winter, because the ad agency they worked for represented resorts and paid them in vouchers. After their daughter was born everything changed: during his three weeks of paternity leave her husband would fetch her anything she wanted, and the rest of the time he was training for a hundred-mile ride and watching the Tour de France. She says it was learning that she had needs for ‘collaboration, partnership, having her effort seen’ that actually pried open the problems between them; before that she carried a large amount of resentment inside her with no idea how to handle it.

— Jen
24:01

When you're triggered, the problem isn't the person who interrupted you

The host says her biggest trigger is being interrupted — the moment her husband interrupts her she flies into a rage, most recently over how much postage a package needed. She always assumed the cause was simply that her husband interrupted her, and that if he'd just stop, everything would be fine. In July 2018, recording with intergenerational trauma expert Dr. Rebecca Babcock-Fenerci, she traced it live to her father, a special education teacher who treated sixteen-year-olds like ten-year-olds and for a stretch lectured her every night. Later, in the car, she interrupted her daughter, and her daughter said ‘Don't interrupt me, Mama,’ and her husband looked at her and said ‘She is you.’ She says that was the moment she realized it had never just been about the two people in the room, and out of it she developed the ‘Tame Your Triggers’ course, which grew from a one-month module to ten weeks.

— Jen
30:27

The handmade book she made while pregnant was full of expectations for her child

The host describes a book she made while pregnant: three covers, one in the middle and one on each side, opening in both directions, brown book cloth with a beige spine, the word ‘me’ cut out of the board on one cover, and on the reverse cover the word ‘you’ embossed, the concept being ‘you came out of me.’ On the ‘me’ side she wrote her own feelings and thoughts; on the ‘you’ side she wrote to the Carys she hadn't yet met. She reads out the earliest batch of ‘expectations for you’: I hope you're smart, or at least average; I hope you're the kind of kid who shakes hands with people instead of hiding behind my legs; I hope you like both roughhousing and pretty things; I hope you don't cry all the time; I hope when you fall you get up yourself and say ‘I'm okay.’ She says looking back now, her biggest mistake was not knowing how to simply be with a person, to let them be their whole self and witness it, without carrying ideas about how they should be.

— Jen
33:14

Kohn's principle two: reconsider your demands first

The host says she once thought she'd play the role of ‘enforcer,’ and even wrote in her pregnancy expectations list ‘I hope you can love me the way I love him.’ She says RIE loosened the soil for her, but what actually planted the seed was Alfie Kohn's Unconditional Parenting. She shares a few of its principles: principle two is to reconsider your demands — ‘maybe when a child won't do what you ask, the problem isn't the child, it's what you're asking’; principle five is to change the way you look, not just the way you act — when you see your child doing something you asked them not to, your first reaction is curiosity about what they're struggling with, not how to make them stop; principle ten is ‘attribute to your child the best motive consistent with the observed facts,’ and she says when she quotes it she changes the ending to ‘consistent with the observed behavior,’ because ‘there are no facts, only what we observe.’

— Jen
39:07

The couple relationship isn't a different subject, it's the same one

The host says she long believed the couple relationship and the parent-child relationship were completely different things — when she started the parenting membership eight years ago she asked Dr. Laura Froyen to write the co-parenting module, because she had no idea how to handle a partner relationship. She says her parenting shift toward collaboration was relatively easy, because she had no ten-year-old habits with her daughter; but she and Alvin had been together ten years and had never learned how to disagree, because most of that time was spent doing hobbies they loved, and she didn't mind doing most of the housework. It wasn't until she took Gottman training that she realized this was almost the same thing she'd been doing with her child all these years — while also thinking the Gottman method itself has a few key flaws. She recruited couples from the membership to record real arguments, using modified tools, and one couple whose husband had refused to discuss a toxic issue — after his wife listened for ten minutes with a genuine intent to understand him — was willing to talk, and in that same session they reached an agreement to move forward.

— Jen
42:34

Parenting was never a thing that happens inside one house

The host says before she had a child she barely interacted with kids and didn't much like them, and assumed that after having one she'd still be the same person who needed no support. Her family is three thousand to six thousand miles away. She says we're never told we'll need support, that family may be unwilling or unable to provide it, so we never think to want it. She mentions a facilitator in her daughter's ‘non-school’ program, in her twenties, with no kids, with a job, who didn't need the money, who scheduled just one afternoon a week simply because she liked being with children — and that created stability for the whole schedule: if one parent facilitator got sick, there was someone else in the week to cover. She says if she'd done something like that when she was younger, she could have created that kind of space for a family while she still had the capacity, and would have learned a little about how to be with children.

— Jen

Glossary

RIE
A parenting approach founded by Magda Gerber, centered on treating infants as whole people worthy of respect.
authoritative parenting
High responsiveness plus high control, treated in Western research as the ‘good’ parenting style.
authoritarian parenting
High control without responsiveness to the child's needs, associated with negative outcomes in Western research.
Special Time
A daily practice of a set period of parent-child time fully led by the child, used to meet the need for connection.
Gottman method
A couples research and coaching system based on observing patterns of interaction between partners.
