Quartz watches nearly destroyed Swiss watchmaking; Rolex won through patience, not innovation

When quartz watches nearly crushed the Swiss watch industry, Rolex didn't follow the crowd into transformation. Instead, it waited for competitors to panic and exit, then counterattacked with a proven luxury strategy—leveraging the patience that only an independent company can afford.

The world's most famous brand is its most opaque

Rolex embodies contradictions: it's one of the world's most recognized brands yet refuses financial disclosure because it's owned by the Hans Wilsdorf Foundation. It might be more secretive than IKEA or Mars. Retailers intentionally erect barriers to purchase, yet Rolex sells over 1 million watches annually at an average price of $13,000; many models appreciate the moment they leave the showroom. This isn't a niche brand trading on scarcity—it's mass-market luxury built on 120 years of precision engineering.

17:00

A total bet on the wrong horse

Hans read accounts of the Second Boer War and noticed soldiers using wristwatches to coordinate military operations. He concluded the wristwatch was the future and placed the company's largest order ever with manufacturer Jean Aegler. But he was wrong—when the war ended, soldiers returned to civilian life and the wristwatch market never took off. Wristwatches in the 1910s were still called ‘wristlets’and worn mainly by women as jewelry. Rolex survived not because it had picked right, but because the company was small enough to survive without a huge market. The real boom had to wait for WWI's tailwind.

34:00

The waterproof crown patent became Rolex's unbreakable moat

Hans's genius wasn't in watchmaking—it was in strategy. Rolex didn't actually acquire its mechanism supplier Aegler until 2004. In October 1925, two Swiss manufacturers applied for a patent on the screw-down crown—the seal that makes a watch waterproof. Hans had the habit of reading the Swiss patent registry daily without fail. He caught the filing immediately, bought the patent, and gave Rolex an exclusive lock on the technology. At that moment, every watch brand was hunting for a waterproof solution, yet only he moved. This single step gave Rolex a permanent grip on the industry's most critical foundational patent.

— Ben/David
40:00

Three moves got Eisenhower to wear it

After European markets collapsed at the end of World War II, Rolex pivoted to America with a three-step play. First, it sent the latest Datejust to Swiss national hero General Henri Guisan; Guisan then gave it to his friend Winston Churchill; Churchill influenced Allied Supreme Commander Eisenhower. When Eisenhower won the US presidency in 1952, he wore that Datejust almost constantly. Rolex ran ads saying ‘The man who guides the world's destiny wears a Rolex.’One key figure's choice outweighed any advertising budget.

58:00

A magazine no one can find set off the collectors' rush

In 1986, Italian dealers suddenly began stockpiling a specific vintage Paul Newman Daytona. The possible trigger: a cover of an Italian fashion magazine—but the magazine was never actually found. Within two years, the secondary-market price of this watch rocketed from $200 to over $30,000. For context, in 1984, Paul Newman himself had casually given his own Daytona—which he'd worn for over a decade—to his daughter's boyfriend. In the luxury market, demand often doesn't come from product innovation. It comes from an inexplicable reputation shift within the taste-making class.

— Ben/David
1:08:00

30% of the market isn't dominance—it's redefining the category

According to Morgan Stanley's LuxeConsult data, Rolex captures 30.3% of Swiss watch industry revenues. Number 2, Cartier, has 7.5%. Number 3, Omega, has 7.5%. Even Patek Philippe, the prestige watchmaker, has only 5.6%—this concentration is extreme even among luxury goods; Louis Vuitton only controls 20% of the handbag market. By volume it's even starker: Rolex makes 1.1 to 1.2 million watches annually—15 times more than Patek Philippe—while charging only a third as much. This isn't traditional luxury competition. It's one player defining an entire category.

2:15:00

Waiting while competitors panicked proved to be the winning move

In the late 1970s, everyone declared mechanical watches dead. Swiss companies panicked and rushed into quartz—Omega even licensed its brand to other manufacturers and lost market share anyway. Rolex explored quartz (Oysterquartz) but didn't mass-produce it. The reasoning: expensive quartz would become commoditized as fast as the transistor radio. Mechanical movements, which require lots of skilled labor, would stay expensive and exclusive forever. Rolex didn't panic. It waited for Jean-Claude Biver to prove the ‘rare craft’strategy worked at Blancpain, then it borrowed that already-validated template for a counterattack.

— Ben
4:23:45

No one honestly needs a watch to tell time

Ben points out that the watch category has undergone a fundamental transformation. You can buy a car and say ‘I need transportation.’You can buy a bag and say ‘I need something to carry things in.’But no one can truthfully say ‘I need a watch to know what time it is.’Modern watches are purely identity symbols and aesthetic choices. This explains why the ostensibly ‘more advanced’Apple Watch never threatened Rolex's top tier—they're not even in the same category. As Ben says, Rolex is ‘not a timekeeping device.’

Glossary

Rolex's screw-down crown sealing system for waterproofing watch cases—a patented design
Rotor
