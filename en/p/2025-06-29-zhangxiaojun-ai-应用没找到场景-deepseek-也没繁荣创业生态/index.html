AI Apps Haven't Found Their Scenarios, and DeepSeek Didn't Make the Startup Ecosystem Boom Either

Wang Mengqiu of Tsingliu Capital's read: the capital winter isn't over, DeepSeek flattened the information gap without creating new scenarios, the general-purpose Agent may be a false premise, and what's truly non-negotiable is vertical workflows.

2:03

The capital winter hasn't actually ended

Wang Mengqiu looks at it from two angles. On money: more capital is willing to write checks this year than in the past two, but the increment comes mainly from institutions that were previously sitting on the sidelines, and relative to the volume of state capital it isn't large. On investable projects: the primary market is no more prosperous than the past two years — still AI, hard tech, low-altitude economy, those few fields; consumption remains deeply inactive, nobody wants to burn money on consumer, and what people are more interested in is companies already profitable and at a certain scale. So if two years ago you called it a winter, it hasn't fully emerged from that yet.

— Wang Mengqiu
4:04

DeepSeek didn't make the application ecosystem boom

Wang Mengqiu argues the core problem for AI application startups isn't whether the underlying large model is cheap enough — it's that applications still haven't found a genuinely exciting scenario, and that isn't something DeepSeek can solve. DeepSeek going open source amounts to information equalisation, flattening the possible usage gap between big and small companies. But everyone's biggest problem was compute to begin with — doesn't DeepSeek need good compute too? So in essence it did not make the application ecosystem boom. The reason no particularly good application has appeared in China over the past two years is that nobody has yet found a good application scenario.

— Wang Mengqiu
5:05

AI hasn't created new scenarios or new demand

Wang Mengqiu says everyone feels this generation of AI is a technological revolution, but what it can currently satisfy and deliver has not created new scenarios or new demand. Take Agents: the industry has been at them since 2023, starting with the auto GPT concept, and both China and the US have been exploring for two years, yet they've never taken off. Even the most vertical travel-planning Agent — plenty of companies in both countries are doing it, trying to automatically plan itineraries and complete hotel and flight bookings — but in actual use the difference from what a human produces is still quite large. People keep revising their needs along the way: they first want to stay somewhere, search and find it's too expensive, so they switch location or even switch destination. So in satisfying existing demand, AI in many domains hasn't achieved a leap in experience, let alone created new demand.

— Wang Mengqiu
9:06

Demand analysis matters more than AI technology

Wang Mengqiu thinks the most important thing isn't AI technology itself but demand analysis: which demand is currently poorly satisfied and could, through this generation of AI technology, achieve a leap in experience. It can't just be a minor improvement — a minor improvement isn't enough to support a truly independent business; it has to be a leap in experience — saving me a lot of time, giving me a result that surprises me. Just as AI talking bots were surprising at first, and DeepSeek too: never mind performance or compute savings, the answers it gives are still far from accurate and its hallucinations are very severe, but the way it talks to you makes you feel it's more human than previous bots — and for Chinese people especially, you're more willing to talk to it.

— Wang Mengqiu
11:07

Using DeepSeek as a fortune teller

Wang Mengqiu says she uses DeepSeek as a fortune teller — when she needs emotional value or is unsure about something, she goes and asks it. You know it's talking nonsense, but it makes you feel very comfortable. She speculates this is because reinforcement learning was used in the post-training stage: the more feedback you give it, the more it learns. For instance, if you read its answers carefully or spend a long time reading, it may take that as good feedback; or you just say outright ‘you're right’ or ‘you're talking nonsense’. She thinks some Yuanbao models also have a lot of emotional value, but use them too much and you feel they're too accommodating, too aware of what you want — a bit like a simp.

— Wang Mengqiu
15:09

The expectation for WeChat Yuanbao is a person

Wang Mengqiu uses WeChat Yuanbao to do background checks on people, asking whether it knows a certain person at a certain company and how it evaluates them; her original intent was for it to dig up information about that person scattered across forums and Xiaohongshu. She admits the demand is unreasonable, but for some reason she feels it's a person, so she can have that demand of it. The result: it found nothing. She thinks this is explicable — a large number of forums don't let AI bots scrape, and a bot abiding by protocol shouldn't scrape either; and whether scraped content is used for training also raises authenticity questions. But from the user-contact level she finds it far below expectations, and from that she wonders: when Yuanbao presents itself as a WeChat friend, the things users want it to satisfy should be different from what Yuanbao as an app should do.

— Wang Mengqiu
25:11

The general-purpose Agent may be a false premise

Wang Mengqiu says Tencent isn't currently committed to building a general-purpose Agent, and she even thinks the general-purpose Agent may be a false premise — whether there's really a split between general and vertical is also uncertain. Tsingliu has been looking at Agent startups from last year through this one: some do general-purpose, others turn a particular workflow — video editing, say — into an Agent to satisfy it, and the latter is quite a real need. She finds this a bit like the distinction back then between large models and upper-layer applications: some teams want to build an Agent framework, so that more application-layer and workflow Agents can be built on top of it — that layer is like the large model as foundation back then. She has always felt a big question mark hangs over whether the general-purpose Agent holds up; at least right now there's no general-purpose Agent on the market, or none done well.

— Wang Mengqiu
35:22

Small organisations favour young founders

Wang Mengqiu says that perhaps post-00s founders at this moment will have a relatively higher probability of success than post-90s founders did in the past, because organisations now tend toward small organisations. The biggest problem with founders who were seen as too young used to be management ability and life experience: building a business takes not only a good product idea and good technical ability, but more importantly the ability to manage an organisation and judge the whole industry — that requires life experience. But in the previous mobile network era, the number of people you ultimately had to manage was large and the complexity was there; in this generation's AI application layer, it's possible a small organisation can build a big business, so post-00s without that much life experience also have a certain probability of making it. But she stresses you don't have to chase investing in post-00s — dividing founders by age is a very reductive thing to do.

— Wang Mengqiu
38:18

What embodied intelligence lacks most is simulators

Wang Mengqiu says Tsingliu has invested in embodied intelligence — in Chen Jianyu's (陈建宇) Xingdong Era (星动纪元) — and will recently try to invest in a company making simulators. Because for embodied intelligence, the most fundamental problem is that there isn't that much data in the real world; you have to simulate well enough in a virtual environment, then transfer the algorithm to the real world, and also find ways to bridge the sim2real gap. So simulators are the infrastructure of the embodied intelligence industry, but this is a very, very hard thing to do. Tsingliu is following Xingdong Era to look for opportunities along the industry chain, just as back then it invested in new energy vehicles and found industry-chain opportunities along the OEMs — but it's still a bit early now, because nobody has entered mass production yet, and supply-chain opportunities aren't visible yet.

— Wang Mengqiu
44:23

Glasses replacing phones is unnatural

Wang Mengqiu bought Meta's Ray-Ban glasses herself and plays with them when travelling. The reasons she's willing to wear them: first, they look fairly good and fashionable; second, they look no different from ordinary glasses. She doesn't like devices that look distinctive — VR headsets are unsustainable, not wearable day to day, a violation of human nature. The glasses combine sunglasses and earphone functions, with bone-conduction earphones; she really dislikes earphones stuck in her ears. But she won't wear them often either, since she isn't nearsighted. She thinks the people making AI glasses now are still quite shallow; the core is figuring out what problem it solves and when a person needs to wear the thing. She doesn't believe glasses can replace phones, because it's unnatural — having something growing on your face all the time is uncomfortable; a phone is heavy in your hand and once you put it down, that's that.

— Wang Mengqiu
58:31

Travel is looking inward

Wang Mengqiu went out roughly every month last year — to Saudi Arabia, Germany, Iceland, Japan. She says the feeling in Saudi Arabia was of a country very actively embracing modernisation, very young, thriving; the local tour guides didn't rely on it for money at all, even paying out of their own pockets for coffee and ice cream. Germany is an already very well done country; going to Germany is seeking inner peace. Iceland she visited during the polar night, with only three or four hours of daylight a day — a strong end-of-the-world feeling. She says travelling now is different from in her twenties: first, she has more money than in her twenties; second, she no longer wants to hit ten-odd places in a day — it's more about looking inward, the changing external environment projected onto the inner self.

— Wang Mengqiu
1:07:35

Greatness cannot be planned

Wang Mengqiu read the biographies of Jensen Huang, Sam Altman and Musk, and made an internal PPT titled ‘Greatness cannot be planned’ — when you truly build a company that great, you actually can't foresee or plan it in advance, but it can be learned. She tried to sum up whether the three share commonalities, and in the end found not many — but Jensen Huang and Musk share one human commonality: they didn't try to be good people, and in daily life, especially working with their teams, they aren't easy people to get along with. Her conclusion: if you want to become a great entrepreneur, you probably shouldn't try to be a good person — there must be very harsh situations, very harsh relationships, and you have to behave harsh.

— Wang Mengqiu
1:12:41

Film must first answer why go to the cinema

Wang Mengqiu says, why do today's moviegoers still go to the movies? There are too many ways to seek emotional value, and going to the cinema is an extremely high-threshold activity. People used to watch films because there was no other way; now scrolling short dramas and short videos costs almost nothing and is more comfortable — for dating you can sit face to face in a café scrolling short dramas. People used to think entering a cinema was a social mode, but really, sitting in the dark watching a film, who's talking to whom? There's nothing social about it. So the film industry must first answer one question: why should people still go to the cinema to watch a movie. She thinks many people in the industry, especially producers, haven't realised this problem, haven't thought about answering why audiences should go, what genre they want to see, what audience to target.

— Wang Mengqiu
1:16:43

Her Story rides the feminist narrative

Wang Mengqiu thinks Her Story (“好东西”) succeeded because it rides the current feminist narrative — it's a purely feminist narrative, different from the earlier sister film B for Busy (“爱情神话”), which was more about doubling the various possibilities of refined Shanghai middle-class life and finally telling a myth of love. She heard Her Story did well at the box office in lower-tier cities, which is a very good breakout, because B for Busy was more in first-tier cities, while Her Story let women in second- and third-tier cities see that women in first-tier cities can live like this. She likes the film and saw it twice, but feels some lines are a bit too slogan-like, and that all the men in it are portrayed as very weak — she has good, strong men around her too.

— Wang Mengqiu
1:23:46

She hates March 8th Women's Day

Wang Mengqiu says she has always hated March 8th Women's Day, because ‘I think this thing is really because you're still too weak, so people specially make a holiday for you’. So she never celebrates it, particularly hates it — and it's been split into Girls' Day and Women's Day; Tsinghua has a March 7th Goddess Day. She says in her own life she has never encountered anything particularly unfair to women — not in her studies, not working at Baidu, not in investing — never met discrimination, condescension or mansplaining. She thinks on the whole it should be equal rights, men and women both seen as independent people, and whether behaviour is reasonable is what matters; don't demand things or make everything a big deal just because someone is a woman — that's not real equality.

— Wang Mengqiu
