India's Nuclear Physics History Is Not a Prelude to the Nuclear Test: Postwar State Intervention Is the Real Watershed

India's early nuclear physics was in sync with the West; the real watershed was postwar state intervention. Hindsight from the 1974 nuclear test only obscures the damage the TIFR model did to universities and the scientific system.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:06

The imperial-tool explanation does not fit Indian physics

When she entered the field, the mainstream of Indian science history was about the imperial period, with keywords like ‘imperial tools’, ‘science and empire’, ‘domestication and adaptation’, and ‘technology transfer’. She says this framework does not apply to the founders of Indian physics in the late 19th and early 20th centuries—they would not recognize themselves in these pictures. She thus wanted to push two things at once: to keep post-independence Indian science history from being monopolized by sociology/STS, and to question the imperial science history framework itself. Imperial institutions (geological survey, botanical survey, etc.) fit the ‘imperial tools’ explanation, but modern physics is a different practice.

— Phalkey
8:09

China and India are not cases but conceptual sites

In her article with Tong Lam in History of Science, they call China and India ‘conceptual sites’ rather than ‘cases’. The origin is Armin Wong's critique of the limits of European experience: the history of science has always measured the whole world by European standards, so India is placed in the ‘waiting room of history’. Treating China and India as sites where the global majority lives can re-establish the particularity of European experience: all knowledge is local but claims universality; the ‘atypicality’ of China and India is not backwardness but an entry point for understanding how science takes root.

— Phalkey
12:10

War, not the nuclear test, redrew the map of nuclear physics

Early nuclear physics narratives are often hijacked by hindsight from the 1974 nuclear test. She returns to the scene of 1932, when nuclear physics took shape as a discipline, and 1939, when the fission paper appeared: the two physics departments in Calcutta and Bangalore were chasing the same frontier—Raman spoke of particle accelerators that ‘every civilized country should have’. War changed everything: accelerators jumped from 27 inches to hundreds of inches, the US led, and Europe's answer was CERN. After independence, India's question became: how can a new country that is not wealthy and lacks talent answer this resource-intensive field? Her book traces the decisions, disappointments, and small joys of three groups of researchers before and after the war, and before and after independence.

— Phalkey
22:10

In the national laboratory contest, the old physics departments lost

The two departments tried to shape themselves as frontiers around equipment, but the scale of their ambition was not enough to match the national-level ‘national laboratory’ ambitions. The Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, conceived in Bangalore during the war and later moved to Bombay, was a new institution that ‘emerged as the times required’, becoming a center that combined facilities, educational institutions, and shapers of nuclear energy and weapons programs. This explains how the accelerator as an object forced institutional change: when the laboratory became a ‘system’, engineering and radio skills became necessary, and the state's role was embedded in every vacuum chamber and oscillator.

— Phalkey
25:11

Big science became the only science, draining universities

After the war, the Department of Atomic Energy became the template for organizing science in India: the later Department of Space, Department of Earth Sciences, and Department of Biotechnology were all built on it. Scientists became civil servants and state employees, with state funding dominant; the cost was that universities were stripped of trained people and resources, and only recently has India seen independent, arms-length science funding agencies. India's bureaucracy is ‘accountable for compliance, not results’, and applying that logic to science further distorts basic research. The nuclear physics laboratory model went from big science to the only science.

— Phalkey
31:14

Even with blueprints, it won't turn—what's missing is the ecosystem

Her documentary Cyclotron is about the daily maintenance of a 1930s American machine shipped from Rochester to India in the 1960s. One detail: the US used specific screw and nut sizes, and for 50 years after the war, the Indian market had no matching sizes, and no manufacturer anywhere in the world still produced those parts. The team, looking at circuit diagrams from library books, assembled it by trial and error. These daily routines of researchers and technicians constitute the material basis of a resource-constrained economy—even if you have the blueprints, without an ecosystem it won't turn.

— Phalkey
35:15

The archives are full of connections; difference is a historiographical disease

She opposes writing Indian science history as a ‘history of difference’: that is a historiographical disease bred by imperial archives, ending in a narrow racial difference theory. The connections in the archives are ready-made—C.V. Raman's idol was Helmholtz; in the 1930s, Cavendish, Göttingen, Berkeley, and Calcutta were chasing the same frontier simultaneously. But historians reserve descriptions like ‘domestication’ and ‘adaptation’ for India, effectively kicking India off the list of participants. She advocates following the archives, not the historian's presupposed problem framework; the ‘incommensurable civilizations’ narrative from 19th-century medical history is wrongly projected onto nuclear magnetic resonance, dam-building, and bridge-building.

— Phalkey
42:19

The historian's job is not to make science interesting

Influenced by Bruno Latour's late essay ‘Why Has Critique Run out of Steam?’, she wants to reconfigure critique in history: from ‘deconstruction’ to ‘engagement’, asking the older philosophical question—why do we want to know. Science is shaping every everyday choice: climate, AI, surveillance, gene technology. She thinks the historian's job is not to make science ‘interesting’ but to help practitioners and ordinary people together understand how science reshapes the horizon of choices. She says she hasn't found the path yet, but her next book will be driven by this reflection, not by writing methodology.

— Phalkey

Glossary

Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR)
India's premier basic physics research institute, which became the center of national science organization after the war.
Department of Atomic Energy (DAE)
The Indian government department managing atomic energy research and infrastructure, the organizational template for all subsequent research institutions.
Coulomb excitation
An experimental technique using charged particles to excite atomic nuclei, one of the main research uses of cyclotrons.
Big Science
A research paradigm organized by the state with large-scale investment, giant facilities, and administrative systems.
global majority
A term referring to people of the Global South and colonized histories, replacing 'Third World/developing countries'.
CERN
The European particle physics laboratory built by multiple countries, seen by Indian science historians as Europe's answer to big science.
