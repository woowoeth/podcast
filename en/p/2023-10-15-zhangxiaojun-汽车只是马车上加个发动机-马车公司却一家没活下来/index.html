A car is just a carriage with an engine bolted on — and not one carriage company survived

A car is not a carriage plus an engine: change the powertrain and the whole technology stack and market change with it, and not one of the carriage makers of the day lived past a century. The test for an EV company today is whether it controls the battery upstream.

5:05

Benz built cars because a failed startup forced him to

Both Mr. Benz and Daimler entered through manufacturing industry, and both went from engines to whole vehicles. What is more interesting about Mr. Benz is that he was a serial entrepreneur — he started out in mechanical manufacturing, his first venture failed and left him deep in debt, and that is precisely what pushed him to study the internal combustion engine. Zhang Junyi's judgment is that ‘the powertrain is always where the money is’, the same logic as today. He only really built Benz automobiles after the age of 40, because you have to accumulate enough engineering, resources and capability before you can create a new model and brand. That rule repeats over the following century and more: most founders of new brands were engineers, and most did not come out of the auto industry, but they had always spent a long time accumulating in some other industry — the example the show gives is a carmaker that just passed its 75th anniversary, whose founder was an aeronautical engineer; Mr. Porsche was the finest designer; Mr. Maybach founded two brands in his lifetime.

— Zhang Junyi
7:10

The first driver was a woman, and they called her a witch

The car was finished in October 1885, but local regulations would not let it run, and it only got its patent and its first public run in 1886. To promote it, someone had to drive it and show it, so Bertha Benz took her two children and simply drove to her mother's house, breaking down several times along the way and refuelling once. There were no petrol stations then — petrol was compounded in pharmacies, because petrol was treated as a medicine at the time, and that pharmacy became the world's first petrol station. People around her called her a ‘witch’, because a car with no horse was far too loud. From this Zhang Junyi says, ‘I think women drivers should hold their heads high.’ Bertha did more than drive: when her husband's venture was struggling she first covered it with her own dowry, and then she became the first test driver.

— Zhang Junyi
10:15

The Red Flag Act: whoever walks ahead sets your speed

Britain enacted the ‘Red Flag Act’, and in essence it was not aimed at cars at all — it was originally about steam locomotives. It was not settled which powertrain would run on early roads — petrol engines, diesel engines, steam engines and electric vehicles all appeared — while the dominant traffic was still carriages and pedestrians, and steam locomotives were loud and easily spooked animals, so the law came about to protect the interests of carriage drivers. In practice it meant a person had to walk in front of the vehicle, and ‘his speed determines your car's speed’, so the car simply could not go fast and had little practical value. Zhang Junyi's judgment is that this indirectly affected the whole of Britain's car development over that period, and America inherited part of it, until it was repealed around 1896. In the same period Britain and America were relatively conservative while continental Europe was more open, because that is where the Industrial Revolution started.

— Zhang Junyi
15:16

The powertrain changed and the carriage companies all vanished

‘Isn't a car just a carriage with an engine added?’ — but the result is that not one carriage company survived across a century. Zhang Junyi says this is an early example he keeps coming back to: when the powertrain changes, the whole market and the whole technology change, and many companies die because of it. The earliest bodies really were converted carriage bodies: Benz's first car had just one row of seats with the original carriage behind it; Daimler put the engine into the carriage he had prepared for his wife's 43rd birthday. Bodywork had been the carriage makers' job, and only later did it gradually merge into the vehicle manufacturers. He pushes this logic straight to today: if a carmaker has no control over upstream, batteries for instance, it will struggle to make money, and every one of the leading EV companies without exception has control over and innovates in the upstream three electrics.

— Zhang Junyi
24:20

China's first car owner was Empress Dowager Cixi

The show says China's first car owner was also a woman — Empress Dowager Cixi, who received a Benz in 1900. Unfortunately this owner never drove it herself and the car never moved, for a reason rooted in her worldview: how could she allow someone to sit in front of her and drive the vehicle. She even gave her driver a suggestion, that he should drive while kneeling, and Zhang Junyi adds, ‘then just think about how you'd control it.’ He treats it as an anecdote, but says it shows that China's awareness of brands like Benz goes back a long way. He notes in passing that in those same years brands like P&G and Coca-Cola were being founded one after another, while over here they were still living through the Qing dynasty — adding himself, ‘if I remember correctly.’

— Zhang Junyi
30:25

The internal combustion engine won on efficiency, not on being advanced

Why was the internal combustion engine the right choice a century and more ago? Zhang Junyi's answer is the relationship between fuel efficiency and cost. The four-stroke Otto cycle is a foundational theory, and on that basis the engine's thermochemical efficiency is relatively the highest, and its failure rate is low. It was not that other energy sources did not exist: external combustion engines and steam engines were running, and there were storage batteries too, but their range was extremely short, they broke down easily and comfort was poor, so that round of technological iteration eliminated them. He sums up energy choice as two things — availability, and cost and efficiency. The car replaced the carriage not because it was faster either; early cars may not even have outrun a carriage, but because it needed no upkeep, was easy to keep clean, and fuel was cheap enough.

— Zhang Junyi
43:30

Daimler is currently splitting itself into three companies

Mergers and splits among carmakers keep cycling. In the early years Daimler and Benz merged, Audi's four rings represent four companies merging, BMW came over from aero engines. In recent years the direction has reversed: what Daimler is doing is a spin-off, one company becoming three — Mercedes passenger car group, truck group, financial services group — which will be listed further, because different businesses have different interests and a spin-off makes better use of the capital markets. Zhang Junyi says the value of merging lies in procurement synergies and product synergies, while the logic of splitting is that certain models are unprofitable on their own, or need more capital, so you let them list and raise money independently. He mentions in passing that Porsche was extraordinarily profitable decades ago and even considered a reverse takeover of Volkswagen, but that too was a family matter.

— Zhang Junyi
45:30

Chinese founders won't put their names on the car

Why has China's new generation of car entrepreneurs not designed a car for their wives? Zhang Junyi says actually they have: Li Bin was the first to propose the ‘queen's seat’, and spent a lot of money on that sliding rail; the vanity mirrors in NIO cars use soft lighting, and things like NOMI get described as a ‘spiritual companion’. But he also says this batch of founders are not automotive engineers, ‘one might even say they may not have enough of the idea of realising romance’; the romantic moment he names is the photo of Li Bin and Natalie holding each other on the day of the IPO. Deeper still is a cultural difference: foreign companies are used to putting their surname on the product, while Chinese entrepreneurs are less personal, and consumers would rather see a product than just the founder's endorsement. He says BYD only became a brand once BYD was explained as Bring your dream, and Chinese people care about having the name match the reality.

— Zhang Junyi

Glossary

An early British law requiring a person to walk in front of a motor vehicle; originally aimed at steam locomotives, repealed around 1896.
Otto cycle
The theoretical cycle of the four-stroke internal combustion engine, established long before Benz built his car.

Ten men who moved from the US military into Ford after WWII, coming out of operations research; several later reached the top of the company.
