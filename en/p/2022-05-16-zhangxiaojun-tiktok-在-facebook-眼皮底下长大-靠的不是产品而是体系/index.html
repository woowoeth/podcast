TikTok Grew Up Under Facebook's Nose, and It Wasn't the Product That Did It — It Was the System

ByteDance won on systems, not individuals: a recommendation algorithm, a UG platform, and a commercialization machine stacked on top of Musical.ly's product shell, then pushed hard with an ARPU playbook benchmarked against Facebook — that's how the world's fifth-largest social app got built.

5:12

Facebook wasn't committed enough, and that's how it let Musical.ly go

Facebook was a very important early customer of TikTok, but it never saw how high the short-video ceiling was. It once made a verbal offer of $1.6 billion for Musical.ly, then hesitated and didn't invest, so Musical.ly went off to talk to Kuaishou and to talk to itself. Facebook later built Lasso to fight TikTok, but the investment wasn't aggressive, and after a while the window closed. The author's judgment: if Facebook had acquired Musical.ly back then, none of what came later would have happened.

— Zhang Jun
6:14

The north star metric shifted from cost per user to UP value

In ByteDance's early days, doing the overseas version of Jinri Toutiao — Newsmaster, later renamed Top Boss — the team was stuck on growth because the math didn't work: what a user costs, how long their lifetime is, how much money they can bring in. Run the numbers and you lose a lot. With TikTok the playbook changed. They no longer only ran their own numbers; they poached Facebook management and got internal data, learning in detail what Facebook's UP value was in each of 100-plus countries, then set targets: in three years reach some fraction of Facebook's UP value in that country, in five years reach another. UP value is average revenue per user — how much revenue each daily active user brings in on average.

— Zhang Jun
8:14

When DAU penetration hits 20-30%, that's the inflection point

Through Douyin, ByteDance worked out a theory of the inflection point in a business model domestically: stage one is mindlessly throwing resources at it, stage two is building the content ecosystem, and stage three is that when daily active penetration reaches 20% to 30%, you can generally expect the inflection point. After the inflection point, retention rises on its own, the share of paid acquisition rises relative to organic growth, and organic growth can take a large share. So the TikTok team wasn't that afraid of low retention — and low retention was exactly what Facebook saw in the product at the time.

— Zhang Jun
12:20

TikTok is a victory of the system, not of people

The conclusion of the author's article on Douyin was this: the victory of ByteDance and Douyin is really a victory of the system. The product form came from Musical.ly. ByteDance was strong in three things: the best recommendation algorithm and information matching, an efficient UG platform (user growth platform), and an efficient commercialization system. Three systems fighting as one, stacked on Musical.ly's product shell, stacked again on earlier overseas experiments — that's what produced the leap. Kuaishou's overseas push was scattered, each product under a different name (like stack video), never building brand recognition; ByteDance played one global board, all brands under the single name TikTok.

— Zhang Jun
16:23

Leave no gap, suppress across the board

Zhang Yiming looks very nice, very gentle and refined, but there's something resolute in him. His globalization strategy comes down to eight characters: leave no gap, suppress across the board. In execution: in any country, if an app overtakes TikTok on the App Store charts, you must spend to push it back down and hold the number one spot long term. Musical.ly co-founders Yang Luyu and Zhu Jun figured out the best product form for short video (full-screen vertical swipe), but turning the product from something small into a global hit depended on Zhang Yiming's ruthless decisions on growth.

— Zhang Jun
22:27

Zhang Yiming watches how executives post on Moments

Zhang Yiming is seen as a very technical boss, but compared with many technical CEOs he loves studying human nature. In picking professional managers he prefers people with an entrepreneurial spirit, possibly with a failed startup behind them. Someone told the author that Yiming pays attention to very granular things: how company executives post on WeChat Moments — he reads it and comments, he offers opinions; he also empathizes with choices of office space, marketing activities, PR activities. The author also mentions the matter of him sending executives to prison: word is many people pleaded for them and outsiders spoke up too, and he still made that decision.

— Zhang Jun
29:34

The strictest Chinese company on data isolation

From TikTok's overseas expansion the author draws several lessons worth borrowing. First, data isolation: TikTok may be the strictest Chinese company on data isolation — Chinese data in China, overseas data overseas, isolated according to each country's laws and the principle of proximity, while still letting different people see different data and support the work. Second, thorough localization: because it's a content product, a cultural product, strongly tied to each country's culture, it needs a stronger local team. Third, building a global team: for example Shou Zi Chew as TikTok CEO, and the important base in Singapore — both are balancing points between Chinese and Western culture. Fourth, handling cultural conflict: the author feels they are still figuring this one out.

— Zhang Jun
33:37

Surviving the Trump ban was heavily a matter of luck

In the last stretch of Trump's presidency he said he would shut down TikTok in the US, and every day various bits of news came out — Oracle and other companies wanting to buy it. In the end TikTok chose to tie itself to Oracle and put its data on Oracle. The author thinks that interval was actually very short, and heavily a matter of luck, because probably everyone thought this hurdle couldn't be cleared. Zhang Yiming was actually in a passive position during that episode, and in the end benefited from his enemy's change of office. The author says if it had gone on a bit longer, he doesn't know whether he could have survived it.

— Zhang Jun
