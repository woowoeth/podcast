Yell—apologize—yell: stuck in the groove of the old record

The episode opens by framing its central question: when something small pushes us past our threshold, we yell at our kids, treat them harshly, use emotional withdrawal to cope—and then fall into the 'apologize—offend again—apologize' cycle. Jen describes this loop as a record needle stuck in an old groove—you intellectually know what to do, but your body repeats the old reaction anyway. This paradox is what the episode sets out to resolve.

Mismatched attachment styles amplify mutual frustration

If a parent is anxiously attached, they constantly seek reassurance—‘Do you really love me?’—which an avoidantly attached child finds annoying. Conversely, when the child is anxiously attached and the parent is avoidant, the parent resents ‘how clingy’ the child is. Two anxious types endlessly confirm for each other; two avoidant types keep their distance and offer only minimal care. These frictions aren't character flaws—they're the accumulated result of both people's attachment histories.

— Jen
9:58

Depression, ADHD, alcoholism: maybe they're the same thing

Jen proposes a view she's increasingly convinced of: depression, anxiety, alcoholism, binge eating or starving, overwork, phone scrolling, even ADHD—these might not be separate problems but different expressions of the same root cause: long-term unmet needs. She argues that diagnosis is essentially society saying ‘we have expectations of you, and if you don't meet them we call you sick.’ These coping mechanisms work by numbing you, pulling you out of the present—and children's ‘misbehavior’ yanks you right back into it, which is why it's so unbearable.

— Jen
11:13

She disagrees that trigger patterns can't be changed

Before appearing on another podcast, What Fresh Hell, Jen listened to an episode about breaking patterns where host Margaret argued that the moment you're triggered is fixed and unchangeable—you can only change your response afterward. Jen disagrees. She's seen parents genuinely shift the trigger point itself, not just their reaction after it fires. She thinks simply seeing ‘reframe your thinking’ on social media isn't enough—that's only cognitive understanding. Next time you're triggered, your body still runs the old path.

— Jen
13:46

Collective empathy makes years of 'forgiveness' suddenly real

Student N's mother was an alcoholic. During years of therapy, N was repeatedly reminded ‘this is all about your mother,’ but saying ‘I forgive her’ always felt hollow. Then on day one of the Taming Your Triggers workshop, surrounded by hundreds of other students sharing their trigger stories, N finally felt empathy—not just sympathy—for her mother. She saw her as a twenty-something new mother carrying unresolved trauma, doing her best. Later N texted her mother that she forgave her. Her mother replied that she felt a weight lift.

— Jen
21:35

Wilderness first aid rule: ask 'is the scene safe' first

Drawing on wilderness first aid training—first assess whether the scene is safe and how to make it safe, only then tend to the injury—Jen advises that when your child falls apart, start by ensuring safety (pick up the baby, step between two fighting kids), then do nothing: put on noise-canceling headphones, stand in place, breathe, breathe again. Regulate yourself first before deciding what's next. Don't force words out while you're dysregulated.

— Jen
26:23

'I need five minutes alone' is not a need

Jen distinguishes needs from strategies to meet them: ‘I need my kid to listen to me’ and ‘I need to leave by 8:30’ are strategies, not needs. Take a parent rushing to work with a kid who won't put on shoes. The real needs are feeling responsible to your boss, competent at work, and connected and collaborative with your child. The child refusing shoes is often their only way to express their own needs for connection, safety, or comfort. Once you both step back from ‘who wins’ over the shoes to the needs underneath, suddenly there are multiple options.

— Jen
30:16

Understanding needs could save 90 hours a month

Jen does the math: if you spend 10–15 minutes negotiating with your kid over brushing teeth and getting dressed, plus an hour on bedtime routine, then resolving these frictions through understanding needs—rather than behavior control—could free up 90 hours a month. She stresses these hours get spent either way. The difference is whether you're spending them regulating your own dysregulation or actually showing up with your kid in line with your values.

Glossary

non-cognitive shift
A change that happens not just intellectually but in the body—not just 'knowing' but 'being' different.
AccountaBuddies
Pairs of workshop participants assigned to support and check in on each other between sessions.
Internal Family Systems (IFS)
A self-guided therapy approach (often supported by friends rather than a therapist) that treats internal conflicts as separate parts to understand rather than trauma to dig into.
temperament mismatch
When a parent and child have fundamentally different inborn personality traits creating friction regardless of effort—or when those traits are too similar and trigger each other.
