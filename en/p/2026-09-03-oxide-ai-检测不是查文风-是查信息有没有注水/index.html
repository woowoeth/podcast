AI Detection Isn't About Style—It's About Information Inflation

The tell of AI writing isn't style but the 'information inflation' where output exceeds input. A hedge fund titan's public admission about his AI-written op-ed became a turning point—Pangram's fourth-gen model has a false positive rate of about 1 in 24,000, and the more models converge, the easier they are to catch.

'Did you use AI?' is the wrong question—ask where the ideas came from

Stanley Druckenmiller published an op-ed in the WSJ that was obviously AI-written; someone ran it through Pangram and got 100% AI. The turning point: he didn't hide it, admitting 'I'm not a good writer by nature, I used AI,' and the WSJ refused to retract. Bryan pushed further: if you admit it, show the prompt. 'Did you use AI?' is a false binary; the real question is where the ideas came from—'write me an op-ed against the Treasury's action' versus 'here are five pages of my notes, polish them into an essay.'

— Max Spero
13:00

Detecting AI is theoretically impossible, but practically sufficient

In 2023, Max asked research friends whether AI text could be detected; the consensus was 'impossible, don't try, models will only get stronger.' Pangram's response was to abandon theoretical counterexamples (like 'have ChatGPT paraphrase any text to disprove it') and focus on practical scenarios: when input information is less than output, that's a trace of AI involvement—Bryan calls it word inflation. This definition also explains why early Yelp/Amazon fake reviews weren't a breakthrough: platforms thought AI reviews were only 3% and not worth the trouble.

— Max Spero
34:30

Catching AI isn't about em dashes—it's synthetic mirroring

Pangram is a classifier model, not a surface-rule em dash detector. The approach: take an open-source LLM, replace the 'predict next token' head with a classification head that outputs a 0–15 AI involvement score. Training data is built via 'synthetic mirroring': compress a human-written article into a prompt (like 'write a 200-word piece covering these three points'), then feed it to a random frontier model to get a human/AI pair on the same topic. A second layer uses editing prompts for clause-level labeling: unchanged text is human, edited text is AI-assisted, and entirely new sentences are AI-generated.

— Max Spero
40:00

A detector's lifeline is false positive rate, not accuracy

Pangram 4's false positive rate is about 1 in 24,000, measured by running millions of pre-2022 documents (when AI ghostwriting was nearly impossible); the false negative rate is about 1 in 300, measured by regenerating with frontier models from real user prompts in WildChat. Not perfect, but the direction is right. Bryan's experience is that false positives are extremely low, and that's the lifeline for whether the product gets taken seriously. Pangram 4's step change came from switching from a 512-token window to token-level output: even a human-written document with two AI sentences in the middle can be flagged word by word.

— Max Spero
46:00

A detector doesn't need 100% accuracy—just make cheating costly

Max says education is a huge market, and this year is the first time people realize 'a truly usable AI detector exists.' The product logic isn't 100% precision but creating friction on the shortcut—making 'throw your homework at AI' go from zero cost to some cost, so students take the next-easiest path, which is doing it themselves. That judgment applies to enterprise too: Oxide's hiring is a writing-intensive process, and LLM-generated boilerplate in 'why I want to join Oxide' is instantly recognizable; the detector just makes it explicit.

— Max Spero
50:00

'False positive' sob stories online are often astroturf ads

Max distinguishes two kinds of opposition. One group genuinely wishes detectors didn't exist: it lets authorities point to AI text in their profession. The other is the humanizer ecosystem's marketing: these tools claim to rewrite AI text to fool Turnitin and Pangram, selling to students caught cheating; they typically also include an 'AI detector' that first flags your text as AI, then sells the rewriting service. Many Reddit posts about 'teacher falsely flagged my essay as 76% AI, gave me a zero' are astroturf for this lead gen—run them through Pangram and they're all AI-written.

— Max Spero
59:00

Stronger models haven't made detection harder—they've made it easier

Asked if they're forever chasing frontier models, Max gives a counterintuitive take: two things happen at once—models get smarter, but post-training that repeatedly teaches 'correctness' and preferences narrows the output distribution far more than in the GPT-2 era. GPT-2 tried to mimic the full distribution of human output; today's models only give 'correct' tokens, not what an ordinary person would say. He and the Oxide trio agree: new models speak with a 'strangely erudite' flavor, and detection hasn't gotten harder—it's gotten easier.

— Max Spero
1:01:00

The next battleground: how much did the human actually input?

Pangram can already guess the model family internally: the probe's top-1 accuracy is 90%, but productization needs 98%. Further out is 'reverse Pangram'—inferring what prompt might have generated a text and how much context the model had: two bullet points or fifteen? That dimension changes the characterization of Druckenmiller's op-ed entirely—giving five pages of notes to polish is collaboration; giving five bullet points to expand is ghostwriting. The next detection debate isn't 'did you use AI?' but 'how much did the human actually input?'

— Max Spero

Glossary

synthetic mirroring
A technique that reverse-engineers a prompt from a human article, then has a model rewrite it to create matched human/AI sample pairs.
humanizer
A service that claims to rewrite AI text to evade detectors, primarily sold to students facing plagiarism checks.
classifier head
The output head of a large model, originally for predicting the next token, replaced with a classification scoring head.
WildChat
A dataset of real user conversations with AI, used by Pangram to estimate false negative rates.
