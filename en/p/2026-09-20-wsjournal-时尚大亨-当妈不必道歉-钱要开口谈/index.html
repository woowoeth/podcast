Fashion Mogul: Stop Apologizing for Being a Working Mom, and Talk About Money

Emma Grede says if women avoid talking about money, money avoids them; by talking about it first, picking the right partners, and doing the business others can't be bothered with, she turned overlooked customers into loyal repeat buyers.

A celebrity is an accelerator, not a partner

Grede says that if you throw a star and a brand together expecting to make money or build credibility, you will quickly find it doesn't hold up. A celebrity buys you one burst of exposure and attention, but it doesn't keep customers; only a strong loyalty to a good product brings them back. That's why she insists on treating the Kardashian sisters as real business partners rather than spokespeople — the partnership has to be bigger than ‘someone's follower count.’ She admits she was lucky to find partners capable of doing that.

— Emma Grede
4:19

Target the market nobody is taking seriously

Grede says that before any business she looks at the competitive landscape first, and knows what's declining and what might be needed. When Good American entered the plus-size market in 2016, the whole industry treated it as a bonus ‘extra 15%,’ and even today's talking points didn't exist. She believes it was rewriting the rules completely in a space where nobody was innovating and customers weren't understood that made the company work. She stresses that she never watches fashion week and never chases trends, because ‘if you're led by trends, you'll die very quickly.’

— Emma Grede
7:28

The harder the business, the more it's worth doing

On Off Season activewear, Grede says women are badly overlooked in fan merchandise: the stands are full of women, and women control the household's purchasing decisions, yet all they can buy is the same generic hoodies and oversized T-shirts. She sees that as a huge business opportunity. She also admits that negotiating licenses with the NFL, NBA and WNBA ‘isn't a question of spending money, it's extremely hard to do,’ but what excites her is precisely doing things that require huge investment and can bring huge returns — provided you really understand where the opportunity is and don't cut corners.

— Emma Grede
10:31

If you don't talk about money, money avoids you

Grede says that managing hundreds of employees and sitting on several company boards, she has observed a pattern at work: avoid conversations about money and money will avoid you. Men talk about money anywhere, anytime — mortgages, credit card rates, investment opportunities, big and small; women lack enough of those conversations, and enough specifics. She believes the money stories we grow up hearing stay with us unless someone tells us something different. That's why she does a podcast and wrote a book: to break that silence rather than hoard the information.

— Emma Grede
13:46

What she cares about is the path to ownership

Asked about her net worth, Grede says the figure Forbes reported ‘missed a little,’ but her favorite line is ‘who cares about those valuations.’ What she really cares about is the path to ownership: as a school leaver with very little education and someone who only came to America less than a decade ago, by any normal logic those numbers shouldn't belong to her. She deliberately and consciously put herself in that position, took on a lot of risk and made a lot of trade-offs. She also pushes back on the idea that you can manifest your way to millions, saying that doesn't work.

— Emma Grede
15:48

Mom guilt is a story handed down to us

Grede says that guilt is a story passed down through generations of grandmothers and mothers, with a default logic: if you're in New York and away from your kids, you should feel enormous guilt. But she says she actually enjoys it — three nights in a hotel, sleeping diagonally across the whole bed. She thinks many women actually feel fine about being away from their kids for a few days, it's just not cool to say so. She acknowledges she has childcare, infrastructure and a good partner, so she isn't permanently in apology mode about it, and says she has cleared that behavior out of her thinking and her vocabulary.

— Emma Grede
17:49

A three-hour mom at most — a joke, and also true

Grede explains her ‘three-hour mom at most’ line: she works Monday to Friday, and not nine to five, so by Saturday she isn't going to sit with her kids all day or make the whole weekend revolve around them — by noon she's ‘had enough of you lot.’ She says it as a joke, but it's also a way of saying you're allowed to be honest about parenting differently, and that a different way doesn't make you a good or bad mother. She thinks the better question is ‘what don't you do, and where do you spend your time,’ which is more useful to any woman.

— Emma Grede
19:50

Women keep putting the rules on trial themselves

Asked how she differs from Lean In or Girlboss, Grede says she doesn't think she's offering anything new, just telling her own truth, and she has the chance to tell it more fully than a 30-second video. She thinks the topic lingers in pop culture because we can't accept it — the rules set for women are constantly judged, discussed and argued over by women ourselves, and some of the responsibility for that is ours. She says every man in the room probably has no idea what she's talking about and just thinks ‘so what, carry on.’ She argues for accepting that our ways differ while the goal is shared: more women in power is a good thing.

— Emma Grede
