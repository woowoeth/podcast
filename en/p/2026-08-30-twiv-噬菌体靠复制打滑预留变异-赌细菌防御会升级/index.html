Phage bet-hedging via replication slippage and EBV antibodies

Slippage is not a replication error but a phage bet: the population always carries a few individuals whose reading frame is shifted by repeats, generating variants not yet selected. Most are adapted to the current environment; when bacterial defenses escalate, the low-frequency backup takes over.

Replication slippage leaves standing variation

Phages do not just wait for random mutations. Their genomes contain simple sequence repeats; during DNA replication, the polymerase occasionally slips, adding or deleting a base or two, shifting the reading frame and toggling a specific protein between on and off. Unlike translational frameshifting, this is a permanent change at the DNA level, inherited by progeny. Jolene's words: making the polymerase slip here or there is much easier and faster than acquiring a new gene or repairing a broken one.

— Jolene Ramsey
14:36

Low-level variation is a bet on the future

Bet-hedging means: most sequences fit the current environment, but a few low-frequency variants might fit the next one. In the bacteria-phage arms race, rather than waiting for a random mutation to be selected, slippage continuously generates low-frequency toggles. Jolene compresses the logic: you always have a low-level variant present that might be better suited to the next environment.

— Jolene Ramsey
18:48

Adenine repeat length determines defense switching

The AGT gene itself trades off between two risks: the α-glucosyltransferase it encodes modifies phage DNA to evade the bacterial type IV restriction-modification system, but modified DNA is recognized by other defenses. A run of adenines in the gene comes in lengths 6, 7, or 8; different lengths shift the reading frame, turning the protein function on or off. One slippage event lets the phage switch rapidly between two defense regimes without maintaining two gene sets.

— Jolene Ramsey
28:25

Reversible reversion frequency is ~10,000-fold higher than point mutation

They chose T4's R2A gene to test: it has a run of six adenines, and the phenotype is trackable—on hosts expressing the Rx defense system, loss of R2 function prevents plaque formation. The experiment showed that reversion frequency in this SSR region is about 10,000-fold higher than typical point mutation. In other words, the phage can both slip to turn off the gene to evade current defenses and use the same mechanism to quickly turn it back on.

— Jolene Ramsey
52:53

The best antibody has no in vitro shortcut

The goal was human monoclonal antibodies that neutralize EBV, but they did not rely solely on in vitro libraries like phage display; instead they returned to hybridomas: fusing immunized mouse B cells with myeloma cells, selecting with HAT medium, and screening on 384-well plates. The reason is that somatic hypermutation and junctional diversity during B cell maturation add or delete bases in CDR3, which is hard to mimic in vitro. To get a perfectly matched antibody, let B cells run the full germinal center reaction in mice, then re-express the heavy and light chain genes in cultured cells.

1:01:15

GP42 antibodies neutralize only B-cell infection

All eight antibodies against GP42 came from two clonal lineages; the mechanism is blocking EBV's GP42 protein from binding HLA class II, so they only block B-cell infection, not epithelial cells, which use a different entry mechanism. The strongest of the eight is ATX42-2. In subsequent humanized mouse experiments, 500 μg intraperitoneal injection and 11 weeks of observation, ATX42-2 prevented tumor formation, viral DNA, and splenomegaly.

1:09:40

Vaccine design now targets key epitopes

The GP350 subunit with alum adjuvant did not induce protective immunity, and GP42's conformational variability makes it hard to elicit the right antibody by conventional immunization. This paper's contribution is defining the key epitope and structural target when GP42 binds HLA class II, allowing vaccine design to work backward. Subsequent work has seen a GP350 ferritin nanoparticle vaccine phase 1 safe and immunogenic, with seronegative participants followed to 540 days without infection; Moderna is also running an mRNA vaccine containing gHgL, GP42, and GP220.

1:42:39

Anti-science may be the brain saving energy

Vincent uses Kahneman's 'fast and slow' to explain anti-science: the brain defaults to low-energy Type 1, while deep fact-checking is Type 2, requiring extra energy and startup cost. The quote on the wall from Joshua Reynolds—'There is no expedient to which a man will not resort to avoid the real labor of thinking'—becomes the footnote to this observation. People without statistical background hearing 'Tylenol causes autism' will accept it via lazy Type 1 rather than looking up the literature. He stresses this is speculation, not a causal conclusion.

Glossary

simple sequence repeats (SSR)
Tandem repeats of short motifs in the genome; polymerase slippage during replication changes the repeat number, toggling gene function.
bet-hedging
Maintaining a minority of variants that are not fit now but may fit future environments, to cope with unpredictable change.
hybridoma
An immortalized cell line formed by fusing an immune B cell with a myeloma cell, continuously secreting a single antibody.
HAT medium
Selective medium containing hypoxanthine, aminopterin, and thymidine, used to select successful hybridoma cells.
humanized mice
Mice engrafted with human hematopoietic stem cells or immune cells to partially reconstitute human immune processes in vivo.
