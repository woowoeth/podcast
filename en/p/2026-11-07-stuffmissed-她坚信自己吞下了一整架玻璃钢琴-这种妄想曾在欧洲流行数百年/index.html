She Believed She Had Swallowed an Entire Glass Piano: How This Delusion Swept Across Europe for Centuries

From the Middle Ages through the early 19th century, Europe saw repeated cases of the delusion "I am made of glass"—King Charles VI of France forbade anyone to touch him, and a royal physician once set fire to a bed to force a patient to jump up unharmed and supposedly "cure" his delusion.

Her father's scandal isolated her from the court

Ludwig I became embroiled in a public affair with Lola Montez, who claimed to be a Spanish aristocrat but was actually Irish. The scandal provoked public outrage in Munich, forcing him to abdicate in 1848. Alexandra was 22 at the time and the only unmarried sibling who hadn't died in infancy—her brothers and sisters had already married into royal families across Europe, leaving her alone in the palace. Her psychological state was already fragile, marked by cleanliness obsessions and a habit of wearing only white clothes.

She became convinced an entire glass piano was lodged inside her

A year after her father's abdication, at age 23, Alexandra was discovered walking in an odd contorted manner, as though moving sideways. When pressed for an explanation, she claimed to have swallowed an entire glass grand piano in childhood, which remained trapped in her body. She lived in constant fear that any collision would shatter the piano inside her, and this terror governed every cautious step she took.

7:18

After the delusion emerged, she became abbess of a convent

Around 1850, she was committed to a psychiatric hospital for treatment and appeared to recover. Beginning in 1852, she published collections of stories, essays, and poetry, and participated in work with a children's theater company. She eventually entered a convent in Munich and rose to become its abbess—evidence that her condition had improved enough to allow her to hold positions requiring genuine authority. She died on September 21, 1875, at age 49.

8:19

Glass delusion troubled Europe repeatedly for centuries

Alexandra was not alone in this experience. From the Middle Ages through the early 19th century, Europe witnessed repeated cases of people convinced they were turning into glass, that they were made of glass, or that they had become a glass object—most commonly reported as a chamber pot or lamp. Sufferers lived in terror of being touched, and even feared that loud noise would cause them to shatter. Many withdrew into complete isolation, which only deepened and reinforced their delusions. Before the medieval period, similar fears had focused on pottery rather than glass; the underlying logic remained constant: one impact and the person would break.

9:19

Setting a bed on fire could cure the glass delusion

King Charles VI believed his entire body was made of glass and forbade anyone from touching him. He wore specially designed clothing reinforced with rib supports to prevent himself from breaking. Around 1614, Ponce de Santa Cruz, the royal physician to King Philip II of Spain, documented the case of a likely royal patient who was prescribed to sleep on a straw mattress as a safety measure against fracturing. The physician then set the mattress ablaze, forcing the patient to jump up suddenly and escape unharmed—this artificial crisis shattered the delusion by proving the patient could endure impact without breaking. Another French court physician, Louis de Cazenave, recorded a case of a glassworker who remained convinced his buttocks were made of glass, reportedly "cured" when the physician spanked him hard enough to break through the delusion.

11:22

Those who believed they were glass tended to be educated

One theory proposes that the people historically documented as suffering from glass delusions were predominantly educated individuals. They may have encountered medical writings describing this syndrome, and those texts may have shaped the specific form their delusions took, rather than the delusions arising spontaneously. Physicians of that era linked the condition to obsessions with chastity, purity, and fate—likely because glass carried symbolic resonance in folklore as a fragile substance whose shattering represented broken fortune.

14:26

The piano symbolized her fear of her own fragility

In the 2012 BBC audio program "The Glass Piano," analyst Susie Orbach observed that Alexandra's specific choice to imagine swallowing a piano—rather than any other glass object—was significant. Pianos ought to evoke softness and musicality, yet in her mind she had ingested something massive and brittle. Had she been able to play it, the piano could have produced music; instead it became a symbol of something beautiful rendered dangerous by her conviction that it would shatter. Historian Erin Sullivan connected this delusion to melancholia, interpreting it as a manifestation of deep fear about revealing her vulnerability to others.

16:32

Without outside intervention, the delusion could only intensify

The hosts emphasize that the royal physicians' aggressive interventions—setting fires, administering spankings—succeeded precisely because they introduced an irrefutable outside reality that contradicted the delusion. The patient's body did not break; the evidence was undeniable. But for someone like Alexandra, confined to a palace with no one to confide in, no such intervention existed. Without anyone to challenge the narrative she had constructed, the delusion only fed upon itself, growing more elaborate with each passing day. One host acknowledges her own anxious temperament and expresses empathy for how, in such profound isolation, intrusive thoughts take root and spiral, step by step.
