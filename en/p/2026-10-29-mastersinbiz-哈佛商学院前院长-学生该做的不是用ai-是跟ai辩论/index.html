Former dean of Harvard Business School: students should debate AI, not use it

He requires students to read AI-generated answers aloud in class, then explain what they agree with and disagree with—this kind of collaborative interrogation is the skill that actually carries into the workplace in the AI era.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:07

Parroting AI answers isn't enough; students must interrogate every line.

Nohria won't accept it when students say "I checked with AI." Instead, he requires them to read the AI's full answer aloud, then asks: Which parts do you agree with? Which do you reject? What follow-up questions did you ask to think deeper? He believes this ability to leverage AI while maintaining rigorous skepticism will become a mandatory workplace skill, not an optional one.

— Nitin Nohria
2:07

AI is armor, but each person must calibrate it to their own superpowers.

Nohria uses a Marvel analogy: AI is like the armor suit everyone can now equip, but not every superhero has the same powers—the suit must be tuned to each person's strengths. In other words, the differentiator isn't knowing how to use AI; it's how you tune AI into your own distinctive capability. That calibration is what will set future job seekers apart.

— Nitin Nohria
5:12

Some students lock in early; others need years of experimentation to find direction.

After nearly 40 years of teaching, Nohria identifies two types of students. Some know their calling early; he tells them not to waste time experimenting—dive in. Others (himself included) spent undergrad and grad school without knowing what they wanted, discovering their direction only through trial and error. But once body and mind give a clear signal about what matters, commit fully without hesitation.

— Nitin Nohria
7:15

80% of startups end up doing something entirely different from their original plan.

Harvard colleague Amar Bhide's research on hundreds of startups found that 80% end up doing something that's only a distant cousin of their original plan—even the successful ones. Nohria sees this pattern in regular careers too: someone who starts in consulting may not stay there. The goal isn't to predict the endpoint; it's to stay open to adjacent fields while learning which detours are genuine dead ends and which are just temporary discomfort.

— Nitin Nohria
8:17

Give unhappy jobs three to six months before deciding they're dead ends.

Nohria offers a concrete decision rule: if you're unhappy in your job, give yourself at least 3 to 6 months before deciding it's a dead end. The reasoning: if you jump every time you feel dissatisfied, you never develop the resilience that every job and career demands. Careers are marathons, not all-out sprints; slowing down in the middle is normal.

— Nitin Nohria
11:20

Mastery of AI tools is replacing Excel mastery as the baseline professional skill.

J.P. Morgan's fixed-income head Bob Michael told interns that their generation differentiates itself through AI mastery the way the previous generation did through Excel. Nohria agrees with the comparison, and sees it as a correction to the "AI is just a helper" dismissal—proficiency with AI tools is becoming as foundational a workplace skill as spreadsheet mastery once was, not a bonus.

— Nitin Nohria
12:21

Elite college credentials still matter, but lack of them is no longer a barrier.

Nohria concedes that prestigious employers like Goldman Sachs and McKinsey carry their brand for a reason: they do provide genuine learning opportunities—and that hasn't changed. But he's noticed that Harvard Business School students are increasingly willing to start somewhere with no brand cache. Why? Because they've watched Facebook and Google scale from nothing in a decade, so they're willing to take higher risk for the chance to build something transformative.

— Nitin Nohria
14:25

About half of Business School graduates eventually pursue some form of entrepreneurship.

What has surprised Nohria throughout his career isn't whether students will pursue entrepreneurship, but when. He's found that about 50% of Harvard Business School graduates end up choosing some form of entrepreneurial path—whether launching a small consulting firm, doing their own investing, or returning home to run a family business. Some trigger it right after graduation, others wait 25 years; the catalyst is different for everyone, with no universal pattern.

— Nitin Nohria
