RetroByte 1998-12-08

Jobs admits: none of last year's ten most popular games run on Mac

Jobs acknowledges Apple failed to bring even one hit game to Mac for years—this self-criticism was more candid than anything he said about competitors.

AppleSteve JobsProduct strategyCorporate cultureGaming ecosystemWeb portals
This is a recording of Q&A with college IT managers, without the polished rhetoric of product launches—Jobs' assessment of Apple's failures in recent years was more candid than the outside world expected.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:08

Cooperation with Microsoft delivered better Mac software than competition could have

When Jobs arrived at Apple, the company and many users operated under a core assumption: ‘Apple wins, Microsoft loses.’ He considered this thinking crazy and set about inverting it. The results vindicated him: the best browser for Mac is Microsoft's Internet Explorer, with more features than the Windows version; the best Office for Mac is not the Windows release but Office for Mac itself. This outcome resulted not from competition but from partnership.

— Steve Jobs
9:13

Apple refused to compete with white-box PCs on price, only on value

When an IT manager complained that white-box PCs with displays sold for only $800 against Apple's education price of over $1100, Jobs explained: white-box builders average just 35 units per month with inconsistent quality. Rather than compete in that tier—where ‘repairs cost more than buying new’—Apple's strategy was to compress the price gap year by year until customers ‘willingly paid more,’ without ever cramming Mac OS into cheap Intel machines.

— Steve Jobs
24:27

Prioritizing profit over product quality nearly destroyed Apple

Apple's original mission had two parts: make the world's best computer and make money—learned from Hewlett-Packard—so the company could sustain itself and keep building great products. At some point, without anyone noticing clearly, this order inverted: make money first, good computers second. This reversal was subtle—it takes five years to perceive, but five years later it becomes everything. Near-bankruptcy forced Apple to restore the original hierarchy.

— Steve Jobs
30:42

World-class engineers prove fifty times more valuable than average ones

Most disparities between ‘best’ and ‘average’ in everyday life measure 30–50%—a luxury sedan versus a standard taxi, premium seating versus economy—and rarely exceed double. Hardware engineering proved different: the best engineers (Jobs cited Wozniak) were 50 times better than average. This observation shaped his hiring: recruit only people in the 10–50x range. These people naturally gravitated toward each other and self-policed who belonged on the team.

— Steve Jobs
35:00

Television and computers cannot merge because they serve opposite mental states

When asked about ‘digital convergence,’ Jobs dismissed it straightforwardly: ‘TV should do toast.’ Rejecting conspiracy theories, he explained that TV networks weren't hiding anything—they simply satisfied what viewers genuinely wanted: to turn their brains off for half an hour after work. Computers served the opposite purpose: to turn brains on, demand interaction, invite leaning in, reward sitting upright. WebTV had already proven that such fusion ‘completely failed,’ and television and computers would not merge into one device.

— Steve Jobs
48:32

Web portals are commoditizing the same way beige personal computers once did

The internet's greatest gap is the absence of ‘trusted editorial perspective.’ The New York Times derives value not from information itself but from filtering what readers don't need to know—a kind of judgment not yet present online. He criticized current web portals for ‘all looking the same,’ much like how every PC maker once sold ‘beige rectangular boxes.’ The first generation of portals will homogenize as the industry matures, though new spaces will eventually emerge.

— Steve Jobs
52:45

The right team matters more than hiring a permanent CEO

When challenged about Apple's lack of a formal CEO, Jobs replied that he'd looked in the mirror that morning and decided ‘this is not your problem’—and would not give it another thought for a year. His focus was the team itself. Upon arrival, he'd assumed the best people had departed; instead, he found roughly one-third were ‘10–50x level’ talent and another third were burned-out or misfitting ‘barnacles,’ concentrated in senior management. Clearing out the latter unlocked the company's real turnaround.

— Steve Jobs
1:04:10

None of last year's ten bestselling games ran on Mac

A university IT manager presented seven data points questioning Apple's real performance in higher education and consumer markets. Jobs invoked the image of ‘a snake swallowing a mouse’—describing the sacrifice Apple had made over recent years—and emphasized that this ‘mouse must be fully digested; there is no shortcut.’ He acknowledged that last Christmas, zero of the ten most popular games in America ran on Mac, rendering Apple ‘uncool’ in children's eyes. He pledged that four would reach Mac this Christmas, nine next Christmas, with graphics surpassing PC versions.

— Steve Jobs
