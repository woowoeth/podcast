Kindergarten goodbyes: tears are not adjustment failure, but proof of trust

One child is rated a ‘leader’ by teachers yet cries daily at drop-off; another excels at ‘playing the good kid’ and collapses at home. Same root cause: children carry weight at school and only unload it at home.

9:14

A quiet classroom doesn't signal unhappiness or poor fit

The mother observed her daughter's kindergarten classroom was much quieter than preschool, with children sitting still on a mat rather than on her lap and chiming in. She worried the emphasis on obedience sacrificed classroom joy. Janet acknowledges this is a common gap between preschool and kindergarten, but explains it doesn't signal poor adjustment. The teacher and principal both describe the daughter as a ‘leader’—evidence she's thriving, not being crushed by the environment.

— listener mail/Janet
10:21

Perfectionist children naturally feel sharper school-entry pressure

Janet observes that only children or eldest daughters who are high-achievers often want to ‘slide elegantly’ into new environments. They don't want to visibly struggle or be in learning mode; they want to get it right immediately. This personality naturally amplifies the normal stress of adaptation. But it's not evidence the environment is wrong for them—just that this temperament inherently feels the pressure more sharply.

— Janet
14:36

She asks: ‘Will anyone notice I'm crying?’

Before crying at the gate, the daughter sings a song and performs a ‘kiss hand’ goodbye ritual. The moment she starts crying, she wipes her tears immediately and asks ‘Can I cry for a bit?’, then ‘Do I look okay now? Can I go in?’ Janet finds this detail crucial: the child is already worried that showing vulnerability will get her blamed by teachers. This worry itself reveals how hard she's trying to live up to the ‘leader’ role.

— listener mail
16:43

Tears are not adjustment failure, but her doing what's right

Janet shares her own habit of crying before major speeches. The first time it surprised her, but she found that crying made her feel better and allowed her to show up vulnerable and human to her audience. She urges the mother to go further than just ‘allowing’ tears: actively tell the daughter that crying feels good, nobody minds if she walks in with wet cheeks, and in fact others will connect with her more authentically.

— Janet
18:43

Goodbye rituals are lovely, but may mask real anxiety

Janet raises a caution: the mother's pre-gate singing and intimate goodbye gesture are beautiful, but warrant honest self-examination. If they're meant to ‘gloss over’ her own hidden doubts about the school rather than simply celebrate love, the child can see through it. Janet stresses that children always sense our true feelings; the parent must first feel genuinely settled about the environment herself.

— Janet
25:10

Playing the good kid at school exacts a home-life cost

A six-year-old boy is praised by teachers as well-behaved and thoughtful. But at home he spits, shrieks, destroys his older siblings' things, and resists bath time and bed. His parents suspect he's so hard on himself at school to compensate for the chaos created by classmates with special needs who get lots of negative attention. At school he plays ‘good child’; at home he has nowhere for that stress.

— listener mail
26:14

He's absorbing others' emotions, not falling apart himself

Janet reframes the boy's behavior. He's processing and venting the struggles he witnesses from other children in the classroom; he's a sensitive, empathetic child absorbing their emotions and even mimicking their specific behaviors, then needing to expel it all at home. This isn't ordinary meltdown—it's the accumulated cost of playing ‘school's good kid’ while carrying others' weight.

— Janet
28:21

‘I know I don't want to, but I can't stop’

When the boy expresses that he knows he doesn't want to behave this way, and in calm moments says the same thing, but admits he doesn't know how to stop, Janet sees this as a sign of safety and trust in the parent relationship. The parent's job isn't to find him an answer but to stay curious and keep asking what's happening at school, so he knows his parents truly have his back.

— Janet
