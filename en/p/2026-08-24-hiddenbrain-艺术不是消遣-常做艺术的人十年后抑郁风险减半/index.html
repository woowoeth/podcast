Art Is Not a Diversion: Regular Art Participation Halves Depression Risk a Decade Later

Treat art as a daily dose rather than an occasional diversion: cohort studies tracking tens of thousands of people show that regular art participation is associated with roughly half the risk of depression over the following decade, and the relationship holds independent of demographic, behavioural and genetic factors.

Dopamine isn't only released at the peak

Daisy Fancourt says art activates the brain's reward network, such as the dopaminergic system, releasing dopamine and other neurotransmitters. People used to think dopamine only showed up at the high point of an art experience — the moment the chorus comes back, say; we now know it is also released in anticipating those moments: the build-up before the chorus returns, or the point in a detective novel where the murderer is about to be revealed. An art experience is essentially a continuous production of tension and resolution, expectation and confirmation or disappointment, so it amounts to feeding the brain dopamine over and over. People who participate regularly report higher wellbeing and life satisfaction; if they make and create themselves, it also affects self-esteem, sense of meaning and autonomy.

— Daisy Fancourt
12:26

Why horror films can be good for you

Watching horror films or reading sad books looks like the opposite of wellbeing, but Fancourt says the mechanism is paradoxical: in art, people experience positive and negative emotions at the same time. Because they know it is art, the brain sets aside a kind of "aesthetic distance" — the emotion is experienced but does not have to be dealt with; you don't actually have to stop the fear or change the situation. So the brain gets a rehearsal in emotion regulation, and since the brain is itself a prediction machine, this exposure can help it handle complex situations in reality. Sad songs work the same way: they offer a genuine emotional connection, a sense that others have been through it too. But there is a precondition: you have to be aware that it is art. Fancourt says she gets fully absorbed in horror films and loses that distance, and then all that is left is fear.

— Daisy Fancourt
25:55

Regular art participation halves depression risk

Fancourt is an epidemiologist. Using cohort data from the US and UK that track tens of thousands of people over decades, she has published dozens of papers in the past ten years on the relationship between art participation and depression risk decades later. The conclusion: people who participate in art regularly and have an arts hobby have roughly half the risk of depression over the following ten years compared with non-participants. She stresses this is not because those people are healthier, wealthier or live differently — the statistical methods already control for demographics, behaviour, genetics, other aspects of life and even past medical history, and the relationship is independent of those factors. As for causal direction, she says there is indeed a bidirectional effect, but the effect of art participation on future mental health is stronger than the reverse.

— Daisy Fancourt
28:00

Art gives the brain a whole-brain workout

Fancourt says participating in art amounts to an effective whole-brain workout: reading music involves visual processing, playing involves motor responses, and memory, emotion and several other brain regions are recruited at the same time. Neuroimaging studies show that people who participate in art regularly have greater size and connectivity between brain regions. Longitudinally, those who participate more regularly preserve cognition better and even have a younger "brain age" — estimated from connectivity patterns, once people reach older age, amateur or professional art practitioners' brains are on average five to seven years younger than their actual age. She also mentions cognitive reserve: even when the brain has begun to accumulate the plaques typical of Alzheimer's disease, people with high cognitive reserve can compensate for longer and show dementia symptoms later. She incidentally dismisses the "brain training app" approach — those games train one cognitive function at a time, whereas art activates multiple cognitive processes at once, and that is art's advantage.

— Daisy Fancourt
34:19

Schedule art like a daily dose

Fancourt suggests treating art like diet and exercise: set a simple rule, such as ten to twenty minutes a day. Her own approach is a "creative commute" — on the train she is not allowed to scroll her phone, and instead listens to music or reads. She stresses that consistency matters more than the length of any single session: four hours one day and nothing the other six is worse than twenty minutes every day, because regularity is what turns it into a habit and what delivers the immediate benefits for stress and mood; the long-term benefits — brain health, lower risk of mental and physical illness — depend on a habit sustained over months and years. She also mentions a threshold number: the literature shows that at around fifty hours of practice on something, your skill level is enough to let you "take off" on your own and enter flow, so give yourself a few weeks to adjust.

— Daisy Fancourt
38:26

"I can't draw" is manufactured shame

Many people say "I don't understand music" or "I draw like a dying animal." Fancourt thinks this shame shows that society has commodified art participation: it has turned it into something you "have to be good at," or that you are "only allowed to do if you have had special training." Her counterexample is children — all children sing, dance, make up stories, act, and draw and make things nonstop; they don't worry about whether they are any good, they just enjoy the process. So the key reminder is: the health benefits of art come from doing, not necessarily from doing it well. She also suggests planning an art calendar the way you plan meals: book tickets for exhibitions, music festivals, films or performances in advance, and schedule novelty and awe into your diary, complementing the daily dose.

— Daisy Fancourt
41:33

Consuming art and making it are not the same

Fancourt distinguishes passive viewing from active participation. When viewing, people take in a lot of stimulation; when making something yourself, there are additional stimuli such as touch, which many people find healing in itself, and improving skill brings a sense of mastery, self-esteem and accomplishment. For many mental health outcomes, both viewing and participating help, just in slightly different ways; but for outcomes like mobility and pain, active participation is the "active ingredient" that provides extra sensory engagement and mechanisms. She also flags the "hedonic treadmill" effect: you adapt even to things you love, and the returns can diminish, so you should deliberately switch types, add difficulty and bring in entirely new art hobbies, using novelty to keep the brain engaged.

— Daisy Fancourt
43:42

Background music doesn't get you the full benefit

Fancourt says playing music in the background still reduces stress responses, but you don't get the maximum effect. You need to consciously tell whether you are consuming passively or paying full attention. When you pay full attention, emotional responses and stress responses are both amplified, and it is easier to enter flow — that state of complete immersion where you forget everything — which has profound benefits for mental health and for brain health too: it produces synchronisation between specific brain regions and improves overall attention. Shankar uses dessert as an analogy: savour it slowly and the dessert tastes two or three times better. Fancourt agrees, saying that when you pay full attention all the senses are amplified, and dopamine release and wellbeing both increase.

— Daisy Fancourt

Glossary

aesthetic distance
The psychological gap created by knowing something is art, which lets you experience an emotion without having to deal with it.
cognitive reserve
The brain's capacity to compensate for damage; people with higher reserve show dementia symptoms later.
hedonic treadmill
People adapt to things they enjoy, so returns diminish and you need to actively introduce novelty.
flow state
A state of complete immersion in which you forget everything else, beneficial for mental and brain health.
