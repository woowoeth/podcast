Leveraged ETFs track daily returns, not annualized multiples

A leveraged ETF's prospectus states it tracks a multiple of the underlying index's daily return, not a multiple of long-term annualized returns. The fund gains leveraged exposure through swap agreements with banks—for example, a $1 billion 2x fund must enter a $2 billion swap on the underlying asset and rebalance the swap size every day after close to maintain its target leverage ratio. This leverage is not free; its cost fluctuates with borrowing rates, and the sharp rise in rates over recent years has directly increased the cost of holding these products.

— Chris Murray
13:18

Up 10% then down 10%: stock loses 1%, 2x ETF loses 4%

If an underlying asset gains 10% on day one and loses 10% on day two, the arithmetic average return is zero, but after compounding it actually loses 1%—this is the compounding effect all assets experience, unrelated to leverage. With a daily 2x leveraged ETF, day one gains 20% and day two loses 20%, but the result is not a 2% loss; it's a 4% loss. Of the extra 2%, roughly 1 basis point comes from convexity bonus, and about 2.01% comes from volatility drag. These two are often conflated, but are actually two separate effects.

— Marco Sammon
17:21

Daily reset actually keeps you from going to zero

An underlying asset falls from 100 to 75, then to 50—a cumulative 50% decline. If you apply 2x leverage only once initially and never adjust again, after paying back the debt you'd be at zero. But with a daily-reset 2x leveraged ETF, the first day's decline takes your position from 100 to 50, and the second day you lose two-thirds of that lower base, leaving you with $16.67, not zero. Part of this difference comes from convexity bonus, and part is eroded by volatility drag—the convexity bonus itself comes from daily (or any periodic) reset, which static leverage never provides.

— Marco Sammon
25:35

Leveraged ETF trading costs hide in the closing price

Leveraged ETFs trade only at close, and don't pay transaction costs through bid-ask spreads. Instead, they place large orders into the closing auction, directly pushing the closing price itself higher or lower. Suppose a stock should have closed up 8% on a given day; if a leveraged ETF's purchase pushes that closing price up to 9%, this amplified daily return feeds directly into the volatility drag formula (the sum of squared daily returns), making the drag larger. This mechanism becomes significant only when the leveraged product is large relative to the underlying's liquidity and the day's volatility is pronounced—for example, certain Korean single-stock ETFs.

— Chris Murray
41:06

By arithmetic mean return, infinite leverage is optimal

If you evaluate leveraged products using ‘expected terminal wealth’or ‘expected arithmetic mean return’, you reach a counterintuitive conclusion: as long as the underlying has positive expected returns, the optimal leverage is always infinite, because this metric favors volatility—a fund with 10% annualized returns and 40% volatility, even if it only turned $100 into $102 last year, will rank above a fund with 8% returns, low volatility, that turned $100 into $107 last year. Switch to ‘expected log return’or ‘expected log terminal wealth’, and the optimized leverage falls back into intuitive ranges like 2x or 1.5x.

— Chris Murray
50:21

Break-even thresholds jump exponentially with volatility

The ‘break-even return’metric measures how much an underlying must rise for a 2x or 3x leveraged ETF to outperform the underlying itself. Taking a broad market index with a 4% risk-free rate, 0.5% funding spread, and 1% daily volatility as an example: the threshold is an 8.4% rise in the underlying; at 3% daily volatility, the threshold jumps to 32%; at 5% daily volatility (close to Nvidia-scale), a 2x product's threshold becomes 100%; add another layer of leverage for 3x with the same 5% daily volatility, and the underlying must rise 170% before a 3x ETF outperforms the stock itself.

— Marco Sammon
55:41

Lucid's intraday liquidation wipes out the 2x ETF to zero

Lucid's stock gapped down sharply one day due to negative news, prompting market-making banks to worry about insufficient margin in their swap accounts. They chose to liquidate the swap position intraday. The selling pressure from their liquidation pushed the stock price lower still, but by the time the banks finished unwinding, the stock closed down only 16% that day. Investors holding 2x long Lucid leveraged ETF would have expected a loss proportional to 2 times the 16% close—negative 32%—but instead got wiped out to zero. This happened because the liquidation trigger was the intraday low, not the closing price, which is nearly impossible to replicate in historical backtests using closing prices.

— Chris Murray
1:14:35

Inverse ETF inflow of $93 billion dwindles to $13 billion

When you account for ‘when the money actually flowed in’, the results contradict intuition: during the sample period, investors in broad market leveraged ETFs, compared to those who put the same capital into the underlying at the same times, collectively made an extra $50 billion—because they didn't all enter at the worst time (like March 2020). Single-stock leveraged ETFs show much smaller differences. But inverse (short) leveraged ETFs are another story: during the sample period they saw cumulative inflows of $93 billion, but only $13 billion in AUM remains, with 86% of principal vanished.

Glossary

Convexity bonus
Daily reset generates additional positive returns by squaring rather than doubling gains—more pronounced with larger swings.
Volatility drag
Larger volatility on the underlying erodes leveraged product wealth exponentially over time.
Break-even return
The return needed from the underlying for a leveraged ETF's performance to exceed the underlying itself.
Swap
A contract between the ETF and a bank to gain leveraged exposure beyond the fund's own assets.
Expected log wealth
A return metric using logarithmic returns to avoid distortion from extreme paths.
