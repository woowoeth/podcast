The Rest Is History 2026-08-19

Britain knew nothing about Northern Ireland, and hardline arson dragged Ireland toward civil war

The Northern Ireland conflict was not simple sectarian hatred: Britain's information vacuum, loyalist arson staged on purpose, and the civil rights movement's genuine grievances amplified one another and together pulled Ireland toward civil war.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:53

One council house allocation set off the entire civil rights movement

The village of Caledon handed its one available council house to a 19-year-old Protestant, Emily Beatty, while more than 250 Catholic families were still on the waiting list. Austin Curry and the new generation of nationalists shaped by the American civil rights movement narrowed the protest demands to two things: fair housing and ‘one man, one vote’. Ian Paisley immediately defined the movement as a ‘Trojan horse’ for the IRA. That definition was partly right: when NICRA was founded it did include Communist Party members and IRA men, even a Belfast Brigade commander. But the movement quickly outgrew anything the IRA could control, pulling in large numbers of students and ordinary people. Housing injustice was the first match struck.

21:08

Blood in front of the cameras did more than any petition

On 5 October 1968 the Derry march had already been banned, and roughly 400 people took to the streets anyway. The RUC's violent dispersal left about 80 people injured, and MP Gerry Fitt was beaten until his face was covered in blood. Irish television filmed the police attacking marchers and the footage went around the world; Derry then went into several days of rioting. This was the decisive turning point in the escalation: the civil rights movement shifted from petitioning to confrontation, and for the first time the RUC's repressive character was exposed on camera. At the police station Gerry Fitt deliberately did not wash the blood off his face — he said he wanted the outside world to see the blood running down it. The evidence itself became the movement's weapon.

33:06

Off-duty police in the ambush party made the state one of the combatants

On 1 January 1969 the People's Democracy group set out to walk from Belfast to Derry. Bernadette Devlin and the others deliberately chose a provocative route, hoping that violence would expose the repressive nature of the state. On 4 January, as they reached Burntollet Bridge, around 400 loyalists ambushed them — with off-duty B Specials among the attackers — while the RUC stood by on the scene and did nothing. The episode showed something more clearly than the Derry demonstration had: the armed force of the Northern Ireland state and loyalist violence were one and the same thing, and the marchers were both beaten and humiliated. O'Neill promptly launched a ‘back the Prime Minister’ petition and collected 150,000 signatures, but the petition could not repair the fractures inside the Unionist Party.

42:18

The prime minister who won the election was already a dead man walking

In the February 1969 election O'Neill beat Ian Paisley by a narrow margin of 1,400 votes, and that was the only good news he had all night. The results in working-class Protestant districts showed moderate unionism bleeding badly while the hardliners surged across the board, and from that point the Unionist Party split into two camps. O'Neill kept the premiership but lost his authority and became a ‘dead man walking’. He was not defeated by Paisley; he was hollowed out by betrayal within his own camp and by the polarisation of the electorate. The election results said more plainly than any slogan could that Northern Ireland's political spectrum was migrating fast toward the violent end, and that there was no ground left for the centre to stand on.

43:27

The bombers hitting water and power sites were not the IRA but Protestant hardliners

From March 1969 a series of explosions hit water and electricity installations across Northern Ireland, and Ian Paisley publicly insisted the IRA had done it. The actual planners were the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) and his own Ulster Protestant Volunteers. Gusty Spence, the UVF leader, admitted from prison that the point of the bombings was to manufacture the appearance of an IRA threat, to prove O'Neill was weak and force him out. Paisley knew and went on lying. This fact upgrades the ‘Protestant hardliners’ from verbal incitement to armed subversion: part of the supposed IRA war narrative was staged by their opponents' own side, and neither the media nor political rivals could expose it in time. The ‘truth’ of the Northern Ireland conflict was manufactured from the start.

47:46

Northern Ireland's problem is the structure of rule, not individual acts of violence

Bernadette Devlin, 21, won a by-election and became the youngest woman ever to sit in the House of Commons. Her maiden speech on 22 April was blunt: there could be no justice as long as the Unionist Party ruled Northern Ireland; they were corrupt, bigoted and self-serving. She also warned that if Britain sent in troops, the mothers and sisters of those soldiers would not thank them for it. The line was quoted again and again afterwards — the British Army did go in, and got dragged into the decades-long quagmire she had predicted. Her speech mattered not just because it enraged the Commons, but because it located the root of the Northern Ireland problem in the structure of rule itself rather than in isolated acts of violence — a far deeper reading than most politicians in London had at the time.

49:55

London acting inside an information vacuum was more dangerous than acting wrongly

On 7 May 1969 the British cabinet discussed its options for intervention. Jim Callaghan put forward a plan to step in; the Defence Secretary, Denis Healey, said that going in and taking over would be madness, that they would be siding with the Catholics and the civil rights movement against the Northern Ireland government and the UVF — and that they knew nothing about any of it. The remark became an excuse the British government used repeatedly over the following decade. ‘Knowing nothing’ was not just self-deprecation; it was the real political situation: Whitehall had no actionable intelligence about Northern Ireland's sectarian structure, its armed police, or loyalist violence. The consequence was that every move London made was a reaction taken inside an information vacuum — which is more dangerous than simply getting it wrong, because it left the decisions in the hands of extremists in Belfast.

1:04:05

Sending in the British Army turned an internal conflict into Britain against Ireland

By 14 August 1969 the gun battles on Divis Street in Belfast had killed 7 people, injured about 1,000 and destroyed 200 houses; 9-year-old Patrick Rooney was killed in his bed by machine-gun fire from an armoured car. That afternoon the Northern Ireland Prime Minister, Chichester-Clark, formally asked London to send troops, and Jim Callaghan scrawled ‘permission granted’ on a plane. The next day British troops moved into the Falls Road. At first Catholics treated the army as protectors, but the government had no political strategy, only a military reaction. The intervention escalated the conflict from an internal Northern Ireland problem into a confrontation between Britain and the Irish community, and planted the seeds of the later IRA campaign and decades of violence. That is what ‘the brink of civil war’ really means: every side was waiting for an outside actor to intervene, and the intervening party did not know what it was there to do.

Glossary

RUC / Royal Ulster Constabulary
Northern Ireland's Protestant-dominated police force, widely seen as the armed backbone of the Unionist Party.
UVF / Ulster Volunteer Force
A Protestant loyalist paramilitary group that staged bombings in 1969 to frame the IRA.
NICRA / Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association
A civil rights organisation founded in 1967 demanding fair housing and voting rights; its early membership included people with IRA backgrounds.
B Specials
The part-time reserve force of the Royal Ulster Constabulary, largely Protestant hardliners, who took part in the Burntollet ambush.
