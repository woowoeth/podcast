Her Studio Contract Locked Her Weight: Gain and You're in Breach, Diet and You're Dead

In the 1920s, the actress Molly was benched by her studio for gaining weight and ended up on an operating table; in the 1990s, the bride Mary died from the diet drug fen-phen. When the body becomes a commercial asset, punishment and shortcuts lead to the same place.

1:21

The contract locked down her body

In 1920s Hollywood, the big studios controlled production, distribution and theaters, and treated actors as assets on the balance sheet. Molly's contract specified working hours, exclusivity, and one special clause: as long as she wanted to work, she had to keep the same bodily contours forever. When her body changed naturally, the studio invoked the "facial and bodily disfigurement" clause, treated adolescent weight gain as breach of contract and stopped her pay. The contract gave commercial power a legal basis to intervene in the body — that is the foundation of the whole episode's narrative.

11:43

The ultimatum: slim down or you're out

Production manager Al Rockett summoned Molly and delivered the ultimatum: you can get as fat as you like, but the studio won't use you until you get down to the "correct size." At the time Molly weighed only about 20 pounds more than the average screen actress of her height. She wasn't indulging herself — she was a teenager still growing, and always hungry. But 1920s aesthetics demanded the pencil-thin flapper figure. The punishment cut off her income directly and forced her to comply.

15:56

A twelve-week deadline to lose weight

The studio gave Molly twelve weeks to restrict calories and fit back into her old costumes. In the industry of the day, Lila Lee drank only orange juice and lost eight pounds in under two weeks, and Vilma Banky had to lose fifteen pounds before she was hired. The press publicly humiliated Molly's "solid flesh," even inventing a rumor that she wanted to lose weight by catching influenza. Fans wrote letters of protest, but the studio was unmoved. What this shows is systemic pressure: not one person's judgment of her, but an entire industry's standardization.

19:03

The diet black market: from tapeworms to thyroxine

The 1920s "diet craze" spawned a flood of dangerous products: laxative chewing gum, slimming corsets, soaps claiming to dissolve fat. More extreme still were swallowed tapeworms, and Rido Fat, an over-the-counter drug containing concentrated thyroid extract — in 1926 a dieter who overdosed on it quickly developed heart disease and died at 33. Molly tried and still couldn't fit into her costumes, so she turned her gaze to surgery.

25:18

Surgery: ten pounds of fat cut away

Molly found the star surgeon Robert Griffith to perform a lipectomy. The doctor made long incisions at her hips and legs, scraped away about ten pounds of fat, then pulled the skin tight and stitched it. Anesthesia was primitive, there was no penicillin, and open wounds were highly prone to infection; two years earlier a woman in Paris had developed gangrene after similar surgery and had her leg amputated. After the operation Molly looked slender, but was so weak she needed crutches. She satisfied the studio, but paid with her health and her comeback.

29:23

Millions of fen-phen prescriptions

After lawyer Alex MacDonald took on Mary's case, he discovered millions of fen-phen prescriptions being written every year, meaning millions of people — the vast majority women — were sitting on a medical time bomb without knowing it. Mary had taken it for only 23 days, and the damage was already permanent. The Mayo Clinic later confirmed dozens of cases of a rare heart condition linked to fen-phen, and the FDA pulled the drug in September 1997. The companies chose to conceal the risk reports they received in order to protect profits.

35:31

Not vanity — the decisions were rational

Tim Harford's conclusion is that Molly's and Mary's decisions were not vanity or naivety, but entirely understandable. Molly's employer was the source of her income and demanded she get thin; Mary's specialist recommended fen-phen to her. They trusted people who seemed to know what they were doing, and ran into a ruthless commercial logic. Technological shortcuts persist today: GLP-1s have real health benefits, and Brazilian butt lifts can kill. We treat body diversity as a bug rather than a feature, rewarding fast visible change regardless of the cost.

— Tim Harford

Glossary

lipectomy
Surgery that cuts away subcutaneous fat; the technique was primitive in the 1920s and carried a high risk of infection.
fen-phen
The combination diet drug of fenfluramine and phentermine, withdrawn after causing pulmonary hypertension.
primary pulmonary hypertension (PPH)
The small blood vessels of the lungs thicken and close off, blocking the heart's pumping and potentially proving fatal.
