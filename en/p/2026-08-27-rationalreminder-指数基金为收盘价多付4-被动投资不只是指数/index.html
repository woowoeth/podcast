Index Funds Pay 4% More on Rebalancing: Passive Investing Is More Than Indexing

David Booth breaks down the difference between index funds and passive investing: index rebalancing pushes stock prices up 4%, true passivity is scientific pricing plus fine execution; the 10% annual stock return over a century is driven by humanity's desire to improve life.

2:06

Not selling ill-fitting shoes matters more than making the sale

David Booth worked as a commission-based salesman during high school and college. He found that although he wanted to close deals, he wanted to feel good about himself when he went home at night, so he never sold shoes that didn't fit. This experience has nothing to do with investment philosophy, yet it shaped Dimensional's business philosophy: be honest with clients, and if there's no suitable product, say so directly. He admits to regrettable sales early on but decided never to repeat them. In the long run, scientifically grounded practices plus good arguments naturally earn trust.

— David Booth
8:14

Outsiders don't get taken advantage of and dare to challenge assumptions

David's parents lived through the Great Depression and always considered themselves outsiders to the capital markets, afraid of being taken advantage of by insiders, so they never invested. But he points out that the financial science of the 60s and 70s boiled down to two points: long-term returns on stocks and bonds meet expectations, and professional fund managers underperform the market after fees. These two points made it clear to outsiders that public markets are fair to everyone. The outsider mindset also brings a benefit: a willingness to challenge assumptions. The scholars who changed finance back then were also outsiders because they had data, making conclusions that Wall Street hated hard to refute.

— David Booth
22:29

Index funds are a product of marketing genius, not scientific conclusion

David emphasizes distinguishing between passive management and index funds. He notes that the most important change in the financial industry from the 20th century to today is the shift to passive management, but index funds are a product of marketing genius, not something a scientist would conclude. A scientist would not impose the constraint of 'must track an index' on a portfolio, because constraints have an economic cost. Indices themselves are human-managed portfolios; for example, the S&P 500 is not strictly the top 500 companies, and inclusions and exclusions involve judgment. Dimensional considers itself passive, not indexed.

— David Booth
31:34

The cost of trading at the closing price is paying 4% more

When a stock is added to the S&P 500, index funds must buy at the closing price on that day. David says you can ask your broker to guarantee execution at the closing price, but the cost is 'willing to pay any price.' When all S&P 500 managers do this simultaneously, they push the stock price up by an average of 4% above fair value. It's like a three-card monte game: investors think they're not paying transaction costs, but the 4% premium reverts the next day. This is the hidden cost of index funds and a reason passive managers can do better.

— David Booth
35:37

Investors in 1971 had it an order of magnitude worse than today

Asked whether he'd rather be an investor in 1971 or today, David unhesitatingly chose today. In 1971, management fees were as high as 1-2% per year, and NYSE-set commission rates were an order of magnitude higher than today, even 10-20 times higher; to absorb these high commissions, institutions spawned the 'soft dollar' industry, similar to paying a high ticket price for double miles. Custody costs were also high, with some banks still keeping handwritten ledgers. In contrast, today's fees and transparency give ordinary investors a much fairer deal.

— David Booth
40:41

Research is a public good; the gap lies in execution

David points out that financial science has become public knowledge, especially since Fama and French insisted on making their research public, but competitors' new research may not be peer-reviewed. He believes that with thousands of professors repeatedly mining the same data over decades, it's unlikely there will be a disruptive new result. The real difference lies in implementation: Myron Scholes said, 'Ideas are cheap; execution matters.' Execution is not just placing orders; it includes the engineering of portfolio construction. Ken French treats execution as engineering; always buying at the ask and selling at the bid in trading accumulates losses over time, which is why Dimensional developed fine trading techniques.

— David Booth
51:54

10% annual return isn't magic; it's people refusing to sit still

David explains why stocks return about 10% annually over the long term: it's not magic, but humanity's instinct to improve life. In the early days of the pandemic, U.S. stocks fell about 30%; he told clients he didn't know what would happen, but he believed companies would not sit still—they would innovate, shut down, pivot—and the recession lasted only one quarter. This is also why one should hold the market portfolio rather than individual stocks—individual stocks can go to zero, but the market won't. Holding the market portfolio is a bet on the overall economy and the human creativity within it.

— David Booth
1:14:29

No matter how hot private markets get, it's not worth leaving your comfort zone

Pressed on his view of private markets, David says bluntly, 'I'm not tempted.' He likes the transparency of public markets: listed companies must disclose a lot of information, there is price discovery many times a day, liquidity is always available, and tax reporting is timely. He says he already has a strategy he considers fair and doesn't want to leave his comfort zone for something unfamiliar. He even says that if he were an advisor, he would allow clients to play with 10% of their money in individual stocks, private equity, or venture capital, but the remaining 90% must follow a rigorous process.

— David Booth

Glossary

Jensen's alpha
A measure of a portfolio's risk-adjusted excess return; for professional managers, it is negative on average.
soft dollar
In the era of high commissions, brokers returned commissions to institutions in non-cash forms such as research services.
beta
A measure of a portfolio's risk relative to market fluctuations; the market beta is 1.
passive vs indexed
Passive management builds portfolios scientifically and executes finely; indexing forces tracking an index, potentially incurring hidden rebalancing costs.
