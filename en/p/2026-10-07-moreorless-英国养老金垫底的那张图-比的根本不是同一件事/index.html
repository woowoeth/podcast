The chart showing UK pensions at the bottom of Europe compares completely different things

The viral chart showing ‘UK pensions rock-bottom in Europe’ bundles incomparable figures from different countries; broken down, UK retiree income replacement ratios and poverty rates are actually respectable—the difference is whether retirement security comes from the state or from private pensions.

2:08

Three incompatible measurement methods were conflated in the viral chart

The viral European pension comparison chart showed the UK at the bottom with £1,045. IFS researcher Heidi Kajalainen exposed the problem: some countries' figures were average pensions for all retirees, others were minimum guarantees for single people, while the UK's figure was ‘those receiving the full new state pension’—three completely different methodologies bundled into one table. Worse, Italy converted euros to pounds without adjustment, and Austria pays pensions 14 times yearly rather than 12 but didn't annualize, making one figure artificially high and the other artificially low.

— Heidi Kajalainen
4:15

UK's low state pension is supplemented by occupational pension savings

Kajalainen explained that Britain and France have completely different retirement security structures: France relies almost entirely on state pensions with minimal employer-provided occupational pensions, while Britain is the opposite—the state pension covers less than half of retirement income, the rest filled by workplace private pension savings. According to OECD 2022 data, UK retirees' average income is 84% of working-age disposable income, France's is 94%, but countries with structures closer to Britain's—the Netherlands and Denmark—are 80% and 80%, Ireland 90%—the UK performs respectably within its peer group.

— Heidi Kajalainen
6:16

French-style high pensions require French-style high taxes

If Britain wanted to emulate France by having the state pension cover more retirement income, it would need to confront the fact that France's working population pays much higher taxes and social contributions—this is a trade-off, not a one-way increase in generosity. By the latest 2024-25 data, the UK's retiree poverty rate is 14%, while the working-age poverty rate is 19% and child poverty 27%—retirees are actually the least disadvantaged of the three groups.

— Heidi Kajalainen
9:21

UK economy is vastly larger than Russia's by market exchange rates

Rees-Mogg and Tucker Carlson clashed on a show over Russia's economic size, then economist Duncan Weldon clarified with IMF data: by market exchange rates, UK GDP is roughly $4.3 trillion and Russia's $2.7 trillion—clearly Britain is larger; but using purchasing power parity (PPP), the UK becomes $4.7 trillion while Russia reaches $7.5 trillion—which method is ‘correct’ doesn't depend on politics but on what you're measuring.

— Duncan Weldon
12:38

Military purchasing power depends on which GDP measure you choose

Weldon noted that the two GDP measures aren't either-or—it depends on where the money is spent. If Russia is paying soldiers' salaries and domestic expenses, PPP better reflects actual purchasing power because Russia's labour costs are lower; but if importing drones and ammunition from China, it pays world market prices in hard currency, where PPP adjustment is irrelevant and unadjusted market-rate GDP determines actual military procurement capacity.

— Duncan Weldon
15:44

This summer's heat waves caused roughly 5,000 excess deaths

Actuary Stuart MacDonald analysed England and Wales death data and found that during five distinct heat waves this summer, about 55,000 people died, roughly 5,000 more than the expected 50,000—about 10% excess. The late June to early July heat wave caused the most harm; unlike the May heat wave (which showed the ‘harvesting effect’ of brought-forward deaths), the June and August heat waves showed no subsequent mortality dip, resembling instead the continental European pattern of genuinely increasing total deaths.

— Stuart MacDonald
19:57

But 2026 overall had substantially lower death rates than last year

Looking at the full year reverses the picture entirely: 2026 death rates so far are about 2% lower than last year, equivalent to roughly 8,500 fewer deaths if 2025 rates had continued. One major reason is flu deaths plummeting—roughly 50,000 so far this year, far below last year's 58,000 and 2024's 60,000—contradicting last winter's NHS England warning of a ‘super-flu’.

— Stuart MacDonald
23:08

Loch Ness could theoretically hold Earth's entire population

Using the standard physics approximation of 100 litres per human body, and Loch Ness's capacity of roughly 745.2 billion litres, that yields roughly 10 times Earth's current population; considering 100 litres is deliberately overestimated and real volume is probably 60-70 litres, expert Keith Still thinks fitting 14 times the world population is mathematically sound—assuming you liquefy everyone first. If instead you pack people standing in a virtual multi-level car park structure, Loch Ness's average 123-metre depth could theoretically hold roughly twice the world population.

Glossary

Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)
A method of comparing economic size by adjusting for price levels across countries, contrasting with GDP measured at market exchange rates.
Triple lock
The UK rule that state pensions rise annually by whichever is highest of wage growth, inflation, or 2.5%.
Excess deaths
The number of actual deaths exceeding the historical average for the same period, commonly used to measure the severity of heat waves, pandemics, and other shocks.
