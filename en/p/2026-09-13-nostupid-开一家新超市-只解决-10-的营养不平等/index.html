Opening a New Supermarket Solves Only 10% of Nutrition Inequality

Bringing healthy food supply into low-income neighborhoods closes only about 10% of nutrition inequality; the remaining 90% is driven by demand — and demand can be changed by taxes and culture.

2:05

Put a supermarket there and you solve only one-tenth of the problem

The six authors (three economists, the rest from marketing, data science, and real estate) conclude that giving low-income households the same products and prices as high-income households — i.e., eliminating food deserts — would close only about 10% of nutrition inequality; the remaining 90% is driven by demand-side differences. This directly refutes the policy assumption that increasing healthy food supply can significantly reduce nutrition inequality. In other words, the problem isn't whether there's salad on the shelf, but whether people will buy it.

— Stephen
4:08

They pieced together a national diet map

The study links four datasets: the Nielsen Home Scan shopping panel covering 61,000 households, sales data from 35,000 supermarkets (about 40% of U.S. grocery purchases), surveys on nutrition knowledge and preferences, and the opening dates and precise locations of 6,721 new supermarkets across the U.S. between 2004 and 2016, plus data on other retail outlets in each zip code. Stephen says that compared to this dataset, all previous research looks trivial — this is what the "big data revolution" should look like.

— Stephen
6:19

When a new supermarket opens, people just switch stores

The causal design is clean: if this were a supply problem, then when a new supermarket finally opens in a neighborhood, residents should flock to it and start buying fruits and vegetables. They don't. What people do is switch from one supermarket to another that's closer, with essentially no change in what they buy. One reason is that they already drive an average of about five miles to buy groceries — the severity of food deserts is not as high as imagined, because people are already used to driving rather than walking to buy food.

— Stephen
7:22

Taste comes from family, not from supermarkets

Angela quotes her daughter Anya's question: when, where, and how does a person learn to eat nutritiously? The answer points to the family and community you were born into. Stephen himself grew up in a "radically free-range organic" family, making bread and yogurt at home, growing vegetables and raising chickens, and he says he was lucky to be raised with a taste for nutritious food. Conversely, if habits are already unhealthy and habits are extremely stubborn, then a fresh supermarket next door can change very little.

— Angela
11:34

Calorie labels make people eat more

Mistaking the problem for a supply problem leads policy astray. New York City required chain fast-food restaurants to label calories on burgers, and studies found people didn't eat less — sometimes they ate more — because 600 calories for a $2 burger feels like a bargain, "more bang for your buck." Another paper found that Starbucks' voluntary calorie disclosure sometimes drove customers away (especially men), and they often went to Dunkin' Donuts instead.

— Angela
13:41

Complaints about supply shortages are mostly demand shortages

The show solicited examples on Twitter of things "treated as supply shortages but actually demand shortages," and got a flood of replies: fact-based news (there's plenty, just no one reads it), bipartisan politicians (everyone complains there are none, but no one wants their representative to compromise), trade schools (portrayed as low-income and unstable, so consumers avoid them, even though skilled trades are severely short-staffed), sleep (people complain they don't get enough, but actually they actively choose other activities). The common thread: blaming an external supplier is more comfortable than admitting you don't want it.

— Angela
15:46

Sugar taxes really can change behavior

Angela wanted to buy a can of Diet Coke at a corner bakery, was told it was $4.29, and said she wouldn't buy a second can — the sugar tax worked on her. She compares the mechanism to tobacco: warning labels and those ads have an effect, but many believe taxes are the single biggest factor in reducing smoking demand. The paper's authors and many other economists recommend sugar taxes to combat our widespread preference for sugar, because people respond to costs and benefits.

— Angela
24:57

Cultural change is harder than policy, but it has happened

Besides the two other channels — making people richer and getting them through college — there's cultural change. Smoking now carries shame and a sense of "I shouldn't do this" in many communities, and watching Mad Men shows this wasn't always the case in America. Angela points out that cultural icons in various fields — the successful people in movies and music — mostly eat well, exercise, and take care of themselves. The question is how to make these role models more visible without making people feel judged, because another universal rule of human nature is not wanting to feel bad about yourself.

— Angela

Glossary

food desert
A neighborhood lacking supermarkets and fresh food, with only convenience stores and fast-food outlets.
external locus of control
The psychological tendency to attribute outcomes to external circumstances rather than one's own choices.
Nielsen Home Scan
A representative survey panel tracking households' actual grocery purchases.
