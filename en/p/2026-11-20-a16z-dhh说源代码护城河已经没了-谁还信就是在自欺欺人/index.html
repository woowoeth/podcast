Source-code moats are dead; believers in them are deluding themselves

DHH argues agents drive software development costs to zero. Mac and Windows' mouse-driven interfaces are fundamentally incompatible with agent workflows. Linux's historically criticized features—terminal commands, complex configs, verbose error messages—are exactly what agents need.

DHH draws on six months of hands-on experience building Omachi's operating system entirely with agents, covering everything from remapping shortcuts to recreating Lightroom. Dense in examples; for anyone trying to gauge whether AI will reopen the operating-system wars.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:09

Graphical operating-system settings are fundamentally incompatible with agent workflows

Remapping Mac workspace shortcuts required manually clicking through settings menus—agents can't batch-configure the way GUIs force. DHH demonstrated letting an agent modify his running macOS directly, and it worked. After five minutes of that experience, he says you can't go back. Mac and Windows' forty years of mouse-click interaction doesn't fit the agent era.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
7:20

Linux's historical flaws are exactly what agents want most

Terminal dependency, complex config files, obscure error messages—Linux's traditional criticisms. DHH sees them as exactly what agents need: command-line interfaces, predictable editable configs, detailed error information. This year's inversion: the historical "flaws" have become reasons to choose Linux if you're working with AI.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
9:23

Ecosystem lock-in through scarcity breaks when anyone can code

An Omachi community member replaced a Mac-only Lightroom dependency by implementing a better version in two weeks—after Adobe spent over a decade and tens of millions developing it. This shows ecosystem lock-in's keystone is crumbling: application availability once locked you in, but now anyone with agent access can generate software in minutes. This resets mobile competition dynamics entirely.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
13:33

Token throughput speed determines whether software feels alive

Omachi currently runs at 50–120 tokens per second—below the threshold for smooth interaction. Fireworks recently demonstrated 1500 tokens per second, generating a playable Tetris in four seconds and reshaping it in real time as DHH shouted modifications. As token throughput scales to hundreds of thousands and millions per second in the coming years, software will become continuously self-rewriting in real time.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
15:34

Claiming source code as a moat is now self-delusion

DHH compares Omachi's development progress over months with the gap between Opus 4.8 and 5.5, noting this pace is accelerating and new models are likely by Christmas or early next year. His conclusion: anyone who thinks source code—even millions of lines—protects their ecosystem is deluding themselves.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
20:40

Even unlimited token budgets need only three subscriptions in practice

After twenty years of building wealth, DHH can afford unlimited token spending. Over three months, only two weeks came close to maxing his quota. Most work runs on a single $200 20X subscription to Claude—frontier models, not open-source alternatives. And he maintains just three subscriptions total. His point: access isn't confined to those with infinite budgets.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
28:53

Being in the game matters even when trailing by six months

DHH acknowledges that open-source models like GLM and DeepSeek likely lag by six or seven months; many benchmarks are suspect anyway, and they may not keep up with Opus in real-world scenarios. But their existence matters: they run locally on your hardware, they preserve competition, and they prevent two labs from owning the market and inflating token prices. Without them, we'd repeat Google and Meta's capture of 99% of internet advertising value.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
34:59

Three years to surpass macOS's global installed base is realistic

Omachi began last year as DHH's hand-written bash-script hobby—just better computers for him and a few friends. With agents, his ambition exploded: beat macOS globally within three years—180 million total users, which doesn't even rank as a tier-C internet service. He even questions whether twelve months is feasible; his reasoning: if labs truly believe recursive self-improving AI arrives within twelve months, an OS's market share should be surmountable.

— David Heinemeier Hansson
