Phage bet-hedging via 10,000-fold reversion, and first humanized EBV antibodies

Phages hedge their bets through SSR-driven 10,000-fold reversion; humanized EBV antibodies block tumors in vivo; vaccines advance on multiple fronts.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

9:08

Phages deliberately break a fraction of their offspring

Jolene introduces a Nature Microbiology paper: homopolymeric nucleotide repeats (SSRs) in phage genes are prone to slippage during replication, causing insertions or deletions that shift the reading frame. This means a subset of progeny always fails to make functional proteins. That randomness is the basis of bet-hedging: most sequences fit the current environment, but the persistent low-level variants can become survivors if the environment shifts abruptly. The population pays a small cost for long-term viability.

— Jolene Ramsey
21:19

No need to pre-adapt: reversion is 10,000 times faster

The paper measured reversion frequencies at SSR regions and found them about 10,000 times higher than typical point mutations. That order of magnitude means phages can rapidly toggle a gene's function on or off within very few generations, rather than waiting for a rare point mutation. Jolene stresses that this mechanism lets the population avoid pre-adaptation; instead, it continuously generates diversity and waits for the environment to select.

— Jolene Ramsey
25:23

T7 has no SSRs, so bet-hedging is not standard

In the Basel phage collection, T7's genome has no SSRs. Rich comments that T7 is 'businesslike'—straightforward, without these fancy mechanisms. This contrast shows that SSRs are not a universal feature of phages but an evolutionary strategy in certain lineages and ecological niches. Even lytic phages infecting the same host can be organized very differently at the genomic level.

— Rich Condit
50:44

Humanization is hard not in the variable region but the constant region

When converting a mouse antibody into a humanized one, there is a conflict between the variable and constant regions; someone must spend years adjusting the constant region to accommodate the variable region before a therapeutic antibody can be made. Brienne says this was her 'aha' moment—she had not understood why mice were necessary. Vincent adds that directly using human antibodies may not yield the desired antibody; the mouse platform allows screening many clones and engineering the antigen, yielding a full B-cell clonal diversification process, after which classic hybridoma technology is still needed to obtain monoclonal antibodies.

— Brienne, Vincent
54:46

Hybridoma screening ends with just 10 antibodies

The group immunized mice with recombinant GP350 and GP42, generated hybridomas, plated 384-well plates, and used antigen-coupled magnetic beads for high-throughput flow cytometry to detect binding and infection inhibition. Positive clones were subcloned and their heavy/light chain genes sequenced, then expressed as recombinant human IgG1 antibodies. The final result: 2 GP350-binding antibodies and 8 GP42-neutralizing antibodies. The whole pipeline shows the complete path from animal immunization to recombinant antibody.

— Vincent
1:07:07

Antibodies also block EBV challenge in vivo

In two types of immunodeficient mice engrafted with human CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells, 500 micrograms of antibody (ATX42-2, ATX350-2, MO1, etc.) were injected intraperitoneally, followed by intravenous EBV challenge, and observation for 11 weeks. Results showed these antibodies prevented tumor formation, suppressed viral DNA replication, and blocked splenomegaly. Spleens from ATX42-2- and MO1-treated mice had no detectable EBER-positive cells. This means human antibodies protect against EBV challenge in vivo, not just neutralize in vitro.

— Vincent
1:10:11

Alum-adjuvanted subunit is dead; nanoparticle vaccines live

The GP350 subunit vaccine with alum adjuvant failed to induce protective immunity in trials; GP42 is conformationally variable and hard to elicit the right antibodies. But a GP350 ferritin nanoparticle vaccine has shown safety and immunogenicity in phase I, with seropositive participants remaining EBV-free up to 540 days. Also in development are a GH/GP42 ferritin nanoparticle and Moderna's mRNA vaccine (containing four antigens). Vincent expresses disappointment that the paper did not test combinations of GP350 and GP42 antibodies.

— Vincent
1:42:44

Anti-vaxxers may lack not information but the will to think

Vincent re-recommends 'Thinking, Fast and Slow,' quoting Joshua Reynolds: 'There is no expedient to which a man will not resort to avoid the real labor of thinking.' He argues that cognitive economy may partly explain anti-science and anti-vaccine attitudes: deep thinking is costly, and people prefer the effortless Type 1 thinking. He then says, 'That is laziness, cognitive laziness.' This view attributes the science communication dilemma to humans' default cognitive mode, not simply missing information.

— Vincent

Glossary

SSR
Simple sequence repeat: a repeated nucleotide motif in a gene that is prone to slippage during replication, causing insertions or deletions.
bet-hedging
An evolutionary strategy where a population maintains multiple phenotypes simultaneously to cope with environmental changes.
contingency locus
A genomic region that generates variation within a population through a high mutation rate.
hybridoma
An immortalized cell line produced by fusing a myeloma cell with a B cell, used to produce monoclonal antibodies.
