Phage Bet-Hedges With 10,000-Fold Reversion Mutations; First Humanized EBV Antibody

Phages hedge their bets through 10,000-fold reversion mutations in SSRs, humanized EBV antibodies block tumors in vivo, and vaccine efforts advance on multiple fronts.

9:08

Phages deliberately let some offspring break

Jolene introduces a Nature Microbiology paper: homopolymeric simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in phage genes are prone to slippage during replication, causing insertions or deletions that shift the reading frame. This means a fraction of offspring always fail to make protein properly. That randomness is the basis of bet-hedging: most sequences are adapted to the current environment, but a persistent low level of variants can become the survivors once the environment shifts drastically. The population trades a tiny cost for long-term survival capability.

— Jolene Ramsey
21:19

No need to pre-adapt: reversion mutations are 10,000 times faster

The paper measured the reversion mutation frequency in SSR regions and found it roughly 10,000 times higher than ordinary point mutations. That order of magnitude means phages can quickly turn a gene's function on or off within very short generations, rather than waiting for a rare point mutation. Jolene stresses that this mechanism lets the population avoid pre-adapting to environmental change, instead generating diversity continuously and waiting to be selected by the environment.

— Jolene Ramsey
25:23

T7 has not a single SSR; betting is not standard equipment

In the Basel phage collection, the T7 genome has no SSRs. Rich comments that T7 is "businesslike," straightforward, without these fancy mechanisms. This highlights that SSRs are not standard equipment for all phages, but an evolutionary strategy of certain lineages in particular ecological niches. Even virulent phages under the same host can have completely different genome organization.

— Rich Condit
50:44

The hard part of humanization is not the variable region but the constant region

When converting a mouse antibody into a humanized antibody, there is a conflict between the variable and constant regions, and someone must spend years adjusting the constant region to be compatible with the variable region before a therapeutic antibody can be made. Brienne says this was her epiphany moment; she originally did not understand why mice had to be used. Vincent adds that going directly to human antibodies may fail to find the antibody you want, whereas the advantage of the mouse platform is that you can screen large numbers of clones and engineer antigens, obtaining the full B cell clonal diversification process, after which classic hybridoma technology is still needed to obtain monoclonal antibodies.

— Brienne, Vincent
54:46

After hybridoma screening, only 10 antibodies remain

The research group immunized mice with recombinant GP350 and GP42, prepared hybridomas, plated 384-well plates, and used antigen-conjugated magnetic beads for high-throughput flow screening, testing binding and infection inhibition. Positive clones were subcloned and had heavy/light chain genes sequenced, then recombinant human IgG1 antibodies were expressed. In the end they obtained 2 GP350-binding antibodies and 8 GP42-neutralizing antibodies. The whole workflow demonstrates the complete path from animal immunization to recombinant antibodies.

— Vincent
1:07:07

The antibodies also blocked EBV challenge in vivo

Human CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells were implanted into two kinds of immunodeficient mice, 500 micrograms of antibody (ATX42-2, ATX350-2, MO1, etc.) was injected intraperitoneally, then EBV was challenged intravenously and the mice were observed for 11 weeks. The results showed these antibodies prevented tumor formation, suppressed viral DNA replication and blocked splenomegaly. In mice treated with ATX42-2 and MO1, no EBER-positive cells were detectable in the spleen. This means human antibodies protect against EBV challenge in vivo, not just neutralize in vitro.

— Vincent
1:10:11

Alum-adjuvanted subunit is dead; nanoparticle vaccines are still alive

The GP350 subunit vaccine with alum adjuvant failed to produce protective immunity in trials; GP42 is conformationally variable, making it hard to induce the right antibodies. But an existing GP350 ferritin nanoparticle vaccine showed safety and immunogenicity in phase 1, with seropositive participants remaining uninfected with EBV through 540 days. There are also GH/GP42 ferritin nanoparticles and Moderna's mRNA vaccine (containing four antigens) in development. Vincent expressed disappointment that the paper did not try combining GP350 and GP42 antibodies.

— Vincent
1:42:44

Anti-vaccine may not lack information; it lacks the will to think

Vincent re-recommends Thinking, Fast and Slow and quotes Joshua Reynolds: "There is no expedient to which a man will not resort to avoid the real labor of thinking." He suggests cognitive economy may partly explain anti-science and anti-vaccine attitudes: deep thinking is energy-expensive, and people tend toward the effortless Type 1 thinking. He immediately follows with "that is laziness, cognitive laziness." This view attributes the science communication predicament to humans' default cognitive mode, rather than a simple lack of information.

Glossary

SSR / simple sequence repeat
A repeated nucleotide sequence in a gene that is prone to slippage during replication, causing insertions/deletions.
bet-hedging
An evolutionary strategy in which a population maintains multiple phenotypes simultaneously to cope with environmental change.
contingency locus
A genomic region that generates variation in a population through a high mutation rate.
hybridoma
An immortalized cell line produced by fusing a myeloma cell with a B cell, used to produce monoclonal antibodies.
