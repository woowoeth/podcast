Whose Homes Did His Roads Tear Down: Robert Moses's New York Legacy

Moses used federal Title I money to demolish 26 blocks, pushing the poor from one slum to another, yet claimed 98% of relocatees were better housed — his legacy remains unresolved to this day.

The more power, the less explanation needed

After LaGuardia retired in 1946, William O'Dwyer appointed Moses as New York City Construction Coordinator, a position that gave him jurisdiction over every public construction project in the city and a direct line to Washington. By 1959, critics pointed out that he simultaneously held multiple government posts, and his power was unchecked: consultant fees undisclosed, staff salaries undisclosed, trust meetings held behind closed doors, and land could be seized at will. Reporter Bob Baum noted that private companies could do these things, but as part of a government entity, the public had a right to know. Moses was thus often called the "Construction Czar" or "Urban Planning Czar."

3:07

Roads first, at the cost of entire communities

In 1948, Moses began building the Cross Bronx Expressway, a seven-mile road cutting through the already heavily developed Bronx. Dozens of apartment buildings were demolished, hundreds of families displaced, and many streets severed into dead ends. This community fragmentation was later cited as one reason for rising local crime rates. Those affected were mostly the least resourced, predominantly non-white communities. In a 1945 Atlantic Monthly article, Moses wrote that arterial improvements were "not merely a strip of pavement laid in a gasoline ditch, but a real improvement of the entire area," and he genuinely believed he was making the best choices for New York, but his methods were often ruthless.

7:12

26 brochures, one visual persuasion campaign

In 1949, Congress passed the American Housing Act Title I, providing $1 billion in loans and $100 million in annual grants for slum clearance. Moses realized New York could secure substantial federal funds, so in the 1950s he had his team produce 26 brochures, each targeting a neighborhood he deemed in need of redevelopment. The brochures contained problem descriptions, photographs, and statistics, and were sent to the New York City Board of Estimate, the City Planning Commission, the Title I federal administrator, and the Housing and Home Finance Agency. In 2014, researcher Temis Kronopoulos analyzed the photographs and found: vacant lots were shown 22 times, the most common image of urban decay; 20 photos showed back-alley garbage and clotheslines; almost no people appeared in the photos. Kronopoulos wrote: "In this context, slum clearance was also a tool of social engineering."

— Tracy V. Wilson
10:18

Media praise first, scandal exposed later

On June 29, 1959, New York Times reporter Wayne Phillips began publishing a four-part series exposing problems in Moses's Title I slum clearance projects: associations with people he shouldn't have been involved with, unregulated funds, and information not shared. But as early as June 14, columns by George Sokolski began appearing in multiple newspapers nationwide, distributed by King Features Syndicate, with titles like "Robert Moses Is a Selfless Man" and nearly identical content, praising him for "constantly building and beautifying, his work never tainted by corruption." These articles appeared two weeks before the New York Times critical series. Looking back, Sokolski appears to have been asked to preemptively cover for the coming scandal.

— Holly Frey
13:22

Mayor's ban and public tantrum

After the scandal broke, Mayor Wagner did two things: first, he investigated Louis Pokras's role in the Harlem slum clearance project, and because Pokras had organized crime connections, Wagner publicly stated he would never allow him to sponsor any New York development project; second, he banned the city from working with Moses's favored architect Melvin E. Kessler, whose previous involvement in Upper West Side redevelopment saw developer Manhattan Town Incorporated take Title I funds but build no buildings, demolish no buildings, and simply collect rents while the buildings decayed. Moses publicly responded that the mayor's "devastating charges and misstatements" had scared off venture capital, and said "Title I is a dead duck." Other officials said Title I would continue and praised Moses. In media reports, Moses appeared to be throwing a public tantrum.

— Tracy V. Wilson
15:26

"Moses Must Go": an open letter

In December 1959, the Metropolitan Housing Council began distributing leaflets in New York addressed to Mayor Robert Wagner. At the top, in bold, it read: "For New York's development, Moses must go." The leaflet listed ongoing problems with slum clearance projects: tenants sold along with the land; indecent relocation practices continuing; luxury housing built at the people's expense while those more in need of housing were pushed from one slum to another; and as former rent administrator Charles Abrams pointed out, slum clearance had become "Negro and Puerto Rican removal." The leaflet demanded: more low-rent housing, an end to subsidizing luxury housing, and building new housing before demolishing old. It compared the situation to cancer and reiterated: "Moses must go."

— Holly Frey
21:32

Fire Island showdown: Rockefeller accepts the resignation

In 1962, Moses clashed with New York Governor Nelson Rockefeller over a Fire Island road plan. Moses wanted to build a road spanning the entire island, and after a 1962 storm destroyed most houses, he thought his opportunity had come. But environmental groups and islanders opposed it, and residents proposed an alternative: making Fire Island a national seashore protected area, which gained federal support. Public pressure gave the governor an opportunity to halt the road project and suggest Moses reduce his government posts to focus on the 1964 World's Fair. Moses submitted his resignation; he had previously threatened other governors with resignation, but they had all refused, because many public projects would collapse without him. Rockefeller accepted, triggering a media storm. Moses claimed the governor had demanded he immediately resign as chairman of the State Parks Commission to make way for his brother Lawrence; Rockefeller said a transition had been discussed for over a year.

— Tracy V. Wilson
26:38

World's Fair: 50 million vs. projected 70 million

The 1964 New York World's Fair drew about 50 million visitors, but Moses had projected 70 million. The New York Times reported that only 85% of the grounds were ready at opening, 15 of 139 pavilions were unfinished, and many completed pavilions had no exhibits. Some exhibitors arrived with nowhere to put their displays. A fair spokesman told the media that visiting all the other options would take over a week, and the unfinished pavilions would be completed before then. Some attractions were finished within weeks of opening; others were abandoned entirely. After the fair, Moses no longer served as New York City Construction Coordinator. In September 1966, his wife Mary died after a long illness, and less than a month later, Moses remarried Mary Grady, who served as secretary at the Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority. In 1968, Rockefeller merged Triborough into another transportation agency, and Moses was removed — his last New York government position.

— Holly Frey
28:42

"The Power Broker": one book that set the tone for fifty years

In 1970, Moses published "Public Works: A Dangerous Trade," reviewing his career, and wrote: "History repeats itself. Soon, as it has time and again, the opposition will quiet down and claim they always supported the improvement." In 1974, journalist Robert Caro published "The Power Broker," detailing how Moses accumulated power, painting an overall negative portrait, and it became a bestseller. Moses issued a statement through his PR team calling the book "full of errors, baseless charges, malicious, unfounded personal attacks, and random punches thrown at almost everyone in public life." He also said Caro fabricated the notion of "helpless displaced people encountering a cruel public works dictator, abusively scattered into the worst slums," and countered that 98% of those moved from demolished communities got better housing. Caro's book won the Pulitzer Prize, and whether accurate or not, its portrayal of Moses has been indelible ever since.

Glossary

Title I
The slum clearance and community development provisions of the 1949 U.S. Housing Act, providing federal loans and grants for urban renewal.
slum clearance
The demolition of urban neighborhoods deemed blighted, redeveloped for public or private projects, often displacing residents.
Power Broker
Robert Caro's 1974 biography of Robert Moses, winner of the Pulitzer Prize, which profoundly shaped public perception of Moses.
King Features Syndicate
A newspaper syndicate founded by Hearst that distributed column articles simultaneously to multiple newspapers nationwide.
