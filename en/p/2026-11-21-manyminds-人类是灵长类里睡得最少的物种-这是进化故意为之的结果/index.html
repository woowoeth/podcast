Humans sleep less than any other primate species—by evolutionary design

Cross-cultural comparison shows hunter-gatherers without electricity sleep shorter and have lower sleep efficiency, yet report sleeping well—the real difference lies not in sleep duration, but in circadian rhythm amplitude.

European segmented sleep is a seasonal phenomenon, not human nature

The ‘first sleep, second sleep’ pattern that Robert Ekirch studied came from pre-industrial European agricultural populations, with the key driver being longer nights after the autumn equinox—when lab subjects are exposed to longer darkness, their sleep naturally becomes segmented. Samson emphasizes this should not be read as a universal conclusion about how humans should sleep, but as a local adaptation to light conditions; many people on Reddit treating it as a universal wellness hack is a placebo effect.

12:18

Human free time comes from sleeping less than chimpanzees do

Chimpanzees spend six hours a day chewing food (without fire), plus need 11.5 to 12 hours of sleep, leaving just seven hours of free time; humans, learning to cook with fire, need only one hour of chewing, sleep an average of 6.78 hours, freeing up substantial time. This extra time lets humans ‘venture into the night’ for activity and socializing, forming a lifestyle resembling cathemerality, but sustaining this overload mode long-term isn't healthy.

— David Samson
18:26

REM sleep is harder to wake from than deep sleep, and is when emotions heal

N3 deep sleep is cellular and immune-level repair (memory T cells lock in antibodies at this stage), while REM sleep's ‘phasic REM’ phase is harder to wake from than N3, with brain energy expenditure even exceeding wakefulness, and 80% of dreams occur here. Matthew Walker calls it the ‘emotional therapist’ because REM strips emotional intensity from traumatic memories—the folk wisdom that ‘you'll feel better after a good sleep’ holds in sleep science.

— David Samson
28:52

By evolutionary model, humans should sleep 11 hours

Samson and Charles Nunn in 2019 compared primates using phylogenetic comparative models and found humans should sleep 11 hours by kinship calculation, but actual sleep is far less—humans are the primate that sleeps shortest, with the highest REM proportion, a derived trait showing that evolutionary history involved some special pressure compressing sleep time, not a natural baseline.

— David Samson
31:56

Hunter-gatherers sleep worse yet call themselves good sleepers

A 54-population cross-cultural comparison shows: grid-connected societies average 88% sleep efficiency and 7.1 hours duration, while small-scale off-grid societies have only 74% efficiency and 6.4 hours—objectively worse sleep. Yet when asked if Hadza or Tsimane people sleep well, they widely say yes. Samson's explanation isn't ‘they don't know what they're missing,’ but that their circadian rhythm amplitude is far higher than industrialized populations—what really affects wellbeing and depression risk is rhythm strength, not sleep duration itself.

— David Samson
38:13

How the full moon affects sleep depends entirely on culture, not just light

Toba population data shows activity clearly increases on full-moon nights, matching the intuitive prediction of ‘moonlight increases activity’; but Hadza is opposite, because they hold the important Epeme ceremony on new-moon (complete darkness) nights—darkness ensures participant anonymity, and after the ceremony there's an ‘Epeme hangover’ rebound effect the next day. The same lunar phase variable produces opposite sleep-activity patterns in different cultures.

— David Samson
44:31

A whole household almost never sleeps simultaneously

In a Hadza camp, from when the first adult falls asleep to when the last wakes up, an average of 8 people sleep simultaneously, but expanding the scope to a typical 25-30-person forager camp, the time all sleep at once approaches zero (of 30,000+ minutes of sleep data, only 18 minutes of full group synchrony). This staggered sleep driven by chronotype differences (morning type/night owl) forms the basis of social sleep sentinel duty.

— David Samson
53:51

A two-or-three-pound Chihuahua makes an effective sleep sentinel

A study planned to exclude dog breeds too small to ‘survive in the wild,’ but collaborators insisted on including all sizes. As a result, an owner of a Chihuahua reported that the small dog detected and alerted to a stalker outside the window without her knowledge—showing what humans ‘borrowed’ were the dog's superior senses of smell and hearing, those superhuman perceptual abilities, rather than its size or attack power.

— David Samson

Glossary

cathemerality
An activity pattern with substantial activity during both day and night, unlike purely diurnal or nocturnal animals.
process S and process C
The two classic sleep-regulating mechanisms: process S (homeostatic sleep drive accumulating during wakefulness) and process C (circadian drive, the rhythm signal from the biological clock).
sleep efficiency
The percentage of time spent actually asleep compared to total time in bed.
chronotype
An individual's innate preference for early sleep and wake (lark-type) or late sleep and wake (owl-type).
Epeme
A major social ceremony performed by the Hadza people on new-moon nights, relying on darkness to ensure participant anonymity.
S.H.E.L.L. (shelter, hearth, environmental prep, lux, lookouts)
Samson's five-pillar framework for understanding human sleep evolution, encompassing shelter, fire use, environmental preparation, light, and sentinel duty.
