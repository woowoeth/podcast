LLMs Are Not Autocomplete, They're Our Cognitive Cousins

LLMs are not fancy autocomplete but ‘cognitive cousins’ that share core cognitive principles with humans: attention mechanisms recapitulate production systems, prediction is the dominant training signal; sample efficiency is off by three to four orders of magnitude, but an architectural tweak could close the gap.

16:58

LLMs are not autocomplete, they're humans' cognitive cousins

Sripada offers two competing explanations: LLMs may just be fancy autocomplete, a statistical approximator; or the prediction training signal may be strong enough to compress the core principles of human cognition into the weights. He clearly leans toward the latter, calling prediction ‘the mother of all training signals.’ This choice determines everything that follows: if it's just autocomplete, behavioral similarity is mere imitation; if it's a cognitive relative, then LLMs' psychological effects and architectural features are worth studying as evidence about the human mind.

— Chandra Sripada
23:12

Human psychological effects emerge spontaneously in LLMs

Sripada lists a string of phenomena that reliably show up in human experiments and all appear in LLMs: the difficulty of center-embedded sentences, garden-path sentences, similarity interference, the recency/primacy/middle-loss effects in serial memory, continuity and temporal asymmetry, and disjunctive vs. conjunctive search in visual search. These effects are not hard-coded through prompt engineering; they emerge spontaneously as the model predicts the next word. He stresses that one cannot stop at counting behavioral similarities—the key is mechanistic analysis, which is exactly the starting point for explaining the Transformer with production systems later.

— Chandra Sripada
30:17

System 1 is the weights, System 2 is the context

Kahneman's dual-process theory splits thinking into two systems: System 1 is fast, automatic, intuitive; System 2 is slow, serial, controlled. Sripada argues LLMs give a clear correspondence: in-weight processing is like System 1, in-context processing plus chain of thought is like System 2. This correspondence turns psychology's fuzzy dichotomy into a hypothesis that can be tested against Transformer mechanisms.

— Chandra Sripada
48:17

LLMs supply the mechanism dual-process theory was missing

Sripada points out that dual-process theory in cognitive science is often criticized as ‘a list of adjectives’: one is fast, automatic, effortless, the other slow and dependent on working memory, but with no mechanistic content. LLMs help fill in the mechanism: in-weight and in-context are actually a continuum with clear poles at either end. For a simple prompt like ‘Michael Jordan plays basketball,’ the weights already contain most of the response and no complex structure needs to be built in activation space; for a prompt like ‘Einstein is German, Gandhi is Indian, so Madame Curie is?’ the task structure must first be constructed, making it vulnerable to interference.

— Chandra Sripada
52:56

Conditional rules model the human mind better than step-by-step instructions

Compare two ways of programming a robot to make tea: traditional fetch-execute is step-by-step instructions—‘walk into the kitchen, pick up the kettle, fill it with water’—and it collapses when it hits an unwritten situation like ‘someone put ice cubes in the kettle’; a production system is a set of conditional rules—‘if the kettle is empty, fill it; if the water is cold, boil it; if the water is hot, pour the tea’—making judgments based on state representations, and is therefore far more robust. Cognitive scientists have long felt production systems model the human mind better than itemized instructions. This setup points directly at the Transformer's attention mechanism.

— Chandra Sripada
58:43

The Transformer is actually a production system

Sripada translates the Transformer's core mechanisms into production-system language: attention heads are continuous conditional rules—‘if the key matches the query, add the value’; MLP units are similar, where the rows of the first matrix are features and, if they match the residual stream, the features of the second matrix's columns are added. 99% of the computation in a Transformer is dot products, i.e. ‘a match scalar times a vector.’ This structure was invented independently by computer scientists, yet in ResNet and Attention Is All You Need it walked back onto the production-system path: the residual stream corresponds to a state representation, and the conditional rules correspond to production rules.

— Chandra Sripada
1:09:43

Sample efficiency is off by four orders of magnitude, maybe one architectural change

Sripada concedes that LLMs' sample efficiency is far behind humans: a child may need only about 100 million tokens, while an LLM needs 100 billion to 300 billion—three to four orders of magnitude apart. But he thinks this may be an innate structural problem rather than a fundamental barrier. He cites ResNet: deep networks without residual connections simply cannot learn via gradient descent, and adding a simple adjustment—‘the previous layer plus a function of the previous layer’—turns an unlearnable problem into a learnable one. Perhaps a similar architectural tweak lies ahead that could raise sample efficiency by several orders of magnitude at once and bring LLMs closer to humans.

— Chandra Sripada
1:20:43

The harder the problem, the more different intelligences look alike

Sripada introduces Dan Yamins and Rosa Cao's ‘contravariance’: the harder the problem, the smaller the space of possible computational solutions, so independently evolved systems like macaques, humans and LLMs should end up with more similar internal representations. He adds his own ‘architectural canalization’: since the Transformer is a production-system architecture, this constraint further narrows the solution space. So larger models aligning better with the human brain is not a coincidence but something forced by task difficulty and architecture together. He admits these are speculations, but considers them key to understanding the convergence between LLMs and human cognition.

Glossary

production system
A cognitive architecture that triggers actions via conditional rules, more robust than step-by-step instructions, used to model the human mind.
in-weight processing
The model answers directly using its trained weights without constructing new structure in context, corresponding to System 1.
in-context processing
The model builds task structure from the current prompt before reasoning, corresponding to System 2's controlled processes.
contravariance
The harder the problem, the fewer viable computational solutions, so different systems' internal representations converge.
architectural canalization
The model architecture itself narrows the solution space, making internal representations converge in a particular direction.
chain of thought
The model generates intermediate reasoning steps before answering, akin to System 2's serialized thinking.
