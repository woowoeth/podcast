The avant-garde paradox: the more experimental, the more audiences

Without expert endorsement or advertising, he speaks avant-garde theatre directly to young audiences—and thirty years later, these productions never play to empty houses. *Rhinoceros in Love* has even cycled through eleven generations of actors.

Theatre market broke 100 billion, then started falling

Research data cited by Luo Yonghao showed China's theatre market at roughly 8+ billion yuan pre-pandemic, climbing steadily to break 100 billion last year. But Meng Jinghui confirms it has since contracted. Concerts, however, are booming against the trend; stadiums the size of Beijing's Bird's Nest can now run ten consecutive shows, setting records. Neither blamed cultural recession. Instead they pinpointed channel fragmentation: as entertainment options multiplied, theatre audiences scattered across alternatives. Paradoxically, the resulting scarcity of live experience became the very driver pushing concert sales higher.

Systems that feel stable fear anyone who might change them

When Luo asked why his alma mater never invited him back to teach, Meng's answer was revealing: a well-established system generates its own energy and momentum. Introduce someone with ‘disruptive genes,’ and that energy dissolves—the system stops being itself. This isn't institutional intolerance. Rather, any functioning system instinctively resists people who might destabilize its inner balance, regardless of their achievement. It's a survival reflex.

— Meng Jinghui
1:49:20

Financial cushion is what enables true artistic rebellion

In 1996-97, Meng received a fellowship to study in Japan worth 17,000 yen daily. For six months, he ate the cheapest beef bowls he could find, systematically hoarding cash. When he returned to China with over 40,000 yuan—a small fortune then—his classmate was shocked, thinking he'd taken drugs; he'd simply lost that much weight from saving. But that ‘fortune’ gave him the nerve to return and stage three plays back-to-back, becoming what he calls ‘an upstart in theatre.’ His later reflection: intellectuals need financial cushion to maintain dignity and creative integrity. Without it, your artistic choices start to warp.

— Meng Jinghui
1:56:24

A theatrical hit needs no marketing budget to sustain for decades

Financing *Rhinoceros in Love* meant mortgaging his apartment. The budget was meager—two or three hundred thousand yuan. Withdrawing cash was terrifying: he made three separate trips to the bank, carried 50,000 yuan in a backpack the first time, and spent the entire subway ride watching everyone around him as a potential threat. There was zero advertising budget. Instead, word-of-mouth took over. Result: forty performances in the first year, 500,000 yuan in revenue. The play never stopped running. It's now been performed across eleven generations of actors—proof that if avant-garde work truly captures the mood of its time, it doesn't need promotion. It becomes self-propagating.

— Meng Jinghui
1:59:26

Radical theatre draws bigger audiences, not smaller

Meng's generation did avant-garde work by deliberately bypassing the establishment path—no expert seminars, no waiting for expert approval. Instead, they built direct connections with young audiences through grassroots media and word of mouth, letting viewers grow alongside the creators rather than expecting them to accept a finished aesthetic. When Meng told international colleagues ‘The more avant-garde we were, the more audiences came,’ they didn't believe him. But that was the reality. It directly contradicts the assumption that experimental work means niche audiences. Young viewers were willing to be ‘transformed’ into the next generation of audience members, not passively accept pre-formed taste.

— Meng Jinghui
2:34:53

Admitting you're wrong in rehearsal speeds the work up

When younger, Meng was an absolute authority in the rehearsal room—even when his instinct was faulty, he'd stubbornly defend it rather than admit error. Now he's different. He says plainly in front of the cast: ‘I was wrong. That's not right. Let me think about this again. Let's go eat, then come back and fix it.’ His insight: there's a visceral pleasure in solving the actual problem instead of winning an argument. The dignity younger people guard—not admitting fault—is exactly what middle-aged creators should shed. The more you can let it go, the more efficient rehearsals become.

— Meng Jinghui
3:05:37

Young Chinese audiences follow you awhile, then exit

Meng has noticed a persistent pattern: young Chinese audiences will follow a creator for a time, but around age thirty, they step off the bus and stop engaging. Foreign audiences behave differently—once they board, they rarely get off. They stay. At Rolling Stones concerts in Shanghai, the audience is almost entirely young Chinese; foreign audiences span from twentysomethings to people in their sixties. One reason: adults get busy and drift from the cultural interests that once moved them. But causally intertwined with this is a second reality: Mandarin popular music itself lacks substantial works that keep feeding adult listeners. The ecosystem doesn't hold them.

— Meng Jinghui
3:10:42

International theatre at epic scale finds full houses in China

Last year Wuzhen Theatre Festival hosted *The City of Humans* from Germany—five ancient Greek plays woven into one arc, performed by a 100+ person ensemble with over 400 props. Nine hours of theatre daily, spread across three days. The production was so technically intricate (lighting, sound design) that this was the first time the company had ever toured it internationally. It sold out both runs in the 1200-seat theatre. Around the same time, Meng saw a 24-hour uninterrupted dance performance in Belgium—dancers slept onstage when tired. Scale and format like this simply didn't exist in Chinese theatre festivals before. They're being imported one by one.

— Meng Jinghui
