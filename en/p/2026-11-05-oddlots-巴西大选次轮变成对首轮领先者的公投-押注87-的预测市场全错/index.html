Brazil's runoff was a referendum on the frontrunner; prediction markets at 87% were entirely wrong

Voters despised the incumbent, so the first-round winner drew all the fire onto himself. This let the trailing Lula complete the reversal in the runoff.

3:07

Bolsonaro lost first, not to Lula, but to himself

Bolsonaro faced money laundering charges from his time as a congressman five years earlier; this year he was exposed on WhatsApp asking Banco Master's owner for millions of dollars; before the vote, a magazine revealed him on a boat with a pharmaceutical lobbyist and a prostitute; the plane he used to flee to America was tied to an Argentina money-laundering case and a Texas drug-trafficking case. But all these scandals existed before the first round. What actually changed the outcome: the first round was supposed to be a confidence vote on Lula's 45% support, but once Bolsonaro unexpectedly led, all the fire in the runoff trained on him. Winning the first round became his defeat.

— James Bosworth
6:14

WhatsApp groups proved more effective than traditional campaigning

Lula, who had never used a smartphone before, got his first phone during the campaign. His team set up the ‘Lula Army’WhatsApp group with over 10 million members—more than 5% of Brazilian voters. When a mayor blocked public transit on election day, the group immediately coordinated voters to the polls. Several 30-second clips from his TV debate were perfectly formatted for social media shorts. Whether by design or accident, the effect was excellent.

— James Bosworth
8:14

Prediction markets betting 87% on Bolsonaro were entirely wrong

Before election night, Bosworth himself assigned Bolsonaro a 64% chance of winning; prediction markets at the time were broadly priced at 80%, peaking at 87%. Lula won. When asked if it was a ‘20% tail-risk event,’Bosworth was direct: no, they really got it wrong. They were simply overconfident. Even professional forecasters admitted this wasn't about being right—it was just ‘who was wrong by less.’

— James Bosworth
9:16

Just 1 to 2 percent of voters decided this election

Between the two rounds, turnout changed less than 2%, and the share of invalid or blank ballots barely budged. On Brazil's scale, 1 to 2% meant millions of votes. But Lula's margin was just 4 million. A razor-thin fraction of swing voters decided the entire race.

— James Bosworth
14:25

Trump claims 9-for-9 in Latin America; that number is fabricated

Trump keeps saying he's gone 9 for 9 in recent Latin American elections. Bosworth was direct: Trump made that number up entirely; it just happens to roughly match his actual record. Bosworth reads this wave of right-wing wins as a global anti-incumbent cycle rather than ideological realignment. But regardless of the cause, every Latin American leader—left, center, or right—will have to directly confront Trump over the next two years, until January 2029.

— James Bosworth
17:27

Rubio wants Venezuela's elections in six months; the regime prefers 2028 or 2029

Under the plan Rubio (not Trump) is pushing, Delcy Rodríguez would declare the presidency vacant, establish a new electoral commission, then hold elections six months out—optimally July next year, at latest September or November. But Rodríguez herself wants to delay elections to 2028 or 2029. Bosworth identifies this as the precise conflict between Rubio and other Trump officials, and between Rubio and Venezuelan authorities. Rubio wants the political credit: ‘Venezuelan democracy, on my watch.’

— James Bosworth
21:31

Lula and Machado hate each other but might secretly align

Lula has been too friendly with Chávez and Maduro—leftist dictators—so Venezuela's opposition resents him, holding him partly responsible for the failure to complete a power transition. Now Trump treats Venezuela as a quasi-colony, and that troubles Lula: South America should be Brazil's sphere. Bosworth doesn't expect them to embrace openly, but thinks Lula might support new elections because it aligns with his goal of Venezuela escaping U.S. control.

— James Bosworth
24:34

Machado could win 90 percent but might need to concede the next election

If free elections were held tomorrow—Machado versus Delcy Rodríguez—Machado would win by over 90%, excluding overseas votes. (Roughly a quarter of Venezuelans have already left.) But winning is only the start. She needs to seize actual military and police control—part of why the Trump administration was hesitant about her—then fix ‘one of the worst economic collapses in history’and rebuild multiparty democracy with real checks and balances. That probably means accepting that she or her party loses the next election.

— James Bosworth
