The AI compute bottleneck is no longer GPU—Huang says it's memory

Huang said three years ago he already explained the future memory demand curve to Micron and SK Hynix; this supply tightness is not the historical cyclical memory price fluctuation but structural change in AI compute architecture.

The substance of this episode is concentrated in the last twelve minutes: structural analysis of the memory bottleneck, clarification on why H200 wasn't discussed in China—both hard information; the customer cases at the start can be skipped.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

AI factory customers are moving from pilot to production

Dell added 1,000 AI factory customers this year, bringing the total to 5,000. Michael Dell stressed the key change is not quantity but stage—customers are transitioning from testing and evaluation to true production deployment, with 1,000-GPU-scale operations like Eli Lilly and Samsung. These are real-world implementations in the physical world, not screen-based demonstrations.

— Michael Dell
1:03

Intelligence must run where data is generated

Jensen explained why enterprise AI should run locally rather than in the cloud: intelligence must be produced where context happens, because enterprise data, proprietary knowledge, and security requirements are on-premises. He distinguished ChatGPT from agentic AI—ChatGPT is great but only generates content; agentic AI actually does work, which is the value enterprises will pay for.

— Jensen Huang
4:08

CPU became the harness for agentic AI

NVIDIA introduced Vera CPU. Jensen defined it as the harness for agentic AI—equipping the large language model brain with memory, networking, and tool-calling ability to become a truly functional digital robot. NVIDIA builds the brain (Grace Blackwell, Vera Rubin) and long-term memory architecture; Dell integrates these technical components into enterprise-ready solutions, with the product called Dell AI Data Platform.

— Jensen Huang
7:26

The bottleneck has shifted from GPU to memory

Asked what the biggest supply bottleneck is now, Jensen's first answer was memory, followed by advanced-node semiconductors. He said this is not traditional cyclical memory pricing—three years ago he already explained the future demand curve to Micron's Sanjay Mitra and SK Hynix, who adjusted their capacity plans accordingly. So this supply-chain tightness looks more like a structural uplift driven by AI demand.

— Jensen Huang
10:41

Digital agents lead to physical agents

Jensen compared agents to digital employees: today there are a billion people working worldwide; in the future, hundreds of billions of AI agents will run 24/7, each needing a computer and storage space like an employee. He believes this AI infrastructure buildout will take at least a decade-plus because after digital agents comes much larger-scale physical AI—robots entering real industry. Supply chains have roughly doubled or quadrupled year-over-year for years and still can't meet demand.

— Jensen Huang
12:43

He didn't personally discuss H200 in China

Asked whether H200 can be sold to Chinese customers, Jensen stated clearly that on this China trip he didn't directly discuss H200 with Chinese officials. He attended representing the U.S. in support of President Trump; the specific decision depends on what the President and Chinese leaders work out. He personally judges China's AI demand is similarly vast and will likely be more open long-term, but that's just his personal sense, not a promise in hand. Michael Dell added that Dell has business in China and observes all export controls, hoping for more economic cooperation between the two countries.

— Jensen Huang
15:46

Taiwan remains the global tech manufacturing and R&D center

When the host pressed on China's hard-line position on Taiwan, Jensen didn't comment on politics and stressed he wasn't part of presidential-level dialogue. What he addressed was supply chain: Taiwan remains the center for global tech manufacturing and R&D; TSMC's capacity is still critical. Meanwhile, the U.S. is reindustrializing, building more chip fabs, packaging plants, and computer factories to increase supply-chain diversity and resilience—not to replace Taiwan's role.

— Jensen Huang
18:52

NVIDIA and Dell hint they'll collaborate on GPU-powered PCs

The host directly asked if NVIDIA and Dell would launch personal computers with built-in powerful GPUs; both said they can't disclose plans yet but will announce soon. Jensen took the opportunity to lay out his view of the PC's end state: computing went from mainframes to personal computers; now it's moving from cloud AI to personal AI. AI needs to run where data and scenarios happen—laptops, factories, hospital operating rooms, and autonomous vehicles all need local agents, not just cloud dependency.

Glossary

harness
The execution layer that equips a large language model with tool-calling, memory, and networking capability to autonomously execute tasks.
agentic AI
AI that autonomously uses tools and completes multi-step tasks, distinct from generative AI that only produces content.
AI factory
On-premises AI infrastructure built by enterprises using GPU clusters, the alternative to cloud data centers.
physical AI
The next phase where AI enters the physical world to operate robots and other physical devices.
