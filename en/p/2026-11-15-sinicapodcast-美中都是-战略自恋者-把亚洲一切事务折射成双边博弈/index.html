Asia completed TPP without America returning or China taking over

Obama defended TPP saying ‘we don't write the rules, China will’. After Trump withdrew, the other 11 members completed the deal—neither America returned nor China joined. This shows how regional order actually works: not through binary US-China competition, but through first movers—countries like Chile, New Zealand, Singapore, Brunei that liberalize first and set the template for others to join.

— Evan Feigenbaum
16:27

US and China are the same: both strategic narcissists fixated on each other

US and China have almost nothing in common—different political systems, economic models, strategic logic—yet share one striking quality: both treat nearly all regional affairs as expressions of their mutual competition. Evan calls this ‘strategic narcissism’. He acknowledges this contradicts the Chinese position on Belt and Road as win-win cooperation, but judges that China is learning American behavior. The two countries' patterns are converging.

— Evan Feigenbaum
17:28

America's focus on China security has absorbed its entire Asia strategy

The US securitizes China policy: nearly all economic flows—goods, capital, people, technology, data—now pass through a national-security filter. Simultaneously, it China-izes Asia policy: China becomes the sole reference point for almost every regional decision. The result: the entire Asia policy itself becomes securitized. Regional countries have begun asking what else America actually offers.

— Evan Feigenbaum
23:34

US-Korea and US-Australia alliances shifting from local defense to Taiwan

US-Korea alliance once focused almost exclusively on North Korea; Taiwan is now unavoidable. US-Australia ties, structured since the 1952 Radford-Collins agreement around geographic division of labor, now have America pushing Australia to extend strategy into Taiwan contingencies, expand forward positioning, adjust logistics, and raise defense spending to 3.5% of GDP.

— Evan Feigenbaum
26:34

America wants hegemony without paying the cost of public goods

Evan finds America's regional policy ‘strong on attitude, weak on strategy; strong on security, weak on everything else’. It has no coherent regional vision (no longer pushing trade liberalization) and no consistent logic (why antagonize all allies if the goal is balancing China?—yet China-focused competition has led the US to trade wars with India, not China). America is pursuing ‘empire on the cheap’: maintaining hegemony while closing markets and withdrawing public goods.

— Evan Feigenbaum
30:35

China is adopting America's toolkit of economic coercion

China's Central Asian economic ties have shifted from oil and gas to digital infrastructure and renewables, giving China gravitational pull through geography and economy, without needing to ‘persuade’. But China is also copying American coercion methods. America has the Entity List; China created the unreliable entities list. America sanctions firms and people; so does China. More absurd: Hong Kong's National Security Law Article 29, Section 4 makes compliance with foreign sanctions illegal—China has written extraterritorial jurisdiction into its own statute.

— Evan Feigenbaum
35:37

Solve functional problems with real coalitions, not ceremonial forums

US officials have recently obsessed with building trilateral and quadrilateral mechanisms; someone seriously proposed the US-Japan-Kazakhstan arrangement. Evan's counter: what's the point? His advice to middle powers: stop chasing form. Instead, assemble genuinely capable countries to solve concrete functional problems. DEPA, the digital trade agreement, emerged when four TPP countries collaborated; that's how rules get set in a world where both America and China are faltering.

— Evan Feigenbaum
38:38

Quad worked for nine days, then became purely symbolic diplomacy

Quad started in 2004 as a tsunami-relief mechanism. In nine days, the US, Japan, Australia, and India finished the rescue. No ministerial meetings. No summit. No joint communiqué. No secretariat. Task done—the mechanism dissolved itself. When Japan later revived Quad, the emphasis shifted to form: summits, photo opportunities, declarations. Evan criticizes this: people now debate whether Quad works precisely because it became formal machinery rather than functional response.

— Evan Feigenbaum
