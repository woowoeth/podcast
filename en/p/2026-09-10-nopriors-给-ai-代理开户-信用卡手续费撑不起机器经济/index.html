An Account for Every AI Agent: Credit Card Fees Can't Carry the Machine Economy

Coinbase wants every AI agent to have its own account: 76% of machine payments are under 30 cents, and the minimum credit card fee is exactly 30 cents — the old payment rails can't carry the machine economy.

6:47

76% of machine payments are under 30 cents

Armstrong says agents are already generating a lot of few-cent transactions: VC firms scraping paywalled data for research, recruiters crawling LinkedIn, one agent calling another specialised agent as a tool to fetch source data. But the minimum per-transaction fee on a debit or credit card is a flat 30 cents plus a percentage, so the old rails basically don't work below $1 — paying 30 cents to move 1 cent makes no sense. The number he gives: about 76% of the agentic commerce transactions they see are under 30 cents. That is the hardest reason for stablecoins and crypto rails to cut into the machine economy.

— Brian Armstrong
11:42

Build a "brain" for every service

Coinbase has built a "brain" for every team, every repo, every service and even every employee: inside it are all the incidents that service has had, the financial controls that must be enforced, every A/B test that has been run, the full GitHub history, and which PRs were accepted or rejected. When an agent comes to change code, it reads this brain first. The truly critical step: when a human reviewer finds the agent missed something, they are not allowed to just hand-edit and merge — that correction must be written back into the brain. That way one correction covers all future scenarios, and the one-shot PR rate keeps rising; Armstrong calls this "recursive self-improvement".

— Brian Armstrong
15:00

The CEO runs 10 agents changing code at once

Armstrong says that now, when he hits an idea or a bug, he no longer pings the team all day — he drops the comment into an internal harness (they call it Toshi) and sends over a finished PR for review; he says it's addictive. He has also pushed the CEO back into writing features: have an expensive frontier model break a complex feature into three phases of ten things each, then have it recommend which cheaper model to use for each subtask, then run 10 agents in parallel on phase one. He says that over the course of recording this podcast, all 10 of those things got done and were waiting for his review.

— Brian Armstrong
17:02

What gets killed is tasks, not people

On whether AI will make companies smaller, Armstrong's judgment is: productivity really is rising, but that's a separate matter — what gets automated or eliminated is tasks, not people. He doesn't think a company like Coinbase shrinks to 10 people; rather, the people already there can do vastly more and everything speeds up. At the same time, two-person and five-person companies will indeed produce volumes that used to be unimaginable. The result is that the cost curve of revenue relative to headcount changes: the same group of people can produce far more, and over the long run margins should rise. He doesn't close the door — some companies will still hire at the same pace.

— Brian Armstrong
18:33

88% of revenue no longer comes from Bitcoin trading

Armstrong cites the numbers from the earnings report: 88% of revenue now comes from non-Bitcoin trading. The interesting part is that Coinbase's stock price is still highly correlated with the Bitcoin price. He says he loves Bitcoin, sees it as digital gold and sound money for everyone in the world, but crypto is updating every part of the financial system. The tokenised equity product he just announced is currently only available outside the US, and they are talking to regulators about a US path; he stresses these are real stocks — backed 1:1 by real securities held in custody, not synthetics, derivatives or debt instruments.

— Brian Armstrong
21:57

Prediction markets are more than sports betting

Armstrong says prediction markets are growing extremely fast inside the Coinbase app: within a few months of launch they reached about $100 million in annualised revenue, growing over 100% quarter on quarter. Sports is indeed part of it, and he sees that as entertainment worth celebrating — but he thinks it is much bigger than that, and the societal implications are not yet fully understood. His example is policy: to judge whether a policy should be implemented, you could first form a prediction market on "what will the unemployment rate be one, two, three years after implementation". Further out are big questions with no clear settlement date, like "is there a God" — he calls that an opinion market, with mechanics like buying a stock: you're just betting it will get better or worse.

— Brian Armstrong
31:53

Restore cells to their youthful function

New Limit takes the epigenetic reprogramming route: it doesn't change the cell type (the Yamanaka-lab-style approach does), it only restores the cell's youthful function. The platform first uses AI to search the vast combinatorial space of transcription factors for candidates, runs large-scale pooled screens, then does functional experiments (mostly animal models), and finally goes into human trials. The first batch targets three cell types: hepatocytes, vascular cells and immune-system T cells. The first Phase 1 trial is in alcoholic liver disease (ALD), where patients have almost no option besides a liver transplant and survival after diagnosis at 12 months is very low. Armstrong estimates that this one drug alone, if successful, could be worth $20 billion, but the goal is for a 40-year-old to have the liver, immune system and blood vessels of a 20-year-old.

— Brian Armstrong
41:10

What America lacks isn't land, it's a sandbox

Armstrong's interest in special economic zones comes from a "meta-question": what is slowing down progress — in many places, it's overregulation. He mentions that Coinbase invested in Prospera in Honduras, and thinks they've had ups and downs with the local government but are close to resolving it. During the campaign there was discussion of an idea called "freedom cities": the federal government holds vast tracts of land, take 10 or 100 square miles, and on that land you don't have to go through the usual EPA process — you can build data centres, nuclear power plants, unlimited drone delivery without getting FAA approval item by item — first try what works in a safe, controlled sandbox, and once it works, push it back out to the whole country through the normal process. He says the problem now is that in many cases there is no sandbox to try things in at all, and if entrepreneurs can't iterate they can't solve the problem.

— Brian Armstrong
