Opening Accounts for AI Agents: Credit Card Fees Can't Support the Machine Economy

Coinbase wants every AI agent to have its own account: 76% of machine payments are under 30 cents, and the minimum credit card fee is exactly 30 cents—the old payment rails can't support the machine economy.

6:47

76% of machine payments are under 30 cents

Armstrong says agents are generating lots of few-cent transactions: VC firms scraping paywalled data for research, recruiters crawling LinkedIn, one agent calling another specialized agent as a tool to fetch source data. But the minimum per-transaction fee for debit or credit cards is a flat 30 cents plus a percentage, so the old rails basically don't work below $1—paying 30 cents to transfer 1 cent makes no sense. He gives the number: about 76% of the agentic commerce transactions they see are under 30 cents. This is the hardest reason for stablecoins and crypto rails to cut into the machine economy.

— Brian Armstrong
11:42

Build a "brain" for every service

Coinbase has built a "brain" for every team, every repo, every service, even every employee: it contains all incidents that service has had, mandatory financial controls, every A/B test run, the full GitHub history, and which PRs were accepted or rejected. When an agent comes to change code, it reads this brain first. The truly critical step: when a human reviewer finds the agent missed something, they are not allowed to just manually fix and merge—that correction must be written back to the brain. This way one correction covers all future scenarios, and the one-shot PR rate keeps rising, which Armstrong calls "recursive self-improvement."

— Brian Armstrong
15:00

CEO runs 10 agents at once to change code

Armstrong says now when he has an idea or bug, he no longer pings the team all day; instead he drops a comment into an internal harness (they call it Toshi) and directly sends a finished PR for review—he says it's addictive. He himself has the CEO go back to writing features: have an expensive high-end model break a complex feature into three phases, ten things each, then have it recommend which cheaper model to use for each subtask, then run 10 agents in parallel to execute phase one. He says during the time it took to do this podcast, all 10 things were completed and waiting for his review.

— Brian Armstrong
17:02

What gets eliminated is tasks, not people

On whether AI will make companies smaller, Armstrong's judgment is: productivity is indeed rising, but that's a different matter—what gets automated or eliminated are tasks, not people. He doesn't think a company like Coinbase will shrink to 10 people, but rather existing people can do vastly more and everything accelerates; at the same time, two-person and five-person companies will indeed achieve volumes that were previously unimaginable. The result is a changed cost curve of revenue relative to headcount: the same group of people can produce much more, and long-term margins should rise. He doesn't overstate—some companies will still hire at the same pace.

— Brian Armstrong
18:33

88% of revenue no longer comes from Bitcoin trading

Armstrong cites numbers from financial reports: now 88% of revenue comes from non-Bitcoin trading. Interestingly, Coinbase's stock price is still highly correlated with Bitcoin's price. He says he loves Bitcoin, considers it digital gold, sound money for everyone in the world, but crypto is updating every part of the financial system. His just-announced tokenized stock product is currently only available outside the US, and they are talking with regulators about a US path; he emphasizes these are real stocks—1:1 backed by actual securities held in custody, not synthetics, derivatives, or debt instruments.

— Brian Armstrong
21:57

Prediction markets are more than sports betting

Armstrong says prediction markets are growing extremely fast in the Coinbase app: within a few months of launch they reached about $100 million in annualized revenue, with quarter-over-quarter growth over 100%. Sports is indeed a part, which he sees as entertainment and worth celebrating—but he thinks it's much bigger than that, and the societal implications are not yet fully understood. The example he gives is policy: to judge whether a policy should be implemented, you could first form a prediction market on "what will the unemployment rate be one, two, three years after implementation." Further out are big questions like "is there a God" that have no clear settlement date, which he calls opinion markets, with a mechanism like buying stocks—you're just betting on whether it will get better or worse.

— Brian Armstrong
31:53

Restore cells to their youthful function

New Limit takes the epigenetic reprogramming route: it doesn't change cell type (the Yamanaka lab approach does), only restores cells to their youthful function. The platform first uses AI to search the vast combinatorial space of transcription factors for candidates, does large-scale pooled screens, then functional experiments (mostly animal models), and finally human trials. The first batch targets three cell types: hepatocytes, vascular cells, and immune system T cells. The first Phase 1 clinical trial targets alcoholic liver disease (ALD), where patients have almost no options besides liver transplant, and survival after diagnosis at 12 months is very low. Armstrong estimates this one drug alone could be worth $20 billion if successful, but the goal is for a 40-year-old to have the liver, immune system, and blood vessels of a 20-year-old.

— Brian Armstrong
41:10

What America lacks isn't land, it's sandboxes

Armstrong's interest in special economic zones comes from a "meta-question": what is slowing down progress—in many places it's overregulation. He mentions Coinbase invested in Prospera in Honduras, and thinks they've had ups and downs with the local government but are close to resolving it. During the campaign, an idea called "Freedom Cities" was discussed: the federal government holds large tracts of land, take 10 or 100 square miles, and on that land you don't have to go through the usual EPA process, you can build data centers, nuclear power plants, unlimited drone delivery without needing FAA approval for each item—first test what works in a safe, controlled sandbox, and if successful, push it back to the whole country through normal processes. He says the problem now is that in many cases there is simply no sandbox to try, and entrepreneurs can't solve problems without iterating.

— Brian Armstrong
