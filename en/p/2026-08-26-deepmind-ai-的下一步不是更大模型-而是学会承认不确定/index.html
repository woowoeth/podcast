AI's Next Step Isn't Bigger Models, but Learning to Admit Uncertainty

DeepMind researcher Zubin's 30 years of research shows that current large models' "confident nonsense" stems from a lack of explicit probabilistic representation; Bayesian updating, predictive ensembles, and calibration are key to reliable AI and AGI.

Uncertainty is not a flaw but a prerequisite for decision-making

The core of intelligence is decision-making, and in reality, perception is limited and the future is unpredictable, so any intelligent system must be able to represent uncertainty, update it, and act accordingly. Uncertainty comes in two types: one is inherent randomness in the world, like which way a pedestrian will turn next; the other is unfamiliar situations, like an autonomous car encountering a horse that suddenly jumps out during hail. Mathematically, both can be reduced to probability and manipulated uniformly by the rules of probability theory. If a system can perceive that it is in an unfamiliar situation, it should slow down—this is self-aware uncertainty.

8:15

Neural networks can identify a school bus as a cheetah with 99% confidence

For image classification, we care about both correctness and confidence. Over a decade ago, people discovered that by tweaking a few pixels of a school bus image, a human still sees a school bus, but a neural network says it's a cheetah with 99% confidence. Adversarial examples show that systems can be "confidently wrong." We don't want to see systems that are easily fooled. With LLMs, you ask a question and it answers confidently, but if you challenge it, it changes its answer—this overconfidence makes it untrustworthy. To give AI a sense of its own limitations is exactly what AGI needs to add.

— Zoubin Ghahramani
16:10

A good AI should be better than humans at changing its mind

Bayes' rule is simple: before observing evidence, you have a prior belief, represented by a probability distribution; after seeing evidence, you multiply the prior by the likelihood and normalize to get the posterior belief. This posterior becomes the prior for the next round, and so on. In detective stories, "I suspected him, but new evidence came so I changed my judgment" is a one-sentence description of Bayesian updating. Learning works the same way: a prior over unknown model parameters, data comes in sequentially, and parameters are updated step by step. This provides a rigorous framework for accumulating knowledge and avoiding baseless flip-flopping. A better AI should even be more rational than humans, as adept at handling probabilities as calculators are at multiplication.

— Zoubin Ghahramani
20:26

When it says "I was wrong," it's just next-token prediction

Current large language models do not explicitly represent their confidence in an answer. If you ask how sure it is, it just does next-token prediction; it isn't doing Bayesian inference. It may have learned to mimic reasoning traces from trillions of tokens, so it appears to "admit mistakes"—if you push, it says "Oh sorry, I was wrong," and it won't stick to its position. This is because the training paradigm doesn't prioritize confidence: the data is a mixture of human beliefs, and what's learned is just a soup. To truly build autonomous driving and robots, systems must be able to do causal reasoning about the real world and honestly assess their own uncertainty.

— Zoubin Ghahramani
27:05

Admitting uncertainty actually makes weather forecasts more accurate

Weather is a Bayesian success story. DeepMind's GenCast forecasts 15 days of weather in minutes, much faster than supercomputers. It uses diffusion models to manipulate probability distributions and generates an ensemble of forecasts rather than a single answer. When tracking hurricanes, you have a full probability distribution over future tracks; you run the model repeatedly, and hours later new observations come in and you update the ensemble—that's Bayesian updating. Incorporating uncertainty actually makes predictions more accurate: by honestly acknowledging that sensors are imprecise and model assumptions may be wrong, you get well-calibrated forecasts. Uncertainty is not noise; it's information.

— Zoubin Ghahramani
31:38

If you say 70% chance of rain, it must rain 70% of the time

For repeatable events, uncertainty must be calibrated: if I say the probability of rain is 70%, then on all days when I said 70%, the actual proportion of rainy days should be exactly 70%, and the remaining 30% should be dry. That's the definition of a reliable probability. For one-off events, like the date of humanity's first landing on Mars, Bayesian statistics holds that belief can also be expressed as a probability, which naturally resolves when the event occurs. The challenge is communicating uncertainty to humans without causing over-trust or neglect. For example, in AI-assisted medical diagnosis, every probability the system outputs directly affects treatment decisions and can be a life-or-death call, so uncertainty must be made visible.

— Zoubin Ghahramani
38:28

Retraining every few months isn't learning

The first path, pure scale, has made huge progress but isn't enough. Three breakthrough directions: continual learning—current models are retrained every few months, while humans continuously learn from data streams; Bayesian updating in theory doesn't suffer from catastrophic forgetting, and many continual learning attempts are approximations of it. Energy efficiency—the human brain consumes about 20 watts, while large model training uses orders of magnitude more electricity. New architectures—transformers and diffusion models are the two mainstays, but there may be new architectures co-designed with hardware, such as sparse neural networks. Bayesian methods seem magical, but the cost is extremely slow computation; however, in the 1980s people also said neural networks were too slow. Perhaps we should revisit these old ideas with today's computing power.

Glossary

aleatoric uncertainty
Uncertainty arising from inherent randomness in the world, such as coin flips or random pedestrian turns.
semantic entropy
A method to estimate confidence by comparing the entropy of different semantically equivalent answers generated by a model.
posterior beliefs
The updated probability distribution obtained after Bayesian updating, combining prior and evidence.
calibration
The property that predicted probabilities match the actual frequency of events.
