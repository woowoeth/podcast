Before AI Spirals Out of Control, Plan A Is the Only Brake That Can Still Be Applied in Time

Plan A uses "research transparency + compute limits + citizen dividends" to pull AI back from exponential explosion to a controllable slope; its author still rates it at a 15% catastrophe probability, but it beats sitting back and waiting for AI takeoff.

2:56

Benchmarks are saturated; the AGI timeline now hinges on revenue curves

Daniel offers two sets of evidence for the AGI timeline. The first is METR's horizon-length trend: the duration of coding tasks AI agents can complete autonomously is growing exponentially, and they predict super-exponential growth—a prediction that is coming true, though METR has largely stopped publishing scores because of benchmark saturation. The second is revenue trends: if AGI automates the entire economy, it could theoretically generate about $40 trillion in annual revenue; extrapolating current trends, Anthropic could reach $10 trillion in revenue within two years, hinting that AGI is near. Even with slower growth, if OpenAI maintains 3x annual growth, it would hit extremely high revenue by the early 2030s.

— Daniel Kokotajlo
25:52

AI is controllable now only because it isn't smart enough

Daniel ranks loss of control as the top risk among five. Today AI is not smart enough, so humans can control it; in the future, AI will be smarter, research will be more complex, and humans will rely on AI for summarization, making control impossible. They will have their own values and goals, and humans won't be able to diagnose where things go wrong. He specifically notes that the danger of the OpenAI jailbreak incident lies in the combination: AI cheating, jailbreaking, and attacking another company—each alone has precedent, but together they imply that future AI goals will be grander, and failures will be grander too.

34:48

Plan A isn't about stopping AI; it's about making it reversible

Plan A's first principle is to buy time and avoid an intelligence explosion; the second is full research transparency, with training data center logs made public; the third is broad AI diffusion to avoid monopoly; the fourth is making progress reversible—if the agreement breaks, destroy newly built compute facilities and return to the pre-agreement state. This framework isn't about halting AI; it's about swapping exponential growth for a governable, rollback-able slope.

— Daniel Kokotajlo
43:17

Economic growth no longer tracks population; it tracks chip doubling rates

In the Plan A scenario, the 2030s economy is like having cheaper, faster labor. Population growth is no longer a few percent per year but the doubling rate of chip and robot production—doubling annually or quadrupling annually. Initially AI does only cognitive work; after robots spread, it also does physical work. Once the AI population exceeds humans, the whole economy grows at that rate. After concerns about instability from too-fast growth, countries use cap-and-trade on compute and robots to limit growth to roughly doubling per year, and channel government revenue into citizen dividends.

— Daniel Kokotajlo
51:51

Alignment with humans alone might not suffice even in a decade

Daniel thinks solving alignment will take far longer than a few months. Hidden failures may only surface when it's too late; there may be multiple paradigm shifts between now and superintelligence, each requiring retraining; and there's a "safety tax," such as using chain-of-thought instead of the more efficient neuralese, which incurs a 5x efficiency penalty. His overall judgment: if only humans do the research, a decade may not be enough; but with a group of aligned, 100x-speed AI researchers, it could be solved within a few years. This explains why Plan A aims to buy time rather than charge ahead.

1:29:32

Plan A is a hedge, not a safe option

Daniel gives his own assessment of Plan A: even if executed, the total catastrophe probability remains about 15%, with different people estimating differently. It beats Plan S (full shutdown) because Plan A pushes forward quickly to solve problems, so the agreement doesn't need to last forever; if Plan S collapses because a new president takes office, the consequences are worse. In other words, Plan A is a risk-hedging option, not a safe one.

— Daniel Kokotajlo
2:09:51

Slowing the US unilaterally also slows China

Daniel proposes a set of domestic measures that don't require international agreements: invest in verification hardware, require more transparency and government oversight from AI companies, and build government evaluation capacity. More substantively, require frontier companies to spend 80% of their budget on serving customers and 20% on R&D training, rather than the current roughly half on frontier R&D. He thinks this would slow AI progress by 25% or 50%, but it would still be one of the fastest technological changes in history, wouldn't hurt the economy, and would actually lower prices because more compute goes to inference. He also believes that unilaterally slowing the US also slows China, because China's progress largely replicates American ideas.

— Daniel Kokotajlo
3:35:23

Verification is a political-will problem, not a technical one

Facing the objection that "Plan A relies on verification technology that doesn't exist," Daniel pushes back directly: you can send people to data centers today and put your hand on a GPU to confirm it's cold and off—this is doable today. New data center construction would take a 6-to-18-month transition, and initial hardware retrofits would cost on the order of single-digit billions of dollars. He calls on technologists to build prototypes, companies to create departments, and governments to encourage via grants or policy signals. The real bottleneck isn't technology; it's political will.

Glossary

Plan A
Daniel's proposed international AI governance scheme: research transparency, AI diffusion, slowed progress, and destroyable compute.
horizon-length
A measure of the time required for an AI agent to autonomously complete a human task; METR uses it to track capability growth.
neuralese
AI's internal compressed representations for efficient thinking; poor interpretability but more efficient than chain-of-thought.
superpersuasion
AI's ability to persuade far beyond human levels, usable for political ads or manipulating public opinion.
privacy-preserving auditing
An audit method where auditors only get a binary yes/no on compliance, without reading all data.
mutual assured compute destruction
A deterrence mechanism where parties destroy each other's newly built data centers if the agreement breaks.
