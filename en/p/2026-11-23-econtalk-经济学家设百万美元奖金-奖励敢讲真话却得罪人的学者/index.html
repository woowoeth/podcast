An economist funds a million-dollar prize for scholars who dare to speak uncomfortable truths

Roland Fryer and Bill Ackman created the Carob Trust Prize to reward social scientists who honestly analyze data even when their conclusions are unpopular and later defensible, aiming to counter self-censorship in academia.

1:21

The prize rewards the courage to ask, not the conclusions that follow

Fryer explains that the prize targets scholars who ‘dare ask’ a controversial question and treat data seriously, even if conclusions are initially unpopular and later prove defensible. His example: research examining whether racial disparities in police shootings stem from discrimination, finding the answer is no. The committee favors such work, but only if the author is genuinely investigating rather than deliberately provocative.

— Roland Fryer
6:33

Students had nowhere to hold the difficult conversations Fryer thought essential

Fryer asked 100 undergraduates where they engage in difficult discussions and received one answer: ‘we don't.’ He sees this as the prize's true purpose. If the brightest minds avoid questions about racial achievement gaps from fear of being labeled, the vulnerable populations who most need help will never receive policy innovations from these minds.

— Roland Fryer
12:30

He dismisses his own suffering: I simply won, things went well

When Russ compares him to Milton Friedman and Ignaz Semmelweis—scholars ‘persecuted yet keeping their innocence’—Fryer rejects the heroic framing. He says if he were still working at McDonald's, he'd probably be ‘the angriest Black man in America.’ His equanimity comes more from later good fortune and mentors than from innate resilience.

— Roland Fryer
53:44

Peer review excels at distributing tenure, fails at finding truth

Russ observes that peer review works well for deciding who gets tenure and honors but performs poorly at discovering truth, because reviewers know the author's identity and often judge by personal preference rather than academic merit. Fryer agrees the system is ‘broken’ but resists abolishing it entirely, citing John List as proof: rising from an obscure university through the review process to University of Chicago demonstrates that the system can work.

58:50

Scholars are now using AI to preempt peer reviewer objections before submission

Fryer reveals an emerging pattern: scholars feed papers to LLMs to predict which objections reviewers will raise, then preemptively address them. Meanwhile reviewers receiving those papers are using LLMs to write their critiques. He predicts this trajectory leads to a situation where ‘Claude writes papers for OpenAI and then Claude judges Claude,’ making peer review's problems intractable rather than solvable.

— Roland Fryer
1:01:49

His first time making AI central to teaching cut lectures to fifteen minutes

Fryer compressed his usual one-hour explanation of racial disparities in police shootings to 15 minutes, then had students select an LLM to verify or refute his conclusions. He observed them struggling to get the AI to confirm their existing beliefs. One student, after discovering a particular result, was unwilling to voice it and simply turned the screen toward him.

— Roland Fryer
1:06:09

He and Ackman disagree on almost everything except this fundamental thing

Fryer says he and his funder Bill Ackman disagree on nearly every issue, but share one conviction: scientific progress cannot be achieved through silence. The real victims of scientific stagnation are not scholars but children trapped in poor schools who could benefit from policies currently locked away because authors fear the cost of publication.

— Roland Fryer
