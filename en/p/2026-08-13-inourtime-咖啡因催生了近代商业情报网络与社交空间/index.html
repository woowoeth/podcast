Coffee Doesn't Get You Drunk — It Built the Modern Commercial Intelligence Network

Coffee first kept Sufis awake for prayer; it doesn't intoxicate, it makes people talk, so coffeehouses became clearinghouses for commercial intelligence, and then reshaped the global economy through slave plantations and branding. Today's arguments about the latte are still written inside this five-hundred-year history.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:26

Coffee began as a stimulant for prayer, not a drink

Coffee was not originally a beverage but the berry or cherry of a shrub. In the legend, a shepherd discovered that his goats became unusually excited after eating the berries; religious figures (most likely Muslims) then ate them and found they could stay awake all night to pray. The early use was to steep the whole cherry in water, or grind it into a paste mixed with animal fat as a high-protein snack. It first served the night devotions of mystics such as the Sufis, so coffee's origins are bound tightly to religious ritual rather than leisure. Its spread also advanced along trade routes and pilgrimage routes in parallel.

7:49

The coffeehouse's real innovation was that it had no alcohol

In the Ottoman Empire coffee went from a religious drink to an everyday habit of hospitality. Drinking coffee became a complete ritual: buying it, making it and serving it all had their rules. The coffeehouse became a place where men gathered in the afternoon to relax and talk, performing the social function of the modern café. The key difference was that it contained no alcohol — until then, almost all sociable drinking had depended on alcohol, whereas coffee kept people clear-headed, so it could be used for work, negotiation and long discussion. This setting of ‘sober sociability’ paved the way for the later rise of the European coffeehouse.

14:39

To get into the Levant trade, you had to drink coffee

In 1652 Pasqua Rosée, servant of the Levant merchant Daniel Edwards, opened a coffee stall in a small shed beside London's Royal Exchange — he even moved the coffee business into a proper building for it. Ten years later there were already 83 coffeehouses in the City of London alone. Three reasons explain the explosive growth: the Puritan regime restricted alcohol, coffee itself was addictive, and it was bound up with the Levant trading circle — to do business with that circle you had to drink coffee, and once you started you couldn't stop. The coffeehouse thus went from a novel exotic drink to a standard fixture of urban commercial life.

23:38

Coffeehouses closed into memberships, and that produced Lloyd's

The coffeehouse offered a form of public discussion that neither the university nor the tavern had: one long table, one newspaper, a penny coffee, and strangers could sit down and talk. It became a clearinghouse for merchants, lawyers and scientists. Edward Lloyd opened a coffeehouse in the 1690s specialising in maritime insurance and printed a list of shipping movements, which became Lloyd's List. Whoever read that list first in the morning had commercial intelligence hours ahead of everyone outside. By the late eighteenth century, brokers and underwriters found that closing off the information was worth more, so coffeehouses became membership institutions, evolving separately into the stock exchange and Lloyd's. This path from open to closed explains the birth of modern financial institutions.

29:16

Haiti won its revolution and lost its place in world coffee

When coffee became a mass consumer good in Europe, empires planted it in their colonies. The largest supplier was France, which brought coffee to the Caribbean, especially Saint-Domingue, today's Haiti; by the 1760s-70s it produced more than half the world's coffee, and all of that production depended on slave labour. After the French Revolution, ideas of liberty and equality seeped into the colony, black slaves and free people of colour rose up, and eventually founded the first black republic. The price was roughly 1,000 plantations destroyed and the coffee system collapsing; worse, the Republic of Haiti was refused trade by most of the world's nations and could never return to a leading position in the global coffee trade.

— Jonathan Morris
36:26

America's mass coffee habit was drunk out of the Civil War

America was coffee's first mass market. In the Civil War the Confederate armies consumed large quantities of coffee, generals used caffeine to keep soldiers alert, and the soldiers themselves became addicted; each soldier's coffee ration made roughly 10 cups a day. After the war, demobilised soldiers carried the habit into the expanding western frontier, creating enormous demand. Industrial roasting followed, and in 1873 the first branded coffee, Arioza, appeared, marketed by the Arbuckles company of Philadelphia to westward migrants. By 1913 about 85% of American coffee consumption was branded coffee. Coffee had turned from a bulk agricultural product into a modern industrial consumer good.

— Jonathan Morris
37:38

Brazil's coffee output rested on the last slavery to be abolished

Someone had to meet America's rapidly growing demand, and Brazil took that role. Coffee only reached Brazil in 1727; in the early nineteenth century large plantations formed around Rio and São Paulo, far larger in area than anywhere else in the world. The labour there was slave, and used longer than anywhere else: Brazil only abolished the slave trade in 1850 and slavery itself in 1888. The plantations used destructive agriculture, felling the Atlantic coastal forest, in exchange for large quantities of cheap but not low-quality coffee. From 1870 to 1950 Brazil supplied more than half the world's coffee, in some years as much as 70%; to this day it remains the world's largest producer.

— Markman Ellis
44:46

Chains aren't specialty coffee's enemy — they're its market

The guests disagree on the modern coffee market. Judith argues the world is splitting into two extremes: ‘the low-wage economy of corporate chains’ and ‘the craft consumption of specialty micro-lots’. Jonathan partly objects: he thinks the chains are precisely what created the market for specialty coffee; the real structural split is between the coffee drunk in chains and the mass-market coffee products consumed at home. And most of the world's coffee is in fact grown by smallholders in Africa and Asia, with limited land, growing it as a subsistence crop, who because the coffee industry has long paid poorly often cannot recover their costs — growing coffee can actually be a loss-making business, the cruellest legacy coffee history leaves to the present.

— Jonathan Morris
50:13

Italian coffee culture only really set after World War II

Italy claims to be the authentic home of coffee culture, but it only really took shape after World War II. Gaggia brought out a lever machine that used a piston to force hot water through the coffee grounds at about 9 to 12 atmospheres, extracting espresso, arriving just in time for Italy's rapid postwar urbanisation and the spread of the electricity grid. The Italian coffee bar emerged with it: people stood and drank in a minute or two and left. Italian law set a maximum price for ‘coffee not served at a table’, holding the price down, so drinking standing up became the habit. This espresso and bar culture was re-exported after the war to Britain, Australia and elsewhere, so that coffee once again ‘colonised’ Britain — today Britain has more coffee shops than tea shops.

— Jonathan Morris
55:55

A latte is mostly milk, but the argument is only about beans

The vehicle for espresso's revival in Britain was almost entirely milk drinks rather than straight black coffee. Italians drink eighty percent black coffee, while British consumers drink ninety percent white. The latte then became a symbol of the middle-class liberal: in America, Starbucks' expansion path long concentrated in blue states, and in Britain people use ‘Guardian-reading latte drinker’ to refer to a political group. Milk makes up the great majority of the drink, yet discussion of coffee always circles around the ‘romantic’ bean and rarely asks where the milk comes from or whether it is organic. This blindness to milk is itself a class question worth examining.

Glossary

Sufis
A mystical branch of Islam that first used coffee to stay awake for night prayer.
Levant Company
The chartered seventeenth-century English company trading with the Ottoman Empire, which brought coffee into the British market.
Lloyd's List
A shipping movements list that began in a coffeehouse and later developed into a maritime information and insurance institution.
Robusta
A disease-resistant, cheap, more bitter coffee bean variety, promoted in the late nineteenth century because of leaf disease.
Moka pot
A stovetop steam-pressure household coffee maker, popular in the 1930s and once championed by fascist aesthetics.
espresso
A concentrated coffee extracted quickly with high-pressure steam, the core product of the post-war Italian coffee bar.
