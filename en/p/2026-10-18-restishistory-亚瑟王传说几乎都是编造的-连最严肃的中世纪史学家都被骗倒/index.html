The Rest Is History 2026-10-18

Nearly the entire Arthur legend was fabricated, deceiving even the most rigorous medieval historians

Geoffrey of Monmouth fabricated nearly all the episodes—Arthur's conception, the sword in the stone, Merlin's prophecies—in his Historia Regum Britanniae, packaged it as factual history, and fooled the most authoritative medieval historians.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:10

Arthur's conception turned on an act of magical deception and identity fraud

Uther Pendragon fell in love with Igraine, wife of Gorlois, the Duke of Cornwall, but Tintagel Castle sat on a narrow promontory where three armed men could hold off an entire British army. Unable to force his way in, Uther turned to the prophet Merlin. Merlin used magic to transform Uther into Gorlois's likeness, tricking the guards and slipping into the castle where he bedded Igraine—that single night, she conceived ‘the most famous of men’: Arthur. In Geoffrey of Monmouth's telling, Arthur's very birth rests on an act of identity fraud.

— Tom Holland
8:22

Almost all the Arthur legend's defining episodes were Geoffrey's invention

Before Geoffrey of Monmouth, Arthur appeared only in obscure Welsh texts and a handful of Latin chronicle entries, never beyond Welsh margins. After Geoffrey's Historia Regum Britanniae appeared, Arthur's conception, the sword Calaburnus (that is, Excalibur), the spear Ron, Mordred's betrayal, Arthur's transport to Avalon—nearly all these episodes that would become the Arthur legend's core were Geoffrey's invention. More crucially, he packaged the book as ‘history’ not fiction, and claimed his source was an ancient Welsh manuscript given to him by the Archbishop of Oxford—yet no one has ever seen evidence such a manuscript existed.

11:32

One historian's astonished endorsement made the hoax credible across Europe

In January 1139, the English chronicler Henry of Huntingdon passed through the Abbey of Bec in Normandy, read Geoffrey's just-completed manuscript, and declared himself ‘astounded’. Henry himself was a respected historian and archdeacon; his endorsement carried enormous weight, elevating Geoffrey's book from a collection of tall tales to credible history. A decade later, another chronicler, Alfred of Beverley, went further, saying it would be shameful for a historian not to have read this ‘monumental work’. Welsh writers like Gerald who voiced skepticism were largely ignored.

14:36

The fictional Arthurian empire was weaponized to justify real conquest

Geoffrey depicted Arthur as a world-class conqueror of Scotland, Ireland, Iceland, Norway, Denmark, and Gaul—a figure to rival Alexander the Great. This fictional image had real political consequences: Edward I invoked Arthur's ‘conquests’ to justify his own invasion of Scotland; by Elizabeth's reign, the court astrologer John Dee weaponized Arthur's conquest narrative to legitimize English expansion into the Atlantic, and it was Dee who coined the term ‘British Empire’—using ‘Britain’ rather than ‘England’ precisely because he meant Arthur's legacy.

23:00

Medieval scholars treated Merlin's prophecies as real history and decoded them line by line

Geoffrey's book includes long passages of Merlin's prophecies. Orderic Vitalis, a Norman monk—a serious, reliable historian who had written the history of the Norman Conquest—was shaken by them. He calculated that the prophecies had been made six hundred years prior, then proceeded to match each line of prophecy text to actual events in the six hundred years that followed: ‘the people clad in oak and iron’ matched the Norman Conquest; ‘the lion's whelps turn into sea fish’ matched the drowning of Henry I's only son in the White Ship; ‘the island shall be drowned in tears’ matched the civil war that followed, the ‘Anarchy’. It never occurred to him that Geoffrey himself knew exactly what had happened in those six hundred years and could thus fabricate ‘accurate’ prophecies after the fact.

29:22

Merlin's prophecies actually altered the course of history

These prophecies were not passively interpreted but actively treated as action plans. In 1400, Owain Glyndŵr read Merlin's prophecies, believed himself fated to defeat the English, and raised the banner of Welsh independence (though unsuccessfully); in 1428, Joan of Arc's supporters found her shadow in the prophecies; and Henry VII of the Tudor dynasty—a political operator famed for cold calculation—was similarly spurred by Merlin's prophecies to launch his invasion overthrowing Richard III, which succeeded. A prophecy system widely recognized as fabrication was repeatedly taken as gospel by people across centuries, and on that basis they actually changed history.

55:33

A single ninth-century text is the entire wellspring of the Arthur legend

The Historia Brittonum, written around 829 by the Welsh monk Nennius, contains ‘what can fairly be called the most important passage ever written about Arthur’—the earliest surviving mention of his name. It calls Arthur ‘dux bellorum’ (commander of battles), lists twelve victories against the Saxons, and places the twelfth at the Battle of Badon—which is where Geoffrey later located it at Bath (though Nennius's original never specified where Badon was). Geoffrey's narrative scaffolding for Vortigern, Hengist and Horsa, the red and white dragons—all of it comes straight from Nennius.

1:00:51

The source closest in time to Arthur's era makes no mention of him

Gildas, who wrote in the late fifth or early sixth century—earlier than Nennius and the only author who might have witnessed or heard firsthand of this period—wrote the De Excidio Britanniae essentially as a sermon denouncing the Britons' sins that brought divine punishment. He mentions a ‘proud tyrant’ hiring Saxons as mercenaries, mentions Ambrosius Aurelianus as a Roman gentleman, and mentions the siege of Badon—but nowhere does he mention Arthur. By Gildas's account, the victor at Badon was Ambrosius, not Arthur.
