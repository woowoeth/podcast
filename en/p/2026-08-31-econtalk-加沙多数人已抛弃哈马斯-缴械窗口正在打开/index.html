Most Gazans Have Abandoned Hamas, and the Window for Disarmament Is Opening

Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib, a policy scholar born in Gaza, argues with four layers of opposition, two polls, and demographic data that most Gazans have long been anti-Hamas and that Hamas may be forced to disarm in stages; but host Russ's pessimistic reminder: disarmament for Hamas equals political suicide.

8:03

Gazans have responsibility too; occupation cannot be a shield

Alkhatib says he deliberately kept the book short and colloquial because there is already too much information about Gaza and no one reads long academic texts anymore. The book is written for four audiences: the pro-Israel community, the pro-Palestinian community, Arab and Muslim Americans, and Palestinians themselves. The title is a judgment: Gazans are hostages of Hamas, despite radicalization and peripheral support for some of Hamas's past actions. He asks Palestinians to acknowledge their agency, responsibility, and accountability even under power asymmetry, rather than using "occupation" or "1948" as a shield. Russ responds that the book is only about 85 pages, yet he, who considers himself well-read on the subject, learned a lot.

18:09

The day Hamas seized power, he was applying for asylum

Alkhatib was born in 1990, moved back and forth with his family in the late 1990s, permanently returned in 2000, and went to the U.S. at age 15 as an exchange student. He remembers the optimism at the end of the Oslo process: his family got their first landline and mobile phone, and he saw state institutions emerge in Gaza. Then came the failure of the Camp David talks in 2000, the outbreak of the Second Intifada, and Gaza turned to the other side. On June 14, 2007, the same day Hamas took Gaza by force and drove out the Palestinian Authority, he happened to be interviewing for political asylum and became the first Gazan to receive asylum in the U.S. because of Hamas's control of Gaza. He sees this date coincidence as a synchronization of his personal fate and Gaza's fate.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
21:43

The 2006 election was a gift from America itself

Alkhatib obtained classified discussion materials from a PLO official: before the 2006 election, the Palestinian Authority explicitly warned the Bush administration and Condoleezza Rice that Hamas would win, but the U.S. still pushed for the election. Hamas leader Zahar allegedly later boasted that they deliberately had supporters underreport support in Shikaki polls to prevent the U.S. from canceling the election due to high poll numbers. Alkhatib says Shikaki's polls have long been manipulated, and Hamas documents found by the IDF in Gaza in August 2024 show Hamas correcting poll results and inflating error margins to 20-40% to exaggerate support. That election turned Hamas from a resistance movement into a regime that also governs Gaza, and was the beginning of the path to October 7.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
28:51

Same people, two paths: Gaza builds rockets, West Bank builds a state

This is the framing section of the episode: Gaza and the West Bank became two contrasting experiments after Hamas took power. Hamas turned Gaza into an "armed resistance fortress," using aid and money from Qatar and Iran to build tunnels and rockets, while outsourcing civilian welfare to the UN and international NGOs; the Palestinian Authority in the West Bank, under Salam Fayyad, built professional security forces and genuine state institutions. Alkhatib's criticism points at both sides: the cost of Hamas needs no elaboration, while Israeli PM Netanyahu's government, to maintain Palestinian division, allowed settlement expansion and weakened the PA's legitimacy, effectively giving Hamas's narrative a lifeline. He cites the "South Korea vs North Korea" analogy: had the West Bank moderates been strongly supported back then, the trajectory would have been completely different.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
36:48

External funding cut off, Hamas has only the path of disarmament

Alkhatib's optimism that Hamas will eventually partially disarm comes from hard constraints: Iran and Hezbollah are preoccupied, Qatar's LNG exports are frozen due to the U.S.-Iran conflict, so they can no longer fund Hamas; Hamas has lost most of its experienced fighters and nearly all senior political and military leaders, leaving only the option of accepting "phased disarmament." Russ's response is a clear point of disagreement: if the other side knows you want it to disappear, any concession is fake, because disarmament means ending its control and lifeline. Alkhatib acknowledges there will be militias and tribal revenge, but he believes Gazan society has already distanced itself ideologically from Hamas, and the narrative shift will come earlier than the political shift.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
44:32

Hamas is a lighter version of ISIS and the Taliban

Alkhatib divides Gazans' opposition to Hamas into four layers: the shallowest is "you destroyed Gaza, we pay for your suicidal nihilism"; the second is Hamas members enriching themselves during the war using aid and overseas fundraising, even paying bribes to leave Gaza and then calling from abroad for continued resistance; the third is the overall twenty-year rule of Hamas, including suicide bombings and the destruction of the two-state solution; the fourth is the daily oppression by the Interior Ministry, from banning women from smoking hookah on the beach to Sinwar's 2020 attempt to legislate that women need male guardian approval to leave Gaza. His conclusion is direct: Hamas is "a lighter version of ISIS and the Taliban," only more concerned with public relations because PR keeps NGO and UN money flowing.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
52:36

Poll numbers in a police state are not worth trusting

Russ raises a methodological point: in a police state, poll numbers are meaningless because people dare not tell the truth, so he has always been deeply skeptical of Shikaki polls. Alkhatib offers alternative data: a poll commissioned by the Tony Blair Institute and conducted by Jim Zogby shows 93% of Gazans want Hamas to leave, and 86% hold Hamas equally responsible for Gaza's destruction. Demographics support his judgment: 70% of Gazans have never left Gaza, and nearly two-thirds of the population is under 30, having only experienced Hamas rule and knowing the world through smartphones. He believes the largest group is not ideologically committed resisters but ordinary people driven by economic interests, who are the most reliable foundation for peace.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
54:18

The danger is not Gazans but the organization Hamas

Regarding the argument that "many of the October 7 attackers had worked in Israel and received medical treatment, yet betrayed those who treated them well," Alkhatib acknowledges that from a pre-war population of 2.3 million, they were a minority, but the problem is not individual cases but structure: as long as Hamas retains the capability to gather intelligence, recruit, and launch attacks, any interaction can be exploited under coercion. Once Gaza achieves disarmament and transitional governance, the risk will not go to zero but will drop exponentially to a manageable level. Russ cites the demilitarization of Japan and Germany after WWII as positive historical cases, but doubts whether the external management conditions can be met this time. The core of their disagreement shifts from "Can Gazans be trusted?" to "Will the organization Hamas completely disappear?"

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib

Great Reset
Alkhatib's proposed 100-day zero-violence window to push for direct negotiations, unrelated to Davos.
