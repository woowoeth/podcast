Most Gazans Have Already Abandoned Hamas, and a Disarmament Window Is Opening

Gaza-born policy scholar Alkhatib argues from four layers of opposition, two polls and demographic data that most Gazans turned against Hamas long ago and that Hamas may be forced into phased disarmament — but host Russ's pessimism is a sharp counterpoint: for Hamas, disarmament equals political suicide.

8:03

Gazans bear responsibility too; occupation is no blanket excuse

Alkhatib says he deliberately wrote the book short and conversational because there is already too much information about Gaza and no one reads long academic works anymore. The book is written for four audiences at once: the pro-Israel community, the pro-Palestinian community, Arab and Muslim Americans, and Palestinians themselves. The title itself is a judgment: Gazans are Hamas's hostages, notwithstanding radicalization and peripheral support for some of Hamas's past actions. He demands that Palestinians acknowledge they still have agency, responsibility and accountability under asymmetric power, rather than using "occupation" or "1948" as a shield. Russ responds that the book is only about 85 pages, yet taught him a great deal despite his sense that he had read a lot of the relevant material.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
18:09

The day Hamas seized power, he was applying for asylum

Alkhatib was born in 1990, shuttled back and forth to Gaza with his family in the late 1990s, moved back permanently in 2000, and went to the United States as an exchange student at 15. He remembers the optimism at the tail end of the Oslo process: his family got its first landline and mobile phone, and he saw state institutions appearing in Gaza with his own eyes. Then came the failure of the 2000 Camp David talks, the outbreak of the Second Intifada, and Gaza turning the other way. On June 14, 2007 — the same day Hamas took over Gaza by force and drove the Palestinian Authority out — he happened to be interviewing for a political asylum claim, and became the first Gazan granted asylum in the United States because of Hamas's control of Gaza. He sees that date coincidence as the point where his personal fate and Gaza's fate synchronized.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
21:43

The 2006 election was handed over by the US itself

Alkhatib obtained confidential discussion materials from that period from a PLO official: before the 2006 election, the Palestinian Authority explicitly warned the Bush administration and Rice that Hamas would win, but the US still pushed the election forward. Hamas senior figure Zahar allegedly later bragged that they deliberately had supporters underreport their support during Shikaki's polling so the US would not cancel the election over high poll numbers. Alkhatib says Shikaki's polls were manipulated for a long time, and Hamas documents found by the IDF in Gaza in August 2024 also show Hamas correcting poll results and inflating the margin of error to 20% to 40% to pad its support. That election turned Hamas from a resistance movement into a governing authority ruling Gaza at the same time, and was the beginning of the path to October 7.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
28:51

One people, two paths: rockets in Gaza, a state in the West Bank

This is the episode's framing passage: after Hamas's takeover, Gaza and the West Bank became two contrasting experiments. Hamas turned Gaza into a "fortress of armed resistance," using aid money and funds from Qatar and Iran to build tunnels and rockets while outsourcing civilian life to the UN and international NGOs; the Palestinian Authority in the West Bank, under Salam Fayyad, built professionalized security forces and genuine state institutions. Alkhatib's criticism cuts both ways: Hamas's costs need no spelling out, while Israel's Netanyahu government, in order to keep the Palestinians divided, tolerated settlement expansion and undermined the Palestinian Authority's legitimacy — effectively extending the life of Hamas's narrative. He invokes the "South Korea vs. North Korea" analogy: had the West Bank moderates been strongly supported at the time, things would have gone very differently.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
36:48

With outside funding cut off, Hamas has only disarmament left

Alkhatib's optimism that Hamas will eventually partially disarm comes from hard constraints: Iran and Hezbollah are preoccupied with their own survival, Qatar's LNG exports have been frozen by US-Iran conflict and can no longer supply Hamas, and Hamas has lost most of its combat-experienced members and nearly all of its senior political and military leadership, leaving it only the option of being forced to accept "phased disarmament." Russ's response marks a clear point of disagreement: if the other side knows you want it to disappear, any concession is fake, because disarmament means the end of its control and its lifeline. Alkhatib acknowledges there will be militias and tribal revenge, but argues Gazan society has already distanced itself from Hamas ideologically, and that the narrative shift will arrive earlier than the political one.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
44:32

Hamas is a lightweight version of ISIS and the Taliban

Alkhatib divides Gazan opposition to Hamas into four layers: the shallowest is "you destroyed Gaza, and we pay for your suicidal nihilism"; the second is Hamas members enriching themselves during the war through aid and overseas fundraising, even paying bribes to leave Gaza and then calling from abroad for the population to keep resisting; the third is the whole of Hamas's twenty-year rule, including suicide bombings and the sabotage of the two-state solution; the fourth is the Interior Ministry's daily oppression of ordinary people, from banning women from smoking hookah on the beach to Sinwar's 2020 attempt to legislate that women need a male guardian's approval to leave Gaza. His conclusion is blunt: Hamas is a "lightweight version of ISIS and the Taliban," only more attentive to PR, because PR is what keeps NGO and UN money flowing in.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
52:36

Poll numbers in a police state are not worth trusting

Russ offers a methodological point: in a police state, poll numbers are meaningless because people dare not tell the truth, so he has always been extremely skeptical of Shikaki's polls. Alkhatib offers substitute data: a poll commissioned by the Tony Blair Institute and conducted by Jim Zogby shows 93% of Gazans want Hamas to leave, and 86% hold Hamas equally responsible for Gaza's destruction. Demographics support his judgment: 70% of Gazans have never left Gaza, nearly two-thirds of the population is under 30, and they have known only Hamas rule and learned about the world through smartphones. He argues the largest group is not ideologized resisters but ordinary people driven by economic interest, and they are the most reliable foundation for peace.

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
54:18

The danger is not Gazans, it is Hamas as an organization

Responding to the argument that many October 7 attackers had worked in Israel or received medical treatment there and then betrayed people who treated them well, Alkhatib concedes that against a prewar population of 2.3 million that is a minority, but the problem is not individual cases, it is structure: as long as Hamas retains the capacity for intelligence, recruitment and launching attacks, any interaction can be exploited under coercion. Once disarmament and transitional governance are achieved in Gaza, the risk will not go to zero, but will fall exponentially to a manageable level. Russ uses postwar demilitarization in Japan and Germany as positive historical cases, but doubts the conditions for external administration can be met this time. The core of their disagreement shifts from "can Gazans be trusted" to "will Hamas as an organization disappear entirely."

— Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib
