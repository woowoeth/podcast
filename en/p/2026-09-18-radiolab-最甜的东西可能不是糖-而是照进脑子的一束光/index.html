The Sweetest Thing May Not Be Sugar, but a Beam of Light Shone Into the Brain

Ranked by "sweetness," sugar is not the sweetest; but by the sweetness you actually taste, sugar is nearly impossible to surpass. More disruptive still: shine a laser directly on sweet-taste neurons, and in principle you could create a sweetness humans have never tasted.

The number one spot on the sweetness leaderboard is not sugar

The food industry really does rank sweet-tasting molecules, with sucrose as the benchmark. Honey is about 1.5 times sucrose, aspartame about 200 times, the katemfe fruit of the West African rainforest about 2000 times, advantame about 20000 times, and the top-ranked lugduname about 300000 times — a pinch of it can sweeten an entire swimming pool. But Eric Walters says this ranking measures only potency, meaning "using the least amount of stuff to light up the sweet-taste receptors." It is useful to soda companies and gum companies, and it is a different thing from "how sweet does it actually taste."

— Eric Walters
8:25

Something 20000 times sweeter, and a kid spits it out

Latif gave his own child the number-two entry on the leaderboard, advantame, and the child spat it straight onto the ground and scraped his tongue with his fingers. Scientists say it is 20000 times sweeter than sugar, yet it does not taste sweet. The reason is that there is only one kind of sweet-taste receptor, while there are twenty to thirty kinds of bitter receptors, so making a molecule that triggers only sweetness and touches no bitter receptor is like tiptoeing through a minefield. That is why so many sugar substitutes carry off-flavors.

— Latif Nasser
10:30

The sweet receptor is a dimmer, not a switch

Paul Breslin of Rutgers says that if you keep adding advantame to water, the sweetness does not keep climbing to the moon — it plateaus. The sweet-taste receptor is less like a switch and more like a dimmer: it can get brighter and brighter, but a sugar substitute can only brighten it so far. By the standard of "how far can you turn the dimmer up," Eric Walters says sugar may be the sweetest thing there is — he has never tasted anything sweeter than sugar. The episode goes all the way around and comes back to the starting point, but for a deep reason: glucose is the brain's best fuel, and the sweet-taste receptor evolved for sugar in the first place.

— Paul Breslin
14:39

Strawberries taste sweeter than blueberries, but blueberries have more sugar

Blueberries actually contain twice the sugar of strawberries, yet strawberries taste sweeter. The mechanism is volatiles: the sugar-free odor molecules strawberries release get pumped from the back of the mouth into the nasal cavity as you chew and swallow, and these olfactory signals overlap with taste signals in the brain, giving sweetness a boost. Vision works the same way — dye the same sugar red, or shape it to look very much like fruit, and perceived sweetness rises. Latif did a live experiment with honey plus strawberry flavoring and red coloring, and after tasting it Lulu said it was "noticeably sweeter."

— Linda Bartoszik
18:43

High notes can push sweetness up another ten percent

In research at the Science Museum in London, certain high-frequency tones raised perceived sweetness by 5% to 10%. Latif played them for Lulu, and she said it was "more layered, more sparkling, more crystalline," but she also admitted this step was far subtler than the strawberry step, and that honey left sitting out crystallizes anyway, which could be a confound. As for why sound affects sweetness, the episode states plainly that nobody knows the exact reason — it just keeps showing up in experiments.

— Latif Nasser
21:50

Install bitter receptors on the sweet pathway

Charles Zuker at Columbia University traced the entire sweet-signal route from tongue to brain: tongue to ganglion, to the brainstem, to another station in the brainstem, then to the thalamus, and finally to the cortex — only at the cortex do you say "this is sweet." His experimental method is direct: normally the cells carry sweet-taste receptors, so sweetness activates this pathway; if you swap in bitter receptors there, bitter chemicals will activate the same sweet pathway. He does this not to find the sweetest thing, but to understand how sweetness and taste work in the brain, which might help with addiction, obesity, eating disorders and diabetes.

— Charles Zuker
22:50

Use a laser to make a mouse taste sweetness that isn't there

Zuker's team implanted a light switch only in the sweet-sensing neurons of mice, then inserted an optical fiber into their heads; turn on the laser and those neurons fire, and the brain says "sweet" — even though the animal is eating nothing. Latif pressed him: could you turn the laser all the way up and create a sweetness humans have never tasted? Zuker said that in principle it is imaginable, that you might be able to create the sweetest sweet, and that the sweetest thing could be light. Lulu's reaction was resistance: this amounts to stripping pleasure out of the real world entirely, to the point where you would not even need to leave the house.

— Charles Zuker
25:52

The kind of sugar in raspberries drifts in interstellar dust

A research team at the Center for Astrobiology in Spain observed a cloud of interstellar dust and gas near the center of the Milky Way, read off its composition with spectroscopy, and found large amounts of erythrulose — a four-carbon sugar. It was the first time humans had found sugar in deep space, and a relatively complex sugar at that. As it happens, erythrulose also exists in raspberries. From this Latif joked that somewhere there might really be a space raspberry. This segment pushes "the sweetest thing" from the tongue out to the cosmic scale.

— Latif Nasser

Glossary

potency
The ability to light up sweet-taste receptors with the least amount of substance; the metric the food industry uses for its rankings.
volatiles
Small molecules floating in the air that produce smell and can enter the nasal cavity from the back of the mouth to influence taste.
optogenetics
Implanting light-sensitive proteins into specific neurons so that light can switch their activity on and off.
erythrulose
A four-carbon sugar found both in raspberries and in interstellar dust.
