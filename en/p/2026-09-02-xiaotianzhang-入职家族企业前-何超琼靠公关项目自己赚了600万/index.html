Before joining the family business, Ho Chao-qiong earned over 6 million from her own PR project

She didn't plan to inherit the family business; instead, she first proved she could independently earn and survive through her own PR firm, which earned her father's confidence to let her choose her own position and carve out her own department.

Raised as the 'big sister' managing the family from age five

Ho says she started managing the family schedule at age five—booking vacations, deciding where to eat, all under her control, because her three younger sisters and one younger brother were close in age and her mother needed help. Years later, she learned from her sisters that her ‘discipline’ had been stricter than their mother's, so much so that the sisters would find ways to stay at school to avoid coming home. She later realized her difficulty making friends with peers as a child was the cost of bearing responsibility so early.

Father opened doors to the powerful, but never discussed what she saw

Starting at age seventeen, Ho followed her father to social events, meeting Hong Kong's top business leaders, government officials, and entertainment figures. But her father never debriefed afterward or asked ‘what do you think of this person?’—he'd met too many people to discuss any one of them intentionally. She says the real education came from watching him navigate different situations firsthand, not from what he said directly.

30:21

Disrupting charity circles as an outcast that no one initially trusted

When she returned to Hong Kong and joined the top ten charities, Ho was the youngest. She quickly became seen as a troublemaker for refusing to accept the convention of ‘one dinner and one concert per year,’ with some asking her father to move her elsewhere. She requested to take sole responsibility for a project, and within years she had grown a Viennese Opera Ball fundraiser from the industry standard of 500,000 to 1 million HKD to 3 million HKD; one televised fundraising gala she organized raised over 10 million HKD in a single night. These results won over the uncles and aunts who had doubted her.

Making 6 million a year, but never telling her father

Ho landed an annual retainer contract through her PR firm worth over 6 million HKD—her highest-earning year in her PR career. Right then, her father proposed she join the family business, offering a monthly salary of over one hundred thousand HKD plus a director's seat. She knew she was earning far more than he was offering, but chose not to mention it. Looking back now, she still says, ‘I don't have it, he doesn't know.’

52:48

She built power by creating a position no one else wanted

After joining Wynn, her father gave her a director title but no specific responsibilities; department heads were also reluctant to have her involved in daily operations. Rather than use her status as the boss's daughter to override her teams, Ho met with every department head, assessed the situation, then proposed to her father the creation of a ‘Corporate Affairs department,’ which she then expanded to include HR—a position that threatened no one's territory but made her the only person who could report the true state of the entire company to her father within two years.

1:09:15

Under the most pressure, she does the hardest work first

Ho says she always handles the hardest task first each day, rather than like most people who save the easy ones for the start and push the hard ones to the end. Her logic: avoiding a problem won't solve it, and your avoidance will only create more misunderstandings with the other party, generating unnecessary trouble. Even when the fault lies with her team, she believes you must face it head-on immediately, imagine the worst outcome, rather than wait for others to back down first.

— He Chaoqiong
1:11:17

She avoids perfection to prevent larger failures

Many assume that Virgo Ho is a perfectionist, but she says the opposite is true—she knows life can't be perfect, so she never insists on perfecting every task. Instead, she reminds herself not to overlook small, seemingly simple mistakes in pursuit of a perfect outcome; if you ignore these small issues for the sake of perfection, by the time you discover them, it's often too late to turn back.

1:34:28

That generation became idols not through packaging, but personality

Ho recalls that 80s Hong Kong stars and singers ‘truly had talent’—no image consultants, no agency-manufactured personas, they dressed entirely from their own personalities, and whatever they wore became a trend, not because of paid endorsements. These people became idols precisely because they were different from ordinary people; personal charisma and individuality were the core. Now most of these old friends have gradually left, which gives her a sense of sadness.

Corporate Affairs
The department Ho proposed and founded herself, overseeing brand management, media relations, investor relations, and cross-departmental coordination.
