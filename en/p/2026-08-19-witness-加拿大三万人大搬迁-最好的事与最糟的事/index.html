Thirty Thousand Canadians Were Moved: The Young Were Saved, the Old Lost Everything

Starting in 1954, nearly 30,000 people in Newfoundland were relocated from remote fishing villages to towns. A younger generation gained education and a way out; the elderly and the Inuit lost their homes and scattered until they had no way to reach each other. There is no single verdict on resettlement.

0:00

A village that can't support a school and a hospital should be emptied

Starting in 1954, the Canadian government launched a large-scale resettlement programme aimed at moving nearly 30,000 people from hundreds of coastal communities (outports) in Newfoundland and Labrador to designated growth centres. The official logic was modernisation: these remote settlements could not support modern education and health care, and only concentrated settlement would give people access to roads, schools and electric light. The episode states at the outset that this process would last more than twenty years and thoroughly transform a way of life that had endured for centuries.

— Katherine Harvey
0:00

No power, no road: in the government's eyes, that isn't modern life

Don White, who lived through it, grew up in Port Nelson on Newfoundland's east coast. The early life he describes sounds like another era: the whole village had a single general store, there was no electricity, and people lived by hunting moose, rabbits and seabirds and by catching salmon and lobster; his grandfather had a schooner that crossed the bay each year to bring in vegetables for the winter. The nearest hospital was an hour away by boat, and the road system consisted of footpaths. These details are not nostalgia — they are the premise for understanding the motive for resettlement: in the government's view, this life could not provide the basic conditions a modern citizen needed.

— Don White
2:53

Four children rowing to school forced the decision to move

Don White's schooling was the most direct catalyst for the move: his home was on the south side of the harbour and the school on the north side, so four children had to row an open boat across the harbour themselves, with no adult accompanying them; in winter, when the harbour froze, they had to walk an hour around it. The school had a single classroom, at most 22 pupils across all grades, and it closed after grade six. He says plainly that it was precisely so the next generation could get a decent education and opportunities that the family decided to move. This chain of logic turns "resettlement" from an administrative order back into a family choice.

— Don White
2:53

Industrialise or die — but the government decided where you went

The chief architect of the plan was the premier of the day, Joseph R. Smallwood. In his words: Newfoundland had to industrialise or it would perish; and since the island could barely guarantee even primary education, people could not acquire the skills industry required. In a private recording, he stressed that resettlement was not about offloading a burden — the government set a condition: it would help you move, but the destination had to be approved, because it absolutely did not want people "jumping out of the frying pan into the fire." This double formulation shows that resettlement was both an idealistic vision of development and a form of conditional control.

— Joseph R. Smallwood
6:12

600 dollars could buy the house, but not a fresh start

Resettlement was not free. The government gave each household that moved out a grant of 300 dollars, later raised step by step to 400, 500 and 600 dollars. To save money, islanders invented their own way of moving: they fitted sleds under their houses and dragged them with ropes and cables down to the water, even rolling whole houses — stove, table, curtains and furniture included — onto rafts and floating them along the coast to the new home, with no machinery, all by human power. The other side of the ledger: many people found no way forward after arriving in the towns, instead crowding into cramped quarters and facing unemployment. The subsidy could cover the move, but not the risk of starting over.

6:12

Resettlement saved the young and ruined those who only knew how to fish

Not everyone benefited. One interviewee in the documentary (Edward) calls it the worst thing he has been through: he had no skills but fishing, and was forced to row back alone to the old fishing grounds every summer to do the only work he knew. The Inuit were especially harmed by the plan — after being driven off their land in Labrador, many were permanently cut off from their traditions and communities. Don White also admits that after the move people scattered, and that his best friend (the one he had gone to school with since childhood) lost touch after grade seven or eight; they never met again before the friend died. This section breaks the single-line narrative that modernisation equals progress.

8:58

Going back, there is only weeds: it takes a village to raise a child

Years later, Don White revisited the home he remembered and found nothing left: no buildings, only weeds. Sitting on the spot where the house had been, he broke down — both his parents had died in the meantime. The loss even affected the older generation: his father never managed to adapt to the new life. He says he did not realise at the time that "it takes a village to raise a child," and that the kind of community where neighbours raised one another's children has vanished for good. At the same time, these abandoned "ghost villages" have now become tourist destinations and film locations, a cultural and memory heritage. The ending fuses personal memory with the transformation of a place.

— Don White

Glossary

outport
A remote small fishing village on the Newfoundland coast, once the main form of settlement there.
growth centre
A town designated by the government for concentrated development, to which people were encouraged to move so they could share schools, hospitals and other facilities.
