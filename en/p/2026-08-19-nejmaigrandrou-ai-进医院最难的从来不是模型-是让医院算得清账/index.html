The Hardest Part of AI in Hospitals Was Never the Model — It's Making the Hospital's Math Work

Suchi Saria arrived in hospitals with a sepsis alert adopted by 90% of doctors, and concluded: the blocker for AI isn't model performance, it's trust and the financial ledger under diagnosis-based payment.

12:23

Publishing a paper is not clinical success

She warns this is the step most researchers misjudge. The 2015 Science Translational Medicine sepsis alert model looked beautiful on retrospective data, but when it actually had to be deployed in the emergency department and on the wards, the data there turned out to differ in veracity, completeness and messiness; even the ICD-10 codes used as ground truth for quality were too noisy and could lead a team into a ditch. To fill in ground truth, debiasing, transparency, outcome evaluation and post-deployment monitoring item by item, she later did roughly 40 papers' worth of innovation and published only 20.

— Suchi Saria
18:33

A hospital won't pay just because a paper is good

Saria says making a health system adopt AI a no-brainer involves many layers. First you have to prove the tool is 10x, 15x better than current practice and explain why; then turn a point solution into a platform covering multiple conditions; then work with EMR vendors to compress IT integration to about 80 hours and go live within six weeks; after that comes governance, monitoring, tuning, even planning alongside the FDA; only then do clinical outcomes and financial return come up. Every step is a "surprise," and every step is a hill that must be climbed.

— Suchi Saria
25:37

Software itself can be a treatment

She holds to one judgment: medical innovation shouldn't be only drugs and devices, there should also be pure-software AI-based interventional protocols. These interventions aren't about helping doctors write notes; they use multimodal data from the whole EMR to answer precisely "when, for whom, do what," and to make execution smooth. She says this is the big opportunity for improving care quality, because most AI today clusters around pre-visit transcription and post-visit coding, while the middle ground of actually "caring for the patient" is nearly empty.

— Suchi Saria
31:47

Doctors use an alert voluntarily only if the signal is accurate

The scale Bayesian reported in a 2022 Nature Medicine paper: nearly 750,000 patients, two years, 4,400 clinicians, of whom 2,200 were physicians. The internally critical number is a 90% physician adoption rate. She says this came not from bombarding people with pop-ups but from signal quality: many older tools fire 95% of the time after the doctor has already started treatment, which is worthless; what she designed was an early warning objective that identifies patients ahead of time, so doctors open it voluntarily, enter an assessment, and choose to agree or disagree.

— Suchi Saria
37:56

Under DRG payment, saving a patient early saves money

Under bundled DRG payment, a hospital gets a fixed amount for treating a sepsis patient. A 20-day stay versus a 4-day stay means the extra 16 days of cost come entirely out of hospital profit, and occupy beds other patients could have used; if sepsis isn't identified on admission and isn't documented as present-on-admission, Medicare pays even less. Suchi says that for a 2,000-bed system, these missed cases stack up to $30 million to $40 million a year in financial penalties, and AI identifying patients early amounts to giving the money back to the hospital, which also makes it easy for the CFO to say yes.

— Suchi Saria
46:05

The hardest part is clinical trust, not technology

Asked which medical task AI will have mastered in five years, Suchi doesn't list new algorithms. She says data quality and veracity are hard, but as an AI researcher that part is most within her control; what she finds genuinely difficult is the sociotechnical layer: getting doctors to trust the output, and getting EMR vendors like Epic to make integration easy. In her view, the pain point in medical AI today isn't that models aren't strong enough, it's that the system deciding whether a model can be used was never in the data scientist's hands.

— Suchi Saria
54:13

Clinical multimodal data is still a research blue ocean

She says academic research still has vast open ground. She cites FDA-funded translational safety research and the work of several PhD students: an EMR isn't text plus images, it's thousands of modalities, each with its own missingness patterns, bias patterns and interaction patterns; how to design architectures for that, and how to validate generalization, are unfinished problems. Foundation model companies are under pressure to do well on benchmarks, focused on Q&A over internet text, but real applications aren't Q&A — that's the opening for researchers; pick important problems where the giants haven't already massed their forces.

— Suchi Saria
57:20

The low-hanging fruit is exactly what you must not pick

Her advice to founders is blunt: many startups today sell a grand vision but aren't AI native themselves, and their moat is unclear. Nobody knows how long the market gap will last; if a small company can build a moat fast through execution speed, it can take a shot, but if it can't build a moat even on speed, it will be swallowed by the large model companies. Suchi chose the opposite: everyone told her not to do sepsis because it's hard, requires long-term commitment, and requires hospital relationships, but she has only ever done what others consider impossible; she says hundreds of companies are doing low-hanging fruit like turning medical records into codes, and lasting impact lies in the hard problems no one dares touch.

— Suchi Saria

Glossary

failure to rescue
Failing to detect and intervene in a patient's deterioration in time, leading to avoidable death or severe illness.
DRG
Bundled payment by patient diagnosis, where resources a hospital saves turn directly into revenue.
NTAP
An additional payment CMS gives for new technology, compensating hospitals beyond routine payment.
AI-based interventional protocols
Pure-software treatment protocols: identifying from multimodal data the timing, the target and the action, and helping execute it.
