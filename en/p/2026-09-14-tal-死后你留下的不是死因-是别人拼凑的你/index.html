After You Die, What You Leave Behind Isn't the Cause of Death — It's the Version of You Other People Assemble

All three stories do the same thing: once a person is dead, the people left behind can only reconstruct someone who can no longer speak from fragments — texts, obituaries, Facebook comments — and the version they piece together often bears no resemblance to how the person saw themselves.

After you die, other people will get you wrong

As a kid, Graham was playing on a seesaw at a construction site and dropped a 25-pound curbstone from second-story height, trying to launch a brick off the other end. The brick didn't fly — it shot straight at his face and grazed past his hair. He survived, but decades later what he keeps thinking about isn't the terror of that second, it's something else: if the brick had actually killed him, how would a coroner have read the scene? A brick, a seesaw, a kid — could they have pieced together the truth? That's where the show lays out the premise of the whole episode — death itself isn't the hardest part; the hard part is the judgment other people make about who you were after you're gone.

12:15

She has now outlived her sister

Nadia wrote a long letter to the drug dealer who killed her sister. The sharpest cut in it isn't anger, it's arithmetic: at 6:49 p.m. on March 11, 2018, she outlived her sister's age — she had spent a few more seconds in this world than the sister who was two and a half years older. She says it makes no sense, you bent the universe. She went through her sister's phone, read the texts, listened to the voicemails, trying to figure out the life her sister had stopped sharing with her. She even admits she needs this dealer: because of him, she can say "if it weren't for that person," and she doesn't have to hate her sister, or hate herself.

— Nadia Bowers
20:28

The message she never answered became the last one

A few days before her sister died, her sister sent an inconsequential text asking if she remembered the little stick-figure illustration labeled faible (weak) in their middle-school French textbook. It was an old joke between the sisters. Nadia was still angry about their last fight and deliberately didn't reply, wanting to punish her. But a strong thought surfaced in her mind: you don't want this to be the last message she ever sends you. She still didn't reply. After her sister died, she found a text her sister had sent a friend that night: "How do I stop hating my sister? My family really hates me." The friend replied: "Damn, Nadia the snitch." When she saw the word "hate" next to her own name, she felt hollowed out.

— Nadia Bowers
33:53

He lived to read his own eulogies

Comedian Dave Marr was in a diabetic coma for a month; his friends thought he was going to be taken off life support, more than fifty people gathered at his house for a vigil, and 1,700 Facebook friends started writing eulogies on his wall. Then his parents didn't pull the tube, he was transferred to another hospital, and he woke up. After recovering, he sat in his parents' basement and read from 2 a.m. to 3:30 a.m., getting through about a hundred eulogies. He read that his brother's post trying to set the record straight got buried by the algorithm with only 70 likes, while posts mourning him got double the likes. He read that someone remembered him smashing a storage-room door at a party insisting it was the bathroom, that an ex-girlfriend thanked him for breaking up with her in the car because it was cold outside, that someone said he "sweats unreasonably much."

— Dave Marr
42:06

The man in the eulogies is a stranger to him

After reading all the eulogies, Dave wasn't moved — he felt bad. The self he remembers is someone who shoplifted at Walgreens and spent a night in jail; the self in the eulogies is known by every Walgreens clerk, buys people free Chipotle, treats everyone as an equal. He says it's hard to square the image in his own head with the image people described when they thought he was dead. He insists the coma was his own doing — "it was my carelessness, my recklessness that got me here." Blake's side is a different story: he lost a friend and got him back, and now Dave's laugh is like music to him. The show says both men got a second chance, to love and to be loved, and the latter is much harder than the former.

— Dave Marr
57:33

Nobody mentioned how he died

Retired Navy officer Charlie went in a friend's place to the funeral of a SEAL he'd never met, Eddie Furlani. He couldn't find any reporting on the cause of death and assumed it was a training accident. At the funeral he noticed the back rows were full of big guys in three-piece suits and sunglasses, expressionless the whole time, until Eddie's seven-year-old daughter Zelma broke down crying and was taken out by her stepmother. After the service he called his friend to report back, and the friend said, "I'm glad someone came, I was worried no one would," then added, "because of the way he killed himself." Charlie says that moment was like grabbing a live wire — no reporting, no mention in the obituary, not a word in the eulogy.

— Charlie
59:47

The daughter was crying because she felt ashamed

The day before the interview, Charlie called Eddie's widow to confirm, and only then learned that Zelma had cried and asked to leave the funeral not out of grief but out of shame — she thought everyone knew her dad had killed himself, and that she was a bad person. Charlie says hearing this terrified him: a seven-year-old girl treating herself as an outcast because of her father's death, when the room was full of people who had come to support her. He also talks about veterans losing their "larger mission" after they leave the service, and says he can probably put himself in Eddie's shoes and understand what he was thinking in that moment, but when asked whether he could say more, he stops and says he can't take on that topic.

— Charlie
1:02:00

He wanted to be the sound of that helicopter

Charlie has had suicidal thoughts himself; this ended his Navy career, and he only agreed to talk about it after the mic was off. The host asks whether he wrote the piece because Eddie's funeral could be his own future funeral, and he says no. He explains the real reason: on medevac flights he knew that the sound of the helicopter approaching alone could improve a dying person's vital signs. He says I'm writing to you because I want to be that helicopter sound for someone. The show ends with the National Suicide Prevention Lifeline, 800-273-TALK.

— Charlie

Glossary

Memento Mori
A conscious awareness of one's own mortality; the title of Act Two of this episode, Comento Mori, is a punning rewrite of it.
medevac
A mission using helicopters and the like to move wounded personnel from the battlefield to a medical point; Charlie flew these in Iraq.
frogman
A nickname for Navy SEALs.
