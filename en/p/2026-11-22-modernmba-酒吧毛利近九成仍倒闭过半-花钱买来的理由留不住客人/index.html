Bars Have 90% Margins. Most Still Go Broke.

Bars never sell alcohol—they sell reasons to go out drinking. Reasons bought with money get copied fast and stop working. Only irreplaceable owners and regulars keep bars alive.

Bar economicsIndependent retailProfit marginsTrading downRestaurant chainsNew York
Must-watch for entrepreneurs considering bars as a side venture: behind the gross margin illusion lies the industry's true failure rates and razor-thin real profit margins.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:15

Half of New York's bars don't survive nine years

Liquor license records covering nine states show that half of bars opened nine years ago in Manhattan and Brooklyn have closed, with three-fifths of owners already gone. Denver, Washington, D.C., St. Louis, and Connecticut saw similar closures of over half, while Texas saw nearly half its bars close within seven years. In open markets without liquor license caps, bar survival rates are like a coin flip. Where government strictly limits licenses, survival is higher, but that protected the ‘bar’ as an entity, not the ‘owner’—even in California, half of bars changed hands within eight years.

9:20

Sports bars rent their reasons; alcohol alone won't work

Sports bars never need to invent their own reason; they just rent one from ESPN or the NFL—the broadcast games. But live sports fills only a few evenings a week. Buffalo Wild Wings and Hooters quickly discovered that beer and broadcasts alone can't cover a full week's rent, so they shifted growth to the kitchen: Buffalo Wild Wings grew from $1.6 million to $3.3 million in annual revenue per store, nearly all from food rather than alcohol, and now operates 200 alcohol-free takeout-only locations. The kitchen can reach more people, more hours, more days, while the bar and TV wall stay—only because that's still the reason customers walk in and sit down.

13:21

Bar-and-restaurant chains eventually abandon bars for pure restaurants

Chains like TGI Fridays, Chili's, and Applebee's—neighborhood bar-restaurants—originally got liquor licenses because they had kitchens, and profited from alcohol. But starting in the 90s, states prohibited happy hour promotions and tightened drunk driving liability, so bars could only earn during fixed hours while kitchens faced no such restrictions. Food gradually became the main business: Chili's alcohol dropped from 11% of sales to 9%, and business actually improved; Applebee's fell from 18% to 12% while stores closed. These brands all shifted from ‘places that sell alcohol’ to ‘restaurants that also sell alcohol’—the kitchen is the real moat.

30:32

Old-school dive bars' profit margins fell two-thirds in thirty years

Joe ran a bar in Chelsea for thirty-four years. In the 90s, his first bar had a 50% profit margin; by the 2010s, when Onestar was doing $960,000 annual revenue, margins had fallen to 23%; now that revenue has halved to $480,000, and profit margins are down to 18%. He attributes this to three things: gentrification pushed up rents and young people out of the neighborhood; hybrid work emptied happy-hour crowds from office towers; and American alcohol consumption fell overall—though even he can't explain why that last one happened. For a bar that lives almost entirely on alcohol sales, this isn't rounding error. It's the entire business.

— Joe
36:36

Cocktail bars fail and change hands faster than any other bar type

Cocktail bars should theoretically be the perfect hedge against industry headwinds—the farther from commodity, the higher the premium you can charge. But data show they're the most fragile: half of Manhattan cocktail bars are closed, two-thirds in Brooklyn, and 62% in Texas closed within seven years—higher than any other bar type. Over half of Manhattan cocktail bars changed hands; nearly three-quarters in Brooklyn; four-fifths in Washington, D.C. A $20 cocktail demands a lot of the room, the bartender's presentation, and the neighborhood's spending power. Once customers stop believing that cocktail is ‘worth that price’, bars like this have no retreat—there is no ‘two-beer regular’ base to fall back on.

42:40

Even perfect execution at Lullabi hits a 10% profit ceiling

Lullabi does $1.14 million in annual revenue and is the best case study in the whole show: located in the hottest neighborhood, its bartenders' original recipes earn royalties, and the founder came from top cocktail bars in the world. Operating profit margin is still just 10%. Harrison said extracting this margin is ‘like pulling teeth’; the only bars he's seen exceed 15% profit are nightclubs or chains that spread costs over scale. After the two owners and their team take their share, Lullabi's profit is ‘enough to live on in New York, but just barely’. Even when everything about the space, service, and product is right, the ceiling for a cocktail bar is still very low.

— Harrison
52:47

The most famous cocktail bar in America never turned a profit in six years

Death & Co is the defining brand of modern cocktail revival in New York, and expanded to Denver, Los Angeles, and Washington, D.C., added merchandise, hotels, and an app, went public with a stock sale, and had to file financials with the SEC. Single-store annual revenue averages about $3 million with a gross margin of 78%—a $20 cocktail costs only $3 to pour—but labor eats 55% to 65% of revenue, and after marketing and build-out, every set of financials from 2020 to 2025 shows losses. The company now survives on stock sales, emergency loans from founders, and credit card advances from three locations; it announced no new openings and is cutting costs to stay alive.

56:49

What money buys fails; what money can't buy builds lasting bars

TV walls, chicken wings, arcade games, beautiful bartenders, trendy cocktails—these ‘reasons to come drink’ that money can buy spread fast and fail just as fast, because anyone with money can copy them. What actually holds people: the intangible—atmosphere, community, a bartender who knows your name, years of regular-customer relationships. Onestar can't survive without Joe. Lullabi can't survive without Harrison and Jake. Rockwell can't survive without Alex. These bars stay themselves only as long as the owner does; regulars stay only as long as the owner does. Bar premiums were never about the alcohol. They were about this irreplaceable, non-transferable, can't-sell-it-twice web of things.
