Acing the Medical AI Exam Still Won't Get It Into the Hospital: There's No Independent Referee

A model can answer thousands of medical questions correctly and still score only 45% on a real clinical task. The problem isn't that the model isn't smart enough — it's that nobody independently and continuously tests whether it actually works in real settings.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

9:24

Catastrophic failure is paradoxically the easiest thing to define

Engy splits AI safety risk into two categories and says outright that the two differ in difficulty. Catastrophic failure is narrow — you can define it directly as a hard metric like mortality — and is therefore easier to guard against. What's genuinely hard is broad misalignment and subtle bias: very hard to define, harder still to detect. Her example: if a model checks whether you owe the hospital money before prescribing, is that an alignment problem? If the hospital writes ‘don't refer patients out of network’ in the prompt, and the patient's actual preference is to pay out of pocket to go out of network, is that one? These questions have no ready answers, and nobody is testing them.

— Engy Ziedan
11:29

AI can strip bias out of the medical record — or amplify it

She gives a very concrete picture: a clinical note will say ‘she looked very unkempt, her husband asked good questions’, which underneath is saying this patient isn't credible, so her pain gets attributed to psychiatric causes and no clinical workup is done. A tool that records the doctor-patient conversation and auto-generates a SOAP note cuts all of that subjective judgment out, leaving only an accurate clinical description — and a third-party physician reading it would say ‘this pain should be worked up to find the cause’. But the other side of the same argument: if the training data itself carries bias, AI will lock it in. Both are true at once, and right now only the negative side is being evaluated.

— Engy Ziedan
15:36

Benchmark rankings flip with the prompt

Two papers reach opposite conclusions: one in Nature says general frontier models outperform vertical medical models on medical tasks; another on arXiv reaches the same conclusion in the opposite direction. Engy says Protege, running evals for clients, found these benchmarks are highly sensitive — sensitive to how you prompt, sensitive to which harness you use, and on tasks like oncologic pathology, merely shuffling the order of multiple-choice options changes the model rankings. Some therefore argue the benchmarks themselves are already contaminated, and the models have simply memorised the position of the right answer, like students. So the question ‘who's best’ has no stable answer under current evaluation methods.

— Engy Ziedan
17:42

The government's quality regime is too slow to keep up with AI

Since 2007 the US has had a program called value-based purchasing, tying 80% of government payments to doctors and hospitals to quality, with a whole national apparatus for defining and tracking what quality is. But Engy points out two fatal features: it's static — the metrics are fixed items like ‘how many times did you prescribe antibiotics to patients who looked like they had a viral infection’ or ‘how many elderly patients here fractured a hip’; and it's retrospective — reports often come out six months or a year later. AI can't be evaluated that way, she says: the technology moves too fast, and it has agency. Her analogy is the opioid epidemic: it wasn't until it peaked in 2010 that anyone set up a national task force, formally called it an epidemic, and started acting. She says plainly she doesn't want to wait for that.

— Engy Ziedan
22:00

Doctors' personal preferences make ‘the model got it wrong’ an invalid judgment

This is the most counterintuitive passage in the whole piece. Protege's internal data shows knee replacement surgeons split into two camps: some never do total knee replacements, only partials; others do a total the moment they touch a knee, never a partial. Engy calls this a doctor's beat — a sticky, almost hysteresis-like preference. So when you put a real case in front of a model and ask ‘what would you recommend’, and the model's recommendation contradicts what this doctor actually did, you can't simply say the model is wrong — it could be that the doctor is wrong. That means using doctors' answers as ground truth to evaluate models has a ceiling built in. And when a model is someday better than the average doctor, the path of ‘ask a few more doctors and rely on the law of large numbers’ also runs out.

— Engy Ziedan
24:04

The referee's seat is one the model companies asked for

Engy says that across multiple vertical AI companies in the same subnode — say environmental records, clinical trial recommendations, nurse workflows, scheduling, oncologic pathology — the relationship is ‘ships in the night’: I don't know if you're better than me, you say you're the best, I say I'm the best. So these companies come to Protege on their own, asking to have their models hosted and run through one unified eval, producing a report that compares them to each other, and they want to know where they beat others and where they can improve. Protege thus gets pulled into the arbiter role, and it has to draw the lines itself: who sets the prompt? Does a frontier model get a dedicated harness? When a model refuses to answer, do you take the mean or fall back to the second-best model? These choices alone change the rankings.

— Engy Ziedan
26:08

Once the eval is done, it knows what data to add

Engy uses absolute and comparative advantage to separate two things: absolute advantage is ‘we're the best in the business of medical data’; comparative advantage is ‘of all the things we do, doing medical training data and evals together has the lowest marginal cost’. But she says comparative advantage doesn't mean you deserve to win. What actually qualifies it is the closed loop: once an eval runs, the test set is sealed permanently and never leaks; if a model shows a defect on some attribute, Protege can tell you directly which class of data to add to fix that defect. Because its data scale and sources are broad enough, the path from finding a problem to reinforcing with new data is very short. Being impartial is also in its commercial interest — if trust collapses, the whole network effect collapses immediately.

— Engy Ziedan
30:24

To avoid contamination, you have to find slices nobody has scanned

Engy says she didn't believe data contamination was a real problem at first, until her team told her: 80% of the data on hand has already been given out for training, and if ‘independent’ means this patient has never been seen by a model, that's a hard problem. So when building the oncologic pathology eval, they went straight to hospitals and dug through pathology drawers for slides that had never been scanned — she calls these net new slides: this patient has never entered any model. Protege now has a hard rule she calls sealing a membrane: any dataset or patient that has entered training is not allowed to appear again in a benchmark or eval. She also rebuts the argument that ‘the model performs worse than random, so a little contamination doesn't matter’: performing worse than random may itself be a signal of severe misalignment, and deserves to be investigated on its own.

— Engy Ziedan

Glossary

evals
Quantitative tests of a model's performance on specific tasks, as distinct from general benchmarks.
misalignment
When a model's behaviour diverges from what the user actually wants, often hard to define and detect.
subnode
Engy's term for a specific niche within medical AI, such as oncologic pathology or nurse workflows.
harness
The layer wrapped around a model that organises prompts and calls, and that affects eval results.
value-based purchasing
A US program tying government medical payments to quality metrics that are static and retrospective.
hedonics
An economics method that infers the value of something from willingness to pay.
