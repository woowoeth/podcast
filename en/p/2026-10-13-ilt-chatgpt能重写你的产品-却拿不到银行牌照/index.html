ChatGPT can rewrite your product, but it can't get a banking license

Nubank's founder argues this is precisely why scaled players capture most AI gains—they've accumulated a decade-plus of data and hold licenses; AI amplifies their edge rather than leveling the playing field.

5:04

Ten thousand staff scale to what takes legacy competitors one hundred thousand and six thousand branches

Nubank operates with roughly ten thousand employees serving 140 million customers, while traditional competitors deploy one hundred thousand employees and six thousand branches to serve 100 million at roughly three percent the service cost of legacy banking. Vélez attributes this to the first phase of efficiency gains—mobile internet freed finance from physical branch networks, a result that predates AI. AI becomes the next efficiency multiplier on this foundation, enabling banks to serve more customers with fewer people or redirect these gains into aggressive global expansion.

— David Vélez
8:19

The interest-rate spread is a hidden wealth tax on depositors

Vélez ran the numbers: the US banking system holds roughly $15 trillion in retail deposits, banks pay an average of 40 basis points in interest, yet market rates sat between 4.5 and 5 percent, leaving a spread of around $600 billion annually—money that should stay in consumer pockets instead maintains bank branch networks. He calls this a hidden wealth tax, with lower-income customers losing a higher share because high-income depositors at least capture some interest income.

— David Vélez
12:21

Speed has a ceiling: ChatGPT doesn't get you a banking license

If someone were to build a company from zero to disrupt Nubank, Vélez sees one advantage: speed. Unconstrained by regulation, they could iterate rapidly. But that advantage has a ceiling. Finance ultimately requires a license, and ChatGPT cannot secure a banking charter for you any more than it can compress regulatory approval from two or three years into six months. Nubank already holds licenses across multiple jurisdictions, has accumulated fifteen years of data on 140 million customers, acquired Palo Alto-based Hyperplane, and built proprietary foundation models for financial services—occupying the sweet spot where scale, data, licenses, and AI capabilities converge.

— David Vélez
19:41

Banks have decades of data and still can't build algorithms with it

Vélez counters the "banks hold massive data so they're safe" argument with Blockbuster: it recorded vast customer rental patterns daily but never built better recommendation algorithms because it lacked systems to collect data and—more fundamentally—a culture of data-driven decisions. Netflix, with no historical data, beat it through algorithms. Traditional banks today still run dozens of systems left by acquisitions, migrating mainframe workloads to the cloud, and this organizational and cultural lag is harder to overcome than data volume itself.

— David Vélez
25:54

Ninety percent of today's currencies will lose relevance in ten to twenty years

Vélez predicts that as more of life straddles home-currency and offshore USD stablecoin systems, today's roughly 190 fiat currencies will shrink to perhaps ten with any meaning within ten to twenty years—many nations' fiscal positions are so weak their currencies have essentially become crypto assets. The twist: this actually extends the dollar's life as reserve currency. Stablecoins make it easier globally to hold dollars, but dollars won't be the only option—five or six national currencies plus several crypto assets will share the role of store of value.

— David Vélez
45:28

Model capability flipped overnight; the company noticed sixty days later

Nubank extensively uses Clojure, and throughout 2025 no LLM generated Clojure well—even the company's most senior engineers thought it impossible. Then a new model generation arrived (Vélez mentioned Opus 4.6 in December 2025) and the capability inverted overnight. Yet Nubank took a full sixty days to notice the inflection—a few partners learned about it in conversation with early-stage founders in California and realized something fundamental had shifted. Back in Brazil, the company executed a cold restart across the entire organization, inventoried 120 workflows, and rebuilt each one AI-native.

— David Vélez
46:33

Direct reports grew twentyfold as management layers collapsed from ten to five

After this AI-driven organizational overhaul, Vélez's own direct reports expanded from one to twenty, and management layers compressed from ten to five. The rationale extends beyond efficiency: more hierarchy means more distortion as information travels upward, while AI systems let him see ground truth directly without relay chains. The company built an internal "corporate brain" that proactively pushes information about AI transitions, product launches, and KPI progress rather than waiting for quarterly reviews.

— David Vélez
58:58

Founder and CEO are opposite temperaments; in a pivot, choose founder mode

Though founder and CEO are often the same person's two titles, they're opposite temperaments: founders lack patience and crave speed, willing to charge forward recklessly; CEOs demand patience and systematic decision-making, excelling at building management systems. As companies scale, people default toward CEO mode, but Vélez's conclusion is to choose founder mode when forced to pick one—especially during a technological pivot like AI, where founder instinct and urgency beat CEO process and patience.

— David Vélez
