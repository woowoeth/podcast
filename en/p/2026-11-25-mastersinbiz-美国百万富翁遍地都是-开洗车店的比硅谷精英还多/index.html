Humble Business Owners Accumulate More Wealth Than Tech Entrepreneurs

America has 3 million ‘everywhere millionaires’—private business owners with $5+ million net assets whose combined wealth exceeds the Forbes 400's more than tenfold, built mostly on unglamorous businesses like dealerships, HVAC, and laundromats rather than tech and finance.

1:03

Tax Data on Small Business Owners Reveals Real American Wealth

The authors were recruited by the Treasury Department and Tax Analysis Office while completing their doctorates to build a dataset linking pass-through businesses to their owners and employees. The goal was purely fiscal: calculating tax liability for businesses that don't themselves pay taxes, requiring tracing to individual owners. What began as a narrow tax-policy tool unexpectedly became a research vehicle for studying national income, wealth, and class mobility—the foundation for this book's data.

3:08

Wealth Spreads Across Counties, Not Concentrated on Coasts

The authors initially believed, as many do, that wealth concentrated in tech and finance on the coasts. Mapping ‘everywhere millionaires’ across America revealed otherwise: wealth glows in every county. The Forbes 400 is only the surface. The reality: 3 million private business owners with net assets of at least $5 million drive wealth and inequality statistics, their combined wealth exceeding the Forbes 400's by more than a factor of ten.

6:09

Yacht Records Reveal Millionaires Hidden from Public View

The host, who counts yachts at every new city's marina, wondered where the owners of multimillion-dollar boats made their money. The authors purchased yacht registration data—tax records anonymized by industry only—and traced ownership backward. They found Nancy Mueller, who made yacht wealth from frozen French pastries, and Dick Portillo, who turned a suburban Chicago hot dog stand into a restaurant chain, sold it to Berkshire Partners, and named his yacht ‘Top Dog’.

8:10

Ownership, Not Salary, Becomes the Wealth Driver for the Rich

The authors categorize income as either ‘earn’ (wages) or ‘own’ (capital and business gains). At the 90th income percentile, most income is wages. As you move up the income ladder, the relationship shifts: by the top 0.1%, most income originates from business ownership, not labor. In America, the mainstream path to great wealth is owning something, not collecting a paycheck.

9:13

One Tax Reform Turbocharged Private Business Growth

In 1986, Congress passed a bipartisan tax reform that pushed individual income tax rates below corporate rates for the first time—a critical moment for pass-through businesses. It made pass-through structures more advantageous. Later Bush-era tax cuts further lowered individual rates; the 2017 reform added a 20% deduction for some pass-throughs. The result: pass-through income now represents over 50% of all business income, and nearly all small businesses use the structure, making it the institutional foundation of this book's analysis.

13:18

Half of Top 1% Income Growth Traces to One Tax Reform

The authors performed a counterfactual: what if pass-through income's share of top-tier income had stayed at its 1985 (pre-reform) level, while everything else evolved normally? Even with this single variable held fixed, pass-through income growth alone explains half the top 1% income-share increase from then to now. Data from the Survey of Consumer Finances, covering 2000 onward, shows the top 0.1% derived two-thirds of their wealth growth from private business ownership.

14:20

Starting a Business Beats Wage Work, Even After Failure

When the host raised concerns about survivor bias in the entrepreneurship narrative, the authors countered with data on roughly 10 million entrepreneurial events since 2000: even accounting for high failure rates, entrepreneurs on average earned approximately 10% more than wage workers in comparable circumstances—and this excludes unrealized capital gains from business sales. About 20% of founders pursue multiple ventures, often reaching significant wealth on a second or third attempt. This suggests not guaranteed success, but a relatively sound financial gamble.

16:24

Failing in Business Is American Opportunity, Not European Disgrace

The host relayed an observation from a friend who grew up in Switzerland and lived in America: in Europe, business failure carries a multigenerational stigma; in America, it reads more like a checkbox you can clear and move past. The author, who teaches entrepreneurship to MBA students, confirmed this: international students often must explain career gaps to families back home; in America, ‘going out and trying it’ is treated as a default-approved option.

19:27

Congress is Dominated by Business Owners, Not Tech Founders

Unlike tech and finance elites concentrated in Silicon Valley and Wall Street, nearly every congressional district contains an ‘everywhere millionaire’—a car dealer, say. These figures employ many workers, pay substantial sales taxes, have high community visibility, and poll more favorably than the military, Supreme Court, or Congress itself. They run frequently for office. Approximately a quarter of Congress members are private business owners; including law firm partners, that share reaches 40-50%. State and local government posts—especially low-paying mayoral roles—are even more dominated by business owners.

Glossary

pass-through business
A business whose income is taxed at owner rates rather than company level.
everywhere millionaire
Private business owner with wealth built outside tech hubs, distributed across America.
seller financing
Seller-provided loan to buyer, repaid from future business profits, lowering acquisition costs.
