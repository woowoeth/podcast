Scientists tricked a rat's brain into thinking salt water tasted good

Researchers used optogenetics to amplify reward-signal neurons in rat brains so that when the rat drank salt water it hated, the brain still judged 'this was good'—sensory input and reward signals can be directly severed.

Rationality is economics' limiting case, not how humans actually decide

Camerer describes the utility-maximization model as a ‘limiting case’ in economic theory, like a simplifying assumption in physics—expert tasters with extensive experience who can rank all options might approach perfect rationality, but that is not the state of most people most of the time. The model resembles the endpoint of learning rather than its starting point. Textbook ‘rational actors’ were never what the field actually believed; they were just a convenient launching point.

— Colin Camerer
12:14

Life and death probability questions should add to 100%—they don't

When asked ‘what is the probability you'll live to 75,’ people answer with a number like 90%. But when asked separately ‘what is the probability you'll die before 75,’ the answer will not be 10%—the two should sum to 100%, but they do not. Camerer's collaborator Eric Johnson attributes this to ‘default endorsement bias’: the question frames attention toward survival or death accordingly. Frame effects are not trivial—salad dressing labeled ‘10% fat’ sells differently from ‘90% fat-free’. Advertisers and political consultants have long understood this.

— Colin Camerer
15:02

Chess masters guess numbers like everyone else in this game

In the guessing game where players choose 0–100 and the closest guess to two-thirds of the average wins, most people reason only one or two steps deep—‘others will guess 50, so I'll guess 33; or others will guess 33, so I'll guess 22’—and almost no one reasons all the way to zero. The New York Times ran this with 50,000 players; the data shows 33 appears far more often than 22, and deeper reasoning is nearly nonexistent. Surprisingly, professional chess players perform about the same as amateurs in this game—deep strategic thinking appears to be highly domain-specific and does not transfer across domains.

— Colin Camerer
29:55

Audiences can't reverse-engineer ‘no reviews’ as a bad signal

From 600 major theatrical releases (2000–2009), roughly 10% are ‘cold opens’—studios don't send films to critics beforehand. These films later show the lowest aggregate critic scores, indicating studios knew they had lemons and held them back. But the cognitive hierarchy model predicts that some viewers will read ‘no reviews’ as neutral rather than bad, like Sherlock Holmes' dog that did not bark—most people won't reverse-engineer ‘why isn't anyone reviewing this?’. Because of this reasoning gap, cold-open films outperform their true quality by roughly 15% at the box office.

40:19

Neuroeconomics began with a silent room of economists

In 1997 Camerer and George Loewenstein organized a conference in Pittsburgh bringing together economists and neuroscientists to explore whether economic questions could be studied with neuroscience methods. Most economists stayed silent throughout—in the field, admitting ignorance carries low status. Notably, Peter Diamond, Al Roth, and Paul Romer were all in that room and later won the Nobel Prize, yet except for Romer's one paper on emotion, virtually none pursued this direction. Neuroeconomics actually advanced only when Caltech acquired an unused brain scanner and a few professors were willing to bet their tenure on it.

— Colin Camerer
52:11

People who think one step deeper activate stronger mentalizing circuits

In the guessing game, players who reason forward—choosing 22 instead of 33—show stronger activity in brain regions responsible for ‘mentalizing,’ understanding others' thoughts: the right temporoparietal junction and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex. The test works by showing subjects the same photograph twice, once asking ‘what is this person doing’ and once asking ‘why is this person doing that’; the activation difference in the second version indexes mentalizing. Camerer acknowledges research here is sparse, but existing studies point the same direction: strategic depth and mentalizing circuitry use are linked.

— Colin Camerer
1:00:29

Gym check-in intervals form a nearly perfect power law

Analyzing gym attendance data from roughly 20,000 people over 1,500 days, Camerer plotted visit intervals—many short gaps, many long gaps, no bell curve—and on a log-log graph it traces almost a perfect line (R²=0.97), a ‘burstiness’ that should be extremely rare in social science data. He later discovered mathematicians proved in 1954 that non-FIFO queuing, where ‘priority queue’ admits some people ahead of order, produces power-law distributions; such results appear mostly in physics journals. Their ‘Neural Autopilot’ model—habit-driven decision-making tracking only two values, the last choice and reward reliability—reproduces this effect with a slope of roughly -2.

— Colin Camerer
1:20:23

Light can make rat brains misread foul tastes as rewards

The brain contains neurons encoding ‘reward prediction error’, discovered in 1997 by Schultz, Dayan, and Montague in rodents. Using optogenetics, Camerer's team amplified these neurons when a rat pressed a lever and drank salt water it hated, creating artificial ‘this was good’ signals in the brain—the rat kept pressing despite its facial expressions and behavior showing it disliked the taste. Camerer calls this ‘neural gaslighting’: the sensory system knows the truth, but the hijacked reward signal drowns it out.

— Colin Camerer

Glossary

mentalizing
Inferring others' mental states; the brain process underlying theory of mind.
level-k reasoning (cognitive hierarchy model)
Stepwise strategic thinking where players reason only a finite number of moves deep.
framing effect
Identical information triggers different judgments when presented differently.
burstiness (Poisson non-homogeneity)
Events cluster unevenly with intervals following a power-law distribution, not random or uniform.
Neural Autopilot model
Habit-based decision-making storing only two values—the last choice and reward reliability.
neural gaslighting
Artificially amplifying reward neurons to override sensory judgment of actual experience.
