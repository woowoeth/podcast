Southeast Asia's Governing Capacity Is Mostly a Byproduct of Massacres and Wars

When investing in Southeast Asia, first ask whether the regime can push its orders down to the grassroots: Indonesia's capacity came from a post-massacre purge, Vietnam's from over a decade of war — it has nothing to do with GDP growth.

21:27

Indonesia's national language is not the biggest ethnic group's language

Javanese is the most widely spoken language in Indonesia, at 22%, but it is used only on Java; Malay, at just over 10%, was the language of trading communities, the middle class and the wealthy merchant class, and was spread widely. Huang Yuxiang says making Javanese the national language would have had serious political consequences: it would be "Greater Javanism," with the other islands reduced to extensions of Java and forced to learn the language, and 22% is not an overwhelming share — over the long run it could have turned into civil war. The second reason is even neater: Sukarno at the time had "Greater Indonesia" ambitions, wanting to merge the regions where Malay peoples lived into one country, and Malay happened to be the lingua franca of Malaya. Indonesian and Malay are the same language, differing only the way Mandarin differs from a Shandong dialect.

— Huang Yuxiang
26:31

One massacre produced a nationwide control apparatus

After Suharto came to power he wiped out all socialist forces. Huang Yuxiang first corrects a widely circulated claim: those killed were mainly not Chinese, but socialists and communists; Chinese were swept up because they were associated with socialism, otherwise there would not have been an even larger anti-Chinese pogrom in 1998. His figures are 200,000 to 300,000 direct deaths, followed by roughly three million people processed. From a humanitarian standpoint this was an extremely bad disaster, but he points to one objective consequence: when you kill people you still have to deal with their families, so control had to reach into the villages, and the Indonesian central government used this process to extend its tentacles into every corner, building a nationwide control apparatus that had not existed in the colonial period — the colonizers had occupied only a few ports, leaving the interior to indirect rule by chiefs and aristocrats.

— Huang Yuxiang
50:50

Malaysia's Chinese can't escape the race issue

At independence the Chinese and Malay populations were roughly 40 to 40; today it is about 20 to 60, with the Chinese a minority. Every election has to be fought under an anti-racism banner, yet the power to set the agenda rests with the majority ethnic group. A Malay film about resistance to the British, "Mat Kilau: Kebangkitan Pahlawan," illustrates the rift best: Chinese found it crude, Malay-centric, and portraying Chinese as collaborators with the British, while Malays watched it with their blood boiling — it ended up the highest-grossing local film in Malaysian box office history and was also a big hit in Indonesia. Malaysia is also the only place outside China that has stuck with Chinese-language education, with the "Chinese independent high schools" that do not sit the national unified exams. Laws under Mahathir also required that even if a company was dominated by Chinese capital, it had to appoint a Malay director or form a joint venture.

— Huang Yuxiang
1:00:55

Philippine democratization bought family politics

At its founding the Philippines was one of Asia's most developed countries; the Marcos-era martial law and the later "People Power Revolution" are like the two ends of a pendulum. The People Power Revolution toppled the dictator, at the cost of dismantling the country's originally strong control apparatus, and politics became dependence on big families: the Cojuangco/Aquino family and the Marcos family took turns in power. Duterte was originally a henchman of the Cojuangco/Aquino family, and only got the chance to break free after the patriarchs of both families died one after another. During his term he survived three assassination attempts; his power was not as solid as imagined. One poll showed 90% of Filipinos supported the drug war, while 90% also feared being killed by mistake — supporting it, yet afraid the other side would run amok.

— Huang Yuxiang
1:22:13

Thailand is ruled by the king, governed by the military

To Thailand's west was British Burma, to its east French Indochina; it was a buffer both sides deliberately left in place, so the country had relatively more autonomy and no decolonization crisis; the price was that it never built a strong central government — in Huang Yuxiang's phrase, "orders from Bangkok don't leave Bangkok." Since the constitutional monarchy, Thailand has had more than twenty coups, promulgated more than twenty constitutions, and held more than twenty democratic elections, with a major upheaval every few years. He uses "monarchy network" to explain royal power: the king does not exist on the strength of the royal family alone, but must form an alliance with the military and the monks. Bhumibol reigned for seventy years and died in his nineties, building this system on personal prestige; by the time of his son Vajiralongkorn, who has long lived in Germany and lacks prestige, the old division of labor — "the king plays the good cop, the military plays the bad cop" — leaves only the bad cop.

— Huang Yuxiang
1:33:18

Investing in Southeast Asia starts with whether the regime is stable

Huang Yuxiang's judgment is direct: the more stable the country, the more at ease investors are, because what investors want is predictable; politics with rules is easy to invest in, while long-term chaos is not. The second variable is "who has the final say" — if the central government has the final say, you only have to deal with one counterpart; if every level is different, paying money and buying people off is too much trouble. He gives a concrete difference: Malaysia's problem is that "they take the money and still don't do the job," whereas Chinese take the money and do the job, so although you have to pay, that is just part of the transaction cost and can still be handled. By this standard, Vietnam and Indonesia are both good places to invest; after Indonesia's 1998 democratization local power increased, but the central government's control tentacles can still effectively cover the whole country.

— Huang Yuxiang
1:39:25

Singapore killed the dialects first, then chose English

Ethnic Chinese make up about 75% of Singapore, the other 25% being Malays and Tamils, with four official languages. What Lee Kuan Yew tackled first was not a common language but the Chinese dialect problem: he found that his own speeches had to be given simultaneously in Malay, English, Mandarin, Hokkien and Hakka, and there was no way to communicate outside, so he banned dialects from public and even private broadcasting, and Hokkien essentially disappeared from the public sphere — the process took more than twenty years. Only the second step was the common language. If Mandarin had been chosen, the remaining 25% would have felt discriminated against; in his memoirs he cites the example of Sri Lanka — a majority ethnic group of 75% to 80% made its own language the national language, and a large-scale rebellion broke out — so English was chosen as the compromise everyone could barely accept.

— Huang Yuxiang
1:45:30

Singapore has eleven founding fathers

Huang Yuxiang is currently researching the founding fathers of various countries. Singapore's officialdom has never said who the founding father is, but the widely accepted formulation is "founding fathers of Singapore," eleven in all, including Malays, whites, Chinese and Tamils; Lee Kuan Yew is only the most prominent among them — in English, first among equals. He stresses that the list of founding fathers must be consistent with the state and its ideology, and where it is not, titles have to be added: Malaysia, besides founding father Tunku Abdul Rahman, also has to count Mahathir as "father of modernization." Another common threshold is anti-colonialism — Aung San was willing to cooperate with Japan for this, which may not be acceptable to Chinese, but locals see it as a necessary compromise for independence.

— Huang Yuxiang

Glossary

monarchy network
The alliance of Thailand's king, military and monks, on which royal power depends to actually function.
indirect rule
Colonizers occupied only a few ports, leaving the interior to local chiefs and aristocrats to govern autonomously and pay tribute periodically.
First Among Equals
Lee Kuan Yew's position among Singapore's eleven founding fathers: prominent, but only one of them.
People Power Revolution
The Philippine revolution that toppled the Marcos dictatorship, the first dictator overthrown in Southeast Asia.
