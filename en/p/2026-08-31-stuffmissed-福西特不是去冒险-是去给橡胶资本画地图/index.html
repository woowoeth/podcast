An Explorer Died for a Fictional City, Rooted in a Political Forgery

Percy Fawcett's Amazon expeditions are often told as adventure legends, but the real starting point was border disputes fueled by the rubber trade; his narrative of 'respect' for indigenous peoples is a gross oversimplification, and the City of Z that obsessed him may rest on a forged manuscript.

3:04

His obsession began with a childhood Roman well

Fawcett was born in 1867. His family was nominally aristocratic, but his father, Edward Fawcett, had squandered the fortune and could only serve as a private assistant to Queen Victoria's son 'Bertie' (later Edward VII). Fawcett described a Torquay childhood lacking parental affection, which made him introverted. His sister recalled that as a child he discovered a well full of Roman pottery on Halden Moors and had his siblings lower him down to retrieve the artifacts and take them to a museum. This boldness, stubbornness, and outward-seeking personality later resurfaced in his fixation on the occult and the Amazon.

8:13

An officer who hosted séances still rose to major

In 1901 Fawcett married Nina, and in 1903 their first son Jack was born; that same year he was posted to Spike Island off the south coast of Ireland. Fawcett found the posting dull, so he spent his spare time hosting séances. His interest in the occult was not a fringe hobby; his son Brian later wrote that anyone who sought knowledge beyond the material might be called a mystic. This interest did not hinder his military career—he was promoted to major. This shows that Fawcett's 'rationality' and 'superstition' coexisted, foreshadowing his later credulity toward Manuscript 512.

10:19

His first Amazon trip was a diplomatic mission, not adventure

In 1901 Fawcett joined the Royal Geographical Society and completed specialized training in boundary demarcation. In 1906 the Society's president, Sir George Goldie, summoned him and asked about his knowledge of Bolivia, then told him: border disputes among Brazil, Peru, and Bolivia had escalated because of the rubber trade, and the countries involved would not accept demarcation by any interested party, so the Bolivian government asked the Royal Geographical Society to arbitrate and recommend an officer. For Fawcett, this was a chance to 'escape the monotony of an artillery officer's life'; he was 39, and his wife was pregnant with their second child. The chain of argument here is: rubber's economic value → territorial disputes → Britain as a 'disinterested' third party → Fawcett enters the Amazon as a surveyor. The counterintuitive point is that his initial expedition was not self-directed adventure but commercial and diplomatic arrangement.

14:25

He faced attacks everywhere because 90% of natives were already dead

Before he reached South America, the rubber industry had already transformed the region. Figures cited in the episode: before Europeans arrived, the area that is now Brazil had at least 2,000 indigenous tribes with a total population of about 11 million; in the first hundred years after colonists arrived in the 16th century, 90% of indigenous people died from disease or genocide, and survivors were often enslaved for rubber work. The rubber trade made land extremely valuable, and Europeans moved in and seized indigenous lands, leading natives to view all Europeans as threats—this is the backdrop to Fawcett's encounters with hostility. His own accounts also show he was well aware of European colonial atrocities. This section provides a structural explanation for 'why he was attacked everywhere' and corrects the romanticized narrative of 'explorer in peril.'

17:28

Calling him respectful of natives sets the bar too low

Fawcett's dealings with indigenous people were not solely confrontational. He would require himself and his companions to lay down arms and signal they were not a threat, he learned local languages, and he condemned the earlier European colonists for creating the problems that continued to sour relations. But at the same time, he wrote a great deal that was 'extremely racist' and condescending. The hosts explicitly say that later accounts often describe him as 'respectful of indigenous peoples,' which is an oversimplification; even if he was 'often better' than contemporary European explorers, that bar is shockingly low. The value here: many popular narratives today cast Fawcett as an enlightened explorer, and the episode uses concrete written evidence to dismantle that myth without simplifying his behavior into pure villainy.

19:28

He survived an ambush with an accordion, not a gun

This is one of Fawcett's most famous escape stories. His expedition was ambushed on a river by indigenous archers, and peaceful calls went unanswered. He had his companion Todd sit on a log in the middle of a sandbar with an accordion and start playing, while the others sang loudly and stomped their feet. Before long, the hail of arrows stopped, and a wide-eyed native peered out from the bushes; the expedition was then welcomed into the village and given help. This example shows Fawcett was not only about force; his problem-solving tools were music and clownish performance. It also shows his records contain both observations worth taking seriously and plenty of unreliable exaggeration, setting up contrasts with later descriptions of giant anacondas, peanuts, and double-nosed dogs.

25:32

He believed in a lost city because old documents really described one

Before World War I, Fawcett efficiently completed map surveys (often much faster than expected) while becoming increasingly fascinated by claims of lost cities in the jungle. He named the city he sought 'Zed' and located it in Mato Grosso, a non-coastal state in southern Brazil; it covers about 900,000 square kilometers, is densely forested, and remains one of Brazil's least densely populated areas—in the early 20th century, almost no Europeans had entered it. The chain of argument: many 16th- and 17th-century Spanish and Portuguese explorer documents described 'very advanced large cities' → Fawcett believed they were real → he became increasingly obsessed with finding the City of Z.

29:40

The manuscript he believed without reservation may be a hoax

After the war, Fawcett continued studying old documents, most often citing Manuscript 512, held in the National Library of Rio de Janeiro: a Portuguese adventurer's 1753 account of his travels in the Amazon, sometimes attributed to João de Silvestre de Moraes. Fawcett gave the author the pseudonym Francisco Raposo and wrote that he believed the manuscript's contents 'without reservation,' calling it an 'incomparable story.' However, at the start of the episode, the hosts had already warned that this document is 'very likely a hoax.' So the real suspense is not whether the lost city existed, but why a militarily trained surveyor would become so captivated by a possibly forged 1753 record.

Glossary

Manuscript 512
A 1753 Portuguese manuscript held in the National Library of Rio de Janeiro, describing an Amazon expedition; possibly a forgery.
Royal Geographical Society
A learned society in London that, in 1906, was commissioned by Bolivia to select Fawcett for boundary survey work in South America.
rubber boom
The late 19th to early 20th century surge in South American rubber exports, which drove land grabbing and indigenous enslavement.
Mato Grosso
A non-coastal state in southern Brazil, covering about 900,000 square kilometers; its name means 'big forest.'
City of Z
The lost city Fawcett believed existed in Mato Grosso; he disappeared while searching for it.
