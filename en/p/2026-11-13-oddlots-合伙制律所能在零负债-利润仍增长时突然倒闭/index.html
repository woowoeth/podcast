Partnership law firms can collapse even with zero debt and rising profits

A 95-year-old Wall Street law firm with no major debt and rising profits collapsed within months like a bank run because its growth lagged competitors, proving partnerships fail from relative underperformance, not absolute financial decline.

Partnership law firms persist for historical and regulatory reasons, not efficiency

Partnership structures everywhere require partner ownership for three reasons. First, historically, starting a law practice required only labor, not capital. Second, state bar ethics rules mandate that lawyers own their firms. But the deeper explanation comes from Yale economist Henry Hansman: lawyers' output (billable hours traced to specific clients) is measurable in ways factory workers' output isn't, so partnerships create fewer internal profit-allocation disputes than worker ownership models. This measurability advantage is why Hansman's theory predicts partnerships over employee ownership schemes like at General Motors.

A key partner's departure triggers the run because costs don't fall with revenue

When a star partner leaves, clients follow almost immediately—revenue vanishes overnight. But office leases, pension obligations, and the salaries of junior partners who depended on that partner's client base don't shrink. If that partner was generating more revenue than his own compensation, his departure is pure loss. The profit pool that remaining partners split shrinks directly, forcing compensation cuts—and that's where the run begins.

— John Morley
14:17

Partner distributions become clawable at bankruptcy once the firm goes insolvent

Partners don't receive wages; they receive discretionary profit distributions, legally equivalent to dividends. Once a firm is declared insolvent, any further distributions to partners can be clawed back by creditors under fraudulent transfer law. This creates a calculation every partner silently runs: money in my pocket now might have to go back later.

— John Morley
18:23

Each partner constantly weighs leaving by expected value of compensation

Take a partner at Weil in September expecting $2 million in January. But if the firm has a 50% chance of collapsing by February, that $2 million is really worth only $1 million in expectation. If Gibson Dunn offers $1.5 million with lower failure risk, that's actually higher expected value—appearing to be a pay cut while genuinely being a raise. Every partner is quietly solving this problem. Once enough partners get the same answer, the run begins.

— John Morley
21:25

Falling in the rankings is more fatal than falling profits

Economist Morley's papers predicted exactly this scenario: a firm could collapse despite zero major debt and continued profit growth, provided growth lagged competitors. Weil's per-partner profit ranking fell from #9 in 2020 to #20 in 2025, while profits kept rising every year—just not as fast as Kirkland's. Weil got stuck: too small to be a megaplatform, too big to be a boutique like Wachtell or Cravath.

— John Morley
28:31

Partners' equity vanishes at retirement, so nobody invests for the future

A Microsoft shareholder can hold forever; future value gets priced into trading. A law firm partner's equity expires at retirement—no buyback, no perpetuity. This creates generational conflict: a 31-year-old partner might invest in the firm's future; a 61-year-old retiring in two years has zero incentive. This explains why law firms won't spend heavily to build AI systems that pay off in five years—because today's decision-makers won't be there to enjoy the returns.

— John Morley
36:42

Investor-owned law firms survive runs because partners aren't owners

Australian law firm Slater & Gordon is listed and investor-owned. Its Quindel acquisition turned out to be fraud, leaving the company insolvent. But because lawyers don't own the firm, management could wipe out shareholder equity and hand creditors the company as new shareholders in a restructuring—while lawyers kept their salaries and jobs, like Delta Airlines pilots. At a partnership like Weil, if you tried the same move, every partner walks to Cravath the next morning.

— John Morley
47:54

Law firms will lease AI like office space, not build it

Generational conflict means today's partners won't fund AI that pays off in five years, since different partners will be there then. Firms will license and rent AI technology like they lease offices—not own it like they'd buy a building. Kirkland & Ellis' $500 million AI program over five years looks ambitious until you note their ~$10 billion annual revenue—it's a rounding error, and they've paced it to sync with revenue growth, not to get ahead of it.

Glossary

eat what you kill
Partner compensation directly tied to client revenue they bring in
lockstep compensation
Equal partner compensation based on seniority, regardless of individual client revenue
fraudulent transfer liability
Creditors' right to claw back partner distributions made after a firm becomes insolvent
MSO (managed service organization)
Entity separate from a law firm that holds non-legal business assets and can accept outside investment
generational conflict
Retiring partners' equity expiring with no value removes incentive for current partners to invest for the future
