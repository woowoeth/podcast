The widowed magazine editor who pushed Thanksgiving to become a national holiday

Over three decades, Sarah Josepha Hale lobbied governors, congressmen, and presidents by letter, hoping a nationwide Thanksgiving would prevent civil war. The holiday took hold, but after her death, her narrative of unity was replaced by a different myth: Native American and Puritan friendship.

The 1621 meal was set in extreme power imbalance

The episode recounts the 1621 gathering between English colonists and the Wampanoag people, emphasizing that it was no simple friendship story. The English were few and struggling to survive; the Wampanoag outnumbered them at least twenty to one and held real power in the encounter. Historian David Silverman stresses that reducing this meeting to a story of 'gratitude' strips it from its actual historical context.

— David Silverman
4:20

Fifty years later, friendship turned to war

In 1675, Wampanoag chief Metacom appealed to Rhode Island's attorney general, recounting how his father had given land to the English and taught them farming and fishing. Now, the English had reversed positions but no longer respected the Wampanoag, prompting war. The conflict lasted nearly three years; the English won. Thousands of Wampanoag were killed or enslaved to the Caribbean and Mediterranean. Metacom was beheaded, his head displayed for twenty years at Plymouth Colony—the same place where the 1621 meal occurred.

13:44

She raised five children alone by writing after her husband's sudden death

Sarah married lawyer David Hale at twenty-five. After eight years of happy marriage, he died suddenly, leaving her with four children and one on the way, nearly destitute. Convention dictated that a widow without a husband should place her children with relatives, but she refused. Instead, she earned her living writing poetry, essays, and novels. Her anti-slavery novel Northwood established her reputation in Boston intellectual circles and led to an invitation to edit a women's magazine.

20:04

She saw Thanksgiving as a tool to build one American culture

Sarah believed the American Revolution had unified the colonies politically, but not culturally. She hoped that all Americans celebrating Thanksgiving on the same day would foster a sense of belonging to 'one big family.' She publicly declared that if Thanksgiving became fixed, it would become a force promoting the 'national spirit' that could prevent civil war—transforming the holiday from a family gathering into a political project.

— Sarah Josepha Hale
30:24

Southern leaders saw Thanksgiving as a Trojan horse for abolition

In 1856, Virginia Governor Henry Wise replied to Sarah, calling Thanksgiving a 'damned Yankee holiday' because Northern ministers had turned Thanksgiving sermons into abolitionist propaganda. The episode suggests Wise's judgment wasn't entirely wrong—the anti-slavery movement was indeed intertwined with New England religious life at the time. Virginia did not celebrate Thanksgiving that year.

33:34

Lincoln proclaimed Thanksgiving a national holiday five days after Gettysburg

In 1863, following the Battle of Gettysburg, Sarah wrote to Lincoln again. This time, Lincoln responded within five days, issuing a proclamation that made Thanksgiving a national holiday, calling on all Americans to celebrate ‘with one heart and one voice.’ Sarah's decades of lobbying had finally borne fruit—but the holiday was established not as a simple harvest celebration, but as a tool for wartime national unity.

41:02

The 'first Thanksgiving' legend began with a footnote

In 1836, when William Apes publicly critiqued colonial history, he never mentioned Thanksgiving—because most Americans hadn't yet imagined Native Americans and Puritans sharing a meal. That vision came later: in the 1840s, a New England minister publishing original documents added a footnote stating ‘this was the first Thanksgiving.’ The legend grew from that single footnote. But it only truly took hold in American society after Lincoln's 1863 proclamation.

44:05

A 1912 painting embedded civilization versus savagery into American textbooks

Painter Jean-Leon Jerome Ferris completed an oil painting titled ‘The First Thanksgiving’ in 1912. White colonists sit at the table while Native Americans sit on the ground, dressed in artist-imagined feathers and beads. Historian David Silverman points out that this composition was deliberately designed to reinforce an 'English civilization versus Native American savagery' binary—a framework that had shaped white Americans' understanding of themselves and Native peoples for four centuries. By the early twentieth century, this became the standard template for school Thanksgiving pageants.

— David Silverman
