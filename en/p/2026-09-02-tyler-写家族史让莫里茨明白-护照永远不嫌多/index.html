Writing family history taught Moritz: you can never have too many passports

He didn't apply for German citizenship because he feared being expelled from America — he learned from writing his parents' story of escape that the history of the 1930s was only truly burned into a nation's memory in Germany, and a passport is the only insurance you can carry with you.

Only ugly paintings are worth hanging on the wall

Moritz and his wife collect "ugly paintings" — Otto Dix, Frank Auerbach and the like. His reasoning: a decorative painting is exhausted at a glance, whereas a difficult painting has many layers you can keep exploring as you age; it carries history and tells stories. He traces this habit of "looking carefully" back to his reporting career — looking at landscapes, buildings, people, all the same thing. But he explicitly denies that this directly improves his investing; he only says the three are connected when he sizes up a person and considers building a business relationship.

13:00

Children don't ask because they don't think their parents have stories

Asked why children don't ask their parents more about family history while they're alive, Moritz is blunt: they're too self-centred, not curious, they don't understand that everyone has a story and that the deeper you dig the more interesting it gets; they don't know how to ask questions, and they don't know how to listen. He says if he could question his parents now, it wouldn't be one question but hundreds — because one answer leads to the next. But he adds a layer: these were people who lived through extreme trauma, memory gets suppressed, and even with the will, it's hard to dig the answers out.

— Michael Moritz
19:16

He starts his questions at his grandparents' age ten

Moritz says he would start with what his grandparents' lives were like around age ten, because he believes the first ten to fifteen years of a life are shaped profoundly, and everything afterwards echoes off that base. The concrete picture he gives: imagine a ten-year-old boy in 1890 or 1891, living in a small village by a river in central Germany — who woke you up in the morning? Did anyone share your room, your bed? Do you remember what you ate for breakfast? What clothes did you wear to school? His grandfather Max fought in World War I and survived Verdun, and that stretch alone could yield countless questions.

— Michael Moritz
23:37

He got into Sequoia by writing a letter; four firms turned him down

Moritz says his move from journalism to venture capital was entirely unplanned, pure luck. When he was in San Francisco in 1981 and 1982 he knew nothing about Silicon Valley; there were almost no tech companies in San Francisco then, unless you counted Charles Schwab. It was covering Apple, Atari, Microsoft and Oracle that made him realise for the first time that people started companies, and that founders could be just 18 or 20. He couldn't start one himself, so he joined a small publishing company. Later he wrote letters to the heads of five venture firms — in that era you could only write letters, not send email — four rejected him, and Don Valentine brought him into Sequoia. He says Valentine's peers thought he was insane for hiring someone with a history degree, a reporting career and no technical background at all.

30:53

You can never have too many passports

Moritz has obtained German citizenship. He rules out several guesses himself: he isn't afraid of being expelled from the United States, he already has British citizenship; he doesn't think he'd be threatened or intimidated in San Francisco; and he doesn't plan to move to Munich. The real reason he applied came from an observation while writing the book — whether in Western Europe or the United States, the only country that truly drummed the history of the 20s, the 30s and the Holocaust into its entire population was Germany; not France, not England and Wales, not the United States. He sums it up in one line: you can never have enough passports.

— Michael Moritz
38:43

Britain's problem is imperial afterglow plus too much government

Moritz explains Britain's predicament as "the residue of imperial arrogance": the pound was once the world's reserve currency, Britain had the world's largest navy and was the global hegemon, then that position was taken by a more entrepreneurial country, and Britain never recovered from the loss — everything since has been a weak, low-grade stress response. He concedes Britain went through a cycle where government was the answer, and through Thatcher and later Blair, Brown and Cameron, when government was not seen as the answer, but his conclusion is that Britain is broadly trapped by a government that does too much.

— Michael Moritz
43:34

In China he saw where capitalism burned hottest

Moritz attributes Sequoia's nearly two decades of success in China to collective decisions, but gives his own path of observation: going to the mainland in person, having many preconceptions washed away, then quickly realising that if you wanted to see where capitalism burned hottest in the early 21st century, it wasn't in America but in China — despite everyone talking about the Communist Party and state control. The mechanism he offers: there was almost no social safety net, so people had only education or their wits to live by. He also concedes that the last few years have tightened a great deal, and the atmosphere and conditions are no longer the same.

— Michael Moritz
