The UK productivity crisis was a statistical error: 0.7% became 1.3%

Fifteen years of Britain being cast as the worst performer on productivity among developed economies may have been an ONS miscalculation of hours worked — counting people on holiday as working 40 hours inflated the denominator and pushed productivity down.

1:05

The UK productivity crisis was calculated into existence

After the financial crisis, UK productivity growth was estimated at 0.7% a year, the worst in the developed world, and it became the raw material for fifteen years of the "productivity puzzle." After the ONS changed how it measures hours worked, that figure became 1.3%, nearly double. Output and GDP did not change; only the denominator did: Britons actually work far fewer hours than previously thought. Chris Giles says pre-crisis growth was 2%, and 2% means productivity doubles roughly every 35 years, while 0.7% means it takes a century to double; 1.3% falls in between. Britain is no longer an international outlier, but growth is still below the pre-crisis rate.

— Chris Giles
4:16

You cannot trust asking people how many hours they work

The old method simply asked respondents how many hours they worked in a week and took the answer at face value. The problem came at follow-up: if no one answered the door in August, the ONS carried over the 40 hours that person had reported three months earlier and plugged in a 40 — when that person was probably on holiday and had not worked a single hour. The OECD recommended back in 2018 that countries stop doing this; the UK took eight years to change. Giles says that over those eight years people wrote a batch of articles about the "UK productivity crisis" that no longer hold up. He admits this is maddening, but the change is in the right direction.

— Chris Giles
11:38

One in ten Irish people as clergy is a misreading

The book says that in the 1960s one in every ten Irish children became a nun, priest or Christian Brother. The 1961 census shows 18,854 Catholic clergy and nuns, 2,125 Christian Brothers and other religious brothers, and 4,842 seminarians, for a total of 25,821, or 1/104 of the population. The 1951 ratio was 1/127. The original source is research by Mary Daly, who looked only at students sitting the leaving certificate exam in 1966, when less than 20% of the school-age population took that exam — about 12,000 people, roughly one in ten of whom entered religious life. That qualifier was lost in retelling and became "all children." The author has said subsequent editions will be corrected.

14:41

Grid queue numbers are inflated by applications

Between November 2024 and June 2025, cumulative electricity demand in the UK grid connection queue jumped from about 41 GW to 125 GW, tripling in six months, with data centres accounting for 73 GW. But queuing is cheap, like booking a table at a popular restaurant months ahead; many projects will never actually be built or will be scaled down. Ofgem estimates that if all this data centre demand materialised, the cost would be close to £700 billion, nearly a quarter of UK annual GDP. A 2025 government report projected 6 GW would be needed by 2030, while another queue analysis reckons about 20 GW has serious financial commitments behind it. Johnny Gowdy of Regen says nobody knows how much will ultimately be built.

— Nathan Gower
17:45

The data centre emissions comparison counts only direct emissions

Foxglove's analysis says two planned data centres at full load would emit more carbon than ExxonMobil does in the UK. That comparison counts only ExxonMobil's own operational emissions, excluding the petrol and diesel it sells — which is the main source of emissions, something an ordinary reader may not spot. Foxglove also assumes the data centres run at 100% power year-round, while a scholar studying AI's carbon footprint uses 65%. More important is the power source: the planning documents for these two projects say that initially, because they cannot connect to the grid, they will use on-site gas generation. The UK grid as a whole gets about half its power from renewables and only 30% from gas, so these data centres amount to stimulating demand for higher-carbon generation.

— Nathan Gower
23:00

Vinyl's physical limits actually weakened

The 1950s story goes: the closer a record gets to the centre, the lower the linear speed, and the less bass and brightness a groove can hold, so you put the quiet song at the end of a side. Mitchell Sterling's database has 2,284 albums and 27,000 songs, spanning 1953 to the present. Measured by relative loudness, before 1982 closing tracks averaged about 0.65 decibels quieter than earlier tracks; after 1982, when cassette sales overtook vinyl in the US and the constraint disappeared, the gap actually widened to about 1.5 decibels. In the streaming era after 2010, the gap is still twice what it was in the vinyl era. In other words, the less the physical constraint mattered, the quieter closing tracks became.

— Mitchell Sterling
26:02

The quiet ending went from constraint to artistic device

Sterling defines a quiet, acoustic closing track as the "typical quiet acoustic farewell." In the 1950s more than 60% of albums ended with a quiet, acoustic song; as recordings overall got louder, that absolute share slid all the way down to under one-sixth in the 1980s; by the 2000s and 2010s it had climbed back to about a third. Sterling's analogy is the save icon on a floppy disk: the constraint is long gone, the habit remains. He also found in streaming data that an album's last track averages longer, more acoustic and sadder — but that is an average, and hip-hop, heavy metal and electronic music are full of loud exceptions.

— Mitchell Sterling

Glossary

ONS
The UK's Office for National Statistics, which produces official figures on GDP, hours worked and productivity.
productivity puzzle
The UK's unusually weak post-financial-crisis productivity growth, which long defied explanation.
leaving certificate
The Irish secondary school leaving exam, taken by only a minority of students in the 1960s.
loudness wars
The CD-era phenomenon of producers competing to push master volumes ever higher.
