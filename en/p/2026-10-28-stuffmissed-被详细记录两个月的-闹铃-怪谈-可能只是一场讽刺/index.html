Two Months of Documented Bell-Ringing—Possibly Just Satire

A retired officer meticulously documented his household bells ringing uncontrollably for two months, yet repeatedly wavered between "convinced it was not human" and "possibly a hoax"—later scholars believe this contradiction reveals the book was actually gentle satire of credulous spiritualists.

A Poltergeist Case That Reads More Like an Elaborate Prank

On February 2, 1834, servant bells in Bealings House, Suffolk, England began ringing on their own—later becoming cited by paranormal believers as a 'well-documented poltergeist case.' But the hosts open by establishing the tone: this is not a terrifying ghost story, more like a ring-and-run prank with comedic overtones, though the question of whether it really happened has never been settled in nearly two centuries.

The Bells Escalated to Violently Simultaneous Ringing That Defied Explanation

Moore describes nine kitchen bells in a row, spaced about one foot apart, ten feet off the ground and twelve feet from the fire—he deliberately emphasizes these distances to rule out the possibility that heat from the fire caused the bell wires to move. He personally witnessed five bells shake violently at the same time, so intensely that he feared the bells would shake loose from their fixtures, and his son Edward James was present to witness the whole episode.

A Reader's Letter Outlined Exactly How to Catch the Prankster

A reader signing himself "constant reader" offered Moore a solution: the next time the bells ring, lock everyone in the family in one room, secure all outside doors with keys removed, post watchers at every staircase, and conduct a thorough search of the house. He also mentioned that he himself can make multiple bells ring in seconds by pulling on the wires, and gave examples of another household that used the same method to discover the bells were being rung by a newly hired servant.

17:40

Moore's Own Experiments Couldn't Replicate What He Witnessed

Moore initially thought black birds fighting in a pear tree on the west wall might have disturbed the bell wires, but after witnessing the violent ringing himself, he concluded birds could not possibly produce such an effect. Using rope attached to a crank, he could only make the bells ring softly; using a hoe to pull multiple wires at once, he could make at most six bells ring, but the force fell far short of what he experienced—he admitted that even if he experimented for a whole year, he could not replicate that ringing.

— Edward Moore
19:42

Moore Offered an Explanation So Incoherent It Explains Nothing

Moore attempted to provide a scientific explanation, proposing it might be some undiscovered law of electricity or galvanism activated by the combined effect of specific metal alloys and atmospheric conditions—but the passage is syntactically mangled with almost no main verbs. The hosts compare it to the rambling nonsense of Jim Ignatowski the taxi driver, and in the end it explains nothing.

— Holly Frey
27:58

A Paranormal Researcher Systematically Dismantles Moore's Testimony

In 1899, paranormal researcher Frank Podmore published an article pointing out that Moore's testimony contradicted itself: on February 5 he wrote he was 'convinced it was not human,' on February 27 he said 'it could also be a hoax,' and days later switched back to 'convinced it was not of common man'—without ever explaining what changed his mind. More critically, Moore never specified who was actually in the house, never described what happened when the whole family was present at the same time, and explicitly refused the reader's suggested method of locking the doors to investigate.

— Frank Podmore
30:02

Skeptics Still Disagree About What Actually Happened

Based on this, Podmore argued that Moore was actually using the book to gently satirize people who believed in paranormal evidence too easily. But later researcher Trevor Hall countered that Moore was emotionally too invested in the bells to be reliable, and the real explanation was that a servant was playing a prank on him; in 1972, Daniel Cohen argued that the incoherence and pseudo-scientific gibberish were intentional satirical techniques, just so subtle that even today people cite it as a real paranormal case.
