One Jolt Can Show You Your Life Clearly, but Quitting on Impulse Usually Ends in Regret

Life's turning points are often not the product of rational deliberation but of a single unexpected event that kicks a person out of autopilot; yet research shows quitting on impulse usually ends in regret, and the smartest response is sometimes to stay put.

Half of all resignations aren't made after thinking it through

When Anthony Klotz read the turnover literature in graduate school, he found that quitting is often not a slow, rational weighing process: about half of cases are triggered by a one-off event that makes a person stop and rethink their job. What surprised him more was that these studies almost all focused only on "why people quit" — nobody studied "how people fare after quitting." He had himself been kicked out of autopilot at a lumber yard and at General Mills, so he shifted his research focus to what happens after the decision to quit or stay.

— Anthony Klotz
19:44

A DNA test exposed the imbalance between work and family

On the show, Chris Hemsworth took a DNA test and discovered he carries a gene associated with Alzheimer's risk. He called it his greatest fear, because there is a history of the disease in his family; but he also said it was a blessing, because he is still young enough to make changes. The shock did not make him overturn his work entirely — it made him realise he was spending more time on work than with his family, so he began adjusting the ratio, giving more time to family and to the work he truly cares about.

— Anthony Klotz
31:09

Regret over an impulsive quit comes from the next job

Klotz says impulsive quitting is tempting because it instantly reverses the power relationship: normally the company holds the initiative, and the moment you leave, the company needs you. But research shows that unless something genuinely terrible happened, people who quit on impulse mostly regret it afterwards. The source of regret is usually not leaving the old job itself — you often have good reason — but pushing yourself into a situation where you "must find the next job immediately," so you hastily accept a new one and end up facing the same problems at the new company.

— Anthony Klotz
38:17

The smartest response is sometimes to stay put

Early in his career, Bob Iger was an executive at ABC Sports when the company was acquired by a broadcasting group known for frugality. The way of working changed abruptly, and colleagues around him jumped to glamorous sports agencies. He too considered leaving and went to HR to resign; HR told him to think it through first. He went home, discussed it with his partner, and decided to stay. He later moved with the company into Disney and eventually became CEO and chairman. Klotz says that however one judges his leadership style, from a career standpoint the smartest response to a shock is sometimes precisely to stay put.

— Anthony Klotz
41:22

You can solve the problem without quitting, if you speak up

Klotz's friend Jenny worked at a hospital and shared a large office with a toxic colleague; her physical and mental state kept deteriorating. Then one day she walked in and, before she could say anything, the other person started making trouble, and she realised she had to do something. She did not quit, and she did not report the colleague — she found an empty office and told her boss this was a personality incompatibility and asked to move there. The boss agreed. A few weeks later she found she could also redivide the tasks and barely had to deal with the old colleague anymore, and her job satisfaction rebounded sharply.

— Anthony Klotz
44:29

When you speak to your boss, the proposal can't benefit only you

Klotz offers several points for speaking upward: don't bring only problems, bring solutions; the solution must not benefit only you but also your boss, clients, colleagues or the company, and you have to sell that point clearly; don't use the "good news—bad news—good news" sandwich structure, since research shows it works poorly when raising objections; and if your boss is fairly dominant, framing the request as a question rather than a demand or suggestion raises the odds of success.

— Anthony Klotz
1:03:24

Adults don't lack inspiration — they hit the brakes too early

Arp Dijkstra-Haus cites a classic creativity study: asked "what can a paperclip do," young children score far higher than adults. He explains this as two modes of thinking — divergent thinking is the accelerator, convergent thinking is the brake. Divergent thinking is innate and very active in childhood; convergent thinking is learned, intervenes more and more frequently with age, and often intervenes too early in the creative process. What people call having fewer ideas as they get older is, in many cases, hitting the brake too early and snuffing out a thought the moment it surfaces.

— Arp Dijkstra-Haus
1:12:01

Meditation raises the share of subconscious information reaching consciousness

Dijkstra-Haus ran an experiment: first presenting an answer subliminally to subjects (for example, the name of a Beatle or the colour of the American flag), then asking a question with multiple possible answers, to see whether they would use that activated answer. The results showed that after 20 minutes of meditation, people used those answers significantly more often than chance level and than under normal conditions; without meditation they did not. He believes meditation increases the "traffic flow" between consciousness and deep unconscious regions, making subconscious information more likely to surface.

— Arp Dijkstra-Haus

Glossary

jolt
A one-off event that kicks a person out of autopilot and forces them to re-examine their work and life.
collateral jolt
When colleagues or people close to you leave, indirectly prompting you to reconsider whether you should leave too.
nuclear events
Dan McAdams's term for scenes in a life that stand out like boldface type and bring sudden transformation.
divergent thinking
Free-associative creative thinking, like an accelerator, most active in childhood.
convergent thinking
Critical thinking that evaluates feasibility, like a brake, intervening more frequently with age.
three B's
bed, bus, bath — the three everyday settings where good ideas are most likely to surface.
