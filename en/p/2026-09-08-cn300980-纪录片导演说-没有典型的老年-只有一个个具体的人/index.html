Documentary director says: there is no typical old age, only specific people

Fan Shiguang, who shot two seasons of Qianlang (前浪), says most marriages end up lukewarm or worse, and growing old together is rare; what we lack most is not stories, but money, and the willingness to see old people as specific human beings.

Five million in inheritance, and the law fights the will

An elderly person living alone in Shanghai died in a nursing home. Before death she had made a voluntary guardianship notarization with notary Li Chengyang, but had not made a will. While clearing her belongings, the notary found she had left five million in inheritance, including a house, cash and bankbooks. The old person had said many times orally that she wanted to donate it to society and did not want to give it to relatives, but this wish had no legal form. Li Chengyang ultimately chose to follow the law and look for statutory heirs, finding long-unseen relatives and children through archives. There was disagreement inside the notary office too; some colleagues thought the old person's wishes should be respected directly. The compromise was to propose to the inheriting children that 200,000 of the five million be donated to a fund helping old people with no one to rely on, and the children agreed.

Whether they slept together decides whether the inheritance can be given

An elderly man with a terminal illness gave voluntary guardianship and his inheritance to a woman who had cared for him for years and was not a relative, while he had a biological daughter. The notary office debated this fiercely, and one key point was whether the two slept together: if they did not sleep together, the caregiver was only a nanny or live-in helper, and the old man giving his inheritance to a nanny was morally acceptable; if there was a sexual relationship, the matter became improper. The caregiver herself had a marriage, but had not been in contact for years, so it did not exist in fact. So this became an open relationship question, and the notary office did not want to bear the risk of offending public morals because of such a judgment.

16:02

Old people looking for partners increasingly do not register the marriage

The protagonist of the second episode, Li Kangmei, was with Lao Li for eighteen years, from beginning to end without registering the marriage, and without a de facto marital relationship. The hosts found this situation far more common than imagined: after divorce or the loss of a first marriage, many old people choose not to marry when entering a new relationship. They do not necessarily say they love this person very much, but say they want someone to accompany them, someone to care for them, and behind that is a fairly deep need for companionship. Lao Li already had a terminal illness at the time, and Li Kangmei's choice was whether to stay until the last moment, or leave early because there was no de facto marital relationship.

— Ruohan
24:46

Reality TV forces you to take a side every five minutes

The hosts contrast documentaries with reality TV: much of the tension in reality TV is manufactured, backed by a very strong narrative — for example, knowing the audience has opinions about mama's boys, they fabricate a mama's boy in the show. This destroys the audience's acceptance of the complexity of real intimate relationships — every plot point comes out, and every five minutes you need a stance, and that stance must be the morally correct one, then projected onto some character, and no matter where he goes you side with him. Documentaries do the opposite: they show that everyone has it hard, some characters have more screen time and some less, but you can see everyone's difficulties, and many of the characters appear pixelated and anonymous.

— Qing
34:20

The older generation says I sleep badly, not I'm depressed

Medical anthropologist Arthur Kleinman found that young people today have a rich psychological vocabulary and can quickly say I'm depressed, I'm anxious, I'm a bit bipolar; but even when older people express psychological needs, they still use somatized language — I've been sleeping badly lately, I have a bit of a headache — and will not say I sleep badly because I think too much, I'm a bit depressed. The hosts explain: in our culture, it is easier to talk about physical pain; anyone can have a headache or a cold, and there is no stigma; once you talk about depression and anxiety, it is another level of stigma and pain.

— Ruohan
43:53

The killed story idea: she just wanted to play mahjong

Director Fan Shiguang told a story he killed himself: the most diligent auntie in a hospice ward, whose husband had bladder cancer and not long to live. She took the bus every day, arriving at seven or eight in the morning and leaving at five or six in the evening, like going to work, but you could not see love on her face; she just asked doctors and nurses again and again how much longer other people's family members had. Only later did people learn that her son had died of a heart attack two years earlier — a shidu family — and her tears had probably run dry two years before. Someone asked what she wanted to do after her husband died, and she said she wanted to play mahjong, because he had never let her play before. The first scene shot after the husband died was her finding workers to smash the legs of a two-meter bed one by one with a sledgehammer, and buying a mahjong table to put in that spot. This story was ultimately not used, because the team could not find her real inner world and only grasped part of the facts.

— Fan Shiguang
52:28

Growing old together is rare, not the norm

Fan Shiguang said the team did fieldwork with about fifty families, across two seasons, and found that from youth to truly growing old together and going to the end is not the norm, it is rare; most marriages are lukewarm, or even bad. He told of the first season's episode Lovers: an old man with Alzheimer's lost control and hit his wife, and the daughter forcibly sent him to a specialized nursing home. The old woman sneaked off to see him every few days, but the hospital would not let her come because it would affect the old man's mood. After the old man became seriously ill and was sent to the hospital, the old woman finally got the chance to care for him — cutting his nails, washing his hair, washing his feet. More than a month after the old man died, the old woman said through tears, I looked down on him my whole life; when I was young I studied well and he studied poorly, my family was an intellectual family and his was a worker's family, he insisted on pursuing me, and I had no way out, and in the end I muddled through a lifetime with him.

— Fan Shiguang
1:04:18

Learning to drive at 95, one sentence strips away the right

Fan Shiguang said the team worked hard to break stereotypes, because people love to divide into groups and stick labels — old people are like this, they are just confused — and there is deep prejudice in this. In the first season there was a story of a 95-year-old grandfather learning to drive and taking the driving test. He thought about it and realized he had unconsciously said, "Driving is nice, but after all he is 95," and then asked himself: in my sentence, a 95-year-old person is actually stripped of rights — that is, he does not deserve them. Public Security Ministry laws and regulations do not say a 95-year-old cannot learn to drive, but in verbal violence the right is stripped away. The show's bullet comments said a lot of things like can this old man really drive on the road, he is too selfish, what if he hits someone — all these worries are built on prejudice.

— Fan Shiguang

Glossary

Voluntary guardianship
An adult with full civil capacity designates a guardian in advance in writing, who performs the duties after the person loses capacity.
Somatization
Expressing psychological pain as physical symptoms, such as using poor sleep or headaches instead of depression or anxiety.
Shidu family
A family whose only child has died and whose parents no longer have children.
Empty-nest elderly
Old people whose children are not nearby and who live alone as a couple, common among highly educated families in big cities.
