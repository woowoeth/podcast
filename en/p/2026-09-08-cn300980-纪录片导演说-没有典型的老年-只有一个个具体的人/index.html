Documentary Director: There Is No Typical Old Age, Only Specific People

Fan Shiguang, who shot two seasons of Qianlang (前浪), says most marriages end up lukewarm or worse, and growing old together to the very end is rare as hen's teeth; what we lack most isn't stories, it's money, and the habit of seeing old people as specific human beings.

Five million in inheritance, and the law fights the deathbed wish

An elderly woman with no living relatives died in a Shanghai nursing home. While alive she had made a voluntary guardianship notarisation with notary Li Chengyang (李承阳), but she never wrote a will. Sorting through her belongings, the notary found she had left five million in assets, including a house, cash and bankbooks. She had said orally, many times, that she wanted to donate it to society and not give it to relatives, but that wish had no legal form. Li Chengyang ultimately chose to follow the law and look for statutory heirs, tracing through archives to children and relatives she had not seen in years. There was disagreement inside the notary office too; some colleagues thought the old woman's wishes should simply be respected. The compromise was to propose to the inheriting children that 200,000 of the five million go to a fund helping old people with no one to rely on. The children agreed.

11:55

Whether they slept together decides whether the inheritance can be given

An elderly man with a terminal illness gave voluntary guardianship and his estate to a woman who had cared for him for years and was not a relative — and he had a biological daughter. The notary office argued fiercely over it, and one key point was whether the two slept together: if they did not, the carer was merely a nanny or a live-in housekeeper, and an old man leaving his estate to his nanny was morally acceptable; if there was a sexual relationship, the matter stopped conforming to public morals. The carer was herself married, but had had no contact for years, so in effect the marriage did not exist. So it became an open-relationship question, and the notary office did not want to bear the risk of offending public morals on the basis of such a judgment.

16:02

Old people looking for a partner increasingly don't register the marriage

Li Kangmei (李康美), the subject of episode two, was with Lao Li for eighteen years and never registered the marriage from beginning to end, nor was there a de facto marital relationship. The hosts found this far more common than they had imagined: when old people divorce or lose a first marriage and enter a new relationship, many choose not to marry. They don't necessarily say they love the person deeply; they say they want someone to keep them company, someone to look after them, and behind that is a fairly deep need against loneliness. Lao Li already had a terminal illness at the time, and Li Kangmei's choice was whether to stay to the last moment or leave early because there was no de facto marital relationship.

— Ruohan
24:46

Reality TV forces you to pick a side every five minutes

The hosts set documentary against reality TV: much of the tension in reality TV is manufactured, backed by a very strong narrative — knowing, say, that the audience has views about mama's boys, so they fabricate a mama's boy on screen. This destroys the audience's tolerance for the complexity of real intimate relationships: every plot beat, every five minutes, demands a position, and that position must be the morally correct one, then gets projected onto some character, and wherever that character goes you side with him. Documentary works the opposite way: it shows that everyone has it hard, some characters get more screen time and some less, but you can see the difficulty of each one, and quite a few of them appear pixelated and anonymous.

— Qing
34:20

The older generation says I sleep badly, not I'm depressed

Medical anthropologist Arthur Kleinman found that young people today have a rich psychological vocabulary and can quickly say I'm depressed, I'm anxious, I'm a bit bipolar; but the older generation, even when expressing psychological needs, still uses somatised language — I've been sleeping badly lately, I have a bit of a headache — and won't say I sleep badly because I think too much, that I'm a bit depressed. The hosts explain: in our culture it is easier to talk about physical pain, everyone gets a headache or a cold, there's no stigma; the moment you talk about depression or anxiety, it's a different order of stigma and pain.

— Ruohan
43:53

The killed pitch: all she wanted was to play mahjong

Director Fan Shiguang (范世广) told a story he killed himself: the most diligent woman in a hospice ward, whose husband had bladder cancer and not long to live. She took the bus in at seven or eight every morning and left at five or six in the evening, like going to work, but you could see no love on her face — she just asked the doctors and nurses again and again how much longer other people's family members had. Only later did it emerge that her son had died of a heart attack two years earlier; she was a shidu family, and her tears had probably run dry two years before. Someone asked what she wanted to do after her husband died, and she said she wanted to play mahjong, because he had never let her play. The first scene shot after the husband died was her finding workers to smash the legs off a two-metre bed with a sledgehammer, one by one, and buying a mahjong table to put in that spot. The story was never used, because the team could not find her real inner world — they only had hold of part of the facts.

— Fan Shiguang
52:28

Growing old together is rare as hen's teeth, not the norm

Fan Shiguang said the team embedded in roughly fifty families during fieldwork, and across the two seasons found that going from youth all the way to growing old together to the very end is not the norm but rare as hen's teeth; most marriages are lukewarm, or worse. He told of the first season's episode Lovers: an old man with Alzheimer's loses control and hits his wife, the daughter forcibly sends him to a specialised nursing home, and the old woman sneaks off to visit every few days, though the hospital won't let her come because it affects his mood. After the old man is hospitalised, gravely ill, the old woman finally gets her chance to wait on him — cutting his nails, washing his hair, washing his feet. More than a month after he died, she said through tears: I looked down on him my whole life. When I was young I was a good student and he was a poor one, my family were intellectuals and his were workers, he insisted on courting me, I had no way out, and in the end I muddled through a whole life with him.

— Fan Shiguang
1:04:18

Learning to drive at 95: one sentence strips away the right

Fan Shiguang said the team worked hard to break stereotypes, because people love to sort others into groups and stick labels on them — old people are like this, they've gone senile — and there is deep prejudice in that. In season one there was the story of a 95-year-old man learning to drive and taking the licence test; he caught himself saying, offhand, "driving is all very well, but he is 95 after all", and then asked himself: in that sentence of mine, a 95-year-old has in fact been stripped of his rights — that is, he doesn't deserve them. No Ministry of Public Security law or regulation says a 95-year-old cannot learn to drive, but in our verbal violence the right was stripped away. The episode's bullet comments were full of people saying can this old man really be allowed on the road, how selfish, what if he hits someone — and all those worries rest on prejudice.

— Fan Shiguang

Glossary

Voluntary guardianship
An adult with full civil capacity designates a guardian in writing in advance, to act once they lose capacity.
Somatisation
Expressing psychological distress as physical symptoms, e.g. sleeping badly or headaches instead of depression or anxiety.
Shidu family
A family whose only child has died, leaving the parents with no children.
Empty-nest elderly
Old people whose children live elsewhere and who live alone as a couple; common among highly educated families in big cities.
