After Saudi Arabia Cast Him Out, Why Bin Laden Was Happiest in Sudan

Stripped of his citizenship, cut off by his family, and hunted by the United States, Bin Laden built his own commercial and militant network in Sudan — until Washington forced Sudan to expel him.

From war hero to a dissident under soft house arrest

When Bin Laden returned to Saudi Arabia in 1989 he was a genuine celebrity, and recordings of his lectures circulated privately among young people. After Saddam invaded Kuwait in 1990, he volunteered a memorandum to the Saudi Ministry of Defense proposing that he lead Afghan veterans to defend the kingdom, with no need for American or other "infidel" troops. The Saudi royal family was polite to his face and laughed behind his back, and turned him down. After the rejection he began publicly attacking the royal family for allowing U.S. troops to be stationed on holy soil, and aligned himself with the "Awakening Sheikhs." Saudi Arabia did not arrest him — because he was a member of the Bin Laden family — but restricted his passport and sent his older brother Bakr to talk him around. But he no longer listened to his brother.

12:37

The Sudan years were the happiest of his life

At the end of 1992 Bin Laden left Saudi Arabia under the cover of the family's construction projects in Port Sudan, bought salt flats outside Port Sudan, and then moved to Khartoum. There he had wives, children, a large house, a horse farm, and his own business office, and he began building al-Qaeda into an international revolutionary organization. He paid his members salaries, kept in touch with the outside world by fax machine, and simultaneously funded militant activity in Yemen, Libya, the Maghreb and elsewhere. Steve Coll says that across his short and turbulent life, the Sudan years may have been his happiest and most comfortable period.

— Steve Coll
18:58

He claimed more credit than he actually earned

The December 1992 hotel bombing in Aden, Yemen, targeted U.S. troops heading to Somalia, and Bin Laden did have a role in it. But in the October 1993 Black Hawk Down incident in Mogadishu, the forces that actually inflicted American casualties were the militia of local warlord Mohamed Farrah Aidid, and Bin Laden was claiming credit he did not deserve. In East Africa, however, he did build a network, and the later bombings of the U.S. embassies in Nairobi and Dar es Salaam were entirely the work of his network. It was from the embassy bombings onward that he turned from a financier into a direct commander.

— Steve Coll
24:06

He fired at both America and the Saudi royal family at once

After the first World Trade Center bombing in 1993, the investigation showed Bin Laden was a node for funding and weapons. But in the same period he was also using audio tapes to preach inside Saudi Arabia, publicly denouncing the royal family as illegitimate. Steve Coll points out that he had no strategic subtlety whatsoever — someone who wanted to strike America could have avoided offending the Saudi royal family so openly, but he did not do so. The Saudis deployed almost every tool they had: Prince Turki, the head of intelligence, the Badr brothers, his brother Bakr, and even his mother were all sent to persuade him, and all of it failed.

— Steve Coll
26:12

After being struck from the family, he never publicly denounced his brothers

Bakr publicly denounced his younger brother and cut him off from the family fortune, and Saudi Arabia stripped him of his citizenship. But Bin Laden never once publicly denounced his brothers in his life; he laid the responsibility on the Saudi royal family and believed his brothers were merely acting under coercion. His shares in the company were sold to his brother Ghalib, who sympathized with his ideology, and that opaque transaction eventually ended up in a Saudi court, with the shares placed in trust in case of a future reconciliation with the royal family. Steve Coll says that in that Saudi Arabia, nothing was ever monolithic.

— Steve Coll
29:21

American pressure was the real force that changed his situation

Around 1995, his aide Jamal al-Fadl embezzled $110,000 and fled to the U.S. embassy in Eritrea, becoming a reliable inside source. The CIA station in Khartoum began tracking Bin Laden, even having agents wear wigs and dress as Africans to follow him by car. But what truly made it impossible for him to stay was American pressure on the Sudanese government. Sudan wanted international loans and political legitimacy, and in 1994 it quietly handed Carlos the Jackal over to French agents, already showing it would bow to pressure. In the end Sudan informed Bin Laden that he had to leave.

— Steve Coll
33:31

His family refused to go with him to Afghanistan

When he was expelled from Sudan, Bin Laden's family broke apart. Some of his wives and sons were told the next stop was Jalalabad, and they chose to return to Jeddah, with some of the sons rejoining the family business. Steve Coll explains why: in Khartoum they had money, horses, air conditioning, their own bedrooms, schools; Jalalabad was in the middle of a war, with the Taliban attacking the forces that controlled the area, and even housing was uncertain. It was not the same thing at all — Jalalabad was far more savage, a return to the jihad front line.

— Steve Coll
37:42

The CIA set up a dedicated unit to hunt him

Before Bin Laden flew from Sudan to Afghanistan in 1996, the CIA had already set up "Alex Station" at its Langley headquarters, aimed specifically at him. This marked America's recognition that he was a global problem, not merely a problem of one country. Steve Coll points out that the 1990s were the beginning of technological globalization, and Bin Laden himself was an innovator: he used fax machines to communicate clandestinely with Saudi Arabia, and he adopted satellite television very early. The CIA set up Alex Station not only to track him but also to figure out how he used communications technology to connect militant organizations across different places.

— Steve Coll
