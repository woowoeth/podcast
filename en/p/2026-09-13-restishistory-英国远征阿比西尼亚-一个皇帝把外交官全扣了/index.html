The British Expedition to Abyssinia: An Emperor Seizes Every Diplomat

Victoria never answered one letter, so Tewodros II locked the British consul and a batch of missionaries inside Magdala at the top of a mountain — and got an expeditionary force instead.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

One unanswered letter started the war

Tewodros II wrote a letter to Queen Victoria, phrased like a pen pal: ‘How are you? I am well, thank God.’ The letter travelled across the Red Sea, through Aden, Bombay, Suez, Alexandria, Marseille and London — halfway around the world — and ended up filed by the Foreign Office in the India Office records. No one replied. The emperor waited two years, his expectation turning to suspicion, and concluded that Britain was colluding with his Muslim enemies. In 1864 he seized the British consul Cameron and a group of missionaries, and the hostage crisis began.

10:21

He claimed descent from Solomon, and meant it

Abyssinia had a legend: a descendant of Solomon and David would rise, unify the divided kingdom, defeat the Egyptians, and march all the way to Jerusalem, and his name would be Tewodros. In 1855 Kassa, a man of obscure origins who had been a bandit chief, defeated his last rival and proclaimed himself the new emperor, taking the name Tewodros II. He later claimed to be of Solomon's line, though he said nothing of the sort when Plowden first met him. This messianic identity was both the source of his legitimacy and the root of his later paranoia.

21:46

After his wife died he began to lose control

In 1858 Tewodros's wife Tewabech died, and he was grief-stricken. His next wife, the 12-year-old Tiruwork, was cold to him and said, ‘I am speaking with a king greater than you — get out.’ He began drinking heavily, sleeping with women in the camp at random, and admitted to the missionaries that he was living ‘a very unchristian and indecent life.’ In 1860 his friend Plowden was ambushed by rebels and died nine days after being speared. Britain sent a new representative only two years later, and in the interval the emperor's paranoia deteriorated sharply.

26:55

The letter Cameron carried became the fuse

At the end of 1862, when Cameron left the emperor's camp, he took with him a letter in which Tewodros asked Victoria to arrange safe passage for his envoys and said, ‘See how Islam oppresses the Christians.’ Cameron came back saying he had received no reply from London, and the emperor began to suspect he had gone off to Sudan to collude with Muslims. In October 1863 the missionary Stern angered the emperor by publishing his book Wanderings Among the Falashas, and was arrested, whipped and put in chains. In January 1864 Cameron and the other missionaries were seized too, and all the hostages realised the nightmare had only just begun.

39:33

What Britain really feared was France

The Foreign Office did not particularly care whether the missionaries lived or died; what worried them was France. Suez Canal fever was at its height, and if they alienated Tewodros he might turn to Paris, giving France control of the route from the Red Sea to India. So they sent Hormuzd Rassam — a Mosul-born Assyrian, an archaeologist, the man who found Ashurbanipal's palace and the Epic of Gilgamesh, Oxford-educated, a Protestant convert, and an ardent Anglophile. He set off for Abyssinia with a letter from Queen Victoria and two assistants, Dr Blanc and Lieutenant Prideaux.

49:02

Rassam nearly succeeded, then was seized himself

In January 1866 Rassam met Tewodros in his blue diplomatic uniform, and after reading Victoria's letter the emperor was so excited he could not sleep; the next day he dictated a reply calling the queen ‘the defender of religion chosen by God.’ He agreed to release all the hostages, but demanded that Rassam accompany him first on a campaign to put down rebels. Along the way the emperor kept sending gifts: muskets, manuscripts, salmon, antelope, two lion cubs, two monkeys and a baboon. In March 1866 the hostages were finally brought to the lake, but on 13 April, when Rassam went to say goodbye, he found that he too had been arrested.

57:19

He held everyone just to get gunsmiths

Why did Tewodros seize Rassam? There are three possibilities: he had been acting all along; he was overcome at the last moment by hatred of the missionaries; or he genuinely liked Rassam and could not bear to let him go. Philip Marsden thinks the third most likely — the emperor was infatuated with Rassam and saw him as his only channel to British friendship and British technology. The emperor then wrote to Victoria demanding that she send ‘skilled craftsmen, blacksmiths, gunsmiths and artillery experts’ to make guns, and said he would release the prisoners once he was satisfied.

1:02:28

In the cellar he admitted he was mad

Tewodros locked the missionaries in a cellar, came in with a bottle of arak, and sat down among them drinking and talking. At last he said, calmly and sorrowfully: ‘My father was mad. People often said I was mad, and I never believed them. But now I know it is true.’ Rassam urged him not to say such things; the emperor walked to the door, turned back, and in a hoarse whisper said: ‘I am mad. Goodbye.’ Then he left. For nearly two years afterwards they were held on the summit of Magdala, ten thousand feet up, in chains, their spines bent.

Glossary

Abyssinia
The old name for Ethiopia, the term nineteenth-century Europeans used for the kingdom.
Magdala
Tewodros II's mountain-top fortress, about ten thousand feet above sea level, serving as both prison and treasury.
Theodros II
Emperor of Ethiopia, crowned in 1855, born Kassa, who claimed descent from Solomon.
Hormuzd Rassam
Mosul-born Assyrian archaeologist and British diplomat who discovered Ashurbanipal's palace.
Walter Plowden
Britain's first consul in Abyssinia, a friend of Tewodros, killed by rebels in 1860.
Charles Duncan Cameron
British consul in Abyssinia, seized by Tewodros in 1864.
