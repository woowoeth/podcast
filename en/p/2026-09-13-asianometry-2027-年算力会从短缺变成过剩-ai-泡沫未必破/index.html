Asianometry 2026-09-13

Compute goes from shortage to glut in 2027, and the AI bubble may not pop

Usable AI data center capacity is about 15 gigawatts at the end of 2026 and races to 45-55 gigawatts by the end of 2027, a 30-40 gigawatt increment. At $50 billion of revenue per gigawatt, the two giants would have to produce $750 billion to $1.5 trillion, and that number does not hold up.

ComputeSemiconductorsAI investmentData centersTSMC
The first half is on-the-ground observation from Hot Chips and Semicon Taiwan; the second half is the real value of this episode: a bottom-up arithmetic of compute supply and demand, plus the judgment that anti-AI sentiment could swing elections.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:05

The window for new AI chips has reopened

Last year the author wrote that ‘the window for new AI chips has closed, except for Google's TPU’, and here he explicitly takes it back and says he was wrong. The trigger was OpenAI unveiling its in-house chip Jalapeno at Hot Chips, a short talk with high information density. Outside the hall a ring of new AI chip startups was gathered: Etched, MatX, Posetron, Fractile. Etched gave no talk but built presence by handing out hats while its founder roamed the Q&A asking questions. The author cautions that these are hardware companies: they do not just design chips, they have to build their own racks and systems and work in the lab, and recruiting top talent and execution are hard bars. His survival test borrows Nathan Bedford Forrest's controversial line: get there first, and with the most men.

6:08

Semicon Taiwan is really TSMC's request for proposal

First-timers are surprised: neither TSMC nor ASML has a booth. But TSMC dominates the whole show — its teams walk the halls visiting new suppliers and scouting new technology, and many information sessions have TSMC executives on stage laying the company's problems out in public. The author's conclusion is that Semicon Taiwan is essentially an RFP that TSMC issues to the entire semiconductor industry. The technical throughline is clear: future manufacturing has to go big. AI chips are hitting existing limits, most typically the reticle limit, which determines how large an area a lithography machine can print. TSMC had a slide showing it is preparing to build a ‘battleship-class’ chip spanning 14 reticles, and the industry responds fast, as always.

7:10

Glass panel packaging has gone from PPT to the show floor

Two years ago the author made a video on panel level packaging, which was the theme of Semicon Taiwan 2024, when it was still all talks and slideshows. Now AI systems are so large that the industry wants to use square, glass panel interposers to carry chiplets and memory instead of silicon interposers. The author's attitude is reserved: can TSMC make them without warping or cracking? ‘I'll believe it when I see the chip.’ But the ecosystem is already moving, and equipment such as handling robots adapted to square panels has appeared on the show floor. He judges this wave of ‘chipmaxing’ will be one of the most far-reaching technology transitions in semiconductor history, roughly on the scale of the move to 300-millimeter wafers.

8:12

The spot market has vanished, and VCs now help portfolio companies find GPUs

When the author was last in the Bay Area in September 2025, the picture was completely different. Since then long-running agentic AI has swept Silicon Valley, triggered in part by stronger models such as Anthropic's Opus 4.5 around November 2025 and by Open Claw. In February 2026 prices began to rise, everyone scrambled to lock up compute, and Anthropic led the way signing big deals with SpaceX, Google and others. The author was told that by the time of this trip the spot market had completely vanished. For Neoclouds (a term coined by semi-analyst Dan Nishbal in April 2024 for pure cloud vendors that resell AI training and inference compute, such as Coreweave and Nebius) this is a huge boon, and they are confident the shortage will last at least another one to two years. The author met several people who did not know each other and each wanted to start a Neocloud. For AI startups this is a business risk: imagine telling investors your product has to wait until mid-2027 because there is not enough compute. The new way VCs help portfolio companies is to go find compute, and he heard of people scraping together a thousand GPUs and hunting in unexpected places as far off as Eastern Europe.

9:12

Two giants are feasting while compute still goes to waste

By contrast, Anthropic and OpenAI are feasting internally. Dylan recently said on Dashpod that these two AI giants each have about 5 gigawatts of compute. The author infers from this that a considerable share of all that compute may be wasted, and anecdotes he heard confirm that this ‘filthy rich’ state is not being used as efficiently as it could be. He also asked every AI insider he talked to one question: will Google return to the frontier? The general consensus is no, though the explanations are vague. Several people pointed out that Google's decision to sell a large amount of compute to Anthropic is itself a sign that it does not believe. This is a big reversal from the period when Gemini 3 made some people think OpenAI was finished. What the author heard is that Google suffered severe talent drain, plus a culture unfriendly to frontier AI.

11:15

Neolabs are venture capital that treats compute as money

What fascinates the author most is the Neolab: a research startup with basically no revenue that raises money to explore an interesting AI idea, whose founders are often elite researchers and whose business plan is little more than ‘discover an entirely new architecture’. The most famous and earliest, Safe Superintelligence, raised a billion dollars and has still released no product or model. The label is fuzzy and covers recursive intelligence, Humans, Core Automation, Flapping Airplanes and others; those with products include Japan's Sakana AI, Thinking Machines, Reflection AI, plus a batch in verticals such as materials science, world models and robotics. The first reaction is that these assets are dumb: no revenue, no plan to monetize soon. But the author can understand it: historically breakthrough science came from research universities, government labs or Bell Labs, and now university funding is being cut and talent is being sucked into industry. AI is probably the first major technology in the past 50 years to emerge without government involvement, and in this era research is closed and secretive. So let Nvidia, with compute and cash in hand, plus a pile of VCs, fund these asymmetric bets. OpenAI and Anthropic are locked into the transformer paradigm and are unlikely to spend on researching these directions. The probability of failure is high, but most of the money is given in the form of compute anyway; if someone really strikes gold and forces the giants to pivot (say, to a more data-efficient architecture), the giants will most likely buy the discoverer at an eye-watering price, and one such deal pays for everything. The author thinks this is a better use of cash than continuing buybacks, and names Apple.

14:15

Tokens are burning, and nobody has measured ROI

Customers really are burning tokens, but are they getting ROI? The author asked across the ecosystem and the answer remains vague: don't know, nobody has measured it, it is all anecdote. He believes lower barriers to coding will hit knowledge work — financial firms used to hire a pile of reporting analysts to keep management on top of positions, and now two smart young analysts plus Claude writing a dashboard is enough, which he has seen with his own eyes. But for other AI capabilities, such as making spreadsheets and making presentations, the things marketing usually pushes, he has seen no obvious impact. Maybe it just needs more time, maybe it is a problem of intelligence itself. OpenAI recently published a blog on how agents accelerate internal research, but still frames AI's capability as intern level: it can execute routine tasks and is far from a real AI researcher. What he heard is that AI still lacks human research taste, though some believe sufficient scale can achieve it. And one thing holds for everyone in San Francisco: people are working harder than before. If the models are really that capable, why does everyone say they have more work? Maybe AI does not do our work for us but gives us more work.

15:17

Compute flips from shortage to glut in 2027

Everyone says shortage, shortage, two more years of shortage, and the author instead suspects things will not go as expected. His arithmetic is not complicated: usable AI data center capacity at the end of 2026 is estimated at about 15 gigawatts, with OpenAI and Anthropic each taking about 5 gigawatts; the supply chain will respond, and by the end of 2027 that number is estimated to hit 45-55 gigawatts, an increment of 30-40 gigawatts, of which the two giants take about 30 gigawatts. Assume half goes to training, leaving 15 gigawatts combined for inference. Inference is currently modeled at about $50 billion of revenue per gigawatt (a figure from Dylan Patel on Dashpod), and it can also be back-solved from Anthropic's deal to buy 300 megawatts from SpaceX for $1.25 billion a month; Dylan thinks smarter models can eventually push it to $100 billion per gigawatt. On that basis the two should produce $750 billion to $1.5 trillion of revenue in 2027. The author says that number is absurd: most people agree the two will do $150-200 billion this year, and next year he can barely imagine $400-500 billion, but $750 billion to $1.5 trillion is ‘two Walmarts’ or ‘two Toyotas’, a credibility stretch he does not think can be reached. He bets revenue per gigawatt is far below $100 billion or even $50 billion, possibly only a small fraction of it. The infrastructure side looks the same: assume 40 gigawatts lands in 2027, current rent is about $20 million per megawatt, implying a 3-year payback, so those 40 gigawatts would require $800 billion paid to infrastructure providers in 2027 alone. He does not think that can be collected either, which means rents have to fall and someone somewhere has to take a loss. Conclusion: in 2027 the supply chain will deliver a lot of compute, most of it in the second half, and he bets this pushes the market from shortage to glut. He stresses he is not saying the AI bubble will pop, only that we will soon be flooded with compute — 2026 is agentic demand meeting 2025 supply, supply will catch up and probably overshoot; but after that maybe the next stage of growth ignites and demand balloons again, for example persistent AI.

19:23

Anti-AI sentiment could win elections

The author notes that in his previous Hot Chips video someone predicted AI would solve a Millennium Prize problem within a year, and now there is word that an advanced internal OpenAI model produced a proof of the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, so his friend called it right. But what should be a remarkable achievement has been tainted by accusations of plagiarism and data theft. This negative sentiment points to something he sees more and more online and in ordinary people: anti-AI sentiment. Many people hate AI, for different reasons — they hate data centers, hate that it represents everything about billionaires, think it is stealing ‘human knowledge’, or think society simply does not need it. Whatever the reason, this sentiment is growing, and he has a bad feeling: this issue can win elections. San Francisco lives in its own bubble, the city itself is wrapped in its own microclimate, but the people inside should pay more attention to the sentiment growing outside the bubble, because things can spread at a terrifying speed.

Glossary

Neocloud
A pure cloud vendor that resells AI training and inference compute, such as Coreweave and Nebius.
Neolab
A research startup with basically no revenue that raises money to explore entirely new AI architectures.
reticle
The largest area a lithography exposure can print, which sets the upper limit on a single chip's size.
panel level packaging
Using square glass panels instead of silicon interposers to carry chiplets and memory.
RSI
Recursive self-improvement, the ability of AI to autonomously improve its own capabilities.
