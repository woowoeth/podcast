Cerebras Hits 4400 tok/s on a Single Chip: Speed Is the Moat

Cerebras's WSE 3.5 pushes a single chip to 4400 tok/s, and the CS4 rack stuffs three wafers into a 120kW cabinet. The core argument: inference splits into prefill and decode, and speed is the moat.

Inference ChipsCerebrasWafer-Scale ComputingSRAMOpenAICompute
Cerebras rarely opens up its system design this far. This episode delivers hard numbers on rack power, interconnect latency and component reduction — worth hearing for anyone trying to verify whether the SRAM inference route holds up.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:11

Doubling performance without waiting for a new node, by pushing clocks

Cerebras did not wait for a new process node; it aggressively clocked WSE3, nearly doubling frequency and therefore nearly doubling power. It stresses this was not simple binning but voltage- and frequency-targeted design work with TSMC on the same N5 node. Sparse FP16 went from 125 petlops last generation to 250 petlops, while core count stayed at 900,000 and SRAM at 44GB. Physical yield stays near 100% through dynamic routing around defective cores, but parametric yield has always been the external question mark.

8:15

Three wafers per rack: the weird thing becomes a standard form factor

The old design was a 16U box with a single chip on its own power and liquid cooling, with only air cooling on the data center side. CS4 goes rack-level: fans and power supplies at the front panel, a module called the backpack at the rear, and liquid cooling lines running from the top to vertically mounted wafer chips. One rack holds three, and the author estimates roughly 40kW per chip and about 120kW per rack based on the power supply count — sounding on par with Nvidia's NVL72. This is not spec bragging; it is putting wafer-scale into a standard data center form factor.

10:18

IO becomes a swappable module; what they really sell is a rack platform

Per-chip network bandwidth doubled from the previous generation to 2.4Tbps, and the IO module is designed to be replaceable: if 800G/200G/100G or another interconnect standard needs support later, you swap the module rather than redo the system. The backpack is also compatible with future wafer sizes, since wafers are at their physical limit and the power and liquid cooling interfaces are roughly similar. The author argues Cerebras is effectively building a rack-level platform, which is why they dare say CS5 can follow in the second half of 2027.

12:20

OpenAI's big money comes from chips that do not exist yet

WSE3 runs about 2300 tok/s on models that fit entirely on a single chip, and WSE 3.5 reaches 4400 tok/s; GPT-5.6 Sol currently runs at 750 tok/s and approaches 1500 tok/s on the new chip. CEO Andrew Feldman states plainly that deals like the OpenAI one include current supply, but the bulk of revenue comes from future-generation chips, so a platform product like CS4 is the key to fulfilling orders. The previously announced OpenAI partnership corresponds to roughly $20 billion in revenue, which was the growth story told at IPO.

14:24

Capacity is bottlenecked by custom machined parts, not the wafers

Because volumes were small, the first-generation wafer-scale system used a lot of custom machined parts, and infrastructure cost was far above reasonable levels. CS4 cuts component count by half or even 60% and automates heavily so the line can ramp — capacity has always been the question pressed on earnings calls. The author's understanding is that the new-generation system costs more than the previous one, but by how much was not disclosed; early systems are already shipping, with volume by year-end.

17:25

It should not be called disaggregation; it is splitting compute and memory work

Cerebras's recommended usage is GPU for prefill and Cerebras for decode. The author thinks disaggregation is the wrong word and workload partitioning is right: give the compute-hungry part to compute hardware and the memory-hungry part to memory hardware. Nvidia bought Groq for SRAM decode performance, but he is more interested in whether Groq can truly compete once it integrates NVLink; a slide he saw said each NVL72 rack needs 9 Groq racks. As an independent company Cerebras can still work with AMD and AWS, which is where it is more flexible than Groq.

19:28

Fast is not about a nicer experience; it is about running many ideas at once

Cerebras's trade-off is generality exchanged for power and deployment. Specialized chips like Talis can hit 17000 tok/s but only run a single small model; Cerebras runs any transformer model. For coding or agentic workflows, every input waits on a result, so speed is not just experience — it lets you run multiple ideas in parallel and cut waiting. Cerebras's business model is charging customers a premium for high-speed tokens — priced per million tokens, and demand for speed never goes away.

21:32

The speed crown is unchallenged; the bet is on CS5

The author's summary: in AI hardware Cerebras is still the speed king; others compete on context length or math optimization, but nobody beats it on speed. This upgrade from a single 16U box to a 120kW three-system rack amounts to turning the "weird thing" into a standard data center form factor. The next things to watch are whether CS5 lands on schedule in the second half of 2027, and whether future 10-trillion-parameter models can be supported on this platform.

Glossary

Wafer Scale Engine (WSE)
A single chip made from an entire wafer, using cross-mask stitching interconnects to avoid the high-latency links between multiple chips.
Cross-reticle stitching
Stitching lithography mask regions together across a wafer into one giant chip, letting area exceed the limit of a single exposure.
Backpack
The pluggable module at the rear of the CS4 rack, integrating liquid cooling, power and replaceable network IO, shaped like a backpack.
Prefill/Decode
The two stages of LLM inference: prefill processes the input in parallel, decode generates token by token, and the two have different hardware needs.
Parametric yield
The share of chips on a wafer that hit the target frequency/voltage, as distinct from physical yield.
