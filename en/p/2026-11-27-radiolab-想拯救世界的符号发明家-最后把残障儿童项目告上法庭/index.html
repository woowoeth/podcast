The symbol inventor who wanted to save the world sued the disabled children's project

Charles Bliss invented a symbol system meant to replace all languages; when disabled children actually used it to speak, he sued the organization helping them because teachers modified his symbols without permission, ultimately walking away with a $160,000 settlement.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

17:25

The Nazis changed a word's meaning without changing the word itself

Bliss discovered that ‘Deutschland über alles’ originally was a unification slogan from 1848 meaning ‘the nation above individual states,’ which Hitler twisted into ‘the nation above all other nations.’ The words stayed the same, the meaning flipped entirely, yet it persuaded good people to collude in genocide. This became the direct trigger for his later conviction that ‘language itself is dangerous’ and that he had to invent a symbol system independent of language.

18:28

He aimed to use a symbol system to eliminate war itself

Bliss believed that if his symbols were adopted, language barriers between francophones, Finns, English speakers, and Estonians would vanish—even traffic accidents and health problems could be prevented. He repeatedly insisted that if the German people understood symbols instead of being manipulated by words, Hitler would never have happened. This was vast ambition that equated linguistic reform with a plan for world peace, and the seed of the later tragic gap between dream and reality.

22:38

He spent his savings to send out six thousand books and got silence

Bliss spent seven years writing Semantography and mailed copies to six thousand professors, government officials, and national leaders, waiting for orders. Nobody responded. After his wife died, he took a welding job at General Motors, calling it the lowest point of his life—until a letter unexpectedly landed on his desk in 1971.

25:47

Children with cerebral palsy said their first ‘I love you’ through his symbols

Shirley McNaughton found the book by chance at a library—it had never been checked out—and brought it back to teach children unable to speak because of cerebral palsy. The children pieced together sentences using ‘I’ and ‘you’ plus a symbol of a heart pierced by an arrow. One mother described her child's first ‘I love you’ as the happiest moment of her life. For the first time, the symbol system actually helped real people instead of staying a theoretical idea.

28:50

The inventor resented that teachers made his system actually useful

To meet the children's actual needs, Shirley and the teachers added symbols for ‘antonyms’ and filled in pronoun-conjugation rules—changes that helped the children but which Bliss saw as betrayal of his ‘universal language for the cosmos.’ What he found even more intolerable was that teachers treated the symbols as a stepping stone to ‘normal languages’ like English or French—precisely what he had spent his life trying to escape.

32:56

He sued the program that was using his symbols to save children

After anger accumulated for more than a decade, Bliss formally sued the Ontario program for the disabled and Shirley McNaughton personally, even hinting on tape that he wanted to have her ‘locked up for life.’ In 1982 the two sides reached a $160,000 financial settlement—all the money a ‘small basement classroom’ could scrape together. A man who wanted to save the world's languages ended up taking money from a group of children who could not speak.

36:00

The defendant won the case but still tended to him with care

Even at the height of legal warfare, Shirley held no resentment toward Bliss—after the trial ended and his lawyer was putting away files, he still asked for her help, she still took him to dinner, and in the evening she went to his hotel to put drops in his ears. She still believes today that Bliss symbols are ‘pure form of poetry’ and can express what language cannot say.

46:26

Carving perfect snowflakes into negatives was legitimate photography, not forgery

The snowflake photos that German meteorologist Gustav Hellmann had taken were askew and broken, completely unlike the five thousand symmetrically perfect snow crystals Wilson Bentley shot over a lifetime. Investigation revealed that Bentley used a penknife to scrape the background off negatives and even carved into the crystals themselves to produce ‘entirely arbitrary patterns.’ The German called it outright fakery. Bentley countered that correcting flaws was not fakery but getting closer to what a ‘real snowflake’ truly was.
