The Korean Wave Wasn't a Cultural Miracle — It Was a State-Planned Industrial Counteroffensive

From the ruins of the Korean War to BTS and Parasite, the Korean Wave looks like a cultural phenomenon but is really a route two generations — from Park Chung-hee to Kim Dae-jung — used government means to turn culture into an export industry.

Gratitude and resentment toward America are the psychological undertone of the Korean Wave

Before historian Michael Kim grew up in Korea, he first saw the Korean War through the American TV series M*A*S*H: a Korea that, from the American perspective, was shabby, poor, and waiting to be saved. More than ten people on his maternal side died in a single American bombing, yet the elders still understood it all as "necessary to fight North Korea." This mix of gratitude and complicated feeling toward America became the psychological undertone for Korean society's later acceptance of American culture — and its determination to overtake America culturally.

— Host and Michael Kim
15:00

The curfew didn't stop rock — it spawned underground bars

In 1961 Park Chung-hee took power through a military coup, using curfews and limits on hair and skirt length to manage daily life while twisting the country into a machine of high-speed growth. His logic was: this generation's sweat is not for itself, but for the next generation. The government shaped a consensus of "everyone sacrifices" through forced broadcasts of propaganda songs and censorship of dissent. But the curfew instead spawned underground bar culture, and American rock and foreign influences poured in through the cracks.

— Host
20:50

The skill of borrowing and repackaging was honed building cars

Korea's first own-brand car, the Hyundai Pony, used British machine tools and Italian design, was exported in 1974, and at one point became Canada's best-selling model. In Park Chung-hee's export-oriented strategy, chaebol like Samsung, Hyundai, and LG expanded like "frontline soldiers"; the Vietnam War further brought American money and orders in. Korea learned to borrow elements from around the world and quickly turn them into its own products — this ability to "borrow and repackage" was later applied directly to cultural products.

— Host
26:19

The first wave of the Korean Wave began as a dictator's distraction tactic

To keep students off the streets, Chun Doo-hwan pushed the "3S policy": sports, screen, and sex. He lifted curfews, loosened censorship, and propped up film, television, and professional leagues as distractions. As a result, Korean TV dramas were exported to Asian countries as silent master tapes, dubbed and broadcast by local stations. This simple export playbook let Korean dramas become the first wave of the Korean Wave before K-pop, and also laid industrial groundwork for later cultural exports.

— Host
28:25

The Korean Wave wasn't a spontaneous craze — it was state policy after national humiliation

In the 1997 Asian financial crisis, Korea accepted nearly 60 billion USD in IMF rescue, which was called a national humiliation. Citizens queued to donate gold jewelry; more than 3 million people donated over 200 tons of gold. The IMF money came with conditions of openness and deregulation, companies laid off en masse, and 30 years of growth felt wiped out overnight. After taking office, Kim Dae-jung saw the popularity of Korean dramas in Asia and decided to turn the country into an "entrepreneur": the government would fund, legislate, and build infrastructure to support the cultural industry. The Korean Wave was from then on no longer spontaneous among the people, but a national strategy.

— Host
37:22

Korean women get plastic surgery not to look white, but to look like idols

In the 2000s American media framed Korea's plastic surgery boom as "women wanting to look white," even featuring it on Oprah. Korean-American scholar He Jin Li said this was completely at odds with what she saw with her own eyes: the talk in the community was "my older sister did it, I want to look like her," and the goal was Korean actresses and idols — no one said they wanted to look white. Double eyelid surgery existed as far back as the Japanese colonial period; in a highly competitive society with fewer opportunities for women, beauty is seen as an investment and a bonus.

— He Jin Li
41:04

What the world is dancing along to is a song mocking flaunting wealth

Gangnam is Seoul's wealthy district and also home to over 500 plastic surgery clinics; foreign tourists treat it as a pilgrimage site for consumer fashion. But the intent of "Gangnam Style" is satire of this wealth-flaunting lifestyle — the song mocks someone who picks up a hot coffee just to pose, acting cool even if it burns their mouth. After the IMF crisis, the wealth gap widened and youth unemployment rose, and Koreans had become disillusioned with "hard work will let you rise"; the song's cynicism resonated domestically in Korea, while most overseas audiences only saw the fun.

— Host
45:00

The Korean Wave went global precisely because it isn't Korean enough

Michael Kim offers a counterintuitive explanation: the Korean Wave went global precisely because in many ways it is not that "Korean." Korea was colonized, garrisoned, and dragged into globalization throughout its history, forced to stay open to cultures from around the world; this openness let it learn elements from Europe, America, and Japan, then rewrite them into its own version. BTS's goal was never to be Korea's best group, but the world's best dancers and singers — "standing on the same level as the world's strongest" is the Korean obsession.

— Michael Kim

Glossary

Korean Wave
The phenomenon of Korean cultural products — film, TV, music, beauty — being exported overseas on a large scale.
Chaebol
Large Korean family-owned corporate groups such as Samsung, Hyundai, and LG, which grew strong during Park Chung-hee's industrialization.
3S policy
Chun Doo-hwan's use of sports, screen, and sex entertainment to divert public political heat, aiming to make people talk less about democracy.
Entrepreneurial state
A government directly taking on the financing, legislation, and promotion of the cultural industry — the state model Kim Dae-jung used to push the Korean Wave.
National humiliation Day
What Koreans call the day they accepted the IMF rescue, a collective memory of aid intertwined with humiliation.
