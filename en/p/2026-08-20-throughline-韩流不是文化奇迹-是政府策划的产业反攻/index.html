The Korean Wave isn't a cultural miracle — it's a government-planned industrial counterattack

From the rubble of the Korean War to BTS and Parasite, the Korean Wave looks like a cultural phenomenon. It is actually the route two generations of leaders, from Park Chung-hee to Kim Dae-jung, took to turn culture into an export industry using the instruments of the state.

Gratitude toward America, and resentment of it, is the Korean Wave's emotional undercoat

Before historian Michael Kim grew up in Korea, he first saw the Korean War through the American series M*A*S*H: a Korea that was broken, poor, and waiting to be rescued, seen from an American point of view. More than ten people on his mother's side of the family were killed in a single U.S. bombing, yet his elders still understood all of it as ‘what fighting North Korea required’. That mix of gratitude toward America and everything more complicated underneath became the emotional undercoat of a society that would go on to absorb American culture and, at the same time, insist on overtaking America culturally.

— Host and Michael Kim
15:00

The curfew failed to contain rock music and bred underground bars instead

Park Chung-hee took power in a 1961 military coup and governed daily life through curfews and limits on hair and skirt length, while winding the country into a machine for high-speed growth. His logic was that this generation's sweat was not for itself but for the next one. The government forced propaganda songs onto the airwaves and censored dissent to manufacture a consensus that everyone was sacrificing. But the curfew instead bred an underground bar culture, and American rock and foreign influence poured in through the cracks.

— Host
20:50

The knack for borrowing and repackaging was learned building cars

Korea's first car under its own brand, the Hyundai Pony, used British machine tools and Italian design; it was exported in 1974 and for a time became the best-selling car in Canada. Under Park Chung-hee's export-oriented strategy, chaebol like Samsung, Hyundai and LG expanded like ‘frontline soldiers’; the Vietnam War brought still more American money and orders in. Korea learned to borrow elements from around the world and turn them quickly into its own products — a capacity for borrowing and repackaging that was later applied directly to cultural products.

— Host
26:19

The first Korean Wave began as a dictator's distraction tactic

To keep students off the streets, Chun Doo-hwan pushed the ‘3S policy’: sports, screen, and sex. He lifted the curfew, loosened censorship, and cultivated television, film and professional sports leagues as a distraction. The result was that Korean TV dramas were exported to countries across Asia as masters with no sound, to be dubbed and broadcast by local stations. That simple export formula made Korean dramas the first wave of Hallyu, ahead of K-pop, and laid the industrial groundwork for the cultural exports that followed.

— Host
28:25

The Korean Wave wasn't a spontaneous craze — it was state policy after a national humiliation

In the 1997 Asian financial crisis, Korea accepted nearly $60 billion in IMF bailout money, an episode Koreans called a national humiliation. Citizens lined up to donate gold jewelry: more than 3 million people gave over 200 tons of gold. The IMF's money came attached to conditions of opening and deregulation, companies laid off workers en masse, and 30 years of growth felt as if it had been zeroed out overnight. After Kim Dae-jung took office, he saw how hot Korean dramas were across Asia and decided to turn the state into an ‘entrepreneur’: the government would put up the money, write the laws, and build the infrastructure to cultivate a culture industry. From that point on the Korean Wave was no longer something the private sector generated on its own — it was a national strategy.

— Host
37:22

Korean women getting surgery didn't want to look white — they wanted to look like their idols

In the 2000s the American media told the story of Korea's cosmetic surgery boom as ‘women wanting to become white’, a framing that even made it onto Oprah. Korean American scholar He Jin Li says this bore no resemblance at all to what she saw with her own eyes: what people said in the community was ‘my older sister got it done, I want to look like her’, and the reference points were Korean actresses and idols. No one said they wanted to look white. Double-eyelid surgery already existed under Japanese colonial rule; in a fiercely competitive society where women have fewer opportunities, beauty is treated as an investment and an added advantage.

— He Jin Li
41:04

What the whole world was dancing to is a song mocking conspicuous wealth

Gangnam is Seoul's wealthy district and also where more than 500 cosmetic surgery clinics are clustered; foreign tourists treat it as a pilgrimage site for consumer fashion. But ‘Gangnam Style’ was meant as satire of exactly that flashy lifestyle — the song mocks people who pick up a hot coffee for show and act cool even as it burns their mouths. After the IMF, inequality widened and youth unemployment rose, and Koreans had already lost their illusions about effort being enough to change your position. The song's cynicism resonated at home in Korea, while most overseas listeners saw only the party.

— Host
45:00

The Korean Wave went global precisely because it isn't Korean enough

Michael Kim offers a counterintuitive explanation: the Korean Wave could go global precisely because in many respects it isn't that ‘Korean’. Korea was colonized in its history, occupied by foreign troops, and swept into globalization, forced to stay open to the cultures of the whole world; that openness let it learn European, American and Japanese elements and then rewrite them into its own version. BTS's goal was never to be the best group in Korea but to be the best dancers and singers in the world — ‘standing on the same level as the strongest in the world’ is the Korean obsession.

— Michael Kim

Glossary

Korean Wave
The large-scale export of Korean cultural products — television, film, music, beauty — to markets overseas.
Chaebol
Korea's large family-controlled business conglomerates, such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, which grew powerful during Park Chung-hee's industrialization.
3S policy
Chun Doo-hwan's use of sports, screen and sexual entertainment to divert the public's political energy, with the aim of getting people to talk less about democracy.
Entrepreneurial state
The state taking on the financing, legislation and promotion of the culture industry directly — the national model through which Kim Dae-jung drove the Korean Wave.
National humiliation Day
What Koreans call the day the country accepted the IMF bailout, a collective memory in which rescue and humiliation are bound together.
