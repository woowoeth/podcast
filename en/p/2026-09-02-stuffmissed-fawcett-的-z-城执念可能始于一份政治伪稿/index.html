Stuff You Missed in History Class 2026-09-02

An explorer died for a phantom city, and the source was a political forgery

The Z city Fawcett spent his life hunting traces back to a fragmentary 18th-century manuscript of unknown provenance, suspected of being a political fabrication from post-independence Brazil; his final coordinates sit about 200 miles from the public version, and no hard evidence of his disappearance has ever surfaced.

1:03

Fawcett read far more out of it than the manuscript can support

Manuscript 512 describes an expedition finding a vast ancient city: black stone pillars, Roman-style spires, swarms of bats, stone carvings, cave tombs and river gold — but the text never gives any actual location. The manuscript runs only ten pages with large stretches of words missing, and the hosts have to pause at the gaps when reading key passages aloud; they cannot even read straight through whether it is ‘a coastal city or a royal court.’ The narrator hears roosters crowing in the city, while the scouts report no one lives there, so the whole party walks into the city armed at night. What Fawcett read out of it clearly goes far beyond what the text itself can support.

7:10

The manuscript may be a new nation manufacturing an ancient civilization for itself

The manuscript is said to have been found in 1839 in the archives of the National Library of Brazil, but no one knows how it got into the archives; soon after its discovery it made the newspapers, and multiple Brazilian groups sent expeditions to find the city, all of them coming back empty. The manuscript's authenticity is still disputed today, and even if it really is an 18th-century document, it could have been fiction at the time. National Geographic reporter Jordi Canal Solar offered an explanation in 2024: Brazil only broke from Portugal in 1822, and the new republic needed a document proving that an ancient civilization had existed within its territory, something like the great Maya sites of Central America. Note: this is a ‘possible’ explanation, not a settled conclusion.

— Holly Frey
8:12

The lost-city obsession had no single aha moment

Manuscript 512 is often described as the single origin of Fawcett's obsession, but the hosts stress in particular: there was no ‘aha moment’ that made him certain a lost city existed. Each time he went deeper into the Brazilian jungle he went further, and he saw with his own eyes that thick vegetation could hide anything, which convinced him a city could be hidden in there. In 1911 Hiram Bingham's discovery of Machu Picchu (more accurately, his bringing it to the outside world's attention) caused a global sensation and further fed Europeans' mania for jungle cities. At the same time Fawcett claimed he knew from other documents and from Indigenous people that Z existed, yet never once cited a specific source — which became the handle later used to accuse him of being a fraud or an exaggerator.

11:17

Money that could not be traced became the handle for fraud accusations

Fawcett's applications to the Royal Geographical Society for funding did not go smoothly. Peers thought he had been bewitched by the golden-city legends circulating in Brazil, and no one wanted to put serious money into a project ‘wreathed in atmosphere’ like that. He wrote: I have no doubt whatever that the ancient cities exist; I have myself seen the remains of one of them. Right at that juncture, according to some accounts, a mysterious financial group called The Glove stepped forward with money; the names of its members are entirely unknown, as if the money appeared out of thin air. This kind of unverifiable thing, plus his habit of never naming the documents he cited, led many contemporaries to brand him a deceiver or an exaggerator.

— Holly Frey
16:27

The last letter contains no clue, only bugs

On April 20, 1925, the party set out from Cuiabá, and on May 29 Fawcett wrote his last letter to his wife Nina from ‘Dead Horse Camp’ (where in 1920 he had been forced to shoot his sick horse). There is no dramatic clue in the letter, only concrete torments: flies from dawn to dark and even through the whole night, smaller than a needle yet biting like mosquitoes, plus swarms of bees; Jack was in good shape, and Raleigh still had a bandaged leg but refused to turn back. He admitted he could not be sure of lasting longer than the younger men, but he gave precise coordinates and said ‘you need not fear any failure.’ After this letter, the three were never heard from again.

— Holly Frey
20:34

The only physical evidence the search party brought back was a decade-old discard

In 1928 the Royal Geographical Society sent the pilot and travel filmmaker George Miller Dyott to find out what had become of Fawcett — directly against Fawcett's own wishes: before leaving he had said that if he failed, no one should come looking for him, it was too dangerous. Dyott's party was large, with a photographer and a radio operator; after contact with the Kalapalo and Nahukua peoples he claimed to be in danger, sent stories back to the newspapers, and brought back a uniform case bearing the maker's mark. But Percy's younger son Brian countered: that case was an old item his father had thrown away in 1920. Dyott's later book even says the medium he consulted saw Fawcett killed by a tribe, and the hosts judge the whole book to be an old-fashioned ‘macho jungle adventure’ narrative.

— Holly Frey
23:38

He most likely died of hunger and thirst, not at the hands of a tribe

In 1931 a party filming an Amazon documentary stumbled close to the truth. The ethnographer with the expedition, Vincenzo Petrulo, recorded on the east bank of the Kuluene River what the Kalapalo said: three white men were led to the village by Nahukua guides, the Indigenous people gave them food and urged them not to go on, and the next day ferried them across the river anyway; the two younger men had ulcers from blackfly bites and clearly did not want to go forward. Over the following five days the Indigenous people saw the smoke of the overnight campfires every day, and on the sixth day the smoke stopped. Petrulo concluded that Fawcett had not been killed but had died of hunger, thirst or disease in the dense forest on the east bank of the river. He added one more thing: Dyott had not asked about this account back then because he had no interpreter, and the Indigenous people did not like him.

— Holly Frey
29:44

The coordinates are 200 miles apart, yet archaeology is on his side

In 1951 Villas-Boas claimed to have found Fawcett's remains from the Kalapalo, and testing proved they were not his. In 1953 Brian published his father's ‘Exploration Fawcett’, and people immediately noticed the coordinates did not match: the Dead Horse Camp coordinates Fawcett made public were 13°14′ S, 54°35′ W, while the ones he sent to Nina were 11°43′ S — about 200 miles apart, and to this day no one knows whether it was a slip of the pen or a deliberate error to throw off anyone following him. Modern archaeology has partly confirmed his intuition: in 2008 Michael Heckenberger published results of work with the Kuikuro, showing that a complex settlement network once existed in Mato Grosso; today descendants of the tribe also insist their ancestors did not kill Fawcett, and the related accusations were further clarified in David Grann's ‘The Lost City of Z’.

Glossary

An 18th-century manuscript in the National Library of Brazil, named after its accession number, describing the discovery of a lost city.

The legendary ancient city Fawcett was determined to find, which he called Zed/Z.
The Glove
A mysterious group of financiers said to have funded Fawcett's expedition, whose members' identities are unknown.
Dead Horse Camp
The place where Fawcett was forced to shoot his sick horse, and where his last letter was sent from.
