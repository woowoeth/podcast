An Explorer Died for a Fictional City, Born of a Political Forgery

The Z city Fawcett spent his life seeking came from a fragmentary, mysterious 18th-century manuscript, suspected to be a political forgery after Brazil's independence; his final coordinates differ from the public version by about 200 miles, and his disappearance remains unresolved.

1:03

Fawcett read far more into the manuscript than it could support

Manuscript 512 describes an expedition discovering a vast ancient city: black stone pillars, Romanesque spires, bat swarms, stone carvings, cave tombs, and river gold, but it gives no actual location. The document is only ten pages long with many missing words; the hosts pause at gaps when reading key passages, so even phrases like 'a coastal city or a court' are discontinuous. The narrator hears roosters crowing in the city, but scouts report no inhabitants, so the whole party enters the city at night armed. Fawcett clearly read more into it than the text could support.

7:10

The manuscript may be a fabricated ancient civilization for a new nation

The manuscript was allegedly discovered in the archives of the National Library of Brazil in 1839, but no one knows how it got there; soon after, it appeared in newspapers, and several Brazilian groups sent expeditions to find the city, all returning empty-handed. Its authenticity remains disputed; even if it is a genuine 18th-century document, it could be a contemporary fiction. National Geographic journalist Jordi Canal Solar proposed in 2024 that Brazil, independent from Portugal only in 1822, needed a document proving ancient civilizations within its borders, akin to the great Maya sites of Central America. Note: this is a 'possible' explanation, not a conclusion.

— Holly Frey
8:12

The obsession with the lost city had no single aha moment

Manuscript 512 is often cited as the single source of Fawcett's obsession, but the hosts stress there was no 'aha moment' that convinced him of the lost city's existence. Each time he went deeper into the Brazilian jungle, he saw how dense vegetation could hide anything, leading him to believe a city could be concealed there. In 1911, Hiram Bingham's discovery (more accurately, bringing to the world's attention) of Machu Picchu caused a global sensation, further fueling European fervor for jungle cities. Meanwhile, Fawcett claimed to know of Z's existence from other documents and indigenous informants, but he never cited a specific source—this later became ammunition for accusations of fraud or exaggeration.

11:17

Unverifiable funding became a hook for fraud accusations

Fawcett's requests for funding from the Royal Geographical Society were not smooth. Colleagues thought he was bewitched by Brazilian gold-city legends, and no one wanted to shell out for such a 'vibe-heavy' project. He wrote: 'I have not the slightest doubt that the city exists, for I have seen its remains with my own eyes.' At this juncture, according to some accounts, a mysterious financial group called The Glove stepped in to fund him; the members' names are completely unknown, as if the money appeared out of thin air. This unverifiable matter, plus his habit of never naming his sources, led many contemporaries to label him a deceiver or exaggerator.

16:27

The last letter contained no clues, only insects

On April 20, 1925, the team departed from Cuiabá; on May 29, Fawcett wrote his final letter to his wife Nina from 'Dead Horse Camp' (where he had been forced to shoot a sick horse in 1920). The letter had no dramatic clues, only concrete torments: flying insects from dawn to dusk and even all night, smaller than pinheads but biting like mosquitoes, plus swarms of bees; Jack was in good shape, Raleigh had a bandaged leg but refused to turn back. He admitted he couldn't be sure he would outlast the younger men, but he gave precise coordinates and said 'you need have no fear of any failure.' After this letter, the three were never heard from again.

20:34

The only physical evidence brought back by a search party was a decade-old relic

In 1928, the Royal Geographical Society commissioned pilot and travel filmmaker George Miller Dyott to ascertain Fawcett's fate—directly against Fawcett's own wishes: he had said before leaving that if he failed, no one should come looking for him, as it was too dangerous. Dyott's party was large, with a photographer and radio operator; after contacting the Kalapalo and Nahukua peoples, he claimed to be in danger, sent stories to newspapers, and brought back a uniform box stamped with a manufacturer's mark. But Percy's younger son Brian countered: the box was an old item his father had discarded in 1920. Dyott's later book even mentioned consulting a medium who saw Fawcett killed by the tribe; the hosts described the whole book as an old-school 'macho jungle adventure' narrative.

— Holly Frey
23:38

He most likely died of thirst or hunger, not at the hands of a tribe

In 1931, a film crew making an Amazon documentary inadvertently approached the truth. Accompanying ethnologist Vincenzo Petrulo recorded on the east bank of the Kuluene River the Kalapalo account: three white men, led by Nahukua guides, arrived at the village; the natives gave them food, advised against continuing, but ferried them across the river the next day; two young men had ulcers from black fly bites and clearly did not want to proceed. For five days, the natives saw smoke from the overnight campfire; on the sixth day, the smoke stopped. Petrulo concluded that Fawcett was not killed but died of thirst or disease in the dense forest east of the river. He added: Dyott never heard this account because he had no interpreter, and the natives didn't like him.

— Holly Frey
29:44

The coordinates were off by 200 miles, yet archaeology sided with him

In 1951, Villas-Boas claimed to have found Fawcett's bones from the Kalapalo, but tests proved they were not his. In 1953, Brian published his father's 'Exploration Fawcett', and people immediately noticed a coordinate discrepancy: Fawcett's publicly announced Dead Horse Camp coordinates were 13°14'S, 54°35'W, but those sent to Nina were 11°43'S, 54°35'W—about 200 miles apart, still unknown whether a typo or deliberately wrong to deter followers. Modern archaeology partially confirms his intuition: in 2008, Michael Heckenberger published collaborative work with the Kuikuro people showing that complex settlement networks existed in Mato Grosso; today, descendants of the tribe also insist their ancestors did not kill Fawcett, and related accusations were further clarified in David Grann's 'The Lost City of Z'.
