Kids Melt Down the Moment They Get Home — Not Because They've Gone Bad, But Because They Finally Feel Safe

A child dumping the whole day's stress on the people closest to them is normal and healthy; what a parent needs to wear at that hour isn't the play-along "party hat" but the boundary-holding "professional hat" — and that is the real quality time.

1:07

Evening meltdowns are the norm, not something you did wrong

Janet's first piece of advice is to expect it. The child is tired, you're tired, this is the moment of reconnection, and it often feels like the longest stretch of the day. She stresses that this isn't your imagination, isn't unique to your family, and isn't something wrong with you or your child. Being caught off guard makes it far harder. Looked at the other way, a child unloading their emotions onto the person closest to them is itself a positive signal — it means he treats you as a safe base. Other transitions in family life amplify the difficulty: you or your partner starting a new job, the end of a holiday and having to get back into rhythm, a new baby in the house, or a one- to two-year-old sibling starting to walk and talk and becoming a new threat to the older child.

— Janet Lansbury
4:15

In the evening you wear the professional hat, not the party hat

In No Bad Kids, Janet proposes that parents wear two hats. The party hat is sitting beside your child watching him play, playing together, laughing together, eating family dinner together, doing a gentle bedtime routine — usually what gets called "quality time." But during the evening crazy hour, what the child needs is the professional hat: holding the boundary, not just saying it but actually stopping things; expecting that the child will lose control, so you walk in not already overwhelmed; allowing the child to nag and pester repeatedly, reading it as his way of sharing the day's stress, without giving in just to soothe him. Janet says this is quality time too, maybe even more important than party-hat time, because it's harder — harder because you're giving love and getting no love back, and you have to keep acting out of love without any acknowledgment from your child.

— Janet Lansbury
7:28

Hold the boundary and still leave room for the feeling

The third point is to leave the child lots of space for his emotions while you hold the boundary, and to acknowledge them as much as you can. Not just "I can't let you do that," but at the same time: you're having a hard time right now, this really is hard, you really want me to say yes, and I said no, so you're frustrated. Janet stresses that this isn't only because it's positive parenting — it's because it's practical: when a child feels seen, heard and safe inside his emotions, those emotions pass faster. If he doesn't need to keep showing you his feelings through his behavior, he doesn't have to keep performing them for you. She calls this the real quality time, and says that if you can see it that way, you'll walk into these situations with more confidence and more inner calm.

— Janet Lansbury
9:32

You can give up family dinner; you can't give up connection

Janet acknowledges that family dinner has a lot of research behind it, but there's also a fair amount of research pointing to the negative effects of parents being under chronic stress. She suggests lowering expectations around perfect quality time and family dinner, because the most valuable part of family dinner is simply that you carve out a little time to connect with your child in an accepting way — and that can happen at an after-school picnic, in the bath, in the bedtime routine. She gives her own example: when her kids were small they often ate around five o'clock, just her and the kids, because her partner got home late; as they grew up they all had sports and after-school activities, and on many weekdays there was no family dinner, so she spent one-on-one time with each child. She says her kids are still living up to their potential in every way, and the family is close. So this isn't make-or-break; there's still time for family dinner later, and maybe breakfast or the weekend is the slot that fits your family.

— Janet Lansbury
13:39

Audio stories beat screens for the evening hours

The fifth point is to build a new plan together — imperfect, but workable for everyone: eat when hungry, rest when tired, have downtime, let him wander, bounce off walls, cry if he needs to. You accept it, acknowledge it, even try to empathize, but you don't try to correct the behavior. If this family allows videos, pick very slow, very relaxing, low-stimulation content. Janet says she personally has always prioritized audiobooks, and there are plenty of podcasts for kids now, because they have many advantages over screens: they help develop real listening skills, train a child to notice details and build memory, especially when the same story is heard over and over; and they stimulate imagination and visualization. She also mentions a physical book with accompanying audio that a child can hold, which helps him learn to follow the page and can even help with learning to read.

— Janet Lansbury
15:40

He holds it together at school, so he can only fall apart at home

One mother wrote in that her three-and-a-half-year-old son is described at preschool as friendly, caring, making friends — but the moment he gets home he goes wild: running up and down, climbing walls, throwing toys, and what undoes her most is that he hits and kicks his eighteen-month-old sister. Janet's response: look at the school behavior first — if a child has been in school a while and is still coming home with this much stress, you need to check whether the school experience is overwhelming him; but this child is getting a good report, which means he's holding it together at school and falling apart only at home. So none of this is personal. If the parents react strongly to the conflict between him and his sister, what he feels isn't safety but that he's already in trouble and something bad is coming, which is frightening in itself. Janet suggests dialing the reaction down, because aggression toward the sister is very typical behavior at this age, and he knows it gets a strong reaction from his parents — but it's actually a cry for help.

— Janet Lansbury
18:43

Swap "you did wrong" for "let's figure this out together"

Janet suggests sitting down with the child in a quiet moment and saying: this is what I see, you're having a really hard time when you get home, those feelings make you want to run wild, what can we do to help you? She says that by opening this way, instead of "you did wrong and I want you to fix it," you're actually joining him: this is our home, I want to help, let's solve it together. You see him, and you understand he isn't doing this on purpose — most of the time it genuinely isn't within his control. Then you can give him a small window of time and ask how he wants to use it, how he'd like to be helped when you can't be with him, what activities work for him. Maybe he needs a physical outlet, some kind of bouncy equipment. But the most important thing is the overall attitude: yes, you're wound up right now, but we expected it, we're calm, we're not angry at you — that's what changes behavior.

— Janet Lansbury
22:50

When a child is too tired, dinner isn't a boundary you can hold

The second mother has two daughters, five and six, and the younger one just started kindergarten; after school she comes home wild, silly, exhausted, bossy, oppositional. The family already has an after-school snack ready and lets her go to her room for a while, but she can't regulate before dinner; at dinner she lies on the chair, refuses to eat, cries, gets sent to her room, and the family rarely manages to sit at the table together. Janet says this mother is very good at self-reflection and has already answered many of her own questions. She points out that even without the snack, exhaustion often overrides hunger, and sitting down to eat at that hour is simply hard for her. She suggests trying an earlier dinner, maybe just you and the two daughters, or just you and the younger one; family time can happen differently, on the weekend, or — as this mother herself said — you can just drop the expectation while she adjusts to school. Janet specifically points out that getting a child to come eat a very late dinner is not a boundary you can hold, because the child is tired and not hungry, and that's not within your power.

Glossary

party hat / professional hat
Janet's metaphor: the party hat is playing and laughing along, the professional hat is holding boundaries and being the leader — the evening calls for the latter.
yes time
The letter-writer's phrase for letting the child do whatever he wants in his room, a no-saying-no downtime.
