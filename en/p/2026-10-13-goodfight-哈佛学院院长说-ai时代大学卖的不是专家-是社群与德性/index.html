Harvard's Dean: Universities No Longer Sell Expertise—They Sell Character and Community

Universities once charged for knowledge and expert status. Now that AI can replace expert functionality itself, what keeps students are the character and collaboration skills only community can teach.

1:00

Universities shifted from stewarding collections to building community

Deming notes that Harvard's founding gift consisted of 400 books donated by John Harvard—roughly two-thirds of Emmanuel College Cambridge's collection at the time, yet sufficient to establish the university. The first era of universities was the age of scarce knowledge collections; when knowledge itself became abundant, universities reorganized around disciplinary expertise. Now that AI can package expert knowledge into accessible products, the scarce resource universities possess is character and habit—things that can only be learned within a community.

— David Deming
6:00

You can't fully trust research from AI labs

Deming argues universities should remain research centers for two reasons. First, research at cutting-edge AI labs is influenced by commercial interests: companies are unlikely to publish results that damage their bottom line, making it hard to fully trust such work's source and filtering. Second, teaching and research are naturally complementary—explaining a concept to someone who knows less is the best test of whether you truly understand it. This complementarity is partly why top talent chooses university positions over full-time lab work.

— David Deming
10:00

Universities face a shrinking pipeline, not an AI crisis

Deming acknowledges severe pressure on the university business model but argues the root cause is declining high school graduation rates, not AI. Small private colleges in the Northeast will be eliminated and the sector will consolidate further, but public universities may actually strengthen. Four-year university enrollment for 18-25-year-olds has actually risen slightly in recent years. What is genuinely volatile is two-year community college enrollment—which moves counter-cyclically, falling when jobs are plentiful and rising when the economy weakens. Unemployment has remained consistently low.

— David Deming
19:00

Faculty lean left by structural sorting, not by conspiracy

Mounk raises a structural issue deeper than ‘campus political correctness’: fifty years ago, education level barely correlated with voting patterns—the educated even leaned slightly Republican. Today, in virtually every developed democracy, education correlates strongly with leftward politics. The more educated, the more left-leaning. This means the faculty population and the hiring pool themselves are structurally left-leaning, separate from whether individual professors bring politics into the classroom—and far harder to correct.

— Yascha Mounk
31:00

Tenure doesn't change people; by then it's too late

Deming argues that tenure's purpose is to be ‘truly used’—to take research risks or repay the academic community. He himself spent ten years in administrative service after earning tenure. Mounk counters: getting tenure now averages fifteen to twenty years, during which people are trained that ‘publishing another paper matters more than daring to speak.’ By the time they actually possess that security, they've likely already lost the instinct to use it.

42:00

Tests either lock AI out completely or design it in

This semester Deming is pursuing a barbell strategy: on one end, completely block AI by reverting to pen-and-paper blue book exams with no electronic devices. Harvard's Ec10 (Introduction to Economics) has already lowered the weight of term papers and increased midterms from one to three. On the other end, accept that students will use AI when writing long essays. Rather than pretend to enforce a ban that punishes honest students, redesign assignments to incorporate AI into the workflow itself.

— David Deming
49:00

Near-perfect homework masks hollowed-out learning

Brown University's economics course experienced what looked like a ‘cheating scandal’: the professor hadn't explicitly ruled out AI, and suspiciously polished work arrived, with many scores clustering near perfect. When he hastily administered a no-AI final exam, many high-scoring students performed poorly. Deming calls this not cheating but mass ‘learning shortcuts’—students saw someone else's correct answer and mistook comprehension of it for their own understanding. Their grasp was fundamentally brittle.

— David Deming
51:00

Blue books can't measure the work of revision and refinement

Mounk agrees with the barbell strategy's direction but identifies a gap: writing ability isn't producing a passable essay in three hours—it's the process of repeated revision, demolition, and rewriting. That's what blue book exams cannot test. His solution: separate them. Some courses retain pure handwritten closed-book exams; others explicitly permit students to freely use AI on the final paper.

— Yascha Mounk

Glossary

Collegium
A medieval teaching community where students and teachers lived together to learn collectively, which Deming identifies as universities' original organizational model.
Barbell strategy
A polarized approach to AI assessment: either completely prohibit AI through closed-book exams, or openly integrate AI into assignments.
Blue book exam
The traditional American university closed-book handwritten exam without electronic devices, often used to prevent AI-assisted writing.
Tenure track
The long evaluation and promotion pathway from doctoral degree to earning permanent faculty status.
Intellectual vitality
Harvard's metric for assessing whether the campus enables free expression of disagreement and rational debate.
