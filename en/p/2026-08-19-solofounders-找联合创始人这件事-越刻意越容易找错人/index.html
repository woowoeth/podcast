Looking for a co-founder is how you end up with the wrong one

Treat a co-founder as a precondition for starting up, and you usually end up settling for a ‘co-founder of convenience’. Four solo founders explain from experience: run on your own first, and the right person shows up along the way.

2:07

Treat a co-founder as a precondition and you will settle

Julian says it plainly on the show: many people believe ‘you must have a co-founder to start’, so they treat finding one as a precondition for starting up — and that makes them more likely to end up with a bad co-founder. SFP's position is not anti-co-founder; quite the opposite, they think a good co-founder is fantastic. The problem is the order: having an idea first and then going to find someone to fill the slot, versus running first and meeting the right person, produce completely different outcomes. Aldo is the latter — he worked through four different ideas before he met his current co-founder.

— Julian
3:08

A co-founder need not be a CEO and a CTO

Aldo's current company is about ‘turning any computer into a data centre’. His co-founder was originally his angel investor, then volunteered to be his founding engineer, and only later was upgraded to co-founder. The two define the relationship unusually: they don't see themselves as CEO and CTO but as ‘artist, producer’, switching roles whenever the company needs it. The detail shows the shape of a co-founder relationship can be custom-built — there is no need to force it into a standard template.

— Aldo
7:13

Retention is growth, not an optimisation after growth

The line Aldo repeats is ‘retention is growth’. Chai says Aldo taught him which numbers to look at and what a smile curve is, lessons he still uses every day. More important is Aldo's criticism of ‘growth is good so retention can wait’: when growth is good you feel you can ignore retention, but the moment growth stops you regret not having watched it. Many founders, while growth is good, just pour on more spend and leave everything else to luck — and once luck is not on your side, you fall as fast as you rose.

— Aldo
12:27

Pull on the thread, don't stop at the second-best answer

Chai first chose between two directions: travel and software reliability. Because travel had an existing audience and distribution, he built an AI email client for travel first, and quickly found the direction extremely hard to scale. The second idea came from watching user sessions to find pain points, and that too proved hard to scale. He still isn't sure whether those ideas were ‘bad ideas’ — only that he didn't want to do them. Eventually he went back to software reliability and made one key flip: instead of guessing top-down how customers will use your software, he has the software simulate every possible customer action and production scenario, turning reliability into a compute problem.

— Chai
20:35

Tell real feedback apart from fake heat signals

Nemanja says the most important thing he learned at SFP was distinguishing which feedback is worth iterating on and which is just a misleading heat signal. His example is concrete: a Twitter post getting hundreds or thousands of likes doesn't mean you should pay attention to it. If you have free users and free heat but no conversion to paying customers, you are running a free trend, not a business. Every piece of feedback Aldo gave him was the same question: what would make someone pay you 10x more? How do you make it worth 10x to a large enterprise?

— Nemanja
23:43

A co-founder failure can destroy everything

Nemanja saw a counter-example: that company had an excellent idea, a huge market, money from top investors, marquee customers — every condition was perfect — and it collapsed entirely because the co-founder was wrong, and the company shut down. Much of the conversation he heard was ‘these things should have been talked through before starting the company’. That is what made him decide not to force a co-founder search. Aldo's two experiences confirm it: at his first company his co-founder was his dad, he was 17, and the power dynamic was very strange; the second was with a close university friend who spoke at his wedding, but being too close made hard conversations hard, and the co-founders eventually split.

— Nemanja
29:02

You don't have to wait until you start to find people

Aldo's current co-founder is 20 years older than him and was originally his first employee. Aldo says what excites him most is that someone far more experienced, who could have done it alone, chose to trust his vision. His advice: you don't have to wait until you start a company to look for people; the best thing is to bring the right people into the company. This matters especially for solo founders — run on your own first and meet the right person along the way, which is more reliable than locking in a co-founder at the start.

— Aldo
32:08

Solo together means five co-founders

At the end the show asks each of them what ‘solo together’ means to them. Nemanja says it means always having a group of geniuses behind you — for a technical problem or an investment negotiation, there is always someone who gives the right advice and understands his panic. Chai says it's like having five co-founders, because everyone is on a similar journey at different stages, and the problems they hit are ones you will hit sooner or later. Dravya says he did four years as a solo founder because he didn't want to go through the pain of a co-founder split again, and this place feels like family — nobody competes with each other, because the real opponent is the market and the customer.

— Nemanja

Glossary

leaky bucket
A model in which users constantly churn and growth only holds if you keep acquiring new ones — the point being that retention matters more than acquisition.
smile curve
The curve of retention rate over time: it falls first and then rises again, shaped like a smile.
SFP
Solo Founders Program — a programme that puts solo founders together in San Francisco for three months to build companies.
ODF
Open Dev Fund — a one-week programme that several early SFP members started from.
