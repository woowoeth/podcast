The Other M in M&M's Is Actually Hershey's CEO's Son's Last Name

Forrest Mars observed soldiers in the Spanish Civil War carrying heat-proof sugar-coated chocolate, returned to America, partnered with Hershey's CEO's son, and turned this insight into M&M's.

2:05

A child's paralysis became the unlikely origin of a candy dynasty

Frank Mars was born to a Minnesota farm family and contracted polio as a child. The disease left him unable to do the physical work of farm life like other children, so he spent his time indoors. His mother seized the opportunity to teach him to make handmade chocolates and candy during these years—an experience often cited as the spark for his lifelong interest in confectionery.

4:07

A wife's car was the distribution edge that made early candy sales scalable

Frank started making cream candy in his kitchen in Tacoma and sold it locally, but it was his wife Ethel who truly accelerated growth. She bought a car and drove it around the region selling candy. In 1911, when only about 0.66% of Americans owned registered vehicles, having an automobile represented an enormous advantage in distribution. Yet Frank himself lacked business sense, and the company eventually went bankrupt.

6:10

A malted milk idea at a drugstore counter created a blockbuster candy bar

In 1923, Frank and his son Forrest were drinking malted milk at a drugstore counter when Forrest suggested turning the malted drink into a candy bar form. Frank acted on the idea and created Milky Way. The bar became a runaway success, generating $800,000 in sales its first year and forcing the company to hire an entire dedicated sales team to field retailer orders.

7:13

A possibly-dead horse gave Snickers both its name and Depression-era bestseller status

Snickers was allegedly named after the Mars family's favorite horse, though even the official website hedges with ‘allegedly’ and the animal may have already died before the candy was introduced, leaving the full story unverifiable. Yet in 1930, during the depths of the Great Depression, the five-cent Snickers with its peanuts defied economic collapse and became a runaway hit, generating $26 million in first-year sales.

12:23

Denied partnership equity, Forrest left with $50,000 to build Mars bar in Europe

After completing his industrial engineering degree at Yale, Forrest saw his father's chaotic management and weak finances. When he pushed through reforms without permission, he infuriated Frank. Forrest demanded one-third of the company to develop overseas markets—Frank refused. In 1932, Forrest left with $50,000 and the foreign rights to Milky Way. He first apprenticed at Nestlé in Switzerland to learn the trade, then relocated to Slough, England, where he created a richer, more meticulously formulated candy bar that became the Mars bar.

16:27

Spanish Civil War soldiers' heat-proof chocolate inspired M&M's original design

While traveling during the Spanish Civil War, Forrest observed soldiers carrying sugar-coated chocolate beans that would not melt in heat. This observation inspired his vision for M&M's. His business partner was R. Bruce Murray, whose father was the CEO of their rival Hershey, which is why the two M's in M&M's represent Mars and Murray respectively. Like Snickers before it, M&M's initially supplied military rations before entering the civilian consumer market.

18:31

He closed the executive dining room and worked twelve-hour days with factory staff

After his father died, Forrest and his stepmother feuded over ownership for 30 years before he finally convinced her to sell. When the two companies merged, Forrest made his mark immediately: he shuttered the executive dining room and ate in the same cafeteria as factory workers. He also sharply raised wages and distributed profits to all employees—but he demanded they match his own twelve-hour workdays. By summer 2026, the Mars family's wealth reached $129 billion, making them America's third-richest family according to Forbes.

29:55

WWII sugar rationing accidentally preserved Reese's while eliminating all other candies

During World War II, sugar was placed under strict rationing. The H.B. Reese company had produced several candy varieties, but rationing meant only Reese's Cups could continue production—peanuts faced no restrictions, required less sugar than other candies, and the wartime labor shortage allowed the factory to automate. When H.B. Reese died in 1956, the new factory was incomplete; his sons disagreed about whether to sell the company. In 1963, they sold to Hershey for $24 million in Hershey stock.
