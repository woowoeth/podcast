No Irreplaceable Leaders, Only Irreplaceable Luck

Diamond says history has no irreplaceable leaders — Genghis Khan just happened to be born on the wettest steppe in two thousand years. But people like Rachel Carson and Resnick really did move things up by ten or twenty years.

1:09

No irreplaceable leaders, only irreplaceable timing

Diamond's answer to ‘who is the most irreplaceable leader in human history’ is ‘no one.’ Not because leaders don't matter, but because time washes out the difference: Jesus was a charismatic founder, but the Roman Empire of the same period had half a dozen other prophets; Genghis Khan built the largest land empire from China to Poland, but recent research found that the wettest period on the steppe in the past two thousand years happened to fall right around his birth — had he been born twenty years earlier or two hundred miles farther away, we would never have known his name. From this Diamond distinguishes two kinds of questions: why you and I speak English right now has its answer in the domesticable plants and animals of the Fertile Crescent, nothing to do with individuals; why Amazon is what it is today has its answer in Bezos alone.

— Jared Diamond
13:50

Rachel Carson moved environmentalism up by ten years

Cowen challenges Diamond with the environmental Kuznets curve: once per capita income hits 28,000, countries naturally turn green — is Carson replaceable? Diamond concedes the movement would have come anyway, but stresses she at least moved it up by about ten years — the day after her Silent Spring was published, a senator announced a government investigation into toxic chemicals. More crucial was her position at the time: in that era a professional scientist writing popular science was seen as ‘selling out,’ and she was not a university professor, so no one could say that of her; she was a woman, wrote like a poet, and would read her drafts aloud to hear them. Diamond says that even today's best science writer, Richard Dawkins, doesn't write as well as she did.

— Jared Diamond
18:00

The pomegranate juice industry waited five thousand years to appear

Cowen tests Diamond's replaceability theory with the pomegranate juice industry: without the Resnick family, America would eventually have gone down this road anyway. Diamond says no. Pomegranates were domesticated five or six thousand years ago, but there was never a global pomegranate juice industry, because pomegranate juice is extremely hard to make — you cut an orange and squeeze, but the pomegranate's value is inside an extremely hard rind, and the contents aren't pulp but small seeds; scaled production requires a three-story-high tower that crushes whole pomegranates rind and all. In 2002 America had 22 pomegranate growers, 21 of them small farmers with no money and no motive. Lynda Resnick put in about two hundred million dollars and waited 18 years before starting to make money. Companies like Coca-Cola and Nestlé would never allow a division to invest 150 million dollars and not be profitable for 18 years.

— Jared Diamond
29:32

Face-to-face social skill is in cliff-edge decline

Diamond's advice to a 25-year-old who wants to become influential: learn to talk to people face to face, put down indirect communication tools. He has done 60 years of fieldwork in Papua New Guinea and watched Chevron bring in international lawyers from Switzerland to negotiate with villagers who had never left their village — the lawyers thought they'd close it in a day, but the agreement reached on day one, on day two the villagers said ‘we forgot that agreement, the people who signed it don't count, these are a few other negotiators.’ The villagers had negotiated face to face their whole lives, with far more skill than the international lawyers. Diamond says that over the past 40 to 50 years he has watched social skills degrade, and in his 39-year-old child's generation it is especially striking, because most communication is no longer face to face and people no longer understand each other.

— Jared Diamond
33:54

New Guineans may be smarter than Westerners

Diamond answers ‘yes’ outright, and says some people hate him for it. His argument is that natural selection pushed in different directions: over the past ten thousand years European survival depended mainly on surviving infectious diseases like smallpox, tuberculosis and measles, which has little to do with being smart; the main causes of death in New Guinea were coping with an extremely difficult environment and coping with other New Guineans — warfare there was constant. So his impression is that New Guineans on average ask more questions and are smarter. He adds that Westerners usually think they're stupid because they use stone tools — but they lack metal tools because agricultural output was far lower, not because of intelligence. Cowen presses him on a Nature paper claiming West Eurasians have gotten smarter since the Stone Age; Diamond says he hasn't seen it, but for more than a century eugenicists have been trying to prove Europeans are smarter, with no evidence at all.

— Jared Diamond
39:24

The Industrial Revolution happened in Britain because it's an island

Diamond rejects Greg Clark's hypothesis that the British became more patient by intermarrying and lowering their time preference. His explanation is geography: Britain is an island, needs no standing army, and is not constantly invaded — the last successful invasion was 1066. Not needing an army, it gradually developed democracy, and democracy is a productive form of government. Britain also sits on the Atlantic coast, and like the Netherlands, Spain and Portugal, it was one of the countries able to spread across the world, because the Atlantic coast was the testing ground for seafaring technology. He mentions that in the 1600s the British cut off their king's head and Parliament became powerful. On Daron Acemoglu and Jim Robinson's extractive vs inclusive institutions theory, Diamond says he has learned more from Jim Robinson than from any individual in the past 20 years, but thinks they give geography less weight than he does.

— Jared Diamond
44:32

Big business and young people have made him cautiously optimistic

Diamond says that 20 years ago he would have said the most evil and destructive force in the world was big business, which at the time basically didn't care about the environment. But over the past 20 years big business has come to care more and more: Walmart leads on sustainable products, Unilever leads on sustainable fisheries, even oil companies. In 1999 the World Wildlife Fund sent him to do an environmental study of Chevron's oil field in Papua New Guinea; he expected it to be terrible, but found Chevron extremely cautious, because they knew that if they screwed up, New Guineans would sue them, and they wouldn't get the Norwegian oil field development contract either. Another source of optimism is young people: 25 years ago young people didn't care about the environment, now they care very much. The probability he gives is 51% good outcome, 49% bad.

— Jared Diamond
47:46

The most important question is not to find the most important question

Cowen asks what the biggest environmental problem is at the global macro level, and Diamond laughs and says this is what makes interviewing Diamond frustrating: his answer is always ‘the most important thing is not to go looking for that one most important thing, because the world is complicated.’ He uses marriage as an analogy: a couple about to marry asks what the single most important thing for a happy marriage is, and the answer is not to go looking for that one most important thing — you need to get 37 things right, and if you get 36 right but disagree on sex or religion, the marriage breaks. Back to the question itself, he says there are three things we must get right: climate change, inequality, and nuclear risk — including the risk of nuclear war and nuclear winter.

— Jared Diamond
