Every time Diddy changed his name, he washed away a batch of old scandals

Each of Sean Combs's renamings over thirty years was a brand reboot: control the room, control the guest list, control the narrative, and clear out the old scandals one by one — until Cassie Ventura's lawsuit finally couldn't be covered up.

This is not a retelling of a celebrity's fall. It takes apart a liability-shielding machine that ran for thirty years: who stayed silent, who benefited, and how even the reporter herself ends up asking whether she was complicit.

Pop culture had been telling this as a joke for years

More than two decades before Combs was arrested, pop culture was already making jokes about his parties and his temper. The host plays two clips: in the 2000 film Scary Movie, Regina Hall's Brenda describes how Puff Daddy's pool parties get out of control; in a 2008 Mad TV parody of Hannah Montana, P. Diddy films everything on a camera phone. There were earlier warning signs too: a record company executive blacklisted him over his behavior toward women, someone at Howard said they had seen him beat a woman with a belt outside his girlfriend's dorm, and on tour his entourage left behind a stolen handgun. Roundtree's judgment is that this was essentially background noise in the industry — it was just that his spectacle was bigger than every individual incident that surfaced along the timeline, and it covered all the allegations.

8:17

He was the gatekeeper of New York culture

Roundtree says that in that period he was simply the gatekeeper of culture in New York. The people who wanted in weren't only music people; they were power brokers, models, actors. He controlled who got in and controlled the atmosphere of the room, so everyone had to court him. She points out specifically that this network was cashable: being able to control the environment and make people ingratiate themselves for access meant he later turned those connections into business deals, and into that identity of "everyone wants to be around him." And early on all of it was wrapped in the language of "relax, hang out with people in the industry" — it was only the later civil suits that reclassified it: the plaintiffs say those settings were where they were sexually assaulted.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
10:21

Success itself was his shield

Mosley runs through his 2000s empire — records, clothing, vodka, restaurants, television and film — and notes that he and Trump, whom he publicly admired, are the same model: after every scandal he didn't just survive, he got bigger. Roundtree dates the starting point to the City College incident: a charity basketball game he helped promote to raise money for HIV/AIDS was oversold, a stampede broke out, and nine people died. The investigation found him partly responsible for inadequate security, and he paid a large settlement to the victims' families, but reputationally and professionally it was "business as usual." She says his success protected him all along — first Uptown Records covered for him because he had already proven his value, and later so did Mary J. Blige's success.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
18:47

Nine albums signed, one album released

Cassie signed to Bad Boy at 19 and was with Combs for nearly 11 years. Roundtree says the relationship was unconventional: the two never lived together, and she was never included in his family holidays, which was itself a major point of friction between them. She released only one album but was signed for nine more. Sources told Roundtree that Cassie was always making music and always wanted to continue her own music career, but that Combs would deliberately stall singles and albums, using power over her career as a weapon — when they fought he would say, "Your show is canceled. You're not getting your album. This isn't coming out." After a demo that was supposed to be her follow-up album leaked, The Guardian's review said it would have been album of the year.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
24:08

The jury believed the abuse, not the coercion

The prosecution's core theory was that these hotel sexual encounters constituted sex trafficking because the women were coerced into participating. For Cassie, they argued physical violence was the coercive means of keeping her compliant; for the woman code-named Jane, they argued the financial support itself was the coercion. Roundtree thinks coercion is extremely hard to prove. After the verdict a juror came out to explain, and at least one said she believed Cassie's account of domestic abuse but couldn't prove his intent to coerce them, and that's where the charge stalled. The way Jane entered the case is telling too: she never filed a civil suit; the prosecution found her messages in Combs's texts three days after Cassie sued, and she wrote that it felt like reading her own sexual trauma — and she had previously been cooperating with Combs's defense lawyers.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
26:13

This is also a workplace story

Roundtree repeatedly stresses that the prosecution never made this clear: it is also a workplace story, and nearly all the main witnesses had worked for Combs. As employees they were forced to witness domestic abuse, felt threatened themselves, and feared being blacklisted by the industry if they spoke up. Strip away the lurid packaging and it was an environment that pushed employees into the role of personal assistants serving the boss's sex life — supplying drugs, cleaning up humiliating hotel rooms, covering up his domestic abuse of his girlfriend. Their ambitions were to make music and learn the business; their lives revolved around this instead. People who tried to report it or leave were fired, pushed out; some say they were pinned against a car hood, shoved out a door, their arm slammed into a wall. She says it is precisely these consequences that produced a culture of silence.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
32:37

Without co-conspirators, there is no criminal enterprise

The government called it a criminal enterprise, but only Combs was charged. His defense lawyer asked in return: if there was a conspiracy, where are the co-conspirators? Roundtree says the absence of co-conspirators testifying is the prosecution's biggest hole. When you hear words like "conspiracy" and "organized racketeering," what comes to mind is the mob, a big organization with big guns; and when the picture is drawn as one man, his assistant, his old bodyguard friend he grew up with, plus a few fresh college graduates as the co-conspirators, it's hard to line up the concept of "organized racketeering" with "that's it?" Separately, more than twenty men have also accused Combs of sexual misconduct or assault, but their experiences essentially never entered the criminal case — Roundtree says that didn't fit the prosecution's working theory.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
39:54

The comeback script is already being written

Combs is expected to be released in early 2028. Roundtree mentions a Netflix documentary produced by 50 Cent that recently won an editing Emmy; it includes real-time footage from before his September 2024 arrest, in which he is planning his own documentary and comeback story. She says this confirms once again that he has always kept tight control of his image, and that we should expect the same after his release. There have also been recent reports that he is in touch with someone who does digital communications, so he can keep tabs on what social media says about him. Roundtree has no doubt that when he gets out, he will tell these years his own way.

— Cheyenne Roundtree
