The Roman Empire Wasn't Held Up by Its Legions, but by the Enslaved

Rome's administration, army, households, workshops and mines ran almost entirely on enslaved people; after Carthage fell in 146 BC slavery became the engine of the economy, and once Augustus stopped expanding, it reproduced itself through childbirth.

2:27

Pull slavery out and Rome's stool collapses

Emma answers the title head-on: the Roman Empire simply does not exist without slavery. From the bureaucracy to army pay, nearly everyone handling documents was enslaved or a freedman; hauling stone, building roads and keeping cities running all depended on enslaved labour. She uses a metaphor: take slavery out of the Roman whole and it is like pulling one leg off a three-legged stool — the whole thing cannot stand. She then stresses that the experience of the enslaved was deliberately fragmented, with people at different levels under the same roof competing rather than uniting, an order rulers intentionally maintained.

8:25

A freedman's freedom could be revoked at any time

Rome wrote slavery into law very early. The Twelve Tables already contained clauses on the sale of persons and on manumission; freedmen were legally neither free nor slave but a class in between, barred from public office, unable to make certain contracts, with limits placed on their children. More brutally, "insufficient gratitude" could be grounds for re-enslavement. Emma stresses that this design was no accident but an extension of Rome's bureaucratic character: humans were special property, but still property.

— Emma Southern
13:03

Calling them "slaves" is doing Rome's dehumanising for it

Emma argues the word "slave" is itself a dehumanising tool that turns people into an abstraction. She advocates "enslaved person", forcing the speaker to pause half a second and remember the other party is a living human being. She gives the example of a three-year-old boy's tombstone: the child died in an aristocratic household and was commemorated by his parents, who called themselves husband and wife — legally impossible for them; from the stone one can infer the mother's pregnancy, work and childbirth, even though no written record exists. Archaeology gives only fragments; the rest must be filled in by imagination.

— Emma Southern
19:59

Only after Carthage was destroyed did Rome become a slave economy

Emma marks the fall of Carthage as the key shift in the character of Roman slavery. Before that, Rome traded victories with the Samnites and Etruscans, and citizens of the losing side could also be enslaved, with populations moving between wars. After destroying Carthage, Rome swept the Mediterranean without a rival and the number of captives exploded; a few large estates worked by masses of enslaved labour turned Rome formally into a "slave economy" — one that could not have survived two hundred years without enslaved labour. The empire was essentially resource extraction: besides gold and silver, people were a resource being extracted.

— Emma Southern
26:37

The slave economy bought itself another 800 years through childbirth

After Augustus declared the Pax Romana and closed the doors of the Temple of Janus, Rome no longer waged large-scale wars of expansion, and the supply of captive slaves fell sharply. But Rome practised hereditary slavery — the child of an enslaved woman was born a slave. By then the enslaved population was large enough to sustain itself by breeding. Emma also notes explicit incentives: an enslaved woman who bore three sons could gain her freedom; daughters did not count. This self-reproduction kept the slave economy going for roughly another 800 years.

— Emma Southern
33:18

The worst slaves were the ones with no master

The enslaved were not all foreigners. Roman citizens could be sentenced to become gladiators or sent to the mines or quarries, a form of capital punishment. Emma calls this servus poenae (slaves of punishment): they had no particular master, so no one could show them favour, and there was never any possibility of improving their lot. Legally, only two categories of enslaved people were protected: first, the stewards and external representatives of prominent families or the imperial household; second, publicly owned state slaves — the latter guaranteed housing, food and a small wage by the city.

— Emma Southern
1:00:16

Buying your freedom was a path open only to men

Emma points out that older scholarship overestimated how common manumission was: the claim that "every slave was freed before 30" comes mainly from the experience of male bureaucrats and household slaves. There were in fact four routes: a master's gift, release by will, release after promotion in service, and buying it with one's own savings. Augustus restricted manumission by will: the person had to be named, and the proportion freed was capped. But buying freedom was almost exclusively open to men; Emma says she cannot think of a single example of a woman purchasing her freedom. Enslaved women could usually only be freed by marrying their former master, and were locked into a marriage very hard to leave.

— Emma Southern
1:05:54

A "good master's" favour could be revoked at the last moment

Emma closes with the case of Pedanius: this senator under Nero was assassinated by enslaved people in his household, and by law every enslaved person in the house had to be executed as collective punishment — 400 were crucified on that occasion. One motive Tacitus records is that Pedanius had first promised to let the killer buy his freedom, then reneged at the last moment. Emma uses this to show how fragile the Roman moral imagination of the "good master" was — favour at the highest level could be withdrawn at any time, and the price of resistance by the enslaved was dozens or hundreds of lives.

— Emma Southern

Glossary

manumission
The procedure by which a slave gained freedom, used more often in Rome than elsewhere in the ancient Mediterranean; freedmen remained between slave and free.
verna
A person born into slavery, considered more reliable and often holding higher positions within the household.
servus poenae
A person condemned to slavery, with no specific master, so no one could show favour and there was never any chance of escape.
dispensator
The slave in charge of finances and household affairs in a prominent Roman family, of high status and sometimes owning slaves himself.
freedmen
A class legally between free person and slave, barred from public office and from making certain contracts.
Pax Romana
The long period of stability begun by Augustus, in which wars of expansion declined and the source of slaves shifted from captives to household births.
