Drugs Treat the Symptom, Talk Treats the Cause: Freud Was Not Entirely Wrong

Neuropsychologist Mark Solms defends Freud: talk therapy still beats medication on efficacy, because the root of mental suffering is unmet emotional need, not a chemical imbalance.

Freud lingers because he got there first

Solms argues the primary reason Freud's influence persists is that ‘he got there first.’ He was the first to systematically try to describe and understand the subjective mind — not behaviour, language, perception or cognition. As the person who first drew the map of this territory, the basic cartography belongs to him, and everything since is just filling in details. Freud himself was convinced he was doing science, but science almost by definition excludes subjectivity, which made his enterprise difficult from the very start.

— Mark Solms
10:15

Cognitive neuroscience is retracing Freud's old path

Around 1900 Freud claimed that most mental activity is unconscious, which at the time was seen as an oxymoron, like saying ‘an unmental mental process.’ Today cognitive neuroscience not only acknowledges unconscious mental processes but holds that they make up the bulk of mental function, with consciousness only a tiny, limited resource. Solms points out that modern scientists independently discovered the same conclusion from scratch using different methods, yet often do not know they are retracing Freud's old path.

— Mark Solms
14:26

The mind can only be studied from inside, not observed as an object

What is distinctive about Freud's methodology is that he studied the mind ‘from within the mind,’ treating it as human existence itself rather than an object observable from outside. Twentieth-century behaviourism denied the existence of subjective states both methodologically and ontologically — the ‘psyche’ in the word psychology was excluded from psychology. Freud, by contrast, in the naturalistic setting of the psychoanalytic consulting room, directly recorded patients' reports as unreserved as they could make them, accumulating thousands of hours of the most direct observation of the mind, and then analysed the underlying organising principles.

— Mark Solms
33:43

The most effective psychiatric treatment of the 21st century is still talk

Solms claims that, taking all disorders together, the most effective psychiatric treatment of the 21st century is still talk therapy — this comes from meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials, not personal opinion. All modern talk therapies derive from Freud: the inventor of CBT, Aaron Beck, was himself a psychoanalyst. Compared with psychopharmacology, the gains from talk therapy are maintained on long-term follow-up, whereas symptoms often return after medication is stopped — proof that it reaches the cause rather than suppressing the symptom.

— Mark Solms
36:00

After therapy ends, patients keep getting better for years

Psychodynamic psychotherapy has a distinctive phenomenon: after treatment ends, patients not only maintain their symptom improvement but continue to improve at 6-month, 1-year, 2-year and 3-year long-term follow-up. Solms calls this the ‘sleeper effect’ and considers it evidence that the treatment reaches the causal mechanism. Medication is only symptomatic treatment — stop it and symptoms return; talk therapy changes the causal mechanism that produces psychopathology, and therefore brings lasting change.

— Mark Solms
40:00

Suppressing emotion is prescribing an analgesic for angina

Solms uses chest pain as an analogy: chest pain is a signal of myocardial ischaemia, and a doctor would not just give opium for the pain but treat the narrowed vessels. Likewise, fear stems from the need for safety, anger from a blocked need, separation distress from attachment needs. Emotions are pointers to unmet needs; they ‘mean something.’ Psychopathology arises because a person never learned in development how to satisfy some emotional need, and the goal of psychotherapy is to help the patient find a better way of satisfying it — that is treating the cause.

— Mark Solms
45:00

Insight alone changes nothing, because change happens subcortically

Gaining insight but being unable to change behaviour happens because deep unconscious processes depend on subcortical memory systems — the basal ganglia, cerebellum, amygdala and so on. Cognitive neuroscience has the saying that ‘non-declarative memory is hard to learn and hard to forget’; these structures have low plasticity and change slowly. So therapy is slow work and hard labour, and people always want to avoid it. Psychoanalytic therapy is precisely designed to handle this kind of problem, which cannot be changed by direct decision.

— Mark Solms
47:08

Cure comes through love, but not the romantic kind

‘Cure through love’ is Freud's phrase. Solms explains that this is not romantic love but parental care: a devotion that puts another's needs before one's own. When a person never learned in early life to satisfy some emotional need, the therapist must provide an opportunity to relearn it within the therapeutic relationship. This kind of ‘love’ means an enormous investment of time and energy — exactly the opposite of where healthcare funding and policy are heading today.

— Mark Solms
56:49

The most effective therapy cannot get traction, because it is slow and expensive

Although talk therapy is the most effective, the American healthcare system rarely adopts it. Solms reduces this to three points: treatment takes time and money; the pharmaceutical industry has vast commercial interests and a lobbying machine pushing drugs; and people generally like ‘fast and cheap’ solutions. Germany, Austria, Scandinavia and other countries fund psychoanalysis through the state. Medication as symptomatic treatment is sometimes life-saving and sometimes necessary, but ‘wanting to change your life with a single pill is simply impossible.’

Glossary

dual-aspect monism
The view that mind and brain are one entity seen from two perspectives, from inside and from outside; it originates with Spinoza.
sleeper effect
In psychoanalytic treatment, the phenomenon in which patients continue to improve after treatment has ended, suggesting the treatment reaches the cause.
non-declarative memory
Memory that cannot be put into words and is hard to learn and hard to forget, depending on subcortical structures such as the basal ganglia, cerebellum and amygdala.
psychical reality
A Freudian term for that part of reality which is human subjective experience, as distinct from externally observable reality.
