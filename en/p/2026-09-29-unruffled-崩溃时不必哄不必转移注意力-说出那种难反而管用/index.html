Respectful Parenting: Janet Lansbury Unruffled 2026-09-29

Don't soothe or distract during meltdowns—naming what's hard works better

Two mothers tried a counterintuitive suggestion: name the loss at transitions instead of soothing, distracting, or comforting. After weeks, both their children's door-slamming meltdowns and hair-pulling self-soothing noticeably decreased.

Door-slamming meltdowns emerge within three weeks of school transfer

One mother noticed her 4-year-old daughter saying goodbye happily at her previous school. After transferring to the same school where the mother teaches, the situation deteriorated within three weeks: first, complaints about the cafeteria being too loud and cold; then crying and chasing to the door; finally kicking, biting, and slamming the glass door. The school has asked the mother to skip early pick-up this week.

5:11

Visibility without access: the harder problem than simple separation

Janet highlights an unspoken dynamic the mother hadn't articulated: being in the same building as your primary attachment figure but having no access to her is harder than separation for a determined child. The mother confirmed that discussing this openly, plus letting her daughter choose which door to exit through and get a kiss goodbye, noticeably improved pick-ups. Her older sister began helping with classroom tasks.

7:15

Toddlers resort to hair-pulling when family transitions extend for weeks

A second mother writes that her 21-month-old daughter pulls her hair to self-soothe during frustration, exhaustion, or boredom. The family is managing a life transition that will last another 4–6 weeks. She's already coaching her daughter to say "I'm frustrated," and has tried ice rings, string toys, and pacifiers. But she admits it "really bothers me" to watch.

8:16

Naming the transition's difficulty visibly reduced meltdowns in both families

Janet gives both mothers identical counsel: speak directly to the hardship ("this change is hard," "you might miss the way things were") rather than instinctively soothing, distracting, or reassuring. The point isn't to elicit an answer—it's to let the child feel witnessed. Both mothers report back: naming the feeling itself noticeably reduced hair-pulling and pick-up meltdowns.

— Janet Lansbury
12:21

Parents' language shapes the emotion more than the event does

Janet raises a subtler mechanism: when a child reacts intensely, anxious parents often label it—"you're scared," "you're angry," "you're so upset you're falling apart"—but the child may have only been startled or surprised, something far milder. Parental language teaches children to inhabit emotions larger than reality warrants.

— Janet Lansbury
14:30

Replacing "scared" with specifics lets children approach what they feared

This mother realized "scared" and "afraid" were nearly her daughter's only emotion vocabulary. Reflecting those words back each time was cementing the fear. She shifted to "that bird startled you" or "that's a loud sound" instead of "you're scared." Her daughter stopped reporting fear as often and began approaching things she'd previously avoided—like walking past the parking lot hand-in-hand instead of asking to be carried.

15:31

When meltdowns are truly intense, the guidance becomes less clear

One mother raises a question Janet sidestepped: when the reaction is truly intense—shrieking, hard crying—should she still keep it measured ("wow, you really don't like that") while calmly scooping her up, or shift to something weightier? She hasn't yet figured out the right calibration for genuine meltdowns.

18:33

Witnessing and holding space, not fixing: the parent's real job

Janet's answer: children don't need parents to hit the perfect phrase or diagnose the emotion precisely. Often they need nothing but space to fully release the feeling, with parents ready to pick her up, or not, or simply let her cry on the floor. She frames the role as "present, caring, empathetic, but not here to fix the problem"—something that requires a lifetime of practice.

— Janet Lansbury
