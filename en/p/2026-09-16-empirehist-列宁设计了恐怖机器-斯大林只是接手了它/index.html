Lenin Built the Terror Machine; Stalin Just Took It Over

The secret police, the labour camps and the one-party system were all architecture Lenin left behind. What Stalin did was turn it into a personal instrument of power — the first thing he did as General Secretary was stuff his own people into key posts.

2:06

In 1917 Stalin was a man nobody noticed

The episode opens by recalling the impression left by the previous instalment: Stalin was a bad orator and a slow theorist, and nobody would have guessed he was the next dictator. Guest Giles Milton confirms that judgement and adds a key detail — Stalin rarely appeared in public, was not well known in Russia, and hated public speaking because his Georgian accent was so heavy. This explains how he was later able to accumulate power quietly: nobody treated him as a threat.

— Giles Milton
4:11

Churchill once wanted to wipe out the Red Army with gas

During the civil war, Britain, France, America and Japan all backed the Whites. Churchill, as Minister of Munitions in 1919, pushed Britain to send troops and weapons to Russia against the Reds. More striking still, Britain had developed a lethal chemical weapon late in the First World War, and Churchill thought it more humane than the bloody slaughter in the trenches, so he sent it to use against Trotsky's Red Army. Because of the atmospheric conditions in northern Russia the gas worked poorly, and the whole stock was eventually dumped into the White Sea. This history explains how hard it was when Churchill allied with Stalin in 1941 — he himself was one of the men who had wanted to get rid of Stalin back then.

— Giles Milton
9:27

The terror machine's architecture was Lenin's legacy

Many people put the Cheka, the labour camps and the one-party system all down to Stalin, saying he built them while serving as a political commissar in the civil war. Giles argues this is a complete misunderstanding of Lenin's role: it was Lenin who built this architecture, and Stalin merely took it later and used it for his own political career. The Cheka was the secret police, which later became the more familiar KGB — surveillance, arrest, torture, execution. The host sums up: when we look back at history we often say Stalin was a corrosive influence, that he brought the terror, but the architecture was in fact Lenin's bequest.

— Giles Milton
10:27

Tsaritsyn: Stalin arrested a front-line general

Trotsky appointed Snezarev, a general from the former tsarist army, to command the Tsaritsyn campaign — a typical Trotsky move of using old officers, and one that put him directly in conflict with Stalin. Stalin, as political commissar, arrested the general and all his subordinate officers and locked them on a barge on the Volga. It is said his intention was to sink the boat and drown them. When Trotsky found out he was furious and ordered the general released immediately. This was the first round in the struggle between Trotsky and Stalin, and Trotsky won it decisively. But Stalin later told the story as one of the great civil-war battles in which he personally went to the front, and Tsaritsyn was later renamed Stalingrad.

— Giles Milton
16:44

Stalin threatened to resign whenever he was unhappy

Giles tells a detail that does not fit Stalin's later image: whenever things went badly, for instance when he was not authorised at Tsaritsyn to liquidate Trotsky, he threatened to resign. He wrote a letter saying that without this, my work on the Southern Front would become meaningless and unnecessary, and this gave me the right or the obligation to go even to the devil rather than remain on the Southern Front. The Politburo read the letter with exhaustion and then said, no, Joseph, stay, we all love you. He was a sulker. The picture is one for readers to hold on to: the early Stalin used emotional blackmail, not terror.

— Giles Milton
29:12

Nobody thought much of the General Secretary's job at the time

In April 1922 Stalin was appointed General Secretary. Today the title means the highest power in the state, but at the time it was just a functional description, tedious work. Stalin understood: handled properly, the post gave him complete control of the party. He could plant his own people in key positions and clear out everyone who supported Trotsky. From the day he took it over he made full use of the position, and already had one eye on the future — Lenin's health was poor, he was thinking about who would succeed Lenin, and now there was a chance to fill the party with supporters so that when Lenin died he would be in an advantageous position.

— Giles Milton
32:13

Stalin's apology letter: says sorry without admitting fault

Stalin telephoned Lenin's household. Lenin was gravely ill, and Stalin accused Nadezhda of discussing politics with Lenin as he was dying, saying it was unforgivable. Nadezhda told Lenin, Lenin was furious and insisted Stalin write a letter of apology. The letter is superb: Stalin apologises without apologising — if you think I offended her, then I apologise, but I do not think I really offended her, so there is nothing to apologise for. Lenin grew even angrier. On 4 January 1923 Lenin wrote down his assessment of Stalin: Stalin is too rude, and this defect, entirely tolerable in our midst and in comradely relations, becomes intolerable in the post of General Secretary, and he therefore proposed that the comrades find a way to remove Stalin and replace him with someone else.

— Giles Milton
35:21

If Lenin had not died, Stalin would have put him in prison

Lenin's political life ended with his stroke, and he died ten days later. His wife, Nadezhda Krupskaya, said the most famous thing of all: if Lenin had not died in 1924, he would have been serving time in one of Stalin's prisons. She saw that Stalin was moving against Lenin as he rose. Giles thinks it very likely, because Stalin was absolutely ruthless towards anyone he believed was not on his side. Lenin wanted a simple burial; Stalin decided not to bury him but to embalm and preserve him and put him on permanent display. Within hours of Lenin's death they began draining the fluids and removing the organs, and the heart was never found again.

— Giles Milton

Glossary

Cheka
The Soviet secret police, later evolving into the KGB, responsible for surveillance, arrest and execution.
political commissar
A post in the Red Army ensuring a unit's political correctness; Stalin held this role during the civil war.
General Secretary
In 1922 an administrative party post, through which Stalin gained control over appointments.
White Russians
The armies that opposed the Bolsheviks during the civil war and were backed by the West.
