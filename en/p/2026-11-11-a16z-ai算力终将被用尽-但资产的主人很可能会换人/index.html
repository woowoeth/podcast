AI compute will be exhausted, but its owners probably won't be

In the last fiber-optic bubble, the most-indebted companies collapsed first, but the fiber assets kept working when others took them over. Mike Volpi believes this GPU buildout will follow the same script: the assets will be fully utilized, but their ownership may well change hands.

Mike Volpi led Cisco's acquisitions and network buildout in the 1990s and contrasts the capital structures, asset lifecycles, and M&A logic of the two infrastructure booms from firsthand experience.

4:09

Bandwidth jumped by technology breakthroughs; compute is stuck drinking from Moore's Law

In the 1990s, fiber-optic bandwidth multiplied 40-80x through WDM (wavelength-division multiplexing)—packing multiple colors of light down the same fiber. AI compute expansion is fundamentally different: constrained by Moore's Law, it grows slower. Volpi sees this as the root technical difference between the two buildouts—the pace of supply growth is not even close.

— Mike Volpi
6:14

Assets will be exhausted; their owners will likely not

Volpi reviews the Internet bubble: companies most burdened by debt collapsed first, but their fiber assets didn't disappear—others picked them up cheap and kept using them. He expects the same script to play out: data centers and chips will be fully utilized, even beyond expectations, but whoever ends up owning these assets may well not be the companies building them today.

— Mike Volpi
11:33

H100s should be obsolete; their rental price keeps rising

Four years ago, conventional wisdom said H100-class chips had about five years of useful life—and rented for roughly $1.82 per GPU-hour. By all rights they should have cheapened as they neared end-of-life, but instead they now rent for $2.50-3.00 per hour. Volpi attributes this to extreme compute scarcity: as long as semiconductor production lags demand, older chips stay needed, and asset lifecycles stretch far beyond what finance models assumed.

— Mike Volpi
14:43

Cisco's M&A philosophy: accept one-third total loss

Volpi recalls Cisco's acquisition method: split outcomes roughly into thirds—one-third worked great, one-third was mediocre, one-third failed outright. The company tolerated the failure rate as long as it did it often enough to hit several genuine home runs. Some acquisitions cost $500 million and generated $1 billion in profit within two years. By around 2000, half of Cisco's revenue came from acquired businesses, and no single acquisition ever exceeded 4-5% of the company's market cap.

— Mike Volpi
19:54

NVIDIA's spending on neocloud startups will fail to reshape the market

On NVIDIA's practice of investing in numerous neocloud companies to cultivate more buyers, Volpi argues it won't change the industry's final shape: in any sector where scale dominates, no amount of money pumped into small players will stop consolidation into a handful of giants—like how US aviation ended with three major carriers. NVIDIA does this not for financial returns but to avoid negotiating with only three or four mega-customers, but it's only delaying, not altering, the industry's natural evolution.

— Mike Volpi
34:09

Model routing emerges as token prices diverge 5-7x

For the past two years, the market default was to use only the most advanced frontier-model tokens. But Volpi observes the market growing smarter: top-tier Anthropic tokens and top-tier Kimi tokens differ by 5x, even 7x in price—a gap wide enough to spawn a new business called ‘model routers’ that split requests to different-priced models by task difficulty. He sees this making AI's total demand price-sensitive for the first time, instead of blindly chasing the most expensive model.

— Mike Volpi
43:27

Order bubbles burst: Cisco's market cap fell to one-tenth in six months

Volpi recounts Cisco's collapse: tight delivery windows led customers to habitually over-order to guard against allocation cuts, and Cisco transferred risk to suppliers by paying early to hoard stock. When true demand softened, orders dropped directly to one-quarter of what they'd been. Combined with risk already locked into the supply chain, the company missed 35-40% of quarterly revenue in a single quarter. Stock fell from over $80 to $9, a ninefold decline in six months.

— Mike Volpi
47:45

AI data centers should abandon stat-muxing for deterministic circuits

Internet-era switch design assumed bursty traffic, using stat-muxing to smooth peaks and valleys. AI data centers run GPUs at full load constantly, with highly predictable, nearly-deterministic traffic—especially in training. Volpi argues that today's mainstream Broadcom Tomahawk stat-muxing architecture is ‘enough bandwidth but wrong shape’—what should exist is a new switching architecture designed for full-time load, needing no buffers. This is classic innovator's dilemma: incumbent vendors won't cannibalize their own business. The opportunity goes to new entrants willing to all-in on the new architecture.

— Mike Volpi

Glossary

WDM
Wavelength-division multiplexing; packing multiple signal colors down a single fiber to multiply bandwidth.
Stat-muxing
Statistical multiplexing; traditional switching that shares bandwidth by smoothing traffic bursts.
Neocloud
Emerging GPU cloud providers that rent compute capacity.
KV cache
Key-value cache storing intermediate attention results in GPU memory during inference.
Model router
A system routing requests to different-priced models based on task difficulty.
