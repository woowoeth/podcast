AI bubbles aren't scary in themselves; what's scary is they're increasingly propped up by debt

Historical data shows equity-driven bubbles cause limited economic damage when they burst, but credit-driven bubbles break bones—and this year mega AI companies' debt issuance jumped from roughly $200 billion to $500 billion.

Stock markets are a side hustle, bond markets are the main business

Wigglesworth compares stock markets to trading Pokémon cards: the vast majority of trades are simply one person selling stock to another, with no impact on the company itself. Bond markets work differently—every day they finance new wastewater plants, roads, factories and other real projects, channeling capital through capitalism's most primitive mechanism. By scale, he notes, the US bond market is three to four times larger than the stock market; globally, it's even bigger than the entire banking system. That's why he calls this book the engine of global economics.

— Robin Wigglesworth
19:38

Wartime crisis accidentally invented the modern bond market

In 1171, when Venice and Byzantium went to war, the Doge levied a mandatory «war tax» on citizens proportional to their wealth. To quiet dissent, he promised 5% annual interest. The revolutionary moment came when these receipts could be freely bought and sold. Venice eventually lost that war and never repaid the tax; it became a de facto perpetual bond. But Venetians discovered that tradeable income streams had intrinsic value and could serve as collateral. The model spread: other Italian city-states copied it, then France, Spain, and Holland amplified it further.

— Robin Wigglesworth
27:53

Britain's vast debt burden defeated Napoleon himself

France under Napoleon vastly outmatched Britain militarily, but Britain, with its unified Consols market, could borrow almost unlimited sums and consistently fund allies like Prussia. France's weaker credit meant it paid interest rates two or three times higher. After the war, Britain carried the highest debt-to-GDP ratio in its history. Contemporary doomsayers predicted collapse, but historian Macaulay countered that government bonds could be used as collateral and circulate as money—that debt actually capitalized and strengthened the British economy. This concept is what gives the book its title, «fabulous debt.»

— Robin Wigglesworth
38:12

Railroad bonds built America and sparked a historic depression

After the Civil War, banker Jay Cooke pioneered advertising and circulating bond prospectuses to ordinary citizens, selling US government debt and railroad bonds to the masses. Without bond market finance, transcontinental railroads couldn't have been built. But this debt-fueled infrastructure boom collapsed in 1873: German and Austrian stock bubbles burst, European investors withdrew their US railroad bond holdings, and Cooke's pet project—the Northern Pacific Railroad, which he'd personally underwritten—ran out of money. The ensuing depression, initially called the «Great Recession» until fifty years later the term was replaced with «Long Depression», became the template for financial crises.

— Robin Wigglesworth
54:29

Rating agencies aren't as bad as they look

After 2008, rating agencies became synonymous with failure. But when Wigglesworth reviewed crisis retrospectives, he found something surprising: the actual ratings on RMBS and CMBS products were remarkably accurate. Prices collapsed because nobody trusted anything; when final losses were calculated, most ratings weren't far off actual performance. Only some AAA-rated tranches saw unexpected losses. The real problem wasn't grossly missed judgments—it was the fundamental conflict of interest: rating agencies sold ratings to the highest bidder, a structural problem that persists today.

— Robin Wigglesworth
1:00:41

AI investment may be mismatched again

Wigglesworth was once «profoundly relaxed» about AI bubbles, for two reasons: he doubted his own ability to judge them, and bubbles aren't necessarily bad—infrastructure outlasts them, just as railroads and fiber-optic cables survived their own crashes. His thinking shifted because this wave of AI investment increasingly relies on debt and off-balance-sheet obligations rather than tech giants' internal cash flows. Historically, credit-driven bubbles burst far more violently than equity-driven ones.

— Robin Wigglesworth
1:05:44

AI debt issuance doubled in a year

The numbers tell the story: mega cloud companies issued roughly $500 billion in debt this year, up from about $200 billion last year. Wigglesworth doesn't think disaster is imminent—these companies generate phenomenal cash flow, Meta, Amazon, YouTube print money continuously, and rating agencies now factor in off-balance-sheet commitments. But he flags Oracle as «relatively weak» in the herd, already showing stress. His read: «We're still at the foot of the mountain», with no guarantee that today's signals won't look like early warnings in hindsight years from now.

— Robin Wigglesworth
1:07:49

Ten years of borrowing equals two centuries

The US accumulated about $20 trillion in debt over its 240-year history, then borrowed another $20 trillion in the past decade alone. Wigglesworth traces this to «a series of self-inflicted errors»—massive tax cuts, discretionary wars, tariffs—not responses to genuine crises. He's skeptical of the standard debt-to-GDP ratio and prefers debt-service costs as a share of GDP: currently around 3.5%, versus 2.5% a few years ago. Historically, countries crack under stress around 5%; the CBO projects the US will hit that threshold in roughly ten to twelve years.

Glossary

Consols
Perpetual bonds—undated UK government debt once considered the world's safest asset.
Sukuk
Islamic bonds that pay no interest but deliver fixed returns through asset-lease structures.
Securitization
Bundling large loan portfolios into tradeable bonds and selling them to investors.
Bearer bond
Unregistered bonds that any holder can redeem for interest at any agent bank.
CBO
US Congressional Budget Office; the independent fiscal forecasting body that models future debt trajectories.
