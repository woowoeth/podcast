Yo-yo dieting doesn't wreck your metabolism; habits, not weight, decide lifespan

Weight cycling — yo-yo dieting — does not permanently break your metabolism, and in ordinary people the weight that comes back rarely costs you muscle. What actually shapes health and lifespan is the healthy habits themselves, not the swings in weight.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:37

Nine failed diets is not a failure of willpower

Researchers estimate that as many as half of American adults have been through at least one round of weight cycling, and among people who cycle, the average is 8 rounds by middle age. John, a drummer, is on his ninth attempt at losing weight; he says he has put on and taken off several hundred pounds over the course of his life, and every rebound leaves him feeling that he succeeded and then failed again, so he pins the blame on his own self-control. But as the episode goes on, the hunger hormones and metabolic machinery inside his body turn out to matter far more than willpower in why he cannot hold the loss.

12:47

The body claws the calories back below conscious awareness

Researchers gave one group of subjects a diabetes drug that flushes sugar out through the urine. The subjects lost calories and lost weight, and they did not know it was happening. The group on the drug got hungry sooner and ate more than the placebo group. This plays out beneath the level of conscious awareness, which means the body automatically makes up a calorie deficit. Keeping weight off is like holding a rubber band stretched taut the whole time: relax for a moment and it snaps back.

18:39

A wrecked metabolism is the extreme case, not the rule

The Biggest Loser study followed 16 contestants who lost about a third of their body weight on average, and six years later their resting metabolic rate was still about 500 calories a day below what the models predicted — roughly one Big Mac. But Faan, a professor at the University of Copenhagen, points out that this group was extreme and the sample far too small. Meta-analyses show that in ordinary people the average metabolic suppression is very small, and may even fall inside the margin of measurement error. So after nine cycles, John's metabolism has most likely not been damaged.

22:21

No evidence cycling makes you fatter; the real variable is age

A study in North Carolina found that 74% of the weight lost was fat, but 88% of the weight regained was fat — which sounds like muscle draining away. A German study found no such difference. The key distinction is age: the North Carolina participants were 50-70, the German ones 20-40. People lose muscle as they get older regardless, and it has nothing to do with whether they cycle their weight. Almost no study has tracked subjects through more than one complete cycle, so the claim that every cycle leaves you fatter has no evidence behind it.

29:49

The mice returned to their starting weight; their immune systems remembered

Fat tissue is full of immune cells, and when visceral fat cells are starved of oxygen and die, macrophages arrive to clear them out, which sets off inflammation. Alyssa Hasty had mice gain weight on a high-fat diet for 9 weeks, then lose it on a low-fat diet, then gain again on high fat for another 9 weeks. Compared with mice that had been obese throughout, the cycled mice ended up with exactly the same body weight and exactly the same amount of visceral fat — but their T cells were more elevated and more ‘agitated’. She says the body remembered the fluctuation. That said, this comes from mice switched between extreme diets, and the human evidence is murkier.

34:20

Regain every pound and your diabetes risk is still lower

Observational studies show that people who weight-cycle have higher rates of heart failure and death, but studies of that kind cannot establish cause. The randomized controlled trials give this answer: even among people who lost weight in a supervised program and then regained all of it, the risk of developing type 2 diabetes was still lower than in people who never tried, and their cholesterol and blood pressure were lower too. A survey of 12,000 people found that what actually predicts mortality is habits — fruit and vegetables, exercise, smoking and drinking — not BMI. So a diet you rebound from is not wasted effort; the healthy behavior itself is protecting you.

Glossary

weight cycling
The process of repeatedly losing weight and regaining it; the medical name for yo-yo dieting.
visceral fat
Fat packed around the organs, more prone to triggering inflammation than subcutaneous fat.
macrophage
A type of immune cell whose job is clearing away dead cells and tissue debris.
