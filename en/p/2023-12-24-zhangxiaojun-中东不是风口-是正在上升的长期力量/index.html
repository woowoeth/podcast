The Middle East Is Not a Trend, It's a Rising Long-Term Force

The Middle East's grand narrative is not the frenzy of China-style mass entrepreneurship, but people from many countries converging on a shared opportunity; Chinese companies cut in through efficiency, flexibility and customisation, but cost-effectiveness is not the first choice — alignment with national strategy is.

6:11

The Middle East is not a copy of China's entrepreneurship street

Li Li distinguishes the sense of convergence in the Middle East from China's 2014 entrepreneurship street: China's entrepreneurship street was an unprecedented sensation, young people and university students throwing themselves into an entrepreneurial wave; the Middle East is people from many countries, of different ages and different industries, gathering around a shared search for opportunity — more like an internationalised market. This judgment means that understanding the Middle East through the framework of China-style mass entrepreneurship will distort it — what's there is not the shining fervour of young people, but a pragmatic gathering with more diverse identities, ages and industries.

— Li Li
7:11

Central SOEs gave up margin on infrastructure and bought trust

Chinese central and state-owned construction and infrastructure companies helped the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Qatar build infrastructure, but Li Li learned in interviews that these companies did not earn rich profits from it — they gave up margin, and thereby won very good repute with some Middle Eastern governments, generating a high level of trust locally toward Chinese companies and partners. This mechanism explains why, when Chinese tech companies later entered the Middle East, what they faced was not only commercial competition but also a layer of trust accumulated by infrastructure concessions.

— Li Li
14:12

Registering a company in Saudi is high-threshold, and needs a local guarantee

The threshold for entering Saudi Arabia is quite high: the cost of registering a company is relatively high, and if you want agency rights, you also need a commercial guarantee from a local. More fundamentally, Saudi Arabia's biggest market is still the shock — and the core of the core is that you have to align with Saudi Arabia's grand strategy, the 2030 plan, put forward by MBS, Salman. So tech companies take the digital infrastructure route, and the core point is to help them do things that fit their national strategy. This explains why Chinese companies in the Middle East are not simply selling products, but embedding themselves in the other side's narrative and historical context.

— Li Li
23:15

Chinese companies are efficient and flexible, but cost-effectiveness is not the first choice

Li Li observed that Chinese companies in the Middle East are very efficient, excited and solid, able to fulfil customisation requirements, relatively flexible, and relatively cost-effective on price. But the key turn is: because they are very rich, cost-effectiveness is not a first-order priority. European and American companies came in early, and locally there is affection and trust toward them, but it is also very delicate. For Chinese companies to wrestle commercially with Europe and America, what they rely on is not low prices, but efficiency, flexibility and customisation capability.

— Li Li
29:24

A forty-thousand-dollar order rolled up to ten million dollars

A cybersecurity company suddenly received a Middle East order during the pandemic, forty thousand dollars, and the founder made a big exception, sending two people to find tickets by every route and transfer through every connection, painfully making their way to the UAE. The cost of one person beyond salary is 500,000 RMB a year, so the investment in these few people was higher than the order amount. But from 40,000 dollars to 1 million dollars, to five or six million dollars, and this year roughly 10 million dollars, it was built up step by step. Li Li's conclusion: do not miss any small order in the Middle East — order requests that come in must be picked up.

— Li Li
36:25

Local university students get paid; a PhD gets over thirty thousand dollars a year

Li Li found that if you apply directly to a local university in the UAE, rather than as an exchange, they can pay you a salary, give you money. Master's students get money too, PhD students get money too, and a PhD is roughly over thirty thousand dollars a year. Abu Dhabi, in that place within the emirate, has no tax. Behind this is their structural move to recruit young talent globally: from young elites to talent to university presidents, all are recruiting talent worldwide, and one of the presidents was poached from MIT, and is also Chinese.

— Li Li
39:25

From a domestic big tech firm to the Middle East, salary doubles

Li Li met some young people who went from a certain domestic big tech firm to work in the Middle East; they may have studied in the US, returned to China and joined a big tech firm. Asked how they felt, they were extremely happy, quite happy. The reason is that they felt it was extremely competitive in China, 996 was a lot of pressure, all kinds of competition, many people competing; the direct feeling over there is that their salary doubled, work pressure was a cup smaller, and there is still a lot of room to compete. Their whole state is also relaxed, yet full of expectation for the future, with light shining in their glasses.

— Li Li
50:29

Invest in India by looking micro; invest in the Middle East from macro down

Li Li compared the difference in investment logic: investing in the Indian market does not look much at the whole country's strategy, does not understand from the macro level, just understands from the micro level whether the business is viable and that's OK; the meso level is whether the industry is on the eve of an explosion. But now it is different — now you look from macro down, and only after seeing clearly top-down do you go bottom-up on how to expand that market. This shift means that investment judgment on the Middle East cannot rely only on the business model; you must first understand national strategy and the geopolitical landscape.

— Li Li
