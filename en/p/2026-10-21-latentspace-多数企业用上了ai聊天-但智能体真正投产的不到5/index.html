Latent Space 2026-10-21

Most enterprises have deployed AI chat—fewer than 5% have agents truly in production

Getting Claude and Codex to employees is simple—90% of enterprises have done it. Actually putting agentic systems into production for non-engineering tasks, only 5% have managed it. That gap between deployment and real use is the episode's core data.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:17

Story points as pay: top-to-bottom span is just 4×

Tenex pays engineers in 'story points' rather than hourly rates. Founder Alex's bet is that AI will create unprecedented variance in individual output—but currently the gap between top and bottom earners is only about 4×, far from the 10× dream. The real difficulty isn't detecting engineers who inflate story points—that's checked by an AI strategist role paid on customer retention and growth. The hard problem is pricing the hours engineers spend on architecture and customer communication, not writing code.

10:25

Opus 4.5 to 5.5 in one year has doubled per-engineer output

CJ sees last December's Opus 4.5 as the breakpoint where models could finally handle long-running tasks. By now, with Opus 5.5, the same engineers complete roughly twice as much work in the same time. But the story-point yardstick itself is loosening—the company recalibrates its story-point standards at least quarterly, because as models improve, each story point needs to deflate in dollar terms, or the company ends up paying twice for the same productivity gain.

— CJ
15:11

Token spend has climbed to 2× the cost of headcount

Most AI startups spend roughly as much on tokens as on headcount—the high end is 5 to 10×. Tenex reports 1:1 to 2:1, above the average. They did worry about token-maxing becoming the cost bottleneck, but they judge that Opus 5.5 has pushed the model to a Pareto point in cost-per-task—six to eight months ago was the real window to worry. Now models are dropping price faster than usage grows; most economically valuable tasks run fine on Opus 5.5, and it's not worth paying for a 10× smarter model.

— Alex
19:29

Enterprise agent adoption is a collapsing funnel at every step

Deploying Claude or Codex to employees: 90 out of 100 enterprises do it. Training and enablement: drops to 60%. Agentic systems for engineering teams: most people estimate 70–80%, but it's really only about 50%. Actually running agentic systems in production for non-engineering work: 5%. That final leap is the widest chasm between FOMO-driven surface adoption and genuinely delegating workflow to agents.

— Alex
24:42

Vertical AI companies like Harvey are structurally doomed despite current success

Facing Harvey and OpenEvidence—vertical-AI companies that have won their niches—Alex and CJ are bearish overall, even though these firms are winning today. The reason: enterprise customers want bespoke 1:1 experiences. That forces software vendors to build out full FDE teams to sit with customers; software and services are converging, and software margins are disappearing. The only moat left is data that ChatGPT can't access—and both Anthropic and OpenAI are acquiring that, they're just not as aggressive about it as vertical players.

— Alex
30:32

A $40K annual SaaS contract replaced with $1K of self-built AI

In Alex's 'Kill My SaaS' competition, a mid-tier SaaS quoted at $40K/year was replaced with a self-built AI alternative for $1K, ready to deploy at the next customer meeting. But he stresses that the higher you go in the stack—Salesforce, SAP, systems of record—the worse a self-build pitch becomes. One mistake carries a bet of hundreds of millions to billions, so enterprises naturally avoid that risk. He rejects claims that Salesforce or SAP face disruption.

— Alex
35:23

AI replaces jobs, not people—but that's still a real hole

CJ notes a16z data showing call centers actually shrinking as AI takes over such work. If that's a skill someone built a career on, AI displacement is catastrophic. But when pitching agent deployments to customers, the core pitch is always 'reallocation,' not 'layoff': freeing people from the deepest technical details to cross-team collaboration and bigger-picture work. That loop of 'next problem' almost never stops in large organizations. Alex adds that employees should ask themselves how replaceable their role will be in a few years, not treat upskilling solely as the employer's responsibility.

38:18

New York's AI circle is smaller but its commercial pool may be larger

Alex describes New York's AI bubble as smaller than San Francisco's, but Tenex and their customers are themselves the 'New York version of San Francisco.' His thesis is that New York doesn't need to copy SF's scale; it should pull SF people to New York to meet customers with real money—a 'reality check.' New York's edge isn't the size of its AI circle; it's that the sheer commercial volume here can let AI technology truly permeate GDP.

— Alex
