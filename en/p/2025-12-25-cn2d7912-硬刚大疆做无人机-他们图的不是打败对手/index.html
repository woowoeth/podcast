Confronting DJI head-on with drones, but victory is not the goal

Consumer drones have nearly all failed. Knowing this, they still entered the market—not to grab share, but because making drones could upgrade the company's technology, manufacturing, and supply-chain capabilities in one move, helping it survive the next cycle.

A father's tears weighs heavier than institutional punishment

In college, Liu Jingkang hacked into the school email system to retrieve exam papers and prove his technical prowess, then publicized it to boast. He was hit with a disciplinary record. But what really revealed the weight of his actions wasn't the punishment—it was seeing his father, whom he'd never seen cry, weep in front of the teacher and apologize. That moment showed him the actual damage to the school's reputation and made him feel, for the first time, that a parent's feedback carries more weight than any external judgment.

1:16:55

His deepest regret: the junior classmates he talked into dropping out

Liu admits that when his first startup failed, he wasn't really a loser himself—his family had resources, and he could take what he'd learned into a good job at a major company. What genuinely haunts him is the few dozen junior classmates he convinced to join. They gave up offers from big tech companies worth multiples of what he was offering, and they forfeited structured mentorship—they wagered their most golden professional years, right after graduation, and the company failed anyway.

— Liu Jingkang
1:57:41

Meta's billions on VR helped the industry and hurt it equally

Luo Yonghao makes a counterintuitive observation: Meta loses money every year selling headsets. While that's elevated VR experience by generations on the surface, the fact that it's backed by a tech giant willing to burn unlimited cash means real hardware companies won't enter to compete. VR ends up with only one player exploring, burning money alone. Liu agrees and adds that Meta also mistimed its strategy: it painted grand metaverse visions without solidifying satisfaction in specific use cases. Users felt let down, and the category spiraled into low engagement.

— Luo Yonghao / Liu Jingkang
2:08:47

AR glasses need a decade to become truly consumer-ready

On his second venture building AR glasses, after surveying 60–70 supply-chain factories Liu was initially optimistic that five years would do. The first engineering prototype revealed gaps far wider than expected: temples had to be light enough not to look ridiculous, battery had to last all day, the device had to stay always-on, and it had to sit on skin without generating heat or sweat. Even scaling these parameters by hundreds or thousands of percent falls short. His revised conclusion: this probably takes 10 to 20 years, not five.

— Liu Jingkang
2:14:49

Rushing hardware to market compounds the damage that follows

The modular-lens camera was rushed to market before CES. The team knew more test time would surface reliability issues, but they shipped early anyway. A pandemic manufacturing halt compounded things, and the product arrived with connection instability and fogging problems. Liu's lesson: don't rush hardware. Software bugs can be patched over-the-air; hardware defects cannot. Once you sell it, even a full recall cannot undo the damage in users' minds.

— Liu Jingkang
2:40:05

Challenging GoPro isn't about winning—it's about growing the whole market

The company never aimed to kill GoPro. Liu's view: three product lines—spherical action camera, thumb camera, wide-angle action camera—each draw their own circle. Each overlaps with GoPro's in some scenarios and can substitute in those moments. But the three circles combined are much larger than GoPro's single circle, because each use case expands the category itself. Revenue surpassing GoPro was the natural result of expanding the pie, not the starting goal.

— Liu Jingkang
2:52:18

Patent warfare, stripped bare, resembles organized crime

When an NPE demanded an outrageous settlement, Liu's company had no experience and thought they couldn't escape. Then a major supplier approached them: if they kept orders up next year, the supplier could ‘internally apply’ and use the power of their patent consortium to have a word with the opposing lawyers. Weeks later, the plaintiff withdrew. Liu describes the feeling as pure organized crime—patent consortiums collect membership fees in exchange for ‘the boss having your back.’ It's a real, legal, compliant side of modern enterprise operations.

— Liu Jingkang
3:13:34

Building drones means betting on latent, unstated demand

An internal executive once asked: if DJI committed not to make spherical cameras, could the company commit not to make drones? After discussion, the answer was still yes to drones. Liu breaks the logic into three layers. First, the mission—capturing and sharing life—requires a freely-moving camera. Second, demand-wise, DJI offers ‘a better horse,’ but what users really need is to get from A to B; drones' tactile control and free perspective solve that more fundamentally. Third and deepest: building drones forces the company to upgrade its entire tech, manufacturing, supply chain, and channel capabilities in one shot. That's the investment that keeps an enterprise ready for the next cycle.

— Liu Jingkang
