Superconducting chip claim: don't change the process node, change the temperature zone — 28nm logic can hit 5nm performance

Move logic into the liquid helium temperature zone and replace transistors with superconducting switches — that's Snowcap's bet: tape out RISC-V first, then claim 28nm-class logic can deliver 5nm-class performance.

Superconducting computingChip architectureLiquid heliumRISC-VFormer Intel CEO
If you're watching the power wall in computing, this episode lays out a third route — don't squeeze transistors, move the whole logic stack into the liquid helium temperature zone; a former Intel CEO has already placed a bet. The risks and the numbers are both on the table, and it's worth a listen.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

The ceiling on extreme overclocking is heat removal, not the chip

This episode opens up from the overclocking industry: everyday chips rely on air cooling, higher-power ones use water cooling, and beyond that you need sub-zero media like dry ice and liquid nitrogen. Extreme overclockers add voltage to chase the highest frequencies, the chip gets hotter and hotter, and the only way to deal with it is ever more extreme cooling. The host says he was an extreme overclocker himself and once held the world number two spot — though it only lasted a day and a half. The key reference point he gives: this kind of overclocking exists just to run a benchmark for a few minutes at 8.5GHz, whereas real superconducting computing is not chasing an instantaneous record but logic at close to zero power.

2:01

With liquid helium overclocking, the expensive part isn't the helium, it's the transfer line

Going from liquid nitrogen to liquid helium, physics starts to interfere. Liquid helium costs about 20 times as much as liquid nitrogen (liquid nitrogen is about one dollar a litre), but that isn't the main expense: getting liquid helium from a large storage tank into a 3-4 kilogram copper overclocking pot through the transfer line costs 5000 dollars per use. Both Intel and Asus have done liquid helium overclocking, and records have broken past 9GHz. The host warns that when temperatures drop this far, material behaviour changes drastically — some platforms have a ‘cold bug’ where the system simply won't boot below a certain temperature; only platforms without a cold bug are suitable for liquid helium play.

4:03

The lure of superconductivity isn't faster, it's zero resistance

Overclocking is about squeezing frequencies out of existing materials; superconductivity is a different story: electrons flow through a superconducting material with no resistance, which amounts to free transport. The vast majority of superconductors today require extremely low temperatures, and room-temperature superconductivity is still stuck in a state of research back-and-forth. The key inference here: if an entire chip's switching logic could operate in the superconducting state, energy per operation would be ‘several orders of magnitude’ better than today's laptops. The host deliberately states it fully and then pulls back — superconductivity is still the holy grail, not yet in hand. This sets up Snowcap's entrance later.

5:07

What the former Intel CEO bet on is the temperature zone, not the process node

Snowcap Compute just closed a seed round, with former Intel CEO Pat Gelsinger sitting on its board. What they're betting on is building superconducting chips that run at roughly 4-15K, the liquid helium temperature zone. Structurally it's no longer billions of transistors but a new kind of switch that works at superconducting temperatures — the Josephson junction. At this stage Snowcap's goal is to first build a RISC-V-class CPU at low temperature, with power consumption close to free; the price is that the whole machine must stay in the liquid helium temperature zone, and the cooling itself is also energy consumption.

7:08

The new variable in superconducting logic is fitting into a standard EDA flow

There is no shortage of long-term researchers: a US defence contractor built chips integrating tens of millions of Josephson junctions, IMEC got to hundreds of millions, and built out logic and EDA tools to go with it. Snowcap's differentiator is compatibility: they say this kind of superconducting logic can go into a standard EDA flow and can be taped out on ordinary CMOS processes, with niobium titanium nitride as the material. The core conclusion the host takes away: the energy efficiency advantage from superconductivity is equivalent to achieving near-5nm chip performance with 28nm logic — note this is not a smaller process node, but the equivalent gain from lower power consumption.

8:12

Cryo CMOS is only an intermediate state; the real hurdle is scale

Snowcap plans to deliver its first chip in 2026, followed by a deployable RISC-V design. For the intermediate state you can look at cryo CMOS: it runs in the roughly 72-77K liquid nitrogen temperature zone, and although there's no superconductivity, the low temperature itself pushes power consumption down. The real hurdle to cross is going from cryo CMOS into superconductivity; the host's doubt about this is ‘can you scale it up at high performance’ — being able to run and being able to commercialise at scale are two different things.

9:12

Cold booting is nothing new; the hard part is keeping the whole machine in liquid helium

This section provides a real-world footnote on low-temperature tape-out: when AMD was most short of money, the first Zen 1 A0 sample that came out could only boot at dry ice sub-zero temperatures because of a design flaw, and was later fixed to run at room temperature. So low temperatures in the test and boot stages are nothing unusual; but keeping an entire processor in the liquid helium temperature zone is an infrastructure problem, including the supply of helium — liquid helium is also the lifeblood of the superconducting magnets in MRI machines, and there was a helium shortage a few years ago.

10:14

Snowcap doesn't sell IP, it sells a closed all-in-one machine

Snowcap positions itself not as selling IP but as delivering a complete system. The host's judgement is that precisely because of the infrastructure barrier of the liquid helium temperature zone, the product form will be a near-closed all-in-one machine, with the software stack rebuilt from scratch. A seed-stage company still has A/B/C rounds ahead of it; however, they have already brought in advisors and former employees with Apple, Nvidia and Google backgrounds. On this topic, it's worth staying sceptical, and worth following.

Glossary

cold bug
The phenomenon where a chip cannot boot or cannot work properly after being cooled too far.
Josephson junction
A new type of switching device made of superconducting materials that works at extremely low temperatures.
cryo CMOS
Low-power CMOS that runs in the liquid nitrogen temperature zone (about 77K), not yet at the superconducting level.
