Superconducting Chip Claims: Don't Change Process Node, Change Temperature Zone—28nm Logic Can Hit 5nm Performance

Move logic to liquid-helium temperatures and replace transistors with superconducting switches—that's Snowcap's bet: tape out a RISC-V first, then claim 28nm-class logic can deliver 5nm-class performance.

superconducting computingchip architectureliquid heliumRISC-Vformer Intel CEO
If you're watching the compute power wall, this episode offers a third path—not squeezing transistors, but moving entire logic to liquid-helium temperatures; a former Intel CEO has placed his bet. The risks and numbers are laid out, worth a listen.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

0:00

The ceiling for extreme overclocking is cooling, not the chip

The episode opens with the overclocking industry: everyday chips rely on air cooling, higher-power ones use water cooling, and beyond that you need dry ice or liquid nitrogen—sub-zero media. Extreme overclockers push voltage to chase maximum frequencies, making chips hotter, so they need ever more extreme cooling. The host says he was once an extreme overclocker and even took second place in the world—though it lasted only a day and a half. His key reference: such overclocking is only for running benchmark tests for a few minutes at 8.5GHz, whereas true superconducting computing isn't about instantaneous records but about logic that runs at near-zero power.

2:01

With liquid-helium overclocking, the expensive part isn't the helium—it's the transfer tube

Going from liquid nitrogen to liquid helium, physics starts to interfere. Liquid helium costs about 20 times as much as liquid nitrogen (liquid nitrogen is about a dollar per liter), but that's not the main expense: the transfer tube that moves liquid helium from a large storage tank into a 3-4 kg copper overclocking pot costs $5,000 per use. Intel and Asus have both done liquid-helium overclocking, with records already past 9GHz. The host warns that material behavior changes dramatically at such temperatures—some platforms have a 'cold bug' where the system simply won't start when cooled too far; only platforms without the cold bug are suitable for liquid-helium play.

4:03

The allure of superconductivity isn't speed—it's zero resistance

Overclocking is about squeezing frequency out of existing materials; superconductivity is another narrative: electrons flow through superconducting materials with no resistance, meaning free transport. Currently, most superconductors require extremely low temperatures, and room-temperature superconductivity remains in a state of repeated research claims. The key extrapolation here: if the entire chip's switching logic could operate in a superconducting state, per-operation energy would be 'several orders of magnitude' better than current laptops. The host deliberately speaks boldly but then pulls back—superconductivity is still the holy grail, not yet in hand. This sets the stage for Snowcap's entry.

5:07

The former Intel CEO is betting on temperature zone, not process node

Snowcap Compute has just completed a seed round, and its board includes former Intel CEO Pat Gelsinger. Their bet is on building superconducting chips that operate at roughly 4-15K, the liquid-helium temperature range. Structurally, it's no longer billions of transistors but a new type of switch that works at superconducting temperatures—the Josephson junction. For now, Snowcap's goal is to first build a RISC-V-class CPU at low temperatures, making power consumption nearly free; the cost is that the entire machine must stay in the liquid-helium temperature range, and cooling itself consumes energy.

7:08

The new variable for superconducting logic is fitting into standard EDA flows

Long-term researchers are not scarce: U.S. defense contractors have built chips integrating tens of millions of Josephson junctions, and IMEC has reached hundreds of millions, along with supporting logic and EDA tools. Snowcap's differentiator is compatibility: they say such superconducting logic can fit into standard EDA flows and can be taped out on ordinary CMOS processes, using niobium titanium nitride as the material. The core conclusion the host heard: the energy-efficiency advantage from superconductivity is equivalent to using 28nm logic to achieve performance close to a 5nm chip—note this isn't a smaller process node but an equivalent gain from lower power.

8:12

Cryo CMOS is just an intermediate state; the real hurdle is scale

Snowcap plans to deliver its first chip in 2026, followed by a deployable RISC-V design. The intermediate state can be seen in cryo CMOS: it operates in the liquid-nitrogen range of about 72-77K, and while not superconducting, the low temperature itself reduces power. The real hurdle is crossing from cryo CMOS into superconductivity; the host's skepticism is 'can you scale up performance at high levels'—being able to run and being able to scale commercially are two different things.

9:12

Low-temperature startup isn't rare; the hard part is keeping the whole machine in liquid helium

This section provides a real-world footnote on low-temperature tape-outs: AMD's first Zen 1 A0 sample, leaked when the company was most cash-strapped, could only start at dry-ice sub-zero temperatures due to a design flaw, and was later fixed to run at room temperature. This shows that low temperatures in testing and startup aren't unusual; but keeping an entire processor in the liquid-helium range involves infrastructure issues, including helium supply—liquid helium is also the lifeblood of MRI superconducting magnets, and there was a helium shortage a few years back.

10:14

Snowcap isn't selling IP—it's selling a closed appliance

Snowcap's self-positioning is not to sell IP but to deliver complete systems. The host's judgment is that precisely because of the infrastructure barrier of the liquid-helium temperature range, the product form will be nearly a closed appliance, and the software stack will need to be rebuilt. A seed-stage company still has Series A/B/C rounds to get through; however, they've already brought in advisors and former employees from Apple, Nvidia, and Google. On this topic, it's worth staying skeptical and also worth following.

Glossary

cold bug
A phenomenon where a chip fails to start or malfunctions after being over-cooled.
Josephson junction
A new type of switching device made of superconducting materials that operates at extremely low temperatures.
cryo CMOS
Low-power CMOS that operates in the liquid-nitrogen range (about 77K) but does not reach superconductivity.
