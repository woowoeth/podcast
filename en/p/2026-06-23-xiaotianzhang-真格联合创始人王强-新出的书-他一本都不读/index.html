Zhenggé co-founder Wang Qiang: He reads none of the new books

His reason: a book pursued by 100 million people today may be remembered by only 10 million next month—bestseller status itself is evidence of what time discards—so he reserves all his reading for old books tested across decades.

Four life choices, each precisely timed with historical waves

Wang Qiang describes only a few pivotal decisions: switching to English after entering Peking University; discovering English wasn't enough and switching to computer science during his US studies; leaving Bell Labs to join New Oriental upon China's WTO accession; co-founding Zhenggé Fund during the 2011 mass-entrepreneurship wave. He credits luck—each step coincided with an era's needs—but emphasizes each choice recycled skills from the previous one rather than following a preconceived plan.

18:13

Fill your stomach before browsing the bookstore, because you'll stay until closing

He calls his practice "book hunting" rather than collecting—the hunter chases freshness, the collector stores for winter. He describes his ritual: eat until full before arriving, stay until closing time. Children once accompanying him learned to bring energy bars because they'd go hungry waiting. This near-ritualistic patience embodies his core principle that reading demands time.

— Wang Qiang
24:18

The more people discuss a new book, the less he reads it

He has a bias against bestsellers: a book read by 100 million today may be remembered by 50 million next month, then 10 million the month after, nearly erased within a year. He lets others "test the path" first, keeping his reading time for what he deems truly important. The criterion isn't market fever but whether an author has been troubled by a question for ten or twenty years and extracted insight universal to humankind.

— Wang Qiang
29:21

Against aphorism worship: rereading, not reading, is the core of reading

He despises "golden quotes" because each one survives only through the sacrificed sentences and logical chain behind it; severed from that structure, the quote holds no weight. Reading itself isn't the point—rereading is reading's true core. A book read once leaves you untouched, a blank; you might as well never have opened it.

— Wang Qiang
39:30

What the AI era lacks most is not answers but questions

Any question now yields dozens of answers from AI in a second, but people forget the original question they set out to ask and whether AI's answers truly address it. AI's own evolution proves the same point: early AI was a black box yielding conclusions; the evolution toward showing complete reasoning chains happened because users trust only what they can spot-check. "Hallucinations" often appear when models quietly bypass or hide certain data to please users.

— Wang Qiang
1:04:48

Token burn transforms funding from staged rounds into simultaneous overlapping rounds

This AI generation of founders differs radically from mobile-internet titans—they carry no legacy constraints and immediately explore applications across different models, putting Chinese founders on equal footing with global peers on the full stack from model to application. But token consumption and compute demand hard currency; when compute can't reach model precision or apps can't scale knowledge scope, competitors crush you fast. A, B, and C rounds may now happen at the same node, not in sequence. The real danger isn't simultaneous fundraising but taking money and letting two years pass with no actual deployment.

— Wang Qiang
1:07:52

Action-based FOMO is acceptable, psychological FOMO is not

Facing the AI wave, he never worries about missing out—investors always miss something. He tells Zhenggé's team: action-based FOMO is fine—go verify, make contact—but not psychological FOMO. Psychological panic makes you ordinary; the real question is "Do you understand this thing? What did you do for it? Who are you?", not pure terror of being left behind.

— Wang Qiang
1:23:05

Translating Alice without footnotes: letting Chinese itself replace the wordplay

Translating “Alice's Adventures in Wonderland”, Wang Qiang set himself an almost impossible constraint: zero annotations. When the White Queen asks what bread is made of and Alice answers "flour", the Queen presses: where did you pick that flower?—flour and flower homophone-linked in English. After months of thought, he found 白花花 (baihuahua), Chinese for finely-milled flour, naturally fuses "flour" and "flower" imagery, replacing English's sonic trick.

— Wang Qiang
