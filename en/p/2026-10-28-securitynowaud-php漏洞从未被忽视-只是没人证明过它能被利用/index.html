The PHP vulnerability nobody could prove—until AI did

A vulnerability discovered in 2004, independently rediscovered during 2020 and 2024 audits, and shelved due to lack of exploitation proof was turned into a working remote code execution chain by AI in days—threatening 70% of all websites.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

16:22

Fourteen years of inaction masked a critical vulnerability nobody had proven could work

The PHP vulnerability allowing unauthenticated remote code execution has existed in default PHP configurations since version 5.4 (March 2012), a 14-year window that the industry acknowledged but tolerated precisely because no one had demonstrated successful exploitation. The patched versions released last week finally closed this hole, but its persistence underscores Steve's key point: lack of demonstrated weaponization can indefinitely delay fixes for known, severe vulnerabilities.

— Steve Gibson
25:27

Broad coordination destroys secrecy; the only question is who leaks, not if

Both Google's Whiz Security team and Califf independently discovered the vulnerabilities and reported them privately to the PHP Foundation, agreeing to keep them confidential for two full months from August through October. Steve raises a pointed question: once you tell multiple people to maintain a secret, you've eliminated the possibility of controlling who learns it. The rational actors in any expanding secret circle eventually realize that with so many people already knowing, blame diffuses—making the leak inevitable rather than an exception.

— Steve Gibson
52:49

2.6 million exposed servers, 99.998% vulnerable—an error margin you can't dismiss

Volcanaries' scan of the public internet identified 2.6 million servers running PHP. Of these, 99.998% carried the vulnerability or showed signs of it. The math becomes punishing when you add what Volcanaries also found: 1.1 million of these servers (42%) run unsupported PHP branches—versions that no longer receive official patches. The WordPress and Laravel sites in that mix (460,000 and 120,000+ respectively) compound the problem: most website operators don't realize PHP is running beneath their application, so they can't proactively patch.

— Steve Gibson
59:57

Twenty years after discovery, AI weaponized a flaw humans had left dormant

Stefan Esser, a PHP security researcher, found a memory-exhaustion remote vulnerability in 2004 but declined to publish exploitation code, deeming it too dangerous. In 2020, another researcher reported a separate memory-depletion issue tied to unbounded variable handling. When OSTIF and Quirks Lab audited PHP in 2024, they hit the same bug again—but still nobody could prove it was exploitable in practice. This August, Califf's team used GPT to pursue the clues Esser left behind, and within days transformed the flaw into a fully weaponized remote code execution chain against WordPress. Where two decades of human research hit a wall, AI punched through in a week.

— Steve Gibson
1:09:06

Cloudflare expects vulnerabilities of this caliber to surface roughly every week from now

Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince told The Atlantic that he is aware of additional vulnerabilities of similar severity already slated for public disclosure, and predicts the internet will remain in sustained crisis mode over the next one to two years. Simultaneously, Crowdstrike reported a sharp acceleration in AI-driven attack campaigns—now detecting new ones every two hours. The barrier to entry has collapsed: a technically unskilled person can now ask an AI how to monetize stolen data and gain enough capability to deploy attacks. The implication is stark: this PHP incident was not an exception but a preview of the new normal.

— Steve Gibson
1:26:24

Coordinated disclosure shattered across three breaches in the 24 hours before release

The carefully orchestrated public disclosure cracked in three directions simultaneously. On October 20th, the CVE identifiers leaked to forums and social media—mistakenly labeled with a perfect 10.0 CVSS score instead of the actual 9.2. Califf then accidentally published a demonstration video; while it was deleted quickly, screenshots spread across the internet. Most damaging: six hours before the scheduled announcement, container vendor Chain Guard unilaterally abandoned the coordination agreement and released patched images, leaving every PHP operator still waiting in the dark exposed to active exploitation. The PHP Foundation's signal was unambiguous: it will not work with Chain Guard again.

— Steve Gibson
2:18:25

Disconnecting from the internet during crisis has clear historical and operational precedent

Steve draws a line between this week's takedowns in the Netherlands—Rotterdam port, government portals, five major political party websites, multiple hospitals—and prior emergency disconnections: Canada's tax authority offline for four days during Heartbleed; federal agencies required offline during SolarWinds and ProxyLogon; Quebec briefly taking nearly 4,000 government websites down during Log4Shell. His conclusion is firm: an attack surface that is offline cannot be compromised. Rather than wait passively to be breached, preemptive infrastructure takedowns are a legitimate emergency response with precedent, and institutions should prepare playbooks for strategic network disconnection in advance.

— Steve Gibson
2:35:42

One certificate authority's private keys were three to eight bits, not 384

Certificate private keys are supposed to use 384 bits of cryptographic randomness. The Indian certificate authority eMudra's keys actually consisted of the numbers 4, 56, 666, 115, 155, 230, and 237—not hashes, but the literal private keys in production. This meant any person could forge certificates that would be trusted as legitimate by every major browser worldwide. eMudra was immediately revoked and removed from all browser trust stores. The cleanup exceeds even Heartbleed's scope because countless IoT devices and aging Linux servers have eMudra's trusted root certificate baked into firmware—devices that will likely never be updated. The security debt is permanent.

— Steve Gibson
