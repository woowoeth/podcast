16:12

The sixth wave of entrepreneurs is the strangest wave

Wu Xiaobo divides the entrepreneurial waves since reform and opening into six. The first wave was rural enterprise farmers; the second was the 1984 urban system reform; the third was the 1992 intellectuals diving into business; the fourth was the 1998 PC internet; the fifth was the 2012 mobile internet. He argues the sixth wave is not a technology wave driven by AI, but entrepreneurship driven by the nation's stage of development. Its characteristics: rapid growth has ended, the market is fully saturated, and this generation are the children of the propertied—the earlier waves were children of the propertyless, living in Beijing basements. Their drive to surpass, their path models, and their sources of capital are all different.

— Wu Xiaobo
17:13

The strongest desire for wealth was in the first wave of farmers

Wu Xiaobo compares the traits of five waves of entrepreneurs: the strongest desire for wealth was in the first wave of farmers; the first to begin to have a public social consciousness were the 1992-school intellectuals and civil servants, whose sense of public social mission was especially great; the 1984 wave were beneficiaries of a catch-up strategy, relying on cost advantages, scale advantages, and lower-tier market advantages to do import substitution, so they especially easily produced big players—Wang Shi came from this wave; the 1998 internet wave were basically undergraduates, globalisation was innate to them, they had a strong sense of capital, and no property-rights problems; the 2012 mobile internet wave was the fiercest and bloodiest, because of the urgency of time—group buying, Didi, shared bikes slashing at each other with knives, constantly burning money, the market taking shape in a very short time, and the profitability was also the greatest.

— Wu Xiaobo
20:01

In the era of revolutionary narrative, entrepreneurs were a conservative force

One responsibility Wu Xiaobo took on in writing A Hundred Years of Ups and Downs was to present the entrepreneurial group during the revolutionary period. In the era of revolutionary narrative, entrepreneurs were in fact a conservative force—revolution wanted to destroy everything, entrepreneurs wanted to preserve everything. He specifically wrote about how the May Fourth Movement went from a few Beijing university students marching to a nationwide strike of students, workers, and merchants: without money it could not have been pushed forward, the strikers had someone paying their wages, and the driving force was a large number of Chinese national capitalists and national merchants, who had political demands and expressed dissatisfaction with the Beiyang government through street movements. This history was covered up in the revolutionary narrative, and that book, A Hundred Years of Ups and Downs, was meant to tell what role business played during the era of great revolution.

— Wu Xiaobo
27:15

The internet generation no longer draws resources from the government

Wu Xiaobo believes Chinese entrepreneurs have been too entangled with politics, sometimes actively, sometimes passively. In the Republican era, John King Fairbank noted in The United States and China that Chinese entrepreneurs did not go catch mice but instead fought for the right to the general apparatus, showing that the problem of official-merchant collusion existed then too. Salt merchants, the merchants of the three cart trades, Huizhou merchants and Shanxi merchants basically all arose from collusion between officials and merchants. After reform and opening, with the dual-track price system, one still had to negotiate resources with the government. But the internet generation is a revolutionary generation, because their own model of wealth creation and resource acquisition no longer needs to obtain resources from the government level, and they have also become a new kind of infrastructure. Today China will no longer produce the kind of entrepreneurial group like the old Shanxi merchants or the 1990s property-rights reform; the soil for resources has disappeared, and the opportunity has completely ended.

— Wu Xiaobo
29:18

Learning from Tencent is hard; learning from Moutai is relatively easy

Wu Xiaobo says that in the future, doing business in China, learning from Tencent is quite hard, while learning from Moutai may be relatively easier. Because we have entered a conservative period, the whole country's period of rapid development has ended, and the cycle in which one could gain wealth by telling a story, a business-model innovation, or a small technology is gone. Now one basically needs to be very immersed in doing one thing, gaining users, and building one's own rule-making ability and resource barriers in a particularly segmented industry—this is what Moutai has been doing. He gives an example: if you just want to run a steady business, open a noodle shop, choosing Moutai will definitely let you run a good noodle shop; learn a few things like the master-apprentice system and rule-setting and you can open a particularly good noodle shop; but opening a noodle shop the Tencent way probably won't work.

— Wu Xiaobo
31:57

AI has a winner-take-all nature and will not bring opportunity to everyone

Wu Xiaobo does not define the current generation as one driven by AI, because AI is a tool and has not produced an ideological transformation in the business world; it is still merely a substitution, such as using a machine to write a document. AI's opportunity does not belong to everyone the way PC internet, mobile internet, and e-commerce startups once did; it has a great platform nature and winner-take-all nature. He gives an example of a company he saw in Hangzhou called Lindi, using ChatGPT for clothing design; if it becomes very strong, it will take all of the entire clothing industry, and in the future Chinese clothing designers may end up using just one or two tools. So AI will not bring huge startup opportunities; instead it may be a winner-take-all logic, and even in segmented fields it may be winner-take-all.

— Wu Xiaobo
33:20

There may be no big business in the future; many people will do small business

Wu Xiaobo judges that most future startups will be small startups, related to small certainties, the so-called leapfrog entrepreneurship, whose scale cannot be large and which for business is certainly not big business. There may be no big business in the future, and a large number of people will have to be satisfied doing a small business. He gives the example of his daughter and son-in-law doing a business—a small tavern, just that big a room. The startup opportunities may still be in using AI tools to do things related to human needs, maybe a teacup, maybe a song. This differs from the earlier waves who started businesses to cater to market demand; this wave is more about being satisfied doing a small business.

— Wu Xiaobo
34:17

I may be my daughter's suffering

Wu Xiaobo wrote You Eat Your Suffering, I Eat My Suffering because he was scrolling on his phone and saw an academician say he works until two o'clock every night so that the next generation will not suffer. He thinks this view is wrong: each generation has its own suffering, and he may be his daughter's suffering. His daughter graduated from the same high school as he did; he was the top liberal-arts student in the school's history, his photo hung on the wall, and when his daughter graduated the school invited him to give a speech as an old alumnus, and classmates went to his daughter and said that's your dad. He realised that when a dad is hung on the wall, the daughter's grades cannot be very bad; if she did not have such a dad, her middling grades would be fine. So parents may work very hard, thinking they are helping their child, when in fact they are pressure on the child.

— Wu Xiaobo
38:24

Advice to the post-90s and post-00s: stop trying to please others

Wu Xiaobo's advice to the post-90s and post-00s is: find something you like, a long-lasting work like Moutai, and stop dreaming, stop trying to please others, just live the life you like and that is good. He mentions that on that day there was news that Zhang Zetian's net worth of 60 billion topped the hot search; Zhang Zetian is post-90s, and in recent years the post-90s have not produced any particularly big entrepreneurs, yet have produced a particularly rich negative loan. He asks rhetorically whether there are post-90s entrepreneurs with tens of billions—Zhang Yiming was born in 1984. He admits that in his own generation there was no such situation locally, living the life you like—isn't that the life you like?

— Wu Xiaobo
