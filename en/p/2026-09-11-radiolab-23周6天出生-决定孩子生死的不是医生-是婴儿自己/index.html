Born at 23 Weeks 6 Days: What Decides the Baby's Fate Isn't the Doctor, It's the Baby

In the gray zone between 22 and 25 weeks, medicine offers no answers; parents can only read "I want to live" from a grasp, a flicker of the eyes—but that movement is just a reflex.

11:33

The 24-week line was drawn by a court

Nita Farahani explains that in 1973, when Roe v. Wade was decided, the Supreme Court wanted to set a limit on abortion rights and chose "viability"—whether a fetus could survive outside the womb. The best medical guess at the time was 24 weeks, because that's when lungs begin forming air sacs and might theoretically allow breathing. But it was only theory; no one had actually taken a fetus out at 24 weeks to test it. In practice, through the 1970s and '80s, most hospitals drew the line at 28 weeks, because it's around then that the lungs start producing surfactant. Only in the late 1970s, with the arrival of artificial surfactant, intravenous nutrition, and tiny ventilators, did the line move back to around 24 weeks.

15:37

The doctor gave three numbers: 53%, 20%, 80%

At 23 weeks 4 days, the doctor told Kelly and Tom the baby would be born within 48 hours. The doctor gave them: no matter what they did, about a 53% chance of death; about a 20% chance the baby would be completely fine; and the remaining 80% meant possible lifelong dependence on a ventilator, brain hemorrhage, blindness, or deafness. The doctor asked them to decide before the birth whether to "do everything." Because once you see the baby, it's hard to say no. Tom said it was the worst night, and they worried they were "torturing this infant for nothing," and that the child would one day hate them for being born.

The nurse turned a yes-or-no question into a conditional one

NICU nurse Diane Loissel didn't give numbers. Instead she told Kelly and Tom: this isn't a yes or no decision. The baby could be born first, and if she came out crying loudly and vigorous, they could go all out to save her; if she came out blue and floppy, they could stop, withdraw support, and hold her until she died. That way, the decision was handed to the baby herself. The plan turned an impossible choice into "give her a chance first, then see."

— Diane Loissel
29:01

Parents see will; doctors see reflexes

"The baby declared herself" is a phrase parents and staff used repeatedly. But the two sides understood it completely differently. What parents saw was will and determination; nurse Tracy Hullett said she deliberately used more neutral words, because a 23-week baby's brain has almost no folds on the outside—it's basically smooth—and no one knows what it can think or feel. Keith Barrington was more direct: a baby grasping a parent's finger is actually the grasp reflex, and he never told parents that, because they would think it was intentional. But perhaps it's precisely this ability to "see will" that lets parents make a decision when they can't decide rationally.

— Keith Barrington
31:05

The neonatologist himself was fooled by a reflex

Keith Barrington and his wife Annie are both experts in the ethics of extreme prematurity. Their own daughter was born at 24 weeks 5 days, weighing 720 grams, in the very NICU where he was director. Weeks later, the daughter developed severe complications and didn't move at all for 36 hours, and they decided to let her go peacefully. But when he went back to the NICU to see her, he saw her lips moving—she was sucking on a pacifier a nurse had put in her mouth. He knew it was just a basic brainstem reflex, nowhere near enough to survive. But he still went back to his wife and said: we can't stop, I want to keep giving her a chance. He said if he were more evidence-based, more rational, more scientific, he might have made either choice, but as a father, in that moment, he couldn't let go.

— Keith Barrington
43:37

She opened her eyes, and the doctor decided to operate

Juniper's intestine perforated again, her blood pressure and oxygen crashed, and even 100% pure oxygen couldn't sustain her. The surgeon refused to operate, considering it futile, and her blood pressure was too unstable—she might not survive the surgery; even if opened, her skin was paper-thin and might not be sewn back. But the attending physician, Fauzia Shaquille, saw her suddenly open her eyes wide at the bedside and stare straight at her. She said that usually such critically ill babies have no reaction at all, because every breath is a struggle. But for her to open her eyes and look at us when she had almost no blood pressure and almost no oxygen was a powerful statement: I'm still here, don't give up. So she persuaded the surgeon: without surgery, she would certainly die; with it, she might die, but there might be a tiny chance.

— Fauzia Shaquille
47:42

When they opened her up, everything was falling apart

The surgery ended much faster than expected, and a nurse led Tom and Kelly into a small windowless conference room. The surgeon told them that when she opened the baby and touched the intestine with a probe, everything was "falling apart, falling apart." Juniper survived the surgery, but that was all. Tom said at the time he felt the baby could never live until morning. While driving, he suddenly thought: what if I never finish reading her this book? What if she never knows how Harry, Hermione, and Ron end up? That thought led to all the others—all the things she would never know. He had to pull over and completely broke down.

1:01:01

15 years later: she's alive, but the cost is elsewhere

Juniper is now 15 and about to start high school. She was pulled out of math class for the interview and was happy about it. She said school has always been hard, kindergarten through eighth grade went slowly, and she was very lonely and found it hard to make friends. She is very small, and classmates tease her about it. Physically, she still has a kidney problem and can't eat too much salt. Harder still is the emotional toll: she has PTSD, anxiety, and trouble controlling her emotions. But she says she doesn't think about it much, just lives like a normal person. A few years ago her mom took her to a horse farm, and she fell in love with riding. She says horses taught her to control her emotions—when you're angry, the horse feels it and loses control, so she has to learn to be calm. She now has two best friends, and says the daughter her mom described back then is her.

Glossary

viability
The medical and legal threshold at which a fetus can survive outside the womb, usually set around 24 weeks.
surfactant
A fluid produced by the lungs that prevents air sacs from collapsing and sticking together between breaths; production begins around 28 weeks.
NICU
Neonatal intensive care unit; a ward specializing in the care of premature and critically ill newborns.
micro-premie
A premature infant born at an extremely early gestational age and very low birth weight.
