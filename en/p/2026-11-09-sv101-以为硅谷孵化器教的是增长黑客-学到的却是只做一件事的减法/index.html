Thought the accelerator taught growth hacking, but discovered subtraction to a single thing

The hardest part of subtraction isn't cutting entertainment—it's eliminating the second-most important thing to obsess over the most important one.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:02

HF0's selectivity with ten companies per batch beats Y Combinator's scale

HF0 takes ten companies per batch, three times yearly; Y Combinator takes two hundred per batch, four times yearly. Guo Jing (郭靖) calls Y Combinator ‘spray and pray’—casting wide nets and betting on probability to produce winners. Early-stage accelerators theoretically have lower success rates, so they need larger pools to hedge risk; HF0 does the opposite, shrinking the denominator to its minimum and compensating only through extreme selectivity, making its acceptance rate far lower than Y Combinator's.

— Guo Jing
11:07

OpenRouter made barely enough for coffee, and that was the whole strategy

At HF0's weekly Monday Demo Dinners, each company reports progress on a single metric. OpenRouter didn't report revenue, but weekly token calls—founder Louis often said the company's earnings barely covered two cups of coffee. Months later, Stripe acquired OpenRouter for 7 billion dollars, and HF0 co-founder Evan posted to the alumni group with the caption ‘enough for coffee’.

— Guo Jing
14:09

Jumping from 100 to 150 daily outreach contacts forced growth to emerge

During his growth obsession, Guo Jing consulted growth expert Blake Anderson on influencer partnerships. Guo said he'd contacted over 100 influencers in several months; Anderson said he contacts 150 per day—the order-of-magnitude gap made Guo realize his system was broken, so he rebuilt his outreach, follow-up, and conversion flow; now his team contacts 200 influencers per day.

— Guo Jing
17:12

Price increases to drive growth collide with the conservation of energy

Guo Jing tried raising prices 30% to drive growth, but later realized this was ‘conservation of energy’—the conversion gains from raising prices are offset by potential-user churn and reputation loss. He noticed the best products like OpenAI and Anthropic don't rely on pricing tricks; annual plans are just monthly times 12 at 20% off, numbers decided casually, with real effort focused on model quality itself.

— Guo Jing
21:15

Real subtraction means cutting even the second-most important thing from focus

People think HF0's ‘subtraction’ means founders skip socializing and relaxing; Guo Jing says that's backwards—cutting entertainment is the easy part. Real subtraction is fixating only on what matters most to the company, even excluding the second-most important thing: if a major partnership moves slowly, abandon it, because trying to do everything means doing nothing about what actually matters most.

— Guo Jing
32:22

November 1 arrived and revenue doubled overnight from months of accumulated momentum

In the first half of the program, except for OpenRouter—which had already achieved flywheel effects—the whole cohort saw almost no growth, making Dave question his judgment. On October 30, everyone was still encouraging each other; on November 1, Guo Jing woke to find daily revenue had doubled—the accumulated influencer system and paid event both took effect the same day, then grew another 50%, then doubled again.

— Guo Jing
40:14

OpenRouter's moat may not survive a straightforward price war

Guo Jing observed that OpenRouter works because it executes one thing to extreme simplicity: no aggressive monetization, no reckless fundraising, just ‘a unified interface for calling all large models’. Developers don't rewrite code to switch models, and model makers rush to integrate because the call-volume leaderboard already carries authority. But Guo Jing admits this advantage might not withstand pure price competition.

— Guo Jing
52:37

AI music can master vocal technique but cannot generate imperfect emotional moments

Guo Jing says AI music can now achieve ‘vocal fusion’ humans cannot, yet cannot generate the moments that truly move—those imperfect, rawly emotional instants. He cites Li Zhi (李志)'s song captured on phone video at a Nanjing bar as more alive than the later mastered version, and Shan Yichun (山一纯)'s mid-performance crack and hesitation on The Voice of China moved him more than a perfect take would.

— Guo Jing
