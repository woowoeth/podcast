Homework beyond two hours won't improve children's learning

Beyond two hours of homework, there's almost no additional learning benefit, yet sleep deprivation, late nights, and anxiety keep accumulating—and many high schoolers actually exceed this threshold.

Homework before second grade serves no real purpose

According to the rule of thumb, homework duration should be grade level times 10 minutes—20 minutes for second grade, 10 for first grade, up to about two hours for high school. But data shows that beyond two hours, additional homework yields no significant learning gains. Children actually need homework starting around second grade; younger kids may complete assignments, but it doesn't primarily aid learning. Meanwhile, many high schoolers spend far more than the two-hour upper limit.

10:33

Children won't connect homework to test scores on their own

Braaten shares her son's experience: one day after a test, he had an epiphany—material he'd reviewed went better on the exam. It should be common sense, but no one had taught him to make that connection. Homework and study exist to test what you've learned and improve performance, but if parents don't continuously point out ‘why we're doing this,’ children won't make the connection themselves and won't develop into independent learners.

— Ellen Braaten
16:54

Tutoring homework support now means no independent learning later

Eliza raises a genuine concern: when tutors and peer support handle everything, will children later feel incapable on their own? Braaten admits she once encouraged ‘get help if you can afford it,’ but now sees what happens when kids raised with that support structure enter college and workplaces where the support vanishes—they haven't learned to work independently. Her conclusion: parents must think carefully about how to gradually withdraw support rather than indefinitely stay in the role of safety net.

When her daughter crammed late, she chose sleep over the exam

Eliza describes a real incident: her daughter crammed late for an exam, and by the time she discovered it, it was too late. She let her daughter sleep through and miss the exam—the school rescheduled it later. Eliza admits she didn't have the courage to tell her daughter directly ‘We're putting sleep first, even if grades suffer,’ so she quietly made that choice for her. Braaten sees this as excellent modeling—a parent weighing limited options and explaining that reasoning process to the child.

24:02

When homework causes daily pain, check for undiagnosed learning disabilities

With a child who struggles through homework daily, Braaten says the first thing to investigate isn't attitude—it's whether the child has the skills homework requires. This means checking for undiagnosed or inadequately supported learning disabilities or neurodiversity. Some mildly neurodivergent or ADHD kids are actually good at homework; they just don't want to do it. But children who genuinely hit walls every day usually have a capability gap first, which parents easily overlook.

— Ellen Braaten
32:24

When kids break down, acknowledge feelings before assessing the problem

When a child sits at the desk crying ‘I can't do this,’ Braaten's sequence is: first set aside your own anxiety and worries about tomorrow; acknowledge the child's feeling—‘I understand why you're upset, this does feel overwhelming.’ Then as an adult, decide whether the task can actually be done tonight. If not, tell the child clearly ‘I've assessed this; here's what we'll do’—maybe spend five minutes together, do one problem, or skip it entirely and try a different approach tomorrow.

— Ellen Braaten
36:30

Smart kids taking three hours for thirty-minute work struggle with speed, not ability

Kids who should finish in 30 minutes but take three hours are the group Braaten watches most carefully. They may have ADHD, learning disabilities, anxiety, or simply slow processing speed. Slow processing speed doesn't mean low intelligence—it means completing copying and rote-practice tasks at a slower pace. Braaten argues schools should assess homework load by time spent, not number of problems completed; otherwise these children often end up detained after school because they don't finish assessments before dismissal.

— Ellen Braaten
46:52

Separate emotional problems from homework problems by observing kids without assignments

At the episode's close, Braaten emphasizes that tears and frustration from homework itself are normal and don't automatically signal a mental health problem. What warrants concern: are the tears happening daily and over time? Have they affected grades and relationships? She offers a simple diagnostic tool—watch how the child behaves when there's no homework. If they're fine the rest of the day and only suffer during homework, the problem likely lies with the assignments, not their mental health.

— Ellen Braaten
