Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia CEO over espionage scandal; company has no successor internally

The incoming CFO can only be an interim CEO, because Nvidia's helmsman must be both a top computer scientist and a strong enough executive to browbeat Taiwan suppliers—a combination only Jensen Huang possesses today.

Resignation wasn't one scandal—it was scandal stacked upon scandal

The trigger for Huang's resignation wasn't a single scandal but escalation in layers: first the revelation of a relationship with Alice Wong (suspected Chinese spy), then a separate exposure of a long-running affair with Kimberly Powell, the medical-division VP reporting directly to him. The board convened overnight and announced his resignation before market open. The show frames this as a twist in a spy story—two relationships stacked together finally broke his position.

6:19

The board wanted to keep him, but finances changed the math

Multiple board members hold over $1 billion in Nvidia stock each. Huang has been the only CEO in the company's 33-year history, so by normal logic they would have moved to protect him. But the scandal had already tanked the stock price, and keeping him would only expand the losses. Stephen Witt argues the board's decision was a financial calculation to protect shareholder value, not a moral judgment.

— Stephen Witt
8:31

The commerce secretary ran interference for Huang inside government

Commerce Secretary Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Bessent clashed directly over whether to add Megaspeed to the trade blacklist and whether to close regulatory loopholes in Southeast Asia data centers. Lutnick had been seen as the force inside government delaying export controls to protect Huang's interests. With Huang's fall, Congressional and internal-government pressure to tighten China-related chip restrictions has visibly intensified.

— Anna Swanson
17:02

He saw the world as he wished it to be

Anna Swanson argues Huang consistently denied the existence of chip-smuggling problems for years, even as US authorities uncovered large-scale smuggling cases. Her assessment: this trait—seeing the world as one wishes it to be—was both the source of his long success and possibly the root of his collapse.

— Anna Swanson
26:24

Finding a successor is like replacing Michael Jordan

Stephen Witt frames the succession problem this way: it's like asking ‘who replaces Michael Jordan after he leaves the Bulls?’—there's no ready replacement. Huang had to be a top computer scientist, a fierce enough executive to browbeat Taiwan suppliers, someone able to schmooze clients and call Trump directly. These skills rarely appear in one person; the company essentially needs three people to fill his void.

— Stephen Witt
28:35

The CFO doesn't understand transistors; she can only be temporary

Interim CEO Colette Kress is widely seen as a transitional choice: she's an excellent financial executive but not a world-class computer scientist, and a chip company's CEO must truly understand the technical details from transistors to data centers. Inside Nvidia, talents like Ian Buck excel at engineering but lack experience with customers and suppliers. Finding someone with both sets of skills is extremely difficult.

— Stephen Witt
31:53

Financial disclosures shifted quietly as the scandal simmered

As the scandal festered last year, Nvidia abruptly changed how it disclosed revenue—making it harder to track what percentage of revenue came from Singapore and Malaysia. Anna Swanson points out this is precisely when the Southeast Asian business surge into China (that journalists were trying to document) got deliberately obscured. Colette Kress, as CFO, would have been deeply involved in such financial-disclosure decisions.

— Anna Swanson
38:06

Thirty days ago he wielded enormous power; today he has none

Stephen Witt describes the collapse as Greek tragedy: thirty days ago Huang was one of the most powerful people in AI; now he holds 3% of Nvidia—still valuable—but has no executive power and Trump won't take his calls anymore. The show suggests this illustrates a deeper truth: even in the age of AI, the endings are written by human weakness, not technology itself.

— Stephen Witt
