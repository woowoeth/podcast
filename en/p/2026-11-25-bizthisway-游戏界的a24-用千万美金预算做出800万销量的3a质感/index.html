A24 for Games: 3A Quality From 10-Million-Dollar Budgets

Rather than acquire studios, Kepler (开普勒) secures control through a one-third cash, one-third equity, one-third founder retention structure; through cost discipline and smart design, it produces 3A-quality games on 5-to-20-million-dollar budgets.

The difference between 3A and 2A is just budget and pricing terminology

The game industry lacks strict definitions for Indie, 2A, or 3A—they're roughly divided by development budget and retail price. Indie runs under 1 million dollars, priced 10 to 20 dollars; 3A costs 50 to 100 million-plus and prices at 60 dollars or above. Players don't distinguish by these labels; what affects their experience is the visual fidelity of art assets, not necessarily gameplay or story quality. This gap is where Kepler finds its opening.

18:23

Premium game players are few but create stable, continuous demand for new titles

Free-to-play games demand long-term retention through mechanics like gacha; premium games are one-time purchases under no such pressure. Crucially, premium players don't stay locked into one title for thousands of hours—they actively seek new games. This constant appetite for novelty actually benefits new entrants more than established giants, which is why Kepler chose this space over free-to-play's dominant 90% market share.

— Richie
21:36

Equity structure solves the incentive failure that follows major studios acquiring studios

Microsoft and Sony's acquisition spree in 2018-2019 exposed a problem: founders suddenly had no financial pressure, lost cost discipline, and couldn't influence the parent company's direction—projects got cancelled on a whim. Kepler's structure ensures founders retain one-third of equity to maintain skin-in-the-game cost control, receive one-third in cash for stability, and exchange one-third to join parent governance and support other studios. The retained stake makes cost discipline permanent.

— Richie
30:16

A 10-million-dollar project needs 300,000 to 500,000 sales to break even

Consider a 10-million-dollar game priced at 40 dollars, accounting for platform cuts: it needs roughly 300,000 to 500,000 sales to break even. First-year sales of 500,000 or 1 million are quite profitable. Industry sales have hard ceilings—best-selling games hit roughly 10 million copies, pricing maxes around 60 to 70 dollars. This should create a natural budget ceiling, yet major studios keep exceeding it. The economics constrain small players, not the giants.

— Richie
38:31

A vertical slice demonstrating market-ready quality can reverse a rejected pitch

Expedition 33 (“33号远征队”) first arrived as a rejected pitch: Victoria-era art, traditional JRPG turn-based combat, nothing distinctive. Over a year later, the team—now featuring an ex-Ubisoft director—returned with a 20-minute vertical slice demonstrating production quality and introducing the game's signature dodge-and-parry counter system. Kepler greenlit immediately. The game eventually sold 8 million copies—a 2A budget achieving near-3A sales volume.

— Richie
43:31

Cutscenes showing only character torsos saves crucial hand modeling costs

Expedition 33 achieved high perceived quality on budget through three factors: the director's clear vision eliminated wasted iteration, turn-based combat requires fewer animation assets than action games, and deliberate tricks like showing only character torsos in cutscenes (saving elaborate hand rigging) while camera framing made players never notice. The craft was meticulous but the tricks were invisible.

— Richie
47:36

Studio collection size has a natural ceiling; more isn't necessarily better

Kepler has stopped growing after two years at ten studios, three to four hundred staff. That's the limit because more founders means more shareholder votes—governance complexity explodes. Original plans were eight studios; they went to ten; they might add one or two more if the right fit arrives, but expansion isn't a priority. This restraint reflects Kepler's whole operating philosophy.

— Richie
56:45

Waiving IP and sequel rights is Kepler's key advantage in winning projects

Hot projects attract twelve publishers bidding simultaneously. Kepler wins because: first, it has development teams on staff providing direct support—UI refinement, technical optimization—so creators get a true partner. Second, Kepler never claims IP or sequel rights, leaving artistic control entirely with the creator. This ‘make this one game great’ restraint echoes through everything they do, even though sequels are crucial premium-game revenue.

— Richie
1:01:25

After an 8-million-copy hit, the greatest risk is overconfidence about bigger budgets

After Expedition 33's 8-million-copy success, the real danger is overconfidence: chasing bigger budgets that seem like bigger opportunities while missing smaller, creative works. Kepler's discipline is to find distinctive, profitable projects—not pursue every project as a blockbuster. Every recent year sees games built on one-tenth Expedition 33's budget that still reach 10 million-plus sales. The numbers keep proving bigger isn't necessary.
