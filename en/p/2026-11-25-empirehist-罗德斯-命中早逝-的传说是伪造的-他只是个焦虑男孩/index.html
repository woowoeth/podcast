The myth of Rhodes's fated death was fabricated: he was just an anxious boy

His biographer proves that Rhodes's ‘fated early death’ is a heroic narrative invented by posterity; medical records reveal he was simply an anxious youth terrified of losing his mother and neglected by his family.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:16

Opposition from his father fueled every ambition he pursued

The Rhodes family was a merchant dynasty built on real estate, but his father, traumatized by his grandfather's land suit scandal, completely rejected commerce and became a clergyman, insisting all seven sons enter the clergy. Rhodes wasn't sent to Eton or Winchester like his two older brothers; instead, he attended his father's own ordinary grammar school. Steinberg notes this marginalization left him spending his entire life doing the opposite of his father's wishes: wanting to do business, wanting to become a lawyer (his father hated lawyers), wanting to go to Oxford (his father forbade it). His ambitions were largely revenge against his family structure.

— Johnny Steinberg
7:26

Maternal absence created his lifelong pattern of yearning and dread

When Rhodes was thirteen, he participated in a family game asking him to write ‘whom do you love most’ and ‘who loves you most.’ In the latter column, he wrote ‘I don't know.’ After boarding a ship for South Africa at seventeen, he wrote his mother seven or eight letters in the 24 hours between leaving port and when the pilot left the ship, urgently reporting his every move, terrified of being forgotten by her. Steinberg sees this pattern of both craving and deep doubt about maternal love as a template for how he would handle intimacy throughout his life.

— Johnny Steinberg
10:27

His perception of Black Africans descended from curiosity to amorality

When Rhodes first arrived in Natal, he was fascinated by Black Africans with an ethnographic curiosity, describing their smell, build, and clothing in home letters as novel and amusing. But Steinberg notes this attitude would entirely reverse: Rhodes would come to see Black Africans as ‘people who do not make history,’ to be treated well when useful and badly otherwise, with relationships that became ‘entirely without moral constraint.’ This psychological shift is key to understanding his later colonial policies.

— Johnny Steinberg
15:40

His fortune hinged entirely on his brother's lucky land purchase

Rhodes's brother Herbert bought two mining plots in the early diamond rush, each just 30 feet wide, but they happened to sit at the center of a mineshaft nearly a mile deep that was rich throughout. At the time, 80 to 90 percent of mining plots proved barren, and no one could judge in advance which were which; months later good and bad plots diverged sharply in price, and Herbert's timing happened to catch the window when he could still afford to buy. Steinberg is blunt: without his brother's pure luck, Rhodes could scarcely have made a fortune in diamonds at all.

— Johnny Steinberg
23:04

Black miners worked for tribal weapons, not for personal wealth

The diamond mines at their peak employed roughly 20,000 Black laborers, many sent from hundreds of miles away from Pedi and the Tonga kingdom in organized teams by chiefs with a specific purpose: earning wages to buy guns for their tribes. This happened shortly after the upheaval of the Zulu Kingdom's rise, when every Black polity realized firearms were the new military technology that determined survival, and since they couldn't manufacture them themselves, they had to trade through labor economics to acquire them.

— Johnny Steinberg
28:17

He found genuine intimacy only through the roles of patient and caregiver

Around his mother's death, Rhodes publicly collapsed and wept at the Albert Hall; during his return voyage to South Africa he fell ill, and was nursed day and night by a crew member. Once ashore, he actually convinced this sailor to abandon his sea career and stay to care for him long-term, promising him a mining stake if he recovered. Months later he wrote to arrange for a dying consumptive from the ship to be brought to his side to be cared for until death. Steinberg argues that this ‘patient and caregiver’ relationship was the only mechanism that triggered genuine intimacy in Rhodes's life.

— Johnny Steinberg
31:27

He succeeded through patronage networks, not self-made scrappiness

Mechanization caused the capital threshold for diamond mining to explode; roughly nine in ten prospectors went broke. Rhodes survived not because he had enormous capital himself, but because he shrewdly abandoned his independent-operator identity and became an employee of the well-capitalized Charles Rudd. The widely circulated story of ‘two poor boys partnering to flip their fortunes by selling ice machines’ is fabricated—Rudd was actually a wealthy shipbuilder's son and Cambridge graduate, nothing like a pauper.

— Johnny Steinberg
38:35

His bribery case was buried by his intimate acquaintance, the prosecutor

After an 1874 mine flooding, an engineer named Routh whom Rhodes's side had hired was found to have deliberately sabotaged the pumps and wrote down bribe-takers' names in an open hearing—including Rhodes's. The case could have sent him to prison, but the prosecutor deciding whether to prosecute was Sidney Shippard, an intimate dinner-circle friend from Rhodes's circle of ‘Twelve Apostles’ (and later appointed as Rhodes's executor). Shippard did not prosecute either side citing ‘insufficient evidence,’ but privately warned Rhodes to leave quickly. Rhodes boarded a ship back to England, escaping what amounted to a proven bribery charge.

— Johnny Steinberg
