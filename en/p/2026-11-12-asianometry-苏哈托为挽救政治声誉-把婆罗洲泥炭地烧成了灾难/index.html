Suharto burned Borneo's peatlands into disaster trying to salvage his political legacy

Suharto pushed a million-hectare rice project to sustain his 'Father of Development' reputation, but peatlands proved unsuitable for farming and planning failures triggered what became one of Southeast Asia's largest single-year carbon-release fire events.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:12

Rice shortages destabilized Sukarno and enabled Suharto to seize power

Under Sukarno, rice was more than food—it was hard currency for military and bureaucratic salaries and was prioritized for foreign exchange. Sukarno's rice quotas mobilized the country but backfired: rice did not reach deficit regions, foreign exchange went to imported rice instead of needed fertilizer, Javanese paddies degraded, and output fell 13.6% between 1960 and 1964. The crisis destabilized his regime and handed Suharto the opening for his coup.

6:18

Oil windfall from 1973 embargo made Indonesia's rice self-sufficiency possible

Suharto took power just as the Green Revolution introduced high-yield varieties IR8 and IR5, and Indonesia caught the 1973 Middle East oil embargo windfall: annual petroleum revenue spiked above $5 billion. This money subsidized fertilizer permanently, set a rice price floor, and built irrigation canals and roads. Rice output soared from 12 million tons in 1969 to 22 million tons by 1981. By 1984, Indonesia declared rice self-sufficiency—the foundation of Suharto's "Father of Development" reputation.

7:22

Rice production stalled in the 1990s while population kept growing

By the 1990s, rice output growth collapsed to 0.5–1% annually while population grew 1–2% yearly. Late-1980s urban development claimed about 20% of Java's best farmland. Severe floods and droughts compounded the problem. By 1992, Indonesia returned to rice imports for the first time in a decade; the 1995 drought forced imports of 3 million tons. The loss threatened Suharto's political foundation.

13:38

Peatlands unsuitable for rice farming was established science by the 1970s

The Dayak indigenous people had for generations grown rice only on coastal, shallower, relatively fertile peatlands, and even there harvests were often poor due to unpredictable rainfall. Indonesia's Bogor Soil Research Institute had classified such interior peatlands as "problem soils" since 1974. Yet after visiting a successful Singaporean-Chinese operation on Sumatra's coast, Suharto decided to replicate the model on the completely different deep inland peatlands of Central Kalimantan, ignoring the geological difference and existing scientific conclusions.

16:40

Suharto demanded results by 1997 so construction began before studies finished

Suharto demanded the first harvest by 1997, so construction started in January 1996 before cost-benefit analysis, engineering studies, or environmental impact assessments were complete. Most Indonesian companies and international investors refused to participate; a longtime Suharto contractor reportedly joined only to avoid losing other government contracts. Environmental group WALI tried exposing the threat to endangered orangutans and ecosystems, but could not stop the project.

17:41

Massive drainage canals destroyed peatlands while resettlement housing barely got built

Thousands of kilometers of drainage canals were poorly planned—they cut directly across major peatland domes and systematically drained the layers of water. The plan called for 20,000 homes for Javanese settlers within two years; after ten months, only 600 were built. For Suharto's April 1997 inspection, authorities built a helicopter pad and temporarily replanted rice seedlings to show prosperity. He stayed two hours, announced the project a success, and left.

18:41

Half a million hectares burned in 1997, sending smoke across Southeast Asia

Forest cleared, peatlands dried, illegal burning for clearance sparked fires that satellites detected as early as January 1997. In 1997 alone, over 500,000 hectares of the 1.4 million-hectare zone burned, 80% of it peatland. The carbon released from peat averaging 51 centimeters thick was enormous. Fires flared repeatedly until the June 1998 rains; the total burned area reached roughly 10 million hectares, smoke drifted to Singapore and Malaysia, and the region's carbon emissions were among the largest single-year increases on record.

21:51

Billions spent but left Dayak people and settlers both empty-handed

The project was budgeted at $2–3 billion, partly from Indonesia's "Reforestation Fund." Much money vanished into corruption and fake subcontracts. The Dayak were promised investment and prosperity; instead their farmland burned, fishponds were destroyed by canals, and traditional territory fell to settlers. Some 15,000 Javanese families who relocated found almost no infrastructure. After Suharto fell in 1998 amid the Asian financial crisis, the new government formally canceled the project in 1999.

Glossary

peatland
Waterlogged soil formed from partially decomposed plant matter, carbon-rich but acidic and nutrient-poor.
Mega Rice Project
The 1995 Suharto-led effort to convert Central Kalimantan's peatland forests into a million-hectare rice zone.
Dayak
Indigenous people of Kalimantan who traditionally lived along rivers and practiced small-scale rice farming.
