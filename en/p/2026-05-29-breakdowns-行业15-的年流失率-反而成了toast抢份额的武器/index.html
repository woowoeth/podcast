The restaurant industry's 15% annual churn becomes Toast's unexpected market-share advantage

Every year 100,000 to 120,000 American restaurants close and reopen. High churn would normally be bad news, but for challenger Toast it's provided a constant stream of new customer entry points—it's capturing roughly half of newly opened locations through superior product.

4:04

Retention numbers so high they triggered investor skepticism

When Sean Barrett first invested in Toast in 2020 during the pandemic, he called management directly after seeing their model: the retention numbers seemed too high for the restaurant industry, and he suspected he'd missed something. Management's answer was that the numbers were real. That detail became the foundation for his conviction in Toast's business—the fact that a notoriously low-margin, high-failure-rate industry could generate such sticky customers.

14:32

Taking just 49 basis points, Toast underprices rivals by half or more

Toast's revenue comes from payment processing, software ARPU, hardware, and a small lending business. On a $100 order, roughly 3% goes into the payment processing system; 2.5% goes to banks and clearing networks, leaving Toast with about 49 basis points of net take rate. Most competitors take 75 to 125 basis points; Square takes about 1% but doesn't provide a complete operating system. Toast is running about $200 billion in platform volume, which means it has room to increase rates and reduce costs as it scales.

— Sean Barrett
18:42

Competitors who took the iPad shortcut fell behind permanently

Toast chose to build proprietary hardware early instead of letting restaurants run apps on consumer iPads. Some later competitors saw this as a detour they could skip, opting for the cheaper iPad-plus-app approach. But iPads break constantly in a hot, oily, splashed kitchen; that proved to be the reason these competitors eventually failed. Customers discovered that Toast's proprietary hardware actually works, and the head start Toast got on the supply chain became a moat that later entrants can't replicate in the short term.

— Sean Barrett
24:54

AI-driven marketing automates what restaurants paid agents $2,000 a month for

Toast's new Toast Grow module uses historical data and neighboring restaurant data to automatically identify slow periods, then trigger SMS, website discounts, and social media campaigns. It costs about $500 a month, replacing what restaurants were paying $1,000 to $2,000 monthly to marketing agencies for limited results. Early data from a couple of months of use showed about 8% revenue uplift. For the average restaurant with $1.3 million in annual revenue, that could double SaaS ARPU and deliver a return ratio close to 20x.

— Sean Barrett
29:11

Free delivery forces DoorDash to build a POS system

DoorDash is quietly piloting a POS system, which Sean believes is likely a response to Toast's free delivery feature from about a year ago. DoorDash's delivery takes nearly 15%, and combined with other fees, can reach 30% of an order for restaurants and diners alike. Toast has partnered with Uber Eats to basically erase that cost. The team visited three to four dozen San Francisco restaurants on both platforms and found not a single one willing to switch to DoorDash even if its POS were free—the economics don't work, because payment fee savings don't come close to delivery commissions that would go up.

— Sean Barrett
33:22

High churn markets are easier for challengers to win

The restaurant industry sees about 15% of its locations close and reopen each year. In most software businesses, that's a red flag; for a challenger like Toast, it's the opposite. In a high-retention industry where 99% stay put and only 1% churn, even a great product only gets 1% of new customer opportunities a year. But the restaurant industry has roughly 100,000 new locations a year, and Toast is taking about half of them on the strength of its product. That volume of new-customer entry doors is something challengers in high-retention industries never get.

— Sean Barrett
39:39

The roadmap from 200,000 locations to $10B profit has validation from earlier wins

In 2020, when Toast had only 20,000 to 30,000 locations, it claimed it would reach 200,000; the market widely thought that TAM was too ambitious. Now, in the next couple of years, it's on the verge of hitting 200,000. The team has now set a new target: scaling from $2 billion gross profit to $10 billion, and the basis is products already validated across enterprise customers (like Applebee's), fresh retail, hospitality (like Marriott), and international markets—the UK, Ireland, Canada, Australia. Outside China, the global potential location base is roughly 15 million, and penetration is still very low.

— Sean Barrett
44:01

DCF analysis suggests intrinsic value is twice the recent stock price

There are three valuation angles. First, an 18x P/E on 2027 GAAP earnings—cheap for a company with gross margin growth above 25% and earnings growth above 30% compounded. Second, working backward from 10 years of gross profit scaling from $2 billion to $10 billion, the pessimistic case implies a 4x capital return, or about 10x on a Nasdaq-average valuation. Third, discounting cash flows directly yields a fair value around $50 per share, while the stock was trading at $22 to $23.

Glossary

net take rate
The percentage of a payment that the processor keeps after paying banks and networks; the portion of card processing fees that accrue to the payment processor itself.
SaaSpocalypse
Market concern that AI and open-source software will render traditional SaaS subscription models obsolete.
TAM
Total addressable market; the total market space a company's product could theoretically reach.
MOIC
The ratio of investment proceeds at exit to initial capital deployed; return multiple on invested capital.
flywheel market
A market where network effects activate and market share accelerates once a city reaches roughly 10% penetration; dominance compounds.
