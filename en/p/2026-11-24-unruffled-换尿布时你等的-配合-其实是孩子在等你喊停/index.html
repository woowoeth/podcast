Respectful Parenting: Janet Lansbury Unruffled 2026-11-24

The cooperation you're waiting for is actually your child waiting for you to draw the line

Many parents think respecting their child means patiently waiting for cooperation during diaper changes or letting them hold toys. Janet Lansbury says this is actually because you're not taking care of yourself—what children really need is for parents to actively set limits and know when to stop.

This episode clarifies a commonly misunderstood aspect of respectful parenting: respect is not infinite waiting, but a reciprocal relationship. It's for parents exhausted by diaper-change standoffs and toy-grabbing battles with one-year-olds.

4:09

Treating parent-child conflict as something to avoid is the real problem

A parent writes that they're becoming impatient and resorting to tricks like ‘sneaking hands in’ to speed up diaper changes, feeling they're failing at respectful parenting. Janet identifies the root issue: parents treat conflict with their child as a bad thing to avoid. When a child wants to roll over or play during a diaper change, parents just wait it out—which neither takes care of themselves nor actually helps the child. She reframes conflict: having conflict with the people who matter most is actually a positive, relationship-building learning process, not a sign that respect has failed.

— Janet Lansbury
10:29

Waiting for voluntary cooperation trains you to resort to force

Janet gives specific guidance: around age one, when children can move more, wanting to roll over or grab things during diaper changes is completely normal. But parents don't need to carve out ‘playtime’ for this. The key is to set limits early—gently hold the child in place while narrating: ‘I'll hold you steady for a moment.’ If you wait too long to intervene, your movements become rougher, and the child gets the message that ‘Mom can't hold me down’—which has nothing to do with respecting the child.

— Janet Lansbury
13:37

Feeding and diaper changes need bounded time windows

The correspondent mentions resorting to tricks like ‘feeding through the apron’ to speed things up, and feels guilty about it. Janet responds that feeding itself should be time-limited—when a child slows down or doesn't want to eat, you stop and wait for the next meal. The key isn't to agonize over individual actions, but to redesign your day's rhythm: care moments like diaper changes and meals where the parent is fully present and sets boundaries; then dedicated ‘playtime’ where the child learns to expect this division of time.

— Janet Lansbury
16:41

Children don't need patient parents—they need decisive parents

Janet summarizes: children don't need parents to stay and play out of ‘respect,’ and they don't need parents to strain their patience when a task can't be completed. What children need is for parents to actively manage these moments—that's real self-care, and the sooner you establish this rhythm the better, though it's never too late to start. The core is helping your child change their established patterns, not waiting for them to come to their own realization.

— Janet Lansbury
22:52

Your child is fine—you're the one spiraling

A second parent writes about repeatedly telling a larger child at an IKEA play structure to ‘share’ only to later discover their own child wasn't bothered at all. What escalated was their own anxiety—they'd even forgotten to check their child's actual state. Janet points out that parents' most useful role is often observation rather than intervention. Through objective observation, you often discover the child is in the middle of learning something, not suffering. You should only intervene when there's real risk of physical harm, or when your child shows signs of distress.

— Janet Lansbury
26:00

Taking a toy is different from taking a beloved possession

Janet distinguishes two scenarios: if a public toy is taken away by another child, even if your own child tightly holds on to it, you don't need to forcefully intervene to educate the other child—that's not your job. But if your child brought something they're truly attached to (like a beloved stuffed animal) and it gets taken away, it's worth politely stepping in and asking if they can have it back. She acknowledges this position is controversial and welcomes disagreement from listeners.

— Janet Lansbury
29:06

Walking hand-in-hand undermines your child's balance development

A listener mentions routinely holding their learning-to-walk child's hand on the way to the playground. Janet points out that this seemingly caring gesture actually slows down their walking development—the child thinks they're maintaining balance when really they're leaning on the parent's hand, with no chance to develop their own balance sense. She connects this back to the episode's main theme: more trust and less substituting for them means your child shows more confidence in both physical and social contexts.

— Janet Lansbury
