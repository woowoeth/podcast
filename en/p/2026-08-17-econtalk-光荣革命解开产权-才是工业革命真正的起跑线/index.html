The Industrial Revolution's Hidden Driver: Parliament Untangled Property Rights, Not Technology

The hidden cause of the Industrial Revolution was the Glorious Revolution of the century before: Parliament used legislation to untangle overly complicated property rights, which let investment in agriculture and infrastructure happen at all, and which created the precondition for a return on technical innovations like the steam engine.

Parliament untangled property rights; it did not invent the technology

Russ Roberts reads from the opening of the article, which holds that industrial modernity was born in the English Midlands in the late 18th century — but that what made all of it possible was the Glorious Revolution of the century before. The core mechanism: Parliament used its new powers to untangle property rights that were, at the time, excessively strong and excessively complicated, and in doing so made sustained investment in infrastructure and agriculture possible for the first time. This happened almost entirely peacefully and with the consent of the rights-holders, not through violent expropriation. That is the institutional starting point for the whole conversation, and it is what gave every later land act and infrastructure project its legitimacy.

— Russ Roberts
19:39

A Parliament made of landowners dismantled its own privileges

Ben points out that after 1711 you had to own a great deal of land to sit in Parliament, so Parliament was composed almost entirely of landowners. The ordinary expectation would be that these large landowners would protect their own privileges and expand the space for rent-seeking — but they did the opposite: they untangled their own complicated property rights. Property in the 17th century was nothing like property today. A landowner could not dispose of land freely; there were life tenancies, and land could not be mortgaged, sold or divided, because it was bound up in complicated ‘entail’ settlements. It was precisely this self-binding property regime that formed the single largest institutional obstacle before the Industrial Revolution, and Parliament dismantled these antique restrictions one act at a time.

— Ben Southwood
24:12

Open fields were insurance, and the price was that nobody could innovate

Ben explains two kinds of land that have now largely disappeared: commons and open fields. A common was not something anyone could use — only local landowners could graze on it, according to their ‘stints’, under complicated rules. Under the open-field system, land was cut into many narrow strips scattered across different parcels, which provided insurance but blocked innovation. New crops (such as potatoes and maize) and new techniques (such as horse ploughing) required coordination, and consensus was hard to reach. So even when farmers understood that a new arrangement would raise output, the collective-action problem meant they could not change it on their own; they had to rely on Parliament to coordinate by compulsion.

— Ben Southwood
42:12

Before the 1660s, England's roads were worse than a thousand years earlier

Southwood notes that Parliament passed three kinds of act in great volume: acts resolving family inheritance problems, acts consolidating inefficient small parcels, and acts establishing turnpikes, canals and navigable rivers. Almost none of these existed before the 1660s, and England's roads at the time were extremely bad — worse even than in the era of King Alfred. By 1800, thousands of miles of turnpikes and canals had been built, creating national markets in grain, fertiliser, pottery and textiles, and allowing comparative advantage to operate. This shows that acts of Parliament did not merely rearrange land rights; they directly created an infrastructure network.

— Ben Southwood
49:31

Technology with no market is not worth inventing

Russ sums up Southwood's argument: agriculture became more efficient through legislation and property-rights reform, capital accumulated, and improved infrastructure sharply raised the return on technical innovations such as the steam engine. He draws an analogy to Israel's high-tech industry — without a global market, the innovation would not be worth doing. Infrastructure is the precondition for technology's value to be realised, and government's role was only to permit private parties to build through a reallocation of property rights, not to provide it directly. That means institutional change created the conditions for the technological explosion, while the technology itself already existed.

— Russ Roberts
55:44

The empire that banned printing and the West that capped nuclear are the same thing

Southwood concedes that the Industrial Revolution might have happened even without these reforms, but stresses that institutions can accelerate or obstruct the application of technology. He cites the Ottoman Empire's ban on the printing press and the West's restrictions on nuclear power since the 1970s as cases where institutions slowed technology down. Today's data centres and GPUs are expensive precisely because physical facilities cannot be built: the shortage is not of ideas, it is that application is blocked. The analogy links the historical obstacle of land to today's bottleneck in infrastructure permitting.

— Ben Southwood
1:12:42

Reform has to come from the bottom up, not the top down

Southwood closes on his central point: the West is constrained in physical building, while China builds faster. Most current movements argue for driving reform from the top down, but historically successful changes to land rights have usually come from the bottom up. He calls for pursuing several tracks at once, including agreements at the local level — such as Israel's Tama 38 — which can produce change faster. The ending converts the historical lesson into a present-day policy recommendation, emphasising the value of local experimentation.

Glossary

stint
A grazing allowance on a common, allocated in proportion to how much land you owned, under complicated rules.
land assembly district
A mechanism proposed by Michael Heller that allows land to be redeveloped with the consent of a majority of owners.
Tama 38
An Israeli scheme for rebuilding old buildings that allows redevelopment with the consent of a majority of owners, similar to the historical enclosure movement.
