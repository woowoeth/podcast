Conan Doyle modeled Sherlock Holmes on a forensic surgeon

Joe Bell diagnosed disease by observing patient clothing, accent, and hand wounds—a 'method' his student Conan Doyle transplanted directly to Sherlock Holmes—and Bell himself did secretly help solve multiple murders.

Anesthesia made surgery deadlier before anyone understood infection

After ether anesthesia arrived in 1846, surgeons were no longer constrained by patients writhing on the operating table, so they became more willing to cut deeper. But they still didn't understand bacteria existed. The result: post-operative infection rates rose, and surgery became a 'slow execution.' This counterintuitive causation set the stage for Joseph Lister's entrance—anesthesia solved the pain problem but exposed the infection problem.

9:45

The deadliest force in medicine is not ignorance but vanity

Lister applied Pasteur's germ theory to surgery in the 1860s and explained post-operative infection, but convincing colleagues took a long time. The barrier wasn't technical—it was that surgeons couldn't bear the thought that 'I have been unknowingly killing my patients.' Fitzharris points out: discovery is hard, but changing people's habits is often harder.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
19:52

Bell taught not technique but a way of seeing

Bell told students to recognize disease the way you spot your best friend in a crowd—everyone shares similar features, but you catch that unique detail in an instant. He would have patients speak first about whether they took their medicine on time, rather than finishing their sentences, because patients often lie or hide things. This 'observe first, then construct narrative' training is what Conan Doyle transplanted directly into Sherlock Holmes.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
34:15

Bell secretly helped solve real murders

Bell collaborated long-term with Edinburgh's police pathologist Henry Duncan Littlejohn, often investigating the same case separately and then comparing conclusions. In the case of Eugène Chantrelle poisoning his wife, there's no direct evidence Bell was involved, but before Chantrelle was executed, he scanned the crowd at the gallows and told the police pathologist, 'Give my regards to Joe Bell—he did a fine job putting this noose around my neck.' This became one of the book's most intriguing clues.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
41:29

Bell thought he knew Jack the Ripper's name but kept it secret

In old age, Bell said he had investigated the Jack the Ripper case with a friend (almost certainly Littlejohn), and they each wrote down a name in an envelope. When opened, it was the same person—but they never publicly revealed it. Another suspect, William Henry Bury, was arrested in Dundee for murdering and dismembering his wife, and later the executioner claimed Bury privately confessed to being Jack the Ripper, though such claims are dubious.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
48:57

World War I's weapons outpaced medicine's ability to repair

In WWI, a platoon of 300 could deploy firepower equivalent to 60,000 from the Napoleonic wars. Tanks, flamethrowers, and chemical weapons left many soldiers with facial wounds but alive. A field nurse said 'the science of healing seemed helpless before the science of destruction.' Harold Gillies was pushed by the enormous gap—needing to reconstruct 280,000 faces—almost forced to invent plastic surgery.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
51:15

Society honored amputees but shunned the facially disfigured

WWI soldiers who lost a leg were seen as heroes and victims, but the facially disfigured were often avoided or reviled. These soldiers leaving hospital were required to sit on blue-painted benches to remind passersby not to stare. A soldier named Walter Ashworth, after multiple surgeries, had his fiancée break their engagement, and when he went back to work as a tailor's assistant, his boss required him to stay in the back room so as not to frighten customers.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
1:02:43

Reconstructing a face meant rolling skin into a tube and gradually shifting tissue

Without microsurgical techniques to reconnect blood vessels, Gillies had to roll a skin flap from the chest into a tube, attach one end to the face, wait for it to grow its own blood supply, then cut the other end and gradually move tissue from the thigh to the abdomen to the chest to the face—a process that could take years. Front-line surgeons rushed to stop bleeding and sutured bacteria into the wound, often worsening the injury.

— Lindsey Fitzharris
1:06:19

Gillies performed history's first phalloplasty on a transgender man

In the 1940s, Gillies performed history's first phalloplasty on Michael Dillon, a transgender man. This technique is reportedly still in use today. Dillon was later exposed by British media and besieged by public outcry, forced to leave Britain, but Gillies always supported him, reasoning that people have the right to control every aspect of their identity.

— Lindsey Fitzharris

Glossary

the method
Bell's diagnostic technique of inferring a patient's identity and condition from details like clothing, accent, and hand marks, which Conan Doyle transferred to Sherlock Holmes.
not proven
A third verdict historically available in Scottish courts, standing between guilty and not guilty, with the practical effect of acquittal.
baby farmer
A person in 19th-century England who took payment to raise others' illegitimate or abandoned children, with some using the arrangement to murder infants for profit.
