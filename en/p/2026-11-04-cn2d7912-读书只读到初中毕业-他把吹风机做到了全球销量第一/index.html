He read only through middle school, yet built the world's best-selling hair dryer

He built manned flying vehicles and electric scooters, twice burning through borrowed millions—but by teaching himself brushless motor technology, he priced a hair dryer at one-fifth of Dyson's and made it a global sales leader.

12:09

Grades wounded him, but poverty and bullying did not

Trash-picking as a child, bullied in school as an outsider and poor student, always among the shortest kids in gym class—none of it left psychological scars. The one real wound: poor grades and teacher criticism. His temperament is ‘大条’ (thick-skinned)—he forgets stress easily. This early resilience explains how he could restart after multiple bankruptcies. The foundation was already there from childhood.

13:10

Yahoo's homepage hit him like science fiction

In 2003 he booted a second-hand computer and saw the Yahoo homepage—the shock of ‘all the world's information displayed in one place’ felt ‘特别科幻’. At 13,000 yuan per year, he enrolled in a no-credential computer training school, a major family expense. When they nominated a class leader, he volunteered—his first time building confidence by knowing more than his peers. Before this moment he was extremely withdrawn, afraid even to talk to strangers.

— Ye Hongxin
44:27

He burned millions on a manned flying machine—because scaling a drone looked simple

Around age 25, after selling his Taobao shop, he decided to build a hexacopter he could ride in. His reasoning: ‘I played with drones, so this is just putting a drone scaled up.’ The team scrounged factories and recruited grad students. The result: 20 minutes of flight time, poor reliability, millions in personal funds burned, funding pitches sent to VCs that all disappeared. He compares it to Li Shufu's line about cars—‘just a sofa with wheels’—which the entire country mocked, but which led to a massive business. Fearless ignorance is sometimes where boldness starts.

— Ye Hongxin
1:00:33

He sold scooters to one American buyer who hit $1 billion valuation

After the flying machine failed, the team borrowed over 3 million yuan for a second-generation electric scooter, mortgaging house and car. They built the product but lacked manufacturing capacity and brand channels, so they licensed it exclusively to an American buyer. That buyer took this single product to the US, registered a company, raised funding, and eventually achieved a peak valuation of $1 billion. Looking back, Ye realized it wasn't betrayal—he had zero business and fundraising skills at the time. He should have earned that value himself.

— Ye Hongxin
1:17:44

Two years to get a brushless motor right—and he rewrote it halfway through

When designing Laifen's first hair dryer motor, he started with an outer rotor, then quickly realized high-speed motors need inner rotors: ‘many designs were completely wrong’. Operating at 110,000 rpm, 300+ volts, the motor seized on the bearing. Chinese bearing manufacturers couldn't solve it; the core patents were locked by a Japanese company. Only through a buyer's supplier could he find a solution. What was supposed to take one year took two. His relationship with his business partner deteriorated; they split after launch.

— Ye Hongxin
1:19:46

Great products do not make great businesses

He reviewed why early fundraising failed: strong product instincts, but business logic full of holes—VCs saw through it instantly. His conclusion: product-driven companies succeed far less often than those run by strategists. Jobs is rare precisely because he was both a genius product manager and a capable CEO—that combination almost never appears in business history. Most founders who are strong only at product pay a steep price learning the business side. Ye paid it too.

— Ye Hongxin
1:34:54

Two casual videos went viral—and sales finally took off

After launch, sales were sluggish for four or five months. The turning point arrived by accident: two low-cost videos. One was a KOL explaining the product details. The other was him sitting in on a talk with investor Wang Huiwen (王慧文)—captured on camera, both men bald, talking sharp and direct. The clip spread everywhere: 200 million views globally. Comment sections identified him: ‘this bald head is Laifen's Ye.’ In the first few hours after posting, likes climbed from 10,000 to 200,000; the team watched the counter refresh in shock.

— Ye Hongxin
2:33:30

Accused of copying Dyson—he chose silence over the truth

When Laifen launched a razor, outcry erupted: it copied Dyson's design. The timeline proved him innocent—his patent was filed first, the launch gap was only 28 days (impossible to copy), his evidence chain was complete. Yet Ye decided not to defend publicly. His reasoning: ‘in marketing and PR, perception beats truth. If enough people believe you copied, there's no counter-narrative, and you stay silent—everyone concludes you copied.’ He admits not defending cost him. But if it happens twice or three times, ‘I might never wash it clean for the rest of my life’—so he accepted the loss to avoid compounding damage.

— Ye Hongxin
