One point for orbit, zero for recovery: rocket recovery has no middle state

Zhuque-3's first flight reached orbit but failed to recover; deputy chief designer Dong Kai believes rocket launches are pass or fail with no middle state: one point for orbit, zero for recovery. What really determines the pace of catching up is the recovery range, engine redundancy, and the capacity to build a hundred engines a year.

Zhuque-3's first flight was a 'youth edition', not the full version

Dong Kai explains that Zhuque-3 is a self-developed liquid oxygen methane medium-to-heavy rocket by Blue Arrow Aerospace. The full version is 76 meters long, with stainless steel tanks making up 80% of the hull length, nine engines on the first stage and one on the second. The first flight vehicle was a 'youth edition' shortened by about 10 meters, with a takeoff thrust of 83 tons from the Tianque 12A; the full version will use the Tianque 12B with over 100 tons of thrust. In terms of payload capacity, it can approach the Long March 5 and cover medium rockets downward. The recovery range for the first flight was set at 390 kilometers; if it were to maximize payload, it should be 550 to 600 kilometers.

— Dong Kai
5:26

Calling it a domestic Falcon 9 is high praise; surpassing it in 5 years is hard to be optimistic about

Regarding netizens calling Zhuque-3 a 'domestic Falcon 9', Dong Kai considers this very high praise. Falcon 9 is the only reusable rocket in the world in operational service with extremely strong reliability, having launched 150 times and produced a rocket reused 32 times. But Musk said Zhuque-3 would surpass Falcon 9 within 5 years, and Dong Kai dares not be so optimistic: China's space industry launches about 80 times a year across all models, while Falcon 9 alone launches 150 times a year, equivalent to three launches a week. He believes it is difficult for Blue Arrow alone to achieve this; it is more encouragement and a spur.

— Dong Kai
11:09

Orbit has three grades, recovery only pass or fail

Before Zhuque-3's first flight, the flight outline evaluated orbit and recovery separately. Orbit was graded by perigee altitude as complete success, success, and failure: perigee above 170 kilometers was complete success, 130 kilometers was success. Recovery had only a binary standard: standing at the landing site and not falling for one minute was success; not standing was failure. The overall mission had four grades: only if both orbit and recovery succeeded and perigee met the standard was it called complete success; if only one of orbit and recovery succeeded, it was basic success. In the end, the first flight met the orbit standard but failed recovery, and the commander announced the launch a complete success (for orbit) and the mission a basic success.

— Dong Kai
24:48

Cutting recovery range to 390 kilometers means throwing away 2 to 3 tons of payload

The recovery range directly determines the rocket's payload. After stage separation, the first stage flies inertially toward the recovery site; if the range is fixed, the separation altitude is fixed. The full Zhuque-3 corresponds to a 600-kilometer recovery range; for safety, the first flight cut the range to 390 kilometers, reducing the separation altitude from 76 kilometers to 66 kilometers, equivalent to flying 10 kilometers less. For every 10 kilometers of recovery range added, payload increases by nearly 150 kilograms; if the range were extended from 390 to 600 kilometers, payload could rise by 2 to 3 tons. The first flight landed with 10 tons of propellant, exactly the price paid for safety.

— Dong Kai
38:13

Landing burn uses 5 engines, not to save fuel but to brake

Recovery process: after stage separation, the first stage climbs inertially above 130 kilometers, then turns tail-down. At 80 kilometers altitude, the first burn occurs with three engines working simultaneously for about 40 seconds, aimed at deceleration to prevent excessive dynamic pressure when entering dense atmosphere. At 40 kilometers, engines shut down and the aerodynamic control phase begins, with grid fins and strakes used for trim. At 4 kilometers altitude, the final landing burn occurs. Unlike Falcon 9, Zhuque-3 has 5 engines that can be ignited multiple times, firing in sequence from one to five and then shutting down one by one, with a single engine for final landing. This first flight had a problem during the landing burn phase.

— Dong Kai
41:31

Four of five engines failed to build thrust; the rocket could have been saved

From video and data, during the landing burn the center engine ignited successfully, but the other four exploded before thrust was fully established, failing to generate enough braking force. If the four engines had built thrust, the rocket might have been saved. The five engines were set up as insurance for the first flight—there was no real aerodynamic deceleration data for the first flight, and there was concern that deceleration would be insufficient, requiring extra braking. After this flight, the team has data support for aerodynamic deceleration, and may later remove these two engines, actually improving reliability.

— Dong Kai
51:15

The bottleneck for rapid iteration is production capacity, not whether you can afford to blow up

The real bottleneck for rapid iteration is production capacity, not whether you can afford to blow up. Blue Arrow is preparing a production capacity layout for ten rockets in 2026, with each rocket using 10 engines, meaning preparing 100 engines a year. Even if every recovery test fails, ten flights can still be afforded in 2026. Dong Kai emphasizes that SpaceX's rapid iteration is not at the cost of reduced reliability, but achieves high reliability and high production capacity simultaneously. Domestic strict safety regulation is the prerequisite for the industry to go steady and far, not a restriction on iteration.

— Dong Kai
1:02:37

Catching up should not be measured by a running mindset of 'how many years behind'

Asked about the gap between Chinese and American space, Dong Kai believes the gap has been narrowing: China launched a satellite in 1970, the US landed on the moon in 1969; the gap then was almost too large to look at, but predecessors were neither pessimistic nor blindly optimistic. He refuses to measure it with a running mindset of 'how many years behind', believing that as long as one runs hard, one day they will catch up and surpass. Just as aviation crossed from second-generation to sixth-generation fighters, China's space industry is on the same catch-up trajectory.

— Dong Kai
