Voters hate the Democratic Party not because it's too left, but because it lost

Democrats' approval rivals Trump's lows, but only single-digit voters blame the party for not being left enough—the real anger stems from losing to Trump and swing voters feeling the party doesn't speak for ordinary people.

Blue wave is baseline, but Senate is Republican stronghold

Druke says the national ‘generic ballot’ shows Democrats ahead by 9 percentage points, which at the House level almost guarantees a blue wave in any scenario. But the Senate races this cycle involve states like Texas, Iowa, Ohio, and Alaska—places Trump won by 11 to 13 percentage points in 2024—where baseline party lean is R+10 or even R+13. Even if the national environment favors Democrats by 9 points, it only makes these states ‘competitive’ rather than ‘safe,’ and requires additional factors like candidate quality.

10:00

Polling errors aren't random—they track education and participation gaps

Druke notes that polls don't over- or under-estimate one party by accident. In presidential election years, turnout hits 63-65 percent, reaching a broader cross-section of voters. Midterm elections have lower turnout, making polls more likely to miss lower-engagement voters who lean Trump. These voters call in during presidential years but may not answer polling calls; in midterm years their turnout is also lower, so missing them actually reduces the polling error's impact. This explains why 2018 and 2022 midterm polls were highly accurate, while 2024 was the third consecutive cycle understating Republicans.

— Galen Druke
17:00

Voters dislike Democrats—not because it's too left, but because it lost

Democrats' net approval ranges from -10 to -20, nearly as bad as Trump's. When asked ‘is the Democratic Party too far left,’ only single-digit voters say yes. Druke offers a fourth explanation cutting through all prior theories: voters don't like losers. Many who dislike Democrats actually have voted and will vote for them again—they're just angry about losing to Trump. Deeper down, ordinary voters see Democrats as representing the elite, certain genders or races, rather than ‘people like me’—even though voters actually like what the party says on economic issues.

— Galen Druke
24:00

Claiming socialists have taken over Democrats is hyperbole

This Congress has only two Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) members—Rashida Tlaib and AOC—with next Congress expected to have roughly seven after primary victories. This hardly constitutes a ‘takeover.’ Compare the 2010 Tea Party wave, which brought 45 Tea Party-backed members to Congress. By today's standards, the Tea Party's success then far exceeds the Democratic Socialists' momentum now. Also, primary voting represents only about 20 percent of registered voters, with participants being the party's most ideologically committed and active members. In Maine, Graham Platner had explosive primary support but collapsed to net-negative 40 in the general election.

— Galen Druke
30:00

Moderate Democrats outrun progressives by roughly 4 points

Split Ticket founder Lakshya Jain's ‘wins above replacement’ research found that in highly competitive districts, ‘Blue Dog’ moderate Democrats outperform progressive candidates by an average of about 4 percentage points; Republican ideologically committed candidates face the same penalty. Mounk traces the logic: the two-party system suppresses third parties; primaries with just 20 percent turnout push both parties toward extremes; this 4-point penalty is ‘painful but not fatal.’ Using Michigan: baseline R+1/2, but under a blue wave reaches D+7/8. Subtract 4 and it's still D+3—enough to win a Senate seat. This is why both parties keep repeating the same mistake without real correction.

— Yascha Mounk / Galen Druke
39:00

Maine and Texas Senate races have completely flipped

Harris won Maine by 7 points in 2024, but the latest New York Times/Siena poll shows incumbent Republican Senator Susan Collins ahead by 3 points. She has a personal brand independent of party and runs ahead of the environment among college-educated suburban voters. Texas Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton faced fraud charges brought by his own party and has net approval 20 points underwater. He's currently trailing Democrat James Talarico by 3.5 points. The difference in candidate quality creates an extraordinary situation: ‘Two years ago if you said Texas had better odds than Maine to flip, nobody would have believed you.’

— Galen Druke
47:00

Hispanic voters shifted +35 points to +3—don't set this as baseline

In 2016, Hillary won Hispanic voters by 35-38 points; in 2024, Harris won by only 3-5 points. This is enormous. Druke cautions that such ‘late-joining, early-leaving’ voters have naturally weak party loyalty anyway, and midterm elections feature lower turnout—precisely when these less-engaged swing voters (including young people) are less likely to vote. So the 2026 result shouldn't become a new baseline. By 2028, an estimated 15 percent or more voters will re-enter the electorate, potentially reshaping the landscape again—as 2020's misreadings of Hispanic voting showed.

— Galen Druke
54:00

Harris leads primary polls, but she's probably still overestimated

Harris leads virtually all 2028 primary polling, but Druke sees this as mostly name recognition—her opponents haven't really started campaigning yet. Black voters represent about 20 percent of Democratic primary voters and tend to consolidate behind a single candidate, potentially favoring Harris, though it's not decisive. Druke is more bullish on Georgia Senator Jon Ossoff: he distanced himself when 2020 Democrats proposed unpopular positions like ‘decriminalize border crossing’ and ‘defund police.’ He's now polling to win 8-10 points ahead of environment in a state Trump won, has raised over 70 million dollars, and maintains distance from his party on Israeli-Palestinian issues—giving him room to bridge intra-party divisions.

— Galen Druke

Glossary

generic ballot
A poll asking voters which party they'd like to control Congress, not tied to specific candidates.
wins above replacement
Borrowed from baseball statistics; measures how many percentage points a candidate outperforms a ‘replacement-level’ candidate.
blue dog Democrat
A moderate-to-conservative faction within the Democratic Party in American politics.
DSA
America's largest socialist organization; members include AOC and other members of Congress.
dummander
Miscalculated gerrymandering where an intended safe district ends up flipping against you.
