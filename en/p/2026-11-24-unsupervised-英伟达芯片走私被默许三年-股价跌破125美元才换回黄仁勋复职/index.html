Three years of tolerated chip smuggling; NVIDIA stock at $125 swaps for Jensen Huang's return

The real leverage behind Jensen Huang's reinstatement wasn't national security—it was a stock market collapse. Trump cares whether the S&P 500 crashes, not whether chips reach China's military.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:14

NVIDIA tolerated chip smuggling to China for three years without substantial resistance

Jensen Huang claimed for years on television that server racks were too heavy to smuggle, but Supermicro executives were caught using hair dryers to remove rack labels for imports into China. Blackwell chips were never legally approved for China, yet NVIDIA largely permitted the gray channel. Jordan compares this to banks ignoring money laundering from North Korea and Iran for years until Treasury imposed billion-dollar fines—the semiconductor industry and NVIDIA remain in that ‘see but don't stop it’ stage.

— Jordan
9:25

China likely weaponizes Jensen Huang's partner as leverage, not as an operative

The New York Times reported that Jensen Huang's partner had multiple contacts with China's Ministry of State Security (MSS). Jordan doubts she spent 30 years on a Chinese intelligence payroll, but once a relationship exists, Beijing probably knows and likely tries to exploit that relationship as leverage—entirely different from ‘dispatching an operative to set a honeytrap.’ He emphasizes that Huang's real problem has always been negligence on chip controls; that matters far more than a tabloid scandal.

— Jordan
14:37

The new 132-page rule bans Malaysian cloud companies from serving China within 90 days

The rule operates on two fronts: requiring Commerce Department approval for advanced chips produced at TSMC bound for China, cutting off smuggling; and banning Malaysian and other regional cloud companies from serving Chinese customers after 90 days. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google were briefly excited to grab more chips, but whether this holds depends largely on whether Jensen Huang lobbies to overturn it after his reinstatement.

— Jordan
16:37

Stock market panic, not national security, drove Trump to reinstate Jensen Huang

Jordan admits he did not expect this reversal—he was playing GTA Thursday and Friday, missing the market. By the time he checked, NVIDIA stock had fallen to around $125. That's the signal Trump actually responds to: bond market crashes and S&P 500 drops matter more than abstract fears of chips reaching Chinese military. Rubio and Bessent apparently took chip controls and security risks seriously, but for Trump, stock market fear outweighed those concerns.

— Jordan
22:55

Only 2% of Americans want faster AI progress, reshaping political calculations

Huang cannot control public hatred of AI. Jordan cites a survey showing only 2% of Americans want AI to progress faster—genuine political headwind that could accelerate state-level tightening even before a critical moment arrives within two years. He fears that by 2029, both parties' presidential candidates will say ‘we need to slow this down,’ leaving the industry a narrow window to reshape the narrative. Last week's AI-chaos incident already got blamed on AI.

— Jordan
27:01

AI's cyberattack capability transforms it from productivity tool to weapons system

When confronted with ‘better to sell chips and create dependency than let them self-develop,’ Jordan counters that this logic held before but no longer applies: models can now directly launch cyberattacks, making this a weapons system, not mere productivity software. The opposing argument—‘they'll eventually solve it with Huawei anyway’—misses that Huawei's bottleneck is EUV lithography and high-bandwidth memory equipment shortage, not demand. Withholding chips still delays their military AI.

— Jordan
30:11

Western computing advantage of 10–15x renders joint chip development mathematically insignificant

Jordan concedes U.S.-China chip and model co-optimization does occur, but as long as America doesn't sell EUV lithography, etching and deposition equipment, and blocks smuggling and remote access, Western computing advantage holds at 10–15x—a scale where co-development gains are mathematically negligible. He notes this hinges on timeframe: within 12–18 months, Anthropic's anxiety is completely reasonable; stretched to five or seven years, America faces the entire global semiconductor manufacturing ecosystem, while China's engineering talent and supply chain depth still lag substantially.

— Jordan
34:40

Humanoid robots at conference demo failed visibly; all magnets source from China

At the Coral robotics and machine learning conference in Austin, humanoid robot demos ‘embarrassed everyone’—DeepMind spent hundreds of billions on robots that couldn't stably pick up a pancake on stage, with onlookers amazed to see it happen at all. Almost every booth hid the supply chain behind ‘overseas,’ and when Jordan asked where magnets come from, every answer was ‘only one place sells magnets’—exposing these vendors' actual dependence on Chinese rare earth supplies far exceeds what they admit.

— Jordan
