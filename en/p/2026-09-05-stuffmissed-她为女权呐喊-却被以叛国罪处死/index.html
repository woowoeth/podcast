She Cried Out for Women's Rights, but Was Executed for Treason

Olympe de Gouges is known for her Declaration of the Rights of Woman, but her death was not due to her feminist advocacy; it was because she opposed the king's execution and attacked Robespierre.

The Declaration of the Rights of Woman Responds to the Declaration of the Rights of Man

Olympe de Gouges is most famous for her 1791 Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen, a direct response to the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The original used citoyen; she changed it to the feminine form citoyenne, which looks quite different but sounds similar. Her writing and political activity went far beyond this pamphlet, and her execution was not because she called for women's rights.

— Tracy V. Wilson
6:13

Biographical Truth Is Mixed with Fictional Invention

The first English biography of de Gouges, Women's Rights in the French Revolution, heavily cites her epistolary novel Memoirs of Madame de Valmont as factual evidence, but this book is highly fictional. The protagonist is widely considered to be her own stand-in, while the Marquis de Flaucour represents her possible biological father, the Marquis de Pompignan. This lineage remains unconfirmed to this day.

— Holly Frey

The Comédie-Française Suppresses a Female Playwright

Zamore et Mirza was written in 1784 and submitted anonymously; it was accepted the following year but delayed in production. After de Gouges repeatedly pressed the theater, it revoked her admission rights and sought her arrest. Between 1761 and 1789, the theater accepted only nine plays by women, and she was the only writer treated this way. She later wrote to the Duke of Richelieu, who helped restore her status.

— Tracy V. Wilson

The Slavery Play Becomes the First Abolitionist Drama

In 1789 the play was finally staged, but the setting was changed from the East Indies to the Caribbean, and the characters from Asians to Africans, with the title becoming The Slavery of the Blacks. It is often considered the first French play explicitly supporting abolition and told from the perspective of the enslaved. Abolitionists praised it, while wealthy colonists hired hecklers to disrupt performances, and actors refused to darken their skin for the roles.

— Holly Frey
23:32

The Article Mirrors the Original 17 Articles, Rewriting Each

The Declaration of the Rights of Man granted civil rights but excluded women, and the 1791 Constitution limited voting rights to taxpaying men. De Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman copied the format of the 17 articles, rewriting each one. For example, where the original Article 10 guaranteed freedom of speech, she added that mothers had the right to publicly name the father of their children. The entire work was dedicated to Marie Antoinette, encouraging her to advance women's rights.

— Tracy V. Wilson
28:39

Defending Louis XVI Becomes a Liability

Despite her revolutionary stance, de Gouges believed constitutional monarchy was best for France, and that the king should be a symbol rather than a power. During Louis XVI's treason trial in 1792, she voluntarily defended him, arguing that since the monarchy had been abolished, the Republic could not judge the king for actions taken while he was in office. Her logic was criticized as far-fetched; the king was ultimately executed, and she herself was seen as a royalist.

— Holly Frey
30:43

Federalist Proposal Leads to Her Arrest

In July 1793, de Gouges suggested that French provinces should govern themselves rather than form a unified republic, which was seen as a direct provocation. She underestimated the intense political climate of the Reign of Terror. She voluntarily brought investigators to her office, believing her works would prove her loyalty, but her unfinished play France Preserved, which sympathized with Marie Antoinette, was used as evidence against her. She was ultimately executed on charges of opposing the republican constitution.

— Tracy V. Wilson
