She Spoke Up for Women's Rights and Was Executed for Treason

Olympe de Gouges is known for the Declaration of the Rights of Woman, but she was killed not for her feminist claims, but for opposing the execution of the king and attacking Robespierre.

The Declaration of the Rights of Woman answers the Declaration of the Rights of Man

Olympe de Gouges is most famous for the 1791 Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen, a direct response to the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The original used citoyen; she switched to the feminine form citoyenne, which looks completely different but sounds similar. Her writing and political activity went far beyond this one pamphlet, and her death sentence was not for calling for women's rights.

— Tracy V. Wilson
6:13

Biographical truth is mixed with novelistic invention

De Gouges's first English-language biography, Women's Rights in the French Revolution, draws heavily on her epistolary novel Memoirs of Madame de Valmont as factual evidence, but the book is highly fictional. Its protagonist is widely considered a stand-in for herself, while the Marquis de Flaucour represents her possible biological father, the Marquis de Pompignan. This parentage has never been confirmed.

14:19

The Comédie-Française suppressed a woman playwright

Zamore et Mirza was written in 1784 and submitted anonymously; it was accepted the following year but its staging was long delayed. As de Gouges kept pressing, the theater actually revoked her admission rights and applied for a warrant for her arrest. Between 1761 and 1789 the theater accepted only nine plays by women, and she was the only writer treated this way. She later wrote to the Duke of Richelieu, who helped restore her standing.

— Tracy V. Wilson
17:28

Slavery became the first abolitionist play

In 1789 the play finally opened, but its setting moved from the East Indies to the Caribbean, its characters changed from Asians to Africans, and its title became The Slavery of the Blacks. It is often regarded as France's first play to explicitly support abolition and to tell the story from the perspective of the enslaved. Abolitionists praised it, while wealthy men with colonial interests hired hecklers to disrupt it, and actors refused to blacken their skin for the roles.

— Holly Frey
23:32

The article rewrites all 17 articles one by one

The Declaration of the Rights of Man granted rights to citizens but excluded women, and the 1791 constitution restricted the vote to taxpaying men. De Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman copied the 17-article format and rewrote each article. For example, where the original's Article 10 guarantees freedom of speech, she added that a mother has the right to publicly declare the identity of her child's father. The whole document is dedicated to Marie Antoinette, encouraging her to advance women's rights.

— Tracy V. Wilson
28:39

Defending Louis XVI became the rope around her neck

Despite her revolutionary stance, de Gouges believed a constitutional monarchy suited France best, and that the king should be a symbol rather than a power. In 1792, at Louis XVI's treason trial, she volunteered to defend him, on the grounds that the monarchy had already been abolished and a republic could not try a former king for acts committed while he reigned. Her logic was called strained; the king was executed anyway, and she herself came to be seen as a royalist.

— Holly Frey
30:43

Her federalist proposal led to her arrest

In July 1793, de Gouges proposed that France's provinces govern themselves rather than form a unified republic, which was seen as a direct provocation. She underestimated the fierce political atmosphere of the Terror, and voluntarily brought investigators to her office, thinking her work would prove her loyalty. Instead, her unfinished play France Saved was used as evidence against her because of its sympathy for Marie Antoinette. She was ultimately executed on the charge of opposing the republican constitution.

— Tracy V. Wilson
