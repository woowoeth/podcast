3:18

A demo on a timer is a demo whose success rate you should doubt

Ye Yangsheng taught a set of trade-show appraisal methods: first, check whether the demo is on a timer — a timed performance may mean low confidence in the success rate, requiring someone watching and guarding it, or hardware that cannot sustain long operation; then check whether both arms are doing independent actions at the same time — teleoperation usually finishes one hand before the other, and two hands on different trajectories are most likely autonomous planning; also check whether it can self-recover after failure, and whether it still recognizes things when the lighting changes or a pure white object is placed there. He suggests entering the venue during setup — many companies are actually collecting data on site and retraining, and only Unitree takes a pure simulation route that does not need this step.

— Ye Yangsheng
14:31

The real headline is not a new robot, it is Procurement Day

The biggest difference between this WRC and previous ones is the establishment of Procurement Day, where central SOEs and large enterprises bring procurement projects directly to the venue — that is the real headline. What Ye Yangsheng exhibited was a delivery robot plus robotic arm redoing the hotel food-delivery scenario: the robot picks up the takeout itself, presses the elevator, and delivers it to the room door. Why not use the previous generation's elevator-retrofit approach? Because it basically does not work overseas, possibly involving safety regulations, and the retrofit cost is several times that in China; domestic elevators are special equipment, filing and construction take time, and a no-retrofit solution can speed up delivery. He believes a humanoid robot is defined as a machine whose form and behavior are close to a human, able to use facilities prepared for humans, but it does not necessarily have to have two arms.

— Ye Yangsheng
18:10

Strip out dance performances, and real deployment hitting ten percent would be good

The host read out a set of shipment figures: in the first half of 2026, global humanoid robot shipments exceeded 22,000 units, with Chinese manufacturers accounting for about 97%; AgiBot led with 9,700 units, Unitree with over 7,000, Galbot with 1,100, and the top five together accounting for 86% globally. Ye Yangsheng believes China has a dominant position in complete machines, relying on its manufacturing base; foreign models are strong but cannot compete at all on the body. But after stripping out dance-performance shipments, he believes real deployment demand reaching 10% would already be very, very good; scenarios like space-capsule retail cannot make the ROI work, with a single environment and fixed actions, and they also do not generate data that can improve generalization.

The heat in the capital market is in world models, but very few are actually showing demos on site. Among the three directions Fei-Fei Li mentioned, generation and simulation evaluation can still be demonstrated; the third is a planning-style world model in latent space, predicting the latent vector changes of objects over a future period, which is hard to visualize, and the videos released are no different from VLA demos. Asked whether there is水分 when every company claims VLA plus world model plus full-stack self-development, Ye Yangsheng said the水分 is very large: startups that have actually bought ten-thousand-GPU clusters and continuously buy data for pretraining may be only one or two; many companies hold billions on their books, waiting for others abroad to pave the road before spending, avoiding making a wedding dress for someone else.

— Ye Yangsheng
37:57

Robots sent to factories are not running, so revenue cannot be recognized

Unitree's IPO is the biggest off-site hot topic of this WRC. Ye Yangsheng said a benchmark market cap is good for the industry, proving embodied companies can go public, and the higher the market cap the better for everyone. But the 20-plus companies queuing for IPO must pass strict audits: robots sent to factories but not in use cannot have revenue recognized; signing an acceptance form but not actually running is not acceptable; selling to distributors but not reaching end users is not acceptable; selling to data factories and buying the data back is essentially circular within the body and also not acceptable. Ye Yangsheng said his own company went through this kind of audit, and auditors go to factories to see on site whether the robots are running.

— Ye Yangsheng
40:11

This year's real increment is in the neural layer, not the brain

Ye Yangsheng divides the tech stack into three layers: brain, cerebellum, and neural. At the brain layer, very few companies are truly doing pretraining, models are highly homogeneous, basically following the foreign frontier, and the new direction is using agents to schedule multiple expert models to complete long tasks; cerebellar motion control has improved greatly across the board, but action speed is still an order of magnitude behind humans; the neural layer is this year's real increment — robots have more and more sensors and actuators, requiring high-speed, stable bus protocols that support hardware time synchronization, but there is still no standard definition, and the ones doing this work are actually chip companies and low-level software companies.

— Ye Yangsheng
44:53

Data standards will not be defined, they can only be fought out

The data pain points break down into four layers: there is no industry specification for data formats, each company defines its own, and others must convert before use, which is very costly at large volumes; timing requires tens-of-nanoseconds-level time synchronization, and if sensors only support software time synchronization the precision is insufficient, while hardware time synchronization protocols differ across vendors; there is no consensus on annotation specifications — besides labeling a cup, whether to label transparency and speed is still being tested; and above that, the collection scope depends on the body's design and the brain's training method, so the entire chain cannot be normalized. Ye Yangsheng said their approach to building a data subsidiary is to redo the underlying logic that once made their controllers number one globally — adapt to as many companies as possible, and unify through de facto standards.

— Ye Yangsheng
55:30

This year's progress does not come from world models, it comes from post-training

Asked what brought the industry's progress this year, Ye Yangsheng said it is not world models but post-training: reinforcement learning itself is a kind of post-training, plus higher data quality and stronger engineering capabilities brought by AI coding. But he admits the industry as a whole is anxious and has not yet seen a product that can truly land; on ROI, he observed on site that the gap with industrial robots is not clearly narrowing, but agrees it will converge over time. He expects surprises in data and world models next year, because this year everyone invested heavily in high-quality, low-cost data acquisition and pipeline infrastructure. Next year he will look at only two metrics: whether the actions are fast, and whether it runs from start to finish rather than doing timed demos.

Glossary

VLA / Vision-Language-Action Model
An end-to-end model that maps visual and language inputs directly to robot actions, the mainstream tech stack of this year's demos.
World Model
A model that internally simulates the evolution of the physical world, used for generating scenes, simulation evaluation, or planning, divided into visualizable and latent-space types.
Teleoperation
A human remotely controlling a robot in real time, often used for data collection or remote demonstrations, distinguishable by the continuity of actions.
EMG / Electromyography
Bioelectric signals of muscle discharge collected by a wristband, cross-validating hand posture with vision/touch, a new sensor trend this year.
Data Pipeline
The series of processing steps from collection, annotation, and format conversion to feeding the model, with no unified industry standard yet.
Post-training
The stage after pretraining where the model is further adjusted with reinforcement learning and high-quality data; the industry believes this year's progress is mainly driven by it.
