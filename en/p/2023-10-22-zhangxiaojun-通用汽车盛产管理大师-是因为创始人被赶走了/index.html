GM Churned Out Management Gurus Because Its Founder Was Pushed Out

Founder William Durant was thrown out by the bankers, and that is precisely what made GM fertile ground for professional managers — Sloan's BU system and Drucker's management theory both grew here. At Ford, where the family never let go, professional managers never had a place.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

14:13

He bought 25 companies in three years, then was thrown out

Durant started out as a carriage maker. In 1904 he first bought the struggling Buick; on September 16, 1908, he founded General Motors, whose initial assets were just Buick. He didn't only buy car brands — he bought parts companies too, acquiring 25 companies between 1908 and 1910, 11 of them automakers. The flip side of all that buying was a financial crisis: he spent too fast, bought a dozen-plus companies in two years and still had to digest and integrate them, and he was forced to bring in two banking groups, DuPont and Morgan, to rescue the company. A rescue always comes with conditions: I want management, I want control. Durant was cleared out by the bankers. But he didn't stop — he started Chevrolet from scratch with Louis Chevrolet, and four years later used Chevrolet to take GM back. The old GM was folded in and reorganized into the new GM.

18:44

Only after the founder was ousted did professional managers grow

Ford was an engineer from a farm-owning family, relatively autocratic, and his tight control over the company left no room for professional managers to have a say in early Ford; talented people like Iacocca were all washed out along the way. GM was the exact opposite: an iconoclastic founder who was liable to be thrown out by the bankers at any moment, and the bankers themselves couldn't run the company, so they had to bring in professional managers. That's why GM offered professional managers soil from the very start. Before Sloan, GM had already gone through eight presidents, and Sloan himself didn't study management — he studied electrical engineering, out of MIT. That's why there is now the MIT Sloan School of Management.

24:01

Sloan's management philosophy: centralized policy, decentralized operations

The management system Sloan created was clear: centralized policy, decentralized operations, multiple brands, financial independence. He originated the BU (business unit) mechanism — before him there was no BU system. A BU is essentially an authorization mechanism; it changed Ford's dictatorial mechanism, and they called it a decentralized organization. He also encouraged employees to speak up, having people who disagreed attend the same meetings and express their views — a very democratic thing at the time. He wrote: as a chief executive, being objective and fair is very important; one must have an extraordinary capacity for tolerance; a boss cannot eliminate the distance between himself and his employees; work is a very rigorous matter and cannot be confused with friendship or other social relationships. Both Drucker and Sloan wrote books about GM, and Sloan's was actually forced out of him by Drucker — he felt Drucker's book couldn't express his own views on managing the company, and Drucker wrote the foreword for him.

32:24

Sloan ended the Model T with four sales tactics

The Model T appeared when consumers could put a few hundred dollars on the table; when consumers' finances improved, they wanted to buy a better car. Sloan did four things: first, installment payments — a financial innovation; second, trade-in subsidies for used cars, so an existing car could be swapped for a colored one; third, the closed body — a technological advance, since Ford used an open body to make cars cheap, like a carriage, and consumers couldn't accept eating dust forever; fourth, annual model changes — in the past a model stayed unchanged, now consumers wanted a change every year. This set of things was the exact opposite of Ford's position, and it formed a basic playbook for the auto industry; the carmakers that came after basically operate around this approach.

34:24

GM's bankruptcy wasn't a manufacturing failure, it was a broken financial chain

GM's biggest crisis was its bankruptcy after the 2008-2009 financial crisis. There were two layers to the cause: first, selling cars wasn't profitable and making cars wasn't profitable — it survived on finance, and in the financial crisis it couldn't borrow, so the financial chain snapped; second, on the employee side there was too strong a union, the status quo couldn't be changed, costs couldn't come down, and many employees had worked at the carmaker for generations. The result of the concentrated warming board was that few finance companies failed, but GM went down first. During the bankruptcy reorganization it was split into an old company and a new company: the old company took on the historical liabilities, the new company started light, and an industry-influential professional manager was brought in to run it. The price was that early-founded brands were shut down — without the Chinese market, even Buick couldn't have been saved, and brands like Oldsmobile were killed off immediately.

51:11

CEOs took a billion, worker wages fell seventeen percent

Bloomberg reported that the CEOs of GM, Ford and Stellantis have taken home more than $1 billion in actual compensation since 2010, including equity; over the same period, the wages of American auto workers, whether unionized or not, fell seventeen percent. On an individual level: Mary Barra's take-home pay last year was $34.1 million, Ford's CEO's was $18 million, and the Stellantis one's was $13 million. Worker wages are basically seven or eight thousand dollars. The pay gap keeps widening — that's the reason for the strikes. Auto industry CEO and employee pay is higher than at other listed companies, but far below the internet — and auto company CEOs are not founders, whereas many internet companies still have founders, and Chinese carmakers are mostly founder-led and pay very high salaries.

55:13

Having many children to fight in a group, even insiders can't tell them apart

GM entered China first as Buick, and later wanted to bring in its most core brand, Chevrolet, but Chevrolet looked like a lower-end brand in China — how to bring it in? At the time they came up with the ‘one-hundred-thousand-yuan little Buick’; that model was actually the Opel Corsa, and its price and positioning should have been pushed down to Chevrolet, but there was a gap between Buick and Chevrolet, so the little Buick made it more acceptable to consumers. Later sales and customer segments differentiated, and Chevrolet had to serve as the base, with Cadillac extending above it, forming a pyramid system. Chinese carmakers learned multi-brand strategy, having many children to fight in a group, but they didn't achieve product differentiation, so it became a waste of resources and mutual cannibalization. No matter how cleverly the brand name is chosen, if the communication isn't up to par, even internal employees can't tell them apart — how can you expect consumers to remember them.

58:02

The worst position is low volume with many brands

A friend drew a four-quadrant chart: the strongest companies are multi-brand, high-volume, like BYD or Geely; single-brand, high-volume, like Tesla; in the low-brand, low-volume quadrant there are many perennial companies, but these companies aren't necessarily bad, because some specific models can survive as long as they can secure a model source — Porsche, Lamborghini and Aston Martin all have to attach themselves to a large platform, and need a platform model provider for them. The worst case is low volume with many brands: not enough volume means wasted resources, and many companies are struggling in this position. The need to split brands cannot be thought about purely from the technical platform — it's about how the market should recognize and segment them.

Glossary

BU / business unit
Sloan's original authorization mechanism, splitting the company into independently accounted business units and changing Ford-style autocratic management.
Model T
Ford's single black car model; Sloan ended its myth with multiple brands and annual model changes.
take-home pay
Actual income after taxes and deductions, including salary, bonus, stock, fringe benefits and pension.
Telematics
The in-car communication system GM built earliest, namely the OnStar system.
