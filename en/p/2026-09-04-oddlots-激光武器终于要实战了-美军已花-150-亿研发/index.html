Laser Weapons Are Finally Going to War: The US Military Has Already Spent $15 Billion on R&D

Lasers are called the sniper rifles of directed energy weapons, while microwaves are the electronic shotgun. The US military is shifting from R&D to procurement, but the real shortage is cheap interceptors—can you win every engagement but still lose the war because you can't afford it?

9:14

Lasers are sniper rifles, microwaves are shotguns

Wayne uses the analogy 'lasers are sniper rifles, microwaves are electronic shotguns' to distinguish the two types of directed energy weapons: lasers concentrate energy into a narrow beam, attacking one target at a time with thermal burn-through; microwaves have a wider beam and can affect the electronics of multiple targets simultaneously. The Government Accountability Office notes that high-power microwaves can reach peak powers of 100 megawatts, but comparing that directly to the kilowatts of continuous-wave lasers is meaningless because the two are fundamentally different: lasers try to increase power, microwaves try to amplify area effects. The choice depends on the target—drone swarms are suited to microwaves, while a single ballistic missile can only be engaged by a laser. This is because microwaves can disable drones and make them fall, but unlike lasers, they cannot destroy a single high-speed missile.

14:20

Chemical lasers failed due to size and logistics

After the invention of the laser in the 1960s, the military immediately saw its potential, but the real problems were power and size. In the 1990s, the US-Israeli 'Tactical High Energy Laser' and DARPA's MIRACLE chemical laser both successfully shot down rockets, but the entire systems were bulky and logistically complex, leading to program termination in 2006. Chemical lasers obtain light energy directly from chemical reactions and do not need electricity; for example, the oxygen-iodine laser was mounted on a Boeing 747 (YAL-1) in an attempt to intercept ballistic missiles near their launch area, but carrying chemicals on an aircraft was too dangerous. Today's key metric for technological progress is called SWaP: size, weight, and power. In the past, lasers were as large as a building because they required enormous amounts of electricity to generate the beam, whereas today's goal is to fit them into trucks and warships.

17:24

Handheld laser bottleneck is power compression

When asked why the handheld laser guns of science fiction don't exist yet, Wayne points out that the bottleneck is 'power generation and compression.' The fastest land platform currently is the high-energy laser mounted on a Stryker armored vehicle, with a power of 50 kilowatts, relying on the vehicle's own power generation; if you need a 5-kilowatt beam to shoot down a drone, you might need 15 to 25 kilowatts of input power. Without sufficient power supply, you can only watch the drone fly toward you with no way to respond. Electricity is the core constraint, not a gap in optical technology. This also explains why laser weapons have been in development for decades and are only now approaching the tipping point for large-scale deployment.

20:30

Expensive missiles must be retained

Many people assume lasers can replace multi-million-dollar interceptor missiles, but Wayne warns that if the adversary launches hypersonic weapons, you wouldn't want to gamble on a laser hitting a Mach 5 target precisely. Therefore, the real framework is 'integrated air and missile defense'—a combination of layered defenses. Lasers and microwaves have the advantage of low cost per shot, but you have to wait for the target to come within kill range; against weapons traveling at 4,000 miles per hour, you still need carefully designed interceptors like PAC-3 and SM-3. Israel is the best example: Arrow 2/3 intercepts long-range ballistic missiles, David's Sling handles medium-range threats, Iron Dome stops rockets, and now Iron Beam is being added for drones.

— Wayne Sanders
28:37

Battlefield commanders don't care about unit cost

When asked who thinks about cost when opening fire, Wayne divides decision-making into four levels: tactical, operational, strategic, and national. A frontline platoon or company commander facing a threat will use any means necessary to protect the unit, not caring about the missile's price; but at the theater command and Pentagon level, one must consider whether it's worth using a $4.1 million PAC-3 to shoot down a $30,000 drone. So the demand for 'cheap weapons' comes from the top, not the grassroots. Only national-level decision-makers incorporate ammunition consumption and supply chain issues into their choices. This distinction explains why the US military, after being attrited in Yemen and the Red Sea, is shifting toward low-cost interception solutions.

— Wayne Sanders
30:39

US military is shifting to low-cost munitions

Over the past few decades, the US military has stockpiled increasingly expensive precision interceptors for high-end peer warfare, only to find in Yemen and the Red Sea that adversaries are using drones to attrite them, forcing a change. Wayne says the Pentagon is signing multi-year framework agreements to significantly increase production, but the core realization is that you cannot always use expensive solutions against cheap threats. His key judgment: in future conflicts, 'you can win every engagement but still lose the war economically.' This is the market demand for low-cost interceptors like Coyote, Vampire, and APKWS. Battlefield commanders need more options at hand, not just the ability to fire off expensive missiles.

— Wayne Sanders
35:45

Startups are entering the mid-tier market

When asked whether laser weapons might be stifled by defense giants due to the innovator's dilemma, Wayne believes that the giants will indeed continue to dominate the high-end interceptor market, but the low-end and mid-tier solutions have already gone to five companies that received Department of Defense contracts: Coaspire, Leidos, Anduril, Zone 5, and Castilian. The DoD wants tens of thousands of units, not an increase from 620 to 2,000. So in the short term, the giants have the advantage, but within three to five years, newcomers like Anduril may capture the mid-tier market. This also means that when investing in defense stocks, you can't just look at the traditional giants; you need to see who benefits from the procurement shift.

— Wayne Sanders
39:52

AI killer robots are still far away

Wayne says that inside the Pentagon, AI is currently used only for intelligence analysis, image labeling for analysts, and change detection—not for strike decisions. His previous research at the war college was centered on 'AI and levels of trust.' Between target identification and tactical strike, it remains fully 'human-in-the-loop'—AI can help you detect targets and issue alerts, but the final decision is still made by human commanders and intelligence analysts. Some worry that AI might decide on its own to destroy targets, but Wayne says that future is still a long way off. The risk models are not ready, so the Pentagon currently does not use AI for autonomous killing; instead, it uses AI for military intelligence filtering to replace tedious work.

— Wayne Sanders

Glossary

Directed Energy Weapons (DEW)
Weapons that direct energy at a target, including lasers and high-power microwaves.
High-Powered Microwave (HPM)
A weapon that emits a wide beam of electromagnetic pulses to disrupt target electronics, like an electronic shotgun.
SWaP
Size, weight, and power—the key metrics for whether a weapon system can be deployed.
Exquisite munition
A high-end precision-guided interceptor that is costly but capable of intercepting hypersonic targets.
Shore power
Power drawn from a fixed electrical grid or a ship's own supply, rather than from a self-contained generator.
