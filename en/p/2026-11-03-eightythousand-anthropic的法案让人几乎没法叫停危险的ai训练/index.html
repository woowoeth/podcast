Anthropic's bill makes it nearly impossible to halt dangerous AI training

The bill compresses the power to halt training into a single Commerce Secretary signature and a single DC court decision within 45 days—when models actually cause harm, there's almost no time to hit the brakes.

Bill compresses authority to stop dangerous training through a dangerously narrow bottleneck

Under the federal AI bill that Anthropic privately championed, the only way to halt a model's training is for the Commerce Secretary to personally sign off and submit the case to a single federal court in Washington DC. The government must prove the risk is ‘imminent’, must prove lighter interventions won't work, and judges must review from scratch without deference to regulators. Any halt order expires automatically after 45 days; reauthorizing requires proving the case all over again. More critically, even whether ‘a 10% human extinction probability within the next year’counts as ‘imminent’ is undefined—and that gap determines whether this gate can actually block truly catastrophic risks.

4:02

CEO estimates 25% disaster probability, yet company strips halt authority

The contradiction: this bill comes from a company whose CEO publicly puts the probability of AI ‘causing major chaos’at 25% and has written that ‘recursive self-improvement must proceed with extreme care, if at all’. The host sees why—Anthropic has been in frequent conflict with the Trump administration this year and fears federal discretionary power will be weaponized for political concessions rather than genuinely controlling dangerous research. But he argues the solution shouldn't be stripping away state power too; instead preserve states like California's independent halt authority, or at least let state attorneys general petition the DC court as well. You shouldn't stake everything on one agency's review by a single court.

— Rob Wiblin
5:15

Bill would retroactively eliminate state-level AI safety laws nationwide

The bill not only narrows federal authority but also bars states from requiring AI companies to disclose safety plans, undergo independent audits, or report incidents—effectively retroactively nullifying flagship AI laws already passed in California, Illinois, and New York. There's a subtler provision: states had the power to halt models presenting ‘major catastrophic risk’using ‘statute, regulation, directive, court decision or other action’; under the bill's design, that power eventually disappears too. The result: even if California's government unanimously determined an unreleased model could kill everyone in the state, it would have no power to legislate or seek a court injunction—only hope the Commerce Secretary will run that process.

— Rob Wiblin
8:58

OpenAI fired three safety researchers on the same afternoon over disputed evidence

Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni, and Jasmine Wang were each fired at a 2 p.m. meeting on the same afternoon, with contradictory and unpersuasive justifications. OpenAI later claimed the real reason was ‘broken trust’but refused to explain. According to reports, the trigger was a leak investigation: news had surfaced that OpenAI's new model used ‘looped transformer’technology, which the company wanted hidden. Investigators suspected the leak came via Signal messages between one researcher and outside contact Ryan Greenblatt, but both used disappearing messages—neither could prove innocence. Looped transformers let models perform hidden calculations before outputting chain-of-thought reasoning; the field has long warned this points toward unmonitorable models. All three researchers had previously publicly supported keeping chain-of-thought observable.

— Rob Wiblin
10:12

The evidence for firing the three was far weaker than stated

Disappearing messages are standard in tech and AI circles—you can't convict based on that alone, or everyone from CEO down would be suspect. The Information, which broke this story, has sourced OpenAI's internal secrets for years from investors, ex-employees, even casual conversation—not necessarily these three. Hundreds, possibly two hundred OpenAI employees could oppose looped transformers—this year alone, nearly 400 signed a petition demanding AI research slow for safety's sake. The host personally judges a Greenblatt leak highly unlikely; it doesn't fit his operating style at all. Conclusion: firing was probably error. The cost: the company will struggle to get candid employee input going forward, and the actual leaker may never be found.

— Rob Wiblin
12:52

Google DeepMind's contracts silence employees for life, not just at departure

According to BBC reporting, Google DeepMind's UK employment contracts forbid employees from publicly criticizing anything Google does—for life. The clause surfaced when a former employee tried to criticize Google's sale of AI to the US Defense Department (usable as long as DoD deems it legal), a deal that already prompted resignations over principle. This echoes the ‘non-disparagement agreement’scandal uncovered two years ago at OpenAI: former employees criticizing the company lost stock worth potentially tens of millions, and the clause itself demanded secrecy. After exposure, both OpenAI and Anthropic admitted to similar clauses and promised to drop them. Google DeepMind's version has no secrecy requirement and doesn't forfeit stock, but the lawsuit threat alone creates massive chilling effect on ordinary employees.

— Rob Wiblin
17:31

Speaking up inside requires public channels; speaking out requires going public

The host offers a graduated approach: to stay, calmly and rationally explain specific reasons for opposing a decision in public Slack channels colleagues will see, focusing on the few most critical points, not opposing everything reflexively, and staying open to being persuaded. If reasonable dissent gets no satisfactory response, escalate to publicly stating this makes you question the company's judgment and strategy—and that you're considering leaving. Still no results: submit a thorough resignation statement, then make your case public after departure. His reasoning: companies automate employee work faster by the year, so employees' bargaining power erodes over time. ‘If not now, when?’

— Rob Wiblin
21:39

Outside pressure reshapes corporate governance when insiders organize the pressure

Against OpenAI's plan to hollow out its nonprofit parent and charitable mission, the ‘Not For Private Gain’campaign had few participants but, paired with state attorneys general attention and broader coalition pressure, forced OpenAI to substantially revise: the OpenAI Foundation gained approximately 75 billion dollars in additional equity, and its Safety & Alignment Committee locked down decision rights over a range of safety questions. Similar: a 2024 campaign by former employees for ‘right to raise concerns’that became whistleblower protections in California's SB 53; continued reporting showing OpenAI publicly backing regulation while lobbying against it also pushed OpenAI to support SB 53-type rules and led President Greg Brockman to stop giving to the lobbying PAC ‘Leading the Future’.

— Rob Wiblin
