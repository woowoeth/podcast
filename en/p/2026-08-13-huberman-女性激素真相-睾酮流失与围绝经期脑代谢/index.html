Women's testosterone falls 1% a year; perimenopausal brain metabolism drops 20%

Women should establish a hormone baseline in their 20s, when testosterone is already falling about 1% a year; oral contraceptives lower ovarian cancer risk while possibly shrinking the clitoris by up to 20%; perimenopausal brain glucose metabolism drops an average of 20%, and hot flashes are a disease signal, not a minor nuisance.

1:31

Test your hormone baseline in your 20s, not once symptoms appear

Looked at by decade, the teenage system is not yet mature, and the swings in estrogen and progesterone make a poor baseline. In your 20s you should test estrogen, progesterone and testosterone, and also look at DHEA and the androgen pathway, because if estrogen dominates the ‘pas de deux’ it raises future risk of uterine fibroids and endometriosis. Timing matters: if you can only pick one day, use day 21-22 of the cycle, about a week before your period; if your cycle starts getting shorter (a sign progesterone is falling), move it earlier to day 19-20. Blood tests are cheapest, but dried urine can give metabolomics and is more comprehensive. Also check magnesium — magnesium is involved in clearing estrogen.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
13:30

Testosterone starts falling as early as 28, and confidence goes first

Testosterone is the most biologically abundant hormone in a woman's body, affecting libido, muscle and response to strength training, and also confidence, agency and even risk-taking. It can start declining in your 20s, as early as 28, usually by about 1% a year. Gottfried's target range is the upper half of the normal range. Factors like stress change the rate of decline, so you need to track it rather than go by feel.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
14:50

PCOS is not a reproductive-years problem, it is lifelong cardiovascular disease

PCOS is often treated as a reproductive-years problem, but it is more a lifelong cardiovascular and metabolic risk. The diagnostic criteria are two out of three: cystic-appearing ovaries, clinical hyperandrogenism (excess hair, acne and so on), and a menstrual cycle interval of 35 days or shorter (which she explains as occasionally skipping cycles). There are at least four sets of diagnostic criteria in the literature, which creates confusion. Gottfried points out that after age 50 — average menopause age is 51-52 — elevated testosterone and androgens are the biggest cardiovascular-metabolic driver in women with PCOS. So PCOS management cannot stop just because the reproductive years are over.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
17:10

Normal blood sugar does not mean normal metabolism — test insulin first

In some PCOS phenotypes, hyperinsulinemia drives the ovarian theca cells to overproduce testosterone. Gottfried says she is the ‘biggest fan’ of CGM, because no tool changes behavior like continuous glucose monitoring. But she cautions that blood sugar is a later effect, and insulin should be tested first — the Whitehall study shows postprandial insulin and vascular insulin can change years before blood sugar does. She also sees digital tools like CGM as data democratization: handing body-chemistry data back from doctors to patients, so people become their own clinicians.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
21:08

Running marathons may be driving your cortisol up

Gottfried calls long-term running and marathon-style cardio ‘chronic cardio’; when this kind of training lacks strength training, it often comes with high cortisol. Her own case: at 35 she was extremely exhausted, had abdominal fat and no libido for her husband, and her doctor only gave her birth control pills and antidepressants. She went and got hormone testing and found her cortisol was about 3 times normal, fasting insulin in the 20s, blood sugar 105, mildly abnormal thyroid and low progesterone. At the time she was running four miles three or four times a week, which pushed her cortisol up further. She switched to adaptive training like Pilates and more yoga, and only then brought her cortisol down.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
27:22

The pill really does cut ovarian cancer — the problem is synthetic progestin

Oral contraceptives do lower ovarian cancer risk: about five years of use cuts risk by 50%, because they reduce ovulation; continuous ovulation itself is unfavorable. But Gottfried has strong reservations about synthetic progestin, which is the same class of progestin that showed problems in the Women's Health Initiative (WHI). Side effects include: high-sensitivity CRP rising about 2-3 times, which is pro-inflammatory; a stiffened HPA axis; effects on thyroid function; and oral estrogen also raises SHBG, which soaks up free testosterone like a sponge. The most extreme one: the clitoris may shrink by up to 20%. So for people taking it for acne or painful periods, she recommends addressing the root of the inflammation first, and always getting informed consent.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
33:07

Hot flashes are not a nuisance, they are a signal of falling brain metabolism

Lisa Mosconi's work at Cornell shows that women's brains undergo huge metabolic changes starting around age 40: glucose uptake measured by FDG PET drops by an average of about 20% from premenopause (age 35) through perimenopause to postmenopause. Women with more symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats and difficulty sleeping show more pronounced brain metabolic decline, which Gottfried calls something like a ‘low-grade pseudo-dementia’. Falling estrogen (starting around age 40-43) is thought to be the driver, and this also redefines Alzheimer's disease as a ‘midlife disease’ rather than a ‘disease of old age’. Her conclusion: hot flashes and night sweats are not minor nuisances to be ignored but biomarkers of cardiovascular-metabolic disease, bone loss and brain changes; many perimenopausal symptoms are not ovary-driven but brain-driven.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried
36:54

Get a coronary calcium score before 45 — no one will order it for you

Gottfried says if women could take away only one thing, it would be to get a coronary artery calcium score (CAC) before age 45, and earlier if there is premature heart disease. The test is a chest CT and you can order it yourself. It gives you a ‘fork in the road’: if the score is 0 (hers is 0), at least you have a baseline; if it starts rising around age 45, or if you have high-risk factors like PCOS, you immediately know how much attention to put into cardiovascular-metabolic health. Most traditional doctors will not proactively order this test; you have to ask for it yourself.

— Dr. Sara Gottfried

Glossary

SHBG / sex hormone-binding globulin
A protein that binds and lowers free estrogen and testosterone; oral contraceptives raise it.
CGM / continuous glucose monitor
A device that monitors glucose in subcutaneous tissue in real time, giving immediate feedback on how diet and exercise affect blood sugar.
PCOS / polycystic ovary syndrome
A syndrome characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction and polycystic-appearing ovaries, associated with metabolic disease.
FDG PET / fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography
An imaging technique that assesses brain metabolism by tracking glucose uptake, used to observe metabolic decline.
HPA axis / hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis
The neuroendocrine pathway that regulates the stress response and cortisol secretion; too much stress disrupts its function.
CAC / coronary artery calcium score
Quantifies the degree of coronary artery calcification via chest CT, used to assess cardiovascular disease risk.
