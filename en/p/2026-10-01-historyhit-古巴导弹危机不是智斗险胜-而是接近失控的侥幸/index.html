The Cuban Missile Crisis Was Not a Triumph of Wit, But a Brush With Catastrophe

The notion that Kennedy coolly outwitted Khrushchev is merely the post-hoc mythologized "Thirteen Days" narrative; the truth is that in an oxygen-starved submarine, whether a nuclear torpedo fired hinged on disagreement among three officers and a single mechanical failure.

10:28

Khrushchev's miscalculation stemmed from unchecked personal power

As Soviet General Secretary, Khrushchev wielded virtually unchecked personal power; the Politburo posed no meaningful constraint on his decisions—no independent advisor dared warn him before the crisis began that this was madness. This emboldened his profound miscalculation about how constitutional democracy worked: he believed that if he simply smuggled missiles into Cuba and created a fait accompli, Kennedy, as an elected leader, would have no choice but to accept it. This miscalculation triggered the entire crisis.

13:42

Castro sought protection, not nuclear weapons

After seizing power, Castro faced American hostility, compounded by the Bay of Pigs invasion and the CIA's Operation Mongoose assassination plot. He appealed to Moscow for help—what he wanted was an ironclad military alliance as a protective umbrella. But Khrushchev's response exceeded Castro's expectations by orders of magnitude: not advisors and conventional weapons, but nuclear missiles. Cuba sought security; the Soviet Union instead gave it a role as nuclear war's trigger. This mismatch later left Castro entirely sidelined when both superpowers negotiated the crisis's end.

— Dan Snow
20:17

McNamara was right: this was never a military problem

Kennedy's Defense Secretary McNamara consistently argued that the few dozen missiles in Cuba had not actually altered the US-Soviet strategic balance—the Soviet Union already possessed the capacity to devastate America with intercontinental ballistic missiles and bombers. White House recordings confirm that Kennedy privately agreed with this assessment; he even said the most rational course would be to tell Americans it was not serious and not to worry. But this was politically impossible: he had publicly promised voters that he would never permit Soviet nuclear deployment in Cuba. So from the start, this crisis was not a military problem but a domestic political crisis over the president's political survival.

— Dan Snow
26:24

Low-altitude surveillance flights nearly sparked aerial combat

After Kennedy's public announcement, American reconnaissance aircraft began provocative ultra-low-altitude photography runs over Cuba, inflaming both Cuban and Soviet forces on the ground. On at least one occasion, a Soviet MiG scrambled to intercept an American plane but failed to catch it and turned back. Meanwhile, China seized the opportunity to attack India's border; France questioned the authenticity of CIA photographs and was forced to publicly retract its skepticism; Britain publicly backed America but privately thought the American response excessive. The fate of humanity rested in the hands of pilots and air-defense operators scattered across the region, each one a potential trigger for accidental war.

— Dan Snow
34:45

A Soviet local commander fired without authorization from Moscow

U-2 pilot Rudolph Anderson, an American ace, was killed over Cuba when struck by two Soviet air-defense missiles—the only military fatality of the entire crisis. Critically, the chain of command for that decision was fractured: local Soviet commanders could not reach their superior in Havana—who was either asleep, sick, or drunk—so they made the decision to fire themselves. Washington had no way of knowing this was the action of rogue commanders; all it saw was a Soviet-controlled air-defense missile taking down an American aircraft, and it mistook this as an explicit signal that Moscow had chosen war. The crisis nearly escalated to military response.

— Dan Snow
39:00

A nuclear torpedo's launch hinged on disagreement among three officers

In the Sargasso Sea, the American Navy used practice depth charges to force Soviet submarine B-59 to the surface. The sub's interior had been oxygen-starved for over eighteen hours, with temperatures approaching sixty degrees; Captain Savitsky was driven to order assembly of a nuclear torpedo in preparation to fire. Soviet protocol required three officers to unanimously consent to nuclear weapon launch. Ultimately, another senior officer, Vasili Arkhipov, canceled the order. Some historians regard this as mankind's closest approach to nuclear war; others dispute this assessment—but a signal officer who got stuck on the hatchway ladder delayed the captain's descent from the conning tower long enough for Arkhipov to regain his judgment.

— Dan Snow
48:27

The secret Turkey missile deal was the crisis's actual resolution

Late on October 27, Kennedy sent his brother Robert for a secret meeting with Soviet Ambassador Dobrynin, demanding that the Soviet Union agree to withdraw its missiles from Cuba within twenty-four hours or face a U.S. invasion. In exchange, Robert privately promised that America would secretly withdraw the Jupiter missiles deployed in Turkey—but this part of the agreement had to remain absolutely secret, because disclosure would be politically catastrophic for Kennedy. Robert even told the ambassador that the president was under enormous pressure from the military. This secret deal was later concealed from Congress by Kennedy, as well as from former President Eisenhower.

— Dan Snow
53:36

The 'Thirteen Days' myth erased the real compromise at stake

Robert Kennedy's posthumously published "Thirteen Days" portrayed the Kennedy team as coolly managing the crisis and forcing Soviet retreat—a narrative that dominated public understanding for decades. But declassified Soviet files, White House recordings, and KGB archives reveal this to be more like a self-serving narrative designed to pave the way for Robert's future presidential ambitions—the truth is that Kennedy did indeed secretly agree to withdraw Jupiter missiles from Turkey in exchange for Soviet concessions. Khrushchev paid the price for this retreat: within two years he was removed in a coup and replaced by the harder-line Leonid Brezhnev. America's Air Force Chief Curtis LeMay, the leading hawk, always regarded this as America's greatest defeat.

— Dan Snow
