11:37

A brutal Singles' Day taught him what business actually meant

When the flagship Ecovacs brand moved 10,000 units of its 999-yuan robot in a single day as a bestseller across the internet, the new TEK brand he was leading sold only a few hundred thousand total. Nobody criticized him, but the gap was crushing—he had no idea how to recruit a team or diagnose whether the problem was the product or operations. He later described it as "complete fumbling in the dark." This disappointment, not harsh words, is what first made him serious about learning how to run a business—earlier than when he was formally given management responsibility.

27:18

The play that got 30% of Germany's market in six months

His breakthrough in international markets came in Germany, where he didn't follow his father's playbook of hiring a local foreign GM to negotiate distribution channels. Instead, he transplanted the domestic China playbook—cost-effective products, e-commerce channels, internet marketing—and replaced the existing local management team. The turning point came when a customer opened their laptop right in front of him to price-check on Amazon. He thought: "If you're already searching Amazon anyway, why don't I just sell directly on Amazon?" That model became the template for Ecovacs' international expansion, and he captured 30% market share in Germany in six months.

— David
31:22

Becoming CEO at 29 wasn't the peak of life—it was the peak of ignorance

When he became CEO at 29, he felt he'd reached the peak of his life—but he soon realized it was "an epic miscalculation." Looking back, the hard part wasn't strategy-setting; the three big moves he launched—cutting OEM production, going upmarket, expanding internationally—all proved directionally correct. The real barrier was that he didn't understand how decision-making actually worked inside this traditional Chinese private enterprise. He describes himself as the company's "brain," but his nervous system was never properly wired to the organization's body. It took him two or three years before he saw that disconnect.

— David
43:20

Appetite bigger than capability will explode you from inside

His biggest error as a new CEO came down to one insight in hindsight: he had desires that vastly outpaced his capabilities. When overseas business faltered during the pandemic, the root cause was that his leadership relied on personal effort to drive informal decisions—never systematized into organization-wide capability. The same pattern played out when he tried to enter B2B commercial robotics: just another product of unchecked ambition. He later grasped that B2B and B2C are "completely different businesses," and companies that excel at both are rare. His answer wasn't to contract the vision—it was to learn the tempo of restraint, build the underlying capability first, and stop fantasizing about what should happen next.

— David
58:18

Picking categories is like farming—don't bite random meat off the market

He uses a farming metaphor for how Ecovacs picks product categories: the company's DNA is "agricultural civilization"—you buy a seed, plant it, let the crop grow slowly—not "nomadic civilization," where you spot a big industry and bite off a piece. When the company first sold floor-cleaning robots, consumers in malls still mistook them for electronic scales; Ecovacs just had the patience to be early. Window-cleaning robots followed the same path: the first one existed when he joined the company, and only this year did it hit 2 million units of shipments. The hard standards for category selection: Do you own the underlying technology (sensors, algorithms, control, logistics)? Do you have global distribution reach? Can this category sustain a 200-billion-yuan-scale corporation's growth?

— David
1:10:48

Consumer electronics have no moat; a brand is just last time's memory

Asked what the core moat is in consumer electronics, he said: there isn't one. A brand is just the consumer's memory of their last purchase from you—which helps them decide faster next time. But if the next delivery goes wrong, that memory dissolves. Unlike Coca-Cola, whose formula stays the same forever, consumer electronics products never stop changing. The only real moat is monopoly, and in a world that changes fast with technology constantly erupting, monopoly is theoretically almost impossible. All a company can do is find relative scale advantages and compete on iteration speed.

— David
1:14:45

In the physical AI boom, he hasn't found the actual market to eat

On the humanoid robot wave, he's relatively cautious: physical AI in B2B factory settings might go commercial in three to five years, but consumer home use he estimates at five to ten years out. He likens the current state to "swinging a giant hammer at a tiny nail"—when drones first appeared, Parrot turned them into toys, but DJI found the real use case with professional consumers as a productivity tool. Today almost every humanoid robot company pitches household scenarios, then pivots to factories. Electric cars replaced combustion engines with real, measurable fleet data, but the "meat" that robots are supposedly replacing right now exists only in imagination. He himself doesn't see its actual size.

— David
1:44:16

Second-generation succession is really about bridging two eras' gap

On the core challenge of second-generation succession, his framework is this: the previous generation succeeded on that era's resources (customer relationships, organization, talent). The next generation must judge which of those resources survive the transition to the next era and which need rebuilding—and whether they themselves can actually command the new era's resources. Otherwise, even if succession is "complete," the company may never truly enter its next stage. He also admits that he and his father often see the company's path differently; they may want the same outcome but disagree on how to get there, and it takes constant daily conversation to navigate that. Compared to same-generation entrepreneurs like Wang Huiwen, JK, and Liu Jingkang, he concedes they have a more intense entrepreneurial drive.

— David
