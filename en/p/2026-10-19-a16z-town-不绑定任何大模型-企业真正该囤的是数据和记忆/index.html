Town stays neutral on models; enterprises should hoard data and memory

With no large model guaranteed to dominate in three to five years, Town stays neutral, keeping data and memory ownership with enterprises so they can always switch providers.

Tax software has a fatal flaw: you can only sell it once a year

Automating tax returns looked promising at first—lots of text and structured financial data, exactly what LLMs excel at. The team spent a year turning ‘financial data plus documents into tax forms’ into a working product. But the business model fell apart: tax returns sell once yearly, customers don't evangelize the tax experience, and there's no daily active user loop to create word-of-mouth virality. Meanwhile, established players can apply the same AI techniques to their existing customer base faster than any startup can steal market share. Even projecting $2.5–3 million annual revenue by year two wouldn't sustain venture-scale ambitions, so the team moved on.

11:28

Opus 4.5's release gave the product instant product-market fit

After abandoning taxes, the team explored four directions before focusing on a simple insight: why hasn't anyone built truly usable agents for email and calendar? Former tax customers kept complaining they'd forget context before meetings or field the same questions repeatedly from clients. Everyday knowledge work had never been seriously tackled as an AI product. Then Opus 4.5 launched, showing coding agents could execute with greater autonomy. The insight plus the model's leap in capability created near-instant PMF. From a closed pilot in December to April, the product grew 1–1.5% daily, customer requests flooded in, and by then the team felt ready to go public.

— Jean-Denis Grez
23:13

Cheap tokens make previously uneconomical work suddenly worth automating

Jean-Denis frames Town as a ‘token allocation engine’. As tokens become cheaper, long-tail work that was economically unjustifiable for humans and too expensive for AI alike suddenly becomes worth automating. Consider a restaurant owner: suppliers send price quotes quarterly, often with 20–30% hikes, but the owner is too busy to compare them manually—it's too sporadic to justify hiring someone to monitor. With Town, a manager sets up a monitoring agent that runs monthly and generates alerts in five minutes; it might find nothing for eleven months at $1 per month, but the one month it catches a price spike can save hundreds or thousands. The real product work isn't the technology—it's convincing users that this task is now economically viable.

— Jean-Denis Grez
34:36

Staying neutral on models beats betting wrong on one vendor

When asked how Town differs from OpenAI or Anthropic's built-in connectors, Jean-Denis centers his answer on neutrality. No one can predict which model vendor will dominate in three to five years. If a company locks its product into one model's ecosystem, a shift in capabilities or pricing—or one vendor's stumble—leaves the customer defenseless. Some enterprises have already hedged by adopting multiple vendors: betting on OpenAI for language work but discovering Anthropic's strength in code, then forced to maintain two vendor relationships. Town positions itself as a ‘neutral app layer’ that picks whichever models win at each task—voice, video, slides—and commits never to train on customer data. Once a model vendor ingests your business know-how into their weights and resells it to your competitors, your competitive advantage has moved to your rival's balance sheet.

— Jean-Denis Grez
40:00

AI can route corporate information at speed with proper permissions

Town is building an internal CRM mode: when you ask ‘does anyone know this prospect's finance director?’ your agent queries your colleagues' agents directly, rather than waiting for Slack messages and hoping people volunteer information they should share. Jean-Denis calls humans ‘slow routers’. AI intermediaries can make permission and privacy judgments and return answers in milliseconds. He grants this needs meticulous privacy design, but argues it's inevitable: enterprises must adapt to fewer humans in the loop, and the economic gains will dwarf today's visible risks.

— Jean-Denis Grez
44:09

Coding is make-or-break for large models; enterprise automation is bigger, just slower

Contrast Anthropic and OpenAI's stakes in the coding market—where they can't afford to lose—against their relative caution in the SMB automation space. Jean-Denis argues the SMB market is larger overall, just on a different timeline. Coding is already a nine-figure revenue opportunity for large models; they have everything to lose. But ‘AI helping enterprises do more things better’ won't move their quarterly earnings yet. He predicts rapid acceleration within twelve months. Large model companies are likelier to concentrate bets on outsized verticals—medicine, law, coding—where a single point of failure is catastrophic, leaving fragmented long-tail SMB scenarios to companies like Town willing to grind on niche needs.

— Jean-Denis Grez
52:30

Amazon's advertising business could become a liability in the agent era

As the conversation turns to how agents consume supply and how e-commerce platforms should respond, Jean-Denis flags Amazon's ad business as a potential liability. Amazon's enormous ad revenue depends on customers browsing pages to see ads. Once purchasing decisions move to neutral agents, that flywheel breaks. Walmart faces no such handicap—it has no advertising empire to defend. Jean-Denis cites Muse, a personal agent, as an example: it doesn't need to embed ads; it feeds preference data to Instagram and similar entertainment platforms for more precise ad targeting and closes the sale there, completely decoupling monetization from the agent experience. Pure aggregation plays devalue in the agent era. Amazon's real countermove isn't shutting down Muse; it's building a better one.

— Jean-Denis Grez
58:46

Price comparison sites predict the divide in agent economics

Compare this to Camel Camel Camel, a price-tracking site with eighteen years of history and 20 million US users. The world has always split two ways: people with more time than money will spend two hours hunting coupons and comparing prices; people with more money than time won't. But once agents handle all this errand-running free of charge, the second group will adopt them immediately. Jean-Denis calls these models—credit card rewards, membership perks—instances of ‘breakage models’ that rely on most people never bothering to redeem. That assumption is about to fail. His conclusion circles back to the thread linking all eight points: aggregation plays gain value because human attention is scarce. When agents automate attention itself, the negotiating power of pure supply plays erodes systematically.

— Jean-Denis Grez

Glossary

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)
An engineer deployed to a customer site to develop and maintain AI solutions customized to their operations.
Jevons Paradox
An economic phenomenon where efficiency gains increase total consumption rather than reducing it.
PLG (Product-Led Growth)
A strategy where product experience itself, rather than sales teams, drives user acquisition and retention.
breakage model
A business model that depends on most customers not redeeming available benefits like discounts, rewards, or memberships.
