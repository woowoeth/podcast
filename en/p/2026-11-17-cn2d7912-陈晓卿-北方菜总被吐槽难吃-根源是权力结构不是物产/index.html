One sentence from his father turned a troublemaker into a straight-A student

Chen Xiaoqing had terrible grades in middle school. Rather than punish him, his father seriously told him that given their means, the best they could do was find him a job at the county grain station—taking tickets. When Chen actually visited the position—seeing a slovenly, unkempt old man—he decided on the spot to study hard. He admits his real motivation wasn't ambition; it was being terrified by that specific, concrete image.

Home exists in two forms: one you can return to, one you cannot

Chen makes a distinction: home is both a spatial concept and a temporal one. The spatial home—you can drive back there anytime. But temporal home—that era, those tastes, those people—once it's gone, you can't go back. He cites a former soldier he filmed returning to his birthplace who wept uncontrollably eating childhood stir-fried rice crust—that moment touched his "temporal home."

Forced into photography; he hated it at first

Chen had never touched a camera before university. At his film school interview, he'd prepared poetry planning to audition for broadcasting, but his acceptance notice said photography. His father yanked him back before he could protest further. Chen admits he had "zero talent" for photography before sophomore year—he only kept learning because circumstance and major assignment pushed him. Only later, in the darkroom developing prints, did he accidentally discover the joy of immersion.

2:01:32

The BBC formula for nature documentaries no longer works commercially

Chen relays BBC producer Michael Gunton's observation: early nature documentaries simply handed viewers knowledge; that wasn't enough. So they combined "knowledge + narrative drama + visual spectacle + cutting-edge technology," and audiences bought in. But Chen says that formula collapsed commercially after the pandemic—documentaries were always weak at recouping costs and suited non-commercial entities like state television. Doing this on commercial platforms has become increasingly impossible.

— Chen Xiaoqing
2:07:55

Even OpenAI's CEO couldn't withstand the algorithm for two nights

Both Chen and Luo Yonghao mention relying on "install and immediately delete" to fight short-video app addiction. Luo notes that OpenAI's CEO thought he had strong discipline, so out of curiosity he tried installing TikTok—and after scrolling for two sleepless nights he was so frightened he uninstalled it. Both admit they've tried short-drama apps too, blushing at content so "stupidly shallow you're ashamed to admit watching it," yet stayed up scrolling through anyway. This proves algorithm designers understand human nature better than any self-control.

2:28:01

North-south food divergence stems from power structures, not resource scarcity

Chen explains the regional food divergence: throughout Chinese history, imperial power repeatedly passed from north-based groups to the south, and culturally cultivated people migrated south, bringing refined techniques. What remained in the north was more power-centered, ceremonial food systems. After the republic, without an emperor, the political center stayed north but south moved toward commercial exchange, north toward power-based exchange. This directly explains why "official cuisine" is often criticized as unpalatable—it inherently pursues rarity and spectacle, not the broadest common denominator.

— Chen Xiaoqing
2:47:53

Being a "foodie" was once a serious insult

Chen says that thirty years ago calling someone "food-crazy" was an insult, something that made people feel ashamed. When he first shot food content and met Cai Lan (蔡澜), Cai expressed displeasure at the word "foodie" appearing in articles—Cai came from privilege and never experienced having an appetite treated as a defect. Chen lived through the entire arc: from being mocked for his appetite, to self-deprecating "foodie," to social acceptance.

— Chen Xiaoqing
3:22:26

Whether cold noodles taste good is learned behavior

Chen admits his beloved Kangwon-province naengmyeon isn't validated by orthodox Korean-ethnic taste hierarchies, but he frames this not as a corrupted palate—rather, as "mutual domestication between food and person." As a kid, it was the only taste he could afford; eventually he couldn't live without it. He's eaten the same dish in the Korean presidential residence and locally in Hunchun, yet stubbornly insists his hometown version is most authentic. This shows childhood flavor loyalty outlasts any objective standard.

— Chen Xiaoqing
