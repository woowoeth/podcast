Ashurbanipal's Library: He Was Both a Tyrant and the Earliest Book Collector

Ashurbanipal threw defeated kings into dog kennels and forced people to make bricks from their ancestors' bones — and he also built humanity's earliest systematic library, keeping the Epic of Gilgamesh alive to this day.

0:01

The same man, two completely opposite first impressions

At the British Museum, Tracy read the exhibit panels on Ashurbanipal's library and thought, "this guy's library is astonishing"; in the same gallery Patrick read the other set of panels about him as king of Assyria, and came back with "this guy was really cruel." The same body of material can support two entirely opposite impressions at once, and the rest of the episode pulls that contradiction apart: he really did yoke the defeated king of Elam to his chariot and shut a rival up in a dog kennel, and he really did order texts from across the empire copied and sent to the capital.

— Tracy V. Wilson
4:04

You cannot lay out a timeline for Mesopotamia at all

Tracy wanted to open the episode with a basic timeline and found she couldn't do it. Mesopotamia spans more than three thousand years from the first cities, with civilizations, kingdoms and empires overlapping and influencing one another, so a linear timeline is itself a distortion. Assyrian history is divided into Early Assyrian (from about 2600 BCE), Old Assyrian (from about 2025 BCE), Middle Assyrian (from about 1363 BCE), and Neo-Assyrian (911 to 609 BCE), followed by a post-empire period. Assyrian people still exist today: roughly three to five million people in Iran, Iraq, Syria and Turkey identify as Assyrian.

— Tracy V. Wilson
9:10

His father split the empire in two to prevent civil war

Esarhaddon's father Sennacherib had completely destroyed Babylon and even diverted canals to flood the ruins, and was later assassinated by his own sons, which was read as divine punishment for destroying Babylon. Esarhaddon rebuilt Babylon and positioned himself as its "governor" rather than its king. In 672 BCE he divided his two sons' duties: Shamashumukin took Babylon, and Ashurbanipal was made crown prince of Assyria. The arrangement was meant to prevent a repeat of the civil war that had attended his own accession. The episode says explicitly that this will come back later — because the brothers eventually turned on each other.

— Tracy V. Wilson
9:10

He got an education a king was not supposed to get

Ashurbanipal's education had already begun when he was made crown prince. He learned horsemanship, chariot driving, archery, military tactics and court etiquette — the standard royal package. But he also studied literature and history, knew multiple Sumerian and Akkadian dialects, and could read and write cuneiform. The episode stresses that kings of this era normally did not receive such a scholarly education and often were not literate at all; literacy was the business of scholars and scribes. The likely reason he got it is that early in life he was not being raised entirely as the heir to the throne.

— Tracy V. Wilson
13:14

In the reliefs, he wears writing tools on his belt

Ashurbanipal was a patron of the arts, building temples and palaces and commissioning large numbers of reliefs. Many reliefs show him fighting, lion hunting, and going about daily life amid wealth and luxury; some depict the severed heads of enemies. The episode singles out one detail: in many depictions he wears a riding stylus on his belt, including in scenes where he is killing lions with a sword — Holly jokes that he probably wanted to write in his diary after killing a lion. The reliefs from his reign are regarded as the peak of Assyrian art; the lion hunt reliefs are now in the British Museum.

— Tracy V. Wilson
15:19

Cruelty was not his invention — he took it to an extreme

The episode draws a clear distinction: practices like displaying enemies' severed heads were the norm among rulers of ancient Mesopotamia; but in his own commissioned depictions and in records left by others, Ashurbanipal is seen as having pushed this to an extreme. Specific examples include: salting the land after putting down a revolt, forcing people to grind up their ancestors' bones to make bricks for a palace, making the defeated king of Elam pull his chariot in the triumph, and forcing one rival to live in a dog kennel. About that rival, some accounts say he was forced to wear a dog collar, others that a chain was run through his jaw.

— Tracy V. Wilson
21:39

This was the earliest "systematic collection" library

The library was built in the capital, Nineveh, and tablets have been found in the Southwest Palace, the North Palace, and around the temples of Ishtar and Nabu. The episode stresses two things: first, it is not clear whether these were the library's original storage locations or the result of tablets scattering after the city's destruction; second, this is one of the earliest known libraries to be systematically acquired and organized, a deliberately collected and curated body of knowledge, and the largest collection of Assyrian texts in the world. Excavations yielded more than twenty thousand tablets and fragments, many bearing standardized colophons, indicating a systematic method of labeling and cataloguing. The total number of complete tablets is actually unknown.

— Tracy V. Wilson
22:39

He ordered the whole empire to hand over books, and knew who to ask

Ashurbanipal ordered temples and palaces across the empire to send texts to Nineveh, usually by having a copy made, with him receiving the copy or the original. Surviving documents show he actually wanted the originals, and that subordinates may have "creatively misinterpreted" the instructions in order to keep originals in their own temples or collections. Surviving correspondence also shows he knew exactly where to look for texts he wanted, and who might hold works he did not know about but that were worth acquiring. Holly describes him as having a network of scholarly acquisition librarians. Some texts in the library were written by him, and many tablets carry colophons in his hand.

— Tracy V. Wilson
25:47

The city burned, and the fire hardened the tablets into preservation

Nineveh was sacked in 612 BCE, an event that both preserved and destroyed the library. As the city burned, the tablets hardened in the heat and were then protected by collapsing debris; but wooden or ivory writing boards covered in wax were essentially destroyed. The tablets broke and scattered during the city's destruction, and on excavation it was often unclear where in the capital they had originally been stored. Nineteenth-century excavation made the scattering worse: Austin Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam began excavating Nineveh in 1849, Rassam found another large batch of tablets in 1852, and huge amounts of material were sent to the British Museum; there was also a French archaeological project, so some material is now in the Louvre.

— Tracy V. Wilson
27:48

The fragments cannot be reassembled because no one recorded findspots

Many tablets are marked "Palace of Ashurbanipal, King of the World, King of Assyria," so tablets found at different locations can be confirmed as belonging to the same collection. But during excavation, archaeologists and workers did not fully record the specific findspot of each fragment, making it very hard to tell which fragment should be joined to which. The episode says the priority at the time was getting archaeological treasures back to Britain as fast as possible, not thoroughly recording the find context of every object. The result is that reconstruction is extremely laborious: only a small portion of the tablets have been reassembled, and many remain incomplete. Since the mid-nineteenth century, an average of 35 texts a year have been reconstructed and transcribed, and a great deal of work remains.

— Tracy V. Wilson
28:48

The library itself was the key sample bank for deciphering cuneiform

Researchers first deciphered cuneiform in 1857, only a few years after the library's first tablets were found. One key was the Bisitun inscription: carved on a rock face in Iran's Zagros mountains, today a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and like the Rosetta Stone it exists in three languages — Babylonian, Old Persian and Elamite — recording the account of the Achaemenid king Darius I. Henry Rawlinson published his study of the Old Persian portion in 1849, which made it possible to translate the other two languages. The library provided a large additional corpus of cuneiform samples and was part of the decipherment process.

— Tracy V. Wilson
29:51

Before the library was excavated, Assyrian history was almost only the enemy's account

Before this, the overwhelming majority of information about ancient Mesopotamia and the Assyrian empire came from the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament derived from it, plus Greek and Roman writers. The biblical texts focus mainly on matters relating to the Levant and Egypt, and the Greek and Roman accounts were often written centuries later; neither is a complete, detailed, layered history of the Assyrian empire. Once the library's tablets were read, they became a source of a great deal of new historical information. The episode gives one concrete example: Greek literature turned Ashurbanipal into a semi-mythical figure, Sardanapalus, blending elements of him and his brother Shamashumukin, and saying he was the last king of Assyria, who burned himself to death in his palace along with all his wealth and concubines.

— Tracy V. Wilson

Glossary

cuneiform
A writing system pressed into clay tablets with a reed stylus, the main medium for Assyrian and Babylonian texts.
colophon
Labeling information at the end of a tablet; many of the library's tablets carry standardized colophons, indicating a cataloguing method.
Neo-Assyrian
911 to 609 BCE, the height of the Assyrian empire; Ashurbanipal was one of its last kings.
Sardanapalus
The semi-mythical figure Greek literature made of Ashurbanipal, blending in elements of his brother and saying he died by self-immolation.
Bisitun inscription
A trilingual inscription on a rock face in Iran, similar to the Rosetta Stone, key to deciphering cuneiform.
