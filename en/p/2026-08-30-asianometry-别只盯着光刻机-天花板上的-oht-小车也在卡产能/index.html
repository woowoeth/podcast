The Bottleneck in a Fab Isn't the Lithography Machine—It's the OHT Robots on the Ceiling

The real capacity constraint in a wafer fab isn't just the lithography machine; it's the OHT vehicles scurrying overhead. The 300mm transition made them indispensable, and today the market is a duopoly of Daifuku and Muratec.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:07

The fab's transport problem is set by the bay layout

Fabs are divided into sub-areas by processing zone, and within each zone, tools are arranged in bays. Movement within a bay is called intrabay; movement between bays is interbay. Each bay is equipped with stockers—automated parking towers that hold roughly 150–250 FOUPs. Stockers replaced open shelving, saving cleanroom floor space, aiding WIP tracking, and serving as buffers and zone entry/exit points. The entire transport problem grows out of this layout.

6:12

Cleanroom floors are too expensive, so transport goes to the ceiling

Once a wafer leaves a tool, it must be inside a FOUP; dust would ruin it. Early on, people pushed FOUPs between bays, but a single lot could be worth $100,000, so automation began in the 1980s. First came floor-based AGVs, limited to about 1 foot/second for safety; then rail-guided RGVs were faster but took up floor space and needed guarding. PRI's Aerotrak hung a monorail from the ceiling, and by 1988 such interbay AMHS was common. Cleanroom floors are expensive, so this move was inevitable.

7:13

After interbay automation, humans became the new bottleneck

Once interbay was automated, the bottleneck shifted to operators manually moving wafers from stockers to tools. A skilled operator takes 30–90 seconds per load change, but a bay has many tools. The author recalls hearing constant beeping in a DRAM fab, told it was tools demanding operator attention. Intrabay automation must sense tool processing status and load/unload automatically; any misalignment or timing error stops the tool. In the early days, there were no industry standards, so each company invented its own. Hot lots and SUPER hot lots, being troublesome to program, were often still carried by operators on foot.

9:18

300mm wasn't an upgrade—it was an ultimatum for automation

The shift to 300mm wafers in the late 1990s made manual handling unsustainable. SEMATECH found that 15–20% of tool time in older fabs was wasted waiting for operators or FOUPs; tools were more expensive, heavier, and took more floor space. A loaded 300mm FOUP weighs about 9 kg, exceeding the 'maximum acceptable weight' for repetitive lifting in ergonomics. A survey of Taiwanese 200mm fabs found 40–60% of workers had shoulder discomfort and 30–50% had back problems. High-flow 300mm bays require over 300 moves per hour, versus 125–175 in older fabs—automation was the only option.

11:28

OHT wins by not using floor space, not by speed

The core of second-generation AMHS is the OHT: vehicles run on overhead rails, stop directly above a tool, and use a hoist to pick up or place FOUPs at the tool's front-end interface, completing the move in seconds at up to 60 meters/minute. It doesn't occupy floor space or compete with people, but it's expensive to install, inflexible in routing, and hanging over tools poses a particle risk. To address this, SEMI established common standards for loadport configuration and optical signal exchange between vehicle and tool, ensuring successful handoffs and saving costs. OHT became the only choice for a unified system.

13:30

A system can cost over $100 million, so most fabs kept using people

TSMC was among the first to open a 300mm fab with full-roof AMHS: Fab 12 in Hsinchu, initially running 150nm then moving to 130nm, with 2,000 vehicles making 600,000 moves per day. But the scale and cost are daunting—a complete 300mm interbay/intrabay system runs $50–100 million and takes two years to install. For years afterward, many fabs still relied on manual wafer handling. Automation was not a one-time switch.

14:31

AMHS scheduling has no optimal solution, only trade-offs

Scheduling is done centrally by the fab's control system: assigning vehicles, planning routes, handling congestion, and avoiding deadlocks and down tools. Fabs don't add extra vehicles for peak times; they work with what they have. Hot lots and rework must be encoded as special rules. The author's verdict: there is no optimal algorithm. So operators use simulation to optimize targets like average delivery time, on-time delivery rate, cycle time, or throughput. It's like designing a traffic system for the entire fab.

16:35

The 300mm transition reshuffled the OHT supplier landscape

Daifuku (大福) is the largest OHT supplier. Its predecessor made steel forging machinery and renamed itself after WWII to avoid zaibatsu dissolution, taking the name from the first characters of its Osaka and Fukuchiyama plants. The turning point was a license for Jervis B. Webb chain conveyors, leading to a 1959 supply to Toyota's Motomachi plant and growth with Japan's automakers. The 300mm transition reset the market: PRI, struggling with Aeroloader R&D costs, was sold to Brooks Automation for $500 million; eventually Daifuku and Muratec consolidated the industry and have dominated ever since.

Glossary

AMHS / Automated Material Handling System
The collective term for automated equipment that moves FOUPs within a fab, including AGVs, OHTs, and stockers.
FOUP / Front-Opening Unified Pod
A standardized wafer container, typically holding 25 wafers, used for safe transport between tools.
OHT / Overhead Hoist Transport
A vehicle that runs on overhead rails and uses a hoist to pick up and place FOUPs at a tool's front-end interface.
Stocker
An automated storage/retrieval machine, like an automated parking tower for wafers, serving as a buffer and zone entry/exit.
Interbay / Intrabay
Interbay refers to transport between bays; intrabay refers to transport between tools within the same bay.
