Stuff You Missed in History Class 2026-10-24

No poison found in the corpse—yet the jury hanged him in 75 minutes

Serial beneficiaries of Palmer's insurance policies died in succession; autopsies were bungled; strychnine went undetected in the corpses—the jury still convicted him on motive and circumstance alone. The case remains disputed as a potential miscarriage of justice.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:04

Lifelong schemer from boyhood, building fortune through fraud

Palmer had a long history of dishonesty. Classmates accused him of cheating; he stole from family. While apprenticed at a Liverpool pharmacy, he pocketed customer payments for orders that were never submitted. When confronted, he denied it and lasted just three months. This scheme—take the money, skip the delivery—later became his template for larger frauds.

9:16

Gambling and racing debts pushed him toward more desperate schemes

Palmer's gambling spiraled. He bought or held stakes in multiple racehorses, eventually forced to sell them at loss. Through racing, he befriended Blayden, who lent him money. After Blayden won a large sum and stayed at Palmer's home, he died days later; the death certificate attributed it to ‘pelvic abscess from hip injury.’ Palmer then demanded Blayden's widow repay 60 pounds he claimed Blayden owed him, but couldn't account for the racing winnings or betting records. She refused and grew suspicious.

13:22

Wife and brother each died immediately after insurance policies matured

Seven years into marriage, Palmer took out a 13,000-pound life insurance policy on his wife Anne. Within weeks of paying the first premium, Anne died during a cholera epidemic. The payout was quickly used to settle his debts. The next year, he insured his brother Walter for 14,000 pounds—an earlier application for 84,000 pounds had been rejected—with a creditor paying the premiums. Walter died alone at home. The pattern alarmed insurers, prompting them to investigate his next application: a 10,000-pound policy on his groom George Bates.

18:28

Poisoned friend Cook while embezzling his racehorse winnings and playing nurse

Cook won approximately 3,000 pounds at Shrewsbury races. At a celebration, he drank brandy, then complained his throat was burning; Palmer ridiculed him. Cook then fell into cycles of vomiting and illness. Palmer, posing as a medical friend, remained at his bedside, spooning soup and coffee to him while secretly embezzling Cook's winnings. Days before Cook's death, Palmer bought strychnine from two different pharmacies—three grains total—falsely claiming one purchase was to poison a dog. Cook died in violent convulsions, repeatedly saying he was suffocating.

22:31

Autopsy descends into chaos; evidence tampered with under Palmer's watch

Cook's stepfather demanded an autopsy. Two young, inexperienced examiners—apparently fortified by alcohol—performed the procedure in chaos: spilling organs, colliding into each other. During the confusion, Palmer removed the jar containing Cook's stomach contents to an adjacent room; when discovered missing, it reappeared with its seal broken. Toxicologist Taylor, receiving such a degraded sample, requested a second autopsy. Palmer also bribed the postman to intercept a letter Taylor had written to the coroner, and enclosed a 10-pound note in his own letter to the coroner, demanding the death be ruled accidental. The coroner declined the bribe.

25:39

Wife's remains showed antimony; brother's were too decomposed for analysis, yet both ruled murdered

The scandal prompted exhumation and re-examination of both Anne and Walter. Anne's remains were well-preserved; tissue analysis revealed antimony. Walter's corpse was so decomposed that opening the coffin caused multiple people at the scene to retch and faint. His cheeks had bloated against the coffin sides; one eye and half his mouth hung open in a horrifying grimace. The decomposition was too advanced for meaningful toxicological analysis. Both coroner's inquests ruled the deaths wilful murder.

28:46

Every medical expert admits they have never witnessed strychnine poisoning in humans—only in animals

The trial's critical moment came when two pharmacy apprentices testified that Palmer had purchased strychnine twice within a week before Cook's death—neither purchase properly logged as the law required. Dr. Bamford, too ill to attend, submitted a written statement attributing the death to apoplexy, which other experts on the stand immediately refuted. More crucially: every medical witness confessed they had never personally observed strychnine poisoning in a living human—only in animals. The defence argued that if Cook had truly been poisoned by strychnine, the autopsy samples should have shown it. The jury deliberated 75 minutes and returned guilty.

31:46

Hanged before 35,000 spectators; modern legal standards suggest the trial cannot withstand scrutiny

On 14 June 1856, Palmer was hanged outside Stafford Prison before an estimated 35,000 spectators. Rain the previous night did not deter crowds who queued overnight to secure positions. Rumour attributed as many as 15 murders to Palmer, entirely unproven and never tried. Dickens called him ‘the greatest monster ever to stand in the dock at the Old Bailey,’ and the case inspired the detective character in Bleak House and influenced Hitchcock's poisoner in Suspicion. Scholars today argue that by modern evidentiary standards, the trial cannot withstand scrutiny.

Glossary

strychnine
A deadly alkaloid; typical poisoning presents as violent, uncontrollable convulsions.
antimony
A heavy metal employed as poison in the 19th century; sub-lethal doses cause illness without necessarily causing death.
coroner's inquest
An informal English hearing presided over by a coroner to determine whether a death involves criminal intent; not a formal trial.
apoplexy
A 19th-century medical diagnosis loosely applied to sudden death, lacking rigorous scientific basis.
