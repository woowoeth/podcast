The biggest bug in scientist-founded startups: not knowing who the customer is

For its first five years Horizon sprayed 360 degrees across air conditioners, toys, home appliances and cars; in 2019 it cut half its people and bet everything on automotive, turning from a company firing in all directions into a strategy-driven one.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:15:02

Fundraising is a funnel, and the first meeting must never be in the investor's office

Yu Kai breaks an investor's decision into a funnel model: from never heard of it, to aware, to interested, to doing research, to visiting the team, to forming a view, to playing a card. If you enter this funnel early, you have to spend a long time persuading the other side. So he set himself an iron rule: the first meeting with an investor is absolutely never in his office, it must be in your own office — if the other side comes knocking, it means they are already at the bottom of the funnel, close to playing a card. He used "no time" and "the project is very busy" to filter out the people still at the card-looking stage, and in the end only four institutions were seriously negotiating, each willing to sign an exclusivity agreement.

— Yu Kai
1:22:11

The common disease of scientist founders is spraying 360 degrees

Yu Kai says Horizon at the start only knew it "wanted to build a hardware platform and a software platform for the robotics era," but "which business scenario to enter from, we had absolutely no idea." So automotive, AIoT, toys, home appliances — all were tried, resources spread across so many directions, and "every point was very hard to break through." He attributes this to the typical problem of scientist founders: triggered by a very big vision, believing they have technology, but having no concept at all of the business scenario, so it becomes all kinds of spraying rather than focusing — aiming at one hill and pounding it with all the firepower.

— Yu Kai
1:26:16

Cutting half the people was a deliberate strategic focus

In November 2019 Yu Kai figured out he had to "give up nine to take one," cutting the AIoT direction. The company had 1,200 to 1,300 people at the time, and he cut nearly half, about 600 people. He stresses this was not the "Horizon can't survive" that outside reports said — there were over three billion in cash on the books at the time. He calls this focus the one that remade Horizon: from a company firing in all directions with no strategic thinking into a truly strategy-driven startup team with clear goals and a clear customer definition.

— Yu Kai
1:35:19

Splitting the layoffs into three rounds was to make himself comfortable

The original plan was to cut a bit before the New Year, a bit after, and a bit more mid-year. One night Yu Kai woke from a dream and realized that doing it this way "was essentially to make ourselves comfortable, not to be good to the employees": employees would go home happy for Spring Festival and come back to work only to be told they were laid off. He changed it to finishing all the layoffs within one month, from December 15, 2019 to January 15, 2020, and personally talked to a dozen or twenty people. His judgment: when facing a tough decision, do the right thing.

— Yu Kai
1:49:26

Raising in reverse during the bubble, without raising the valuation a cent

From the second half of 2020 to the first half of 2021 the capital markets were in a frenzy. Yu Kai judged that "the bubble would definitely burst," so he went the opposite way: the shareholders' meeting gave a cap of about 800 million US dollars, and after raising that quickly, he applied to expand it and immediately took another 800 million, 1.6 billion in total, without raising the valuation a single cent. At the time most companies raised two or three hundred million and then raised the valuation, then raised again at a higher valuation. Later the market turned sharply down and most companies couldn't raise money at all; Horizon, with a hundred in hand, was not anxious at all.

— Yu Kai
2:16:45

Autonomous driving is a standard feature; OEMs shouldn't build it in-house

Yu Kai's counter-consensus judgment: the user experience standard for autonomous driving is unified — from A to B, safe, comfortable, efficient, no difference between men and women, old and young, Chinese or Japanese; you can't create differentiation. He uses phones as an analogy: no phone maker builds its own calling function, even Apple doesn't build its own baseband. So OEMs should spend their energy on things like cameras, which have no common standard, vary from person to person, and carry emotional value, and leave standardized autonomous driving to suppliers. He admits the vast majority of OEMs disagree with this view.

— Yu Kai
2:19:46

Li Auto agrees with the logic, but Li Auto is not a car company

Yu Kai says he discussed this with Li Auto, and Li Auto actually agrees that autonomous driving is a standard feature and from that angle not worth building in-house. But the key is "don't forget what Li Auto's ideal is" — Li Auto would love to compete with Google, Microsoft and OpenAI in the future. So doing autonomous driving is its path to artificial intelligence, to sustaining war with war, training the team, and building capability. Yu Kai says that once he understood this, even if one day Li Auto stops working with him, he can accept it, because making cars is only one step for Li Auto.

— Yu Kai
2:23:47

A human brain runs 5000T on 20 watts; the chip draws over 100 watts

Yu Kai says cars and robots are different from cloud computing: the cloud has almost unlimited power and good cooling, while cars and robots depend on their own batteries, with limited power and poor cooling. The human brain can achieve 5000T of compute on 20 watts of power, ten times the compute of Horizon's highest-performance chip, Journey 6P, yet Journey 6P alone draws over 100 watts. To reach 20 watts and 5000T, you must move far away from today's von Neumann architecture and completely merge compute and storage, like the human brain.

— Yu Kai
