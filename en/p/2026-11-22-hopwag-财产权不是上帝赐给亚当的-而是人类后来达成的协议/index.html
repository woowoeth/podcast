Freedom of the seas is ultimately a claim about property

Grotius opposed any state or company asserting sovereignty over the ocean—not for political reasons, but on property grounds: no one can own any part of the sea, so merchant ships have the right to trade freely through it. The argument sounds like a claim about maritime freedom, but to understand it you must first grasp Grotius's answer to a more fundamental question: why does property exist at all?

7:16

Natural law holds even without God

Grotius held that all law derives from some legislator's will, and the legislator of natural law is of course God. But he boldly posed a thought experiment: even if God did not exist, natural law would still be self-evident and binding for us. God is the source of this law, but once created, humans can discover its rules simply by studying nature itself.

— Peter Adamson
9:24

Nature gives us use alone, not possession

Grotius rejected the prevailing view that property rights trace back to Adam and Eve and pass down through inheritance. In the state of nature, he argued, humans have only the right to use things, not to possess them. This distinction echoes a thirteenth-century Franciscan debate over vows of poverty: monks claimed the library books and stores' food did not belong to them—they were merely using them.

— Peter Adamson
10:28

Possession ends the moment continuous use stops

Drawing on Cicero's example, Grotius compared this right of use to finding a seat in a theater—whoever sits down first has the right to it until the performance ends. The same rule applied to enclosure and cultivation: if someone fenced off a meadow to farm it, others should let him use it. But possession was not permanent. Once use stopped—say, to study croissant-making in France for a year—your neighbor could claim the land was no longer yours.

— Peter Adamson
12:31

The hungry retain a legal right to steal bread

Grotius held that even after humans later reached agreement through the Law of Nations and established a true system of ownership, the right of necessity from the state of nature was always retained: in life-or-death emergencies, anyone has the right to use another's things to survive, and must compensate afterward if able. No one would agree to surrender this right, so we can be certain such an agreement was never truly made.

— Peter Adamson
14:35

Water is too fluid ever to be possessed

Grotius argued that the ocean could never be possessed in part or in whole by anyone, since—except for rivers and narrow straits—water was too ‘fluid’ to be enclosed or worked to demonstrate occupation as land could be. The deeper reason: God distributed resources unevenly across the earth precisely to force humans to trade with each other, and the boundless sea ensures that this common right to use it can never be overridden by local claims of possession.

— Peter Adamson
16:37

Latitude and longitude bind the seas as fences do land

Grotius's argument was rebutted by jurist William Welwod, Portuguese friar Serafim Freitas, and Englishman John Selden. They pointed out that while you cannot build a fence in the middle of the Atlantic, lines of latitude and longitude draw boundaries just as well—the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas used a meridian to divide the world between Spain and Portugal. They also countered that land changes too, but that does not prevent land from being owned, so why should water be different?

— Peter Adamson
17:37

This theory began as a Dutch East India Company brief

Grotius's argument for freedom of the seas was originally part of a longer manuscript he drafted for the Dutch East India Company (VOC) and published separately for diplomatic reasons. When the Dutch later wanted to establish a monopoly on eastern trade themselves, this free-trade argument became a weapon against them—the English directly used Grotius's own words to rebut him, spurring him to write a second major work, De Jure Belli ac Pacis, to revise his position.

Glossary

state of nature
A hypothetical stage before political society forms, when humans coexist by nature alone.
Law of Nations
An agreement reached among political states; Grotius used it to explain the origin of property rights.
right of necessity
A retained right to use another's property to survive in life-or-death emergencies, even after private property is established.
