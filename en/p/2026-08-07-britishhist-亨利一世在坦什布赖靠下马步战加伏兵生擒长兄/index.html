Dismounted Knights and a Hidden Ambush: How Henry I Captured His Own Brother

Henry I made his knights fight on foot, then sprang a concealed fourth division against the enemy flank, taking his brother Robert prisoner and ending the fight for Normandy.

4:25

The Anselm dispute cost Henry two and a half months of campaigning

Henry had set 3 May for crossing the Channel to invade Normandy, but was held up by his open conflict with Archbishop Anselm. One moment Anselm was on his sickbed; after eating a stolen partridge he recovered, and then raised his demands on Henry one layer at a time: return the Canterbury church properties, waive the fines on married clergy. Henry was forced to concede all of it, and even made Anselm regent of England, before he could get him onto a ship. The result was a campaign that opened two and a half months late, and it also meant Count Geoffrey of Anjou was killed by a crossbow bolt on 19 May; Anjou fell into a succession crisis from then on, and Henry lost a key ally. This stretch shows how political and military timetables wreck each other.

10:00

Robert only had to hold his ground to win; Henry had to attack

Henry had to go on the offensive, Robert only had to hold. But Normandy was already coming apart internally: nobles were at war with each other, western Normandy had gone over to Henry, and Robert could not even suppress the chaos in his own lands. Henry judged that a frontal assault would cost too much, and chose the political route instead, flipping Robert's allies one by one. This is exactly the overall strategy he went on to attempt: not grinding his opponent down with sieges, but ending the war in the council chamber. The section sets up the quick victory at Tincherbray later: Robert's military strength was not weak, but political collapse left him unable to organise a unified defence.

— Jamie
13:05

The trap at Saint-Pierre was turned back on the men who set it

The abbot of Saint-Pierre came over to Henry with the castle in hand, and suggested he bring only a small personal retinue to take possession, saying the garrison was all willing to swear loyalty. Henry agreed to his face, then turned to his advisers and asked whether it was not too suspicious, and immediately assembled 700 knights, rode 20 kilometres through the night, and arrived before dawn. The defenders had in fact set a trap: Saint-Pierre held a large ducal garrison of 140 men, Robert's relief force was about to arrive, and the plan was to wait for Henry to walk into the net. What arrived instead was an entire army. Mocked by the defenders, Henry flew into a rage and ordered an immediate assault, even setting fire to the abbey; some of the garrison fled, others burned to death. This shows Henry was neither a hothead nor anyone's puppet.

— Jamie
23:09

A humiliation over unripe wheat forced Henry to take over the siege himself

Henry sent Thomas of Saint John to build a siege castle outside Tincherbray, with the job of stopping William of Mortaine's troops from riding out to raid. But after William got into the town with a large body of men and supplies, Thomas dared do nothing but hide inside his fortification. Seeing that his opponent would not come out and fight, William simply had his soldiers harvest the still-unripe green wheat outside the walls — useful as fodder, but more importantly a way of stripping Thomas of every shred of face. When Henry heard about it he was furious, decided he had overestimated Thomas, and came in person with troops to tighten the siege. The outcome was that William really was trapped inside, able only to smuggle out letters begging for relief. The passage shows the practical battlefield value of humiliation and reputation in a medieval siege.

— Jamie
25:00

Robert never declared war; historians put those words in his mouth

Robert's warning letter has been paraphrased by some historians as a threat to declare war if the siege was not lifted, as though Robert had not even noticed the two sides were already fighting. But Orderic's text says: Robert assembled an army and demanded that Henry lift the siege of his lands, or else he would challenge him to a decisive battle — which means something entirely different from declaring war, and implies that Robert knew perfectly well the two sides were already at war, and simply wanted very badly to avoid trading blows with his own younger brother. Jamie makes a point of saying he checked several translations and they all read the same way, warning listeners not to trust second-hand paraphrase. This episode has a sharper sense of source criticism than most history podcasts, which is one reason it is worth listening to.

— Jamie
30:00

Henry's partition terms were deliberately designed to humiliate

Rather than attacking straight away, Henry first sent a letter of moral blackmail: he claimed to be acting not out of ambition but to protect the church and the poor, and berated Robert as a "duke in name only" who indulged wicked men in destroying Normandy. His terms were very specific: hand over every fortress in Normandy and all judicial authority, and give me half the duchy; keep the revenues of the other half for yourself, and the English treasury will pay you the same amount every year; you can enjoy yourself as much as you like, and I will do the work. To Robert, an old comrade-in-arms, this was a double humiliation — and it also alluded to his refusal of the crown of Jerusalem and to the sloth for which God had cursed him. Robert's men were enraged when they heard it and demanded an immediate attack. Henry's rhetoric hit precisely where Robert's circle was most sensitive.

— Jamie
35:00

Refusing the crown of Jerusalem became Robert's fatal weakness in the propaganda war

After the First Crusade took Jerusalem, Robert was invited to become king, and he refused. Church writers such as Henry of Huntingdon read this as sloth so deep he would not even serve God, and said God was therefore taking revenge on him in Normandy. Jamie points out that this was highly effective propaganda: Henry's camp repeatedly invoked "the sloth for which God cursed Robert", so when Henry's letter told him to "go to banquets, hold games, enjoy yourself", it was not a blessing but a poisoned barb. He also suspects the whole narrative very likely originated in Henry's court. The case shows that in medieval warfare the battle for opinion mattered as much as the fighting.

— Jamie
42:08

A hidden fourth division, not brute force, decided the battle

The horns sounded at nine in the morning. Robert's army, led by William of Mortaine and containing many First Crusade veterans, hurled itself at the royal line and at one point pushed the royal lines back. But Henry had concealed a fourth division of more than 1,000 men far out on the flank of the field, commanded by count Helias, who had not dismounted. They suddenly crashed into William's flank and killed 225 men in the blink of an eye. Robert of Bellême, who was supposed to bring the rear division up in support, simply withdrew, abandoning the main body and the duke. The ducal army collapsed and its nobles were taken prisoner one after another. This was the first battle Henry genuinely fought and won in person, and he won it through deployment and concealment rather than force.
