Dismount and Fight on Foot, Plus an Ambush: Henry I Captures His Own Brother at the Decisive Battle for Normandy

Henry I made his knights dismount and fight on foot, then used a hidden fourth division to ambush the flank and take his brother Robert prisoner, ending the struggle for Normandy.

4:25

The church dispute costs Henry two and a half months

Henry had originally set May 3 to cross the Channel and invade Normandy, but was held up by an open conflict with Archbishop Anselm. Anselm, who had been on his sickbed one moment and recovered after eating a stolen partridge the next, kept raising his demands on Henry: return Canterbury's church property, pardon the fines on married clergy. Henry was forced to agree to everything, even making Anselm regent of England, before he could get him onto a ship. As a result the campaign started two and a half months late, and Count Geoffrey of Anjou was killed by a crossbow bolt on May 19, plunging Anjou into a succession crisis and costing Henry a key ally. This section shows how politics and military timing wreck each other.

— Jamie
10:00

Robert only had to hold his ground to win

Henry had to attack; Robert only had to defend. But Normandy was already fractured from within: nobles warred with one another, western Normandy swung to Henry, and Robert could not even suppress the chaos in his own lands. Henry judged that a frontal assault would cost too much and decided to go the political route, turning Robert's allies one by one. This was precisely the grand strategy he later attempted: not to wear the other side down with sieges, but to end the war in the council chamber. This section provides the background for the later quick victory at Tinchebray: Robert's military strength was not weak, but political collapse left him unable to organise a unified line of defence.

— Jamie
13:05

The trap at Saint-Pierre is turned against its makers

The abbot of Saint-Pierre came to Henry with his castle, offering to hand it over, and advised him to bring only a small retinue to receive it, saying the garrison was all willing to swear allegiance. Henry agreed verbally, then turned to his advisers and asked, "Isn't this too suspicious?" He immediately gathered 700 knights, rode 20 kilometres through the night, and arrived before dawn. The garrison had set a trap: Saint-Pierre held a large ducal garrison of 140 men, and Robert's relief force was about to arrive, intending to wait for Henry to walk into it. What arrived instead was an entire army. Henry, having been mocked, flew into a rage and ordered an immediate assault, even setting fire to the abbey; the garrison fled or burned to death. This section shows Henry was neither a hothead nor a puppet to be led by the nose.

— Jamie
23:09

Green wheat humiliation forces Henry to take personal command

Henry sent Thomas of Saint John to build a siege castle outside Tinchebray, tasked with stopping William of Mortaine's troops from sallying out to raid. But after William entered the town with a large force and supplies, Thomas only dared hide inside his fortress. Seeing the other side would not come out to fight, William simply had his soldiers harvest the unripe green wheat outside the walls — good as fodder, but more importantly to humiliate Thomas utterly. When Henry heard, he flew into a rage, believing he had overestimated Thomas, and came in person with troops to tighten the siege. The result was that William really was trapped inside the town, able to send only secret letters begging for relief. This section shows the practical value of "humiliation" and "reputation" in medieval siege warfare.

— Jamie
25:00

A lesson in reading sources: Robert did not declare war

Robert's warning letter has been paraphrased by some historians as "if you do not lift the siege, I declare war," as if Robert did not even realise the two sides were already fighting. But Orderic's original text says: Robert gathered his army and demanded that Henry lift the siege of his lands, otherwise he would challenge him to a decisive battle — which means something entirely different from "declaring war," suggesting Robert was well aware the two sides were already at war and simply desperately wanted to avoid facing his own brother in combat. Jamie specifically says he checked multiple translations and they all read the same, warning listeners not to trust second-hand paraphrase. This episode's awareness of source criticism is stronger than that of most history podcasts, and is one reason it is worth hearing.

— Jamie
30:00

Henry offers deliberately humiliating terms for partition

Henry did not attack directly at first; he sent a letter full of moral blackmail: claiming he acted not from ambition but to protect the church and the poor; calling Robert a "duke in name only" who tolerated wicked men destroying Normandy. His terms were very specific: hand over all the fortresses of Normandy and all judicial authority, and give me half the duchy; you keep the revenues of the other half, and the English treasury will pay you an equal sum each year; you may enjoy yourself to the full, and I will do the work. To Robert, an old comrade-in-arms, this was a double humiliation — it also hinted at his refusal of the throne of Jerusalem and his God-cursed laziness. Robert's men, on hearing it, were furious and demanded to attack at once. Henry's rhetoric struck precisely at the most sensitive spot in Robert's camp.

— Jamie
35:00

Refusing the throne of Jerusalem becomes a fatal public-relations wound

Robert had been offered the kingship after the First Crusade took Jerusalem, and he refused. Church authors such as Henry of Huntingdon interpreted this as laziness so great he would not even serve God, and said God therefore punished him in Normandy. Jamie points out this was extremely effective propaganda: Henry's camp repeatedly invoked "Robert's God-cursed laziness," so Henry's letter telling him to "go to feasts, hold games, enjoy yourself" was not a blessing but a poisoned barb. He also suspects this narrative likely came out of Henry's court. This case shows that in medieval warfare the war of opinion was as crucial as the war of arms.

— Jamie
42:08

The fourth division in ambush decides the battle

At nine in the morning the horns sounded, and Robert's army, led by William of Mortaine, launched a fierce assault on the royal line; many were First Crusade veterans, and they at one point drove the royal lines back. But Henry had hidden a fourth division of more than 1,000 men far off on the flank of the battlefield, commanded by Count Helias, and they had not dismounted. They suddenly slammed into William's flank and killed 225 men in the blink of an eye. Robert of Bellême, who should have led the rear division in support, simply retreated, abandoning the main force and the duke. The ducal army collapsed and nobles were captured one after another. This was the first time Henry had truly taken the field and won, and he won through deployment and concealed positioning, not brute force.

— Jamie
