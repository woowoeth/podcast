Salesforce's M&A Chief: Great Technology Dies Without Distribution

John Somorjai says software can be built faster and cheaper than ever, but startups still need years and constant equity dilution to build sales networks reaching all customers—which is precisely why M&A creates value for both Salesforce and its acquisition targets.

8:14

Test an acquisition internally before committing to purchase

Salesforce piloted FIN on help.salesforce.com and deployed it in just 14 days. Today FIN handles over 40% of website traffic, resolving 65% of queries without human handoff. For Somorjai, this speed from deployment to impact is the core metric for acquisition decisions—the faster value emerges, the better the acquisition.

— John Somorjai
10:15

A built sales force is the ultimate acquisition trigger

Salesforce's 15,000 sales representatives are the moat that forces acquisition. Once a product is integrated into this channel, a small team can instantly reach all customer segments globally. Building distribution yourself takes years and constant equity dilution. This is why acquisition creates value for both Salesforce and its targets.

— John Somorjai
17:38

Acquisitions take six to nine months from start to finish

Software development has never been faster or cheaper, making the build-versus-buy decision harder to judge. Teams evaluate product roadmap, internal talent gaps (especially AI engineers, who are extremely hard to hire), and whether internal build speed can keep pace. But Somorjai notes that acquisition itself takes six to nine months from initial contact to full integration. The real question: can you build faster than that?

— John Somorjai
22:51

The Slack thesis only got stronger with AI agents

When Salesforce acquired Slack in 2020, the thesis was that Slack represented the interface for humans collaborating with applications. No one anticipated the AI agent era. But Somorjai says the original logic remains sound and is now validated beyond expectations, because Slack has become the ideal conversational interface for AI agents. Five years on, Slack revenue has nearly tripled and is Salesforce's fastest-growing product.

— John Somorjai
29:05

Track the future: Salesforce Ventures shifted to AI and robots

Salesforce Ventures started in 2009 backing ecosystem partners like DocuSign and Twilio, then expanded to industry-defining companies like Snowflake and Wiz. In early 2023, it established a $1 billion AI fund and invested in Anthropic, Mistral, Cohere, 11 Labs, and Runway. Now it's moving into physical AI and robotics. The underlying logic never changed: find companies that will create enterprise value in the future, whether or not they're core to today's business.

— John Somorjai
35:21

No new CRM company will ever beat Salesforce

Facing a wave of AI-native CRM startups, Somorjai is direct: existing CRM technology is already good enough, and Salesforce keeps improving it. He has not seen any new CRM differentiated enough to grow into a multi-billion-dollar company. When pressed on a possible SMB market opportunity, he concedes it might exist but dismisses it as too small for venture capital scale.

— John Somorjai
37:27

Valuations above $100 billion have almost no buyers

Somorjai is blunt about the first market: irresponsible behavior is rampant and valuations are widely inflated. Real exit paths are narrow—fewer than 1% of companies go public. Once a valuation exceeds $100 billion, the pool of potential acquirers shrinks sharply. This valuation-to-reality gap will eventually clear. Many companies simply cannot grow to match their valuations. Yet he also notes that each year he sees many dying companies trying to sell—a dynamic the press doesn't report.

— John Somorjai
42:48

ARR hides churn, and security can kill a deal

In due diligence, Somorjai's biggest warning sign from founders is fixating on ARR—it masks true customer churn. Security is a separate, hard constraint. Among 14 due diligence functions, only the security team has unilateral veto power over a deal. He cites one case where penetration testing not only exposed full source code access but also granted attackers entry to the CEO's laptop and employee data—damage too severe to remedy, and the deal died. Most security gaps can be fixed via covenants or pre-closing remediation.

— John Somorjai
