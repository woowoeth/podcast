Stuff You Missed in History Class 2026-10-31

She predicted two warships sinking at séances; Britain used the Witchcraft Act to silence her

Prosecutors declared in court this was fraud, not witchcraft, but what actually prompted them to invoke the 1735 Witchcraft Act was that she had correctly predicted two British warships sinking; the military feared she might leak information before the Normandy landings.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

7:11

Flash photography exposed the séance hoax: newspaper and hangers propping up cloth

Photographer Harvey Metcalf took a picture during a séance Duncan herself authorized, using a flashbulb. The resulting 'spirit manifestation' turned out to be a punch-dummy head with newspaper stuffed inside, draped in fabric strung on coat hangers, with gauze trailing from Duncan's nostrils. The flash killed the theatrical effect created by red lighting, but when the photos were published, Duncan's most devoted supporters remained unconvinced.

9:15

Spiritualist league exposed the ectoplasm formula: cheesecloth, egg white, tissue

The London Spiritualist Alliance investigated Duncan for eight months (October 1930 to June 1931), with her participation in roughly 50 séances for a fee. Investigators examined her clothing, took X-rays, even fed her methylene blue dye to trace if she was regurgitating 'ectoplasm'—that session produced nothing. The inquiry concluded she was a fraud: sampled materials varied in composition but consistently contained cheesecloth, egg white, and tissue paper. Psychologist Harry Price's independent investigation reached the same conclusion and photographed Duncan wearing what appeared to be cheesecloth.

16:30

She accurately predicted the sinking of two Royal Navy warships at séances

In May 1941, an astrologer attended Duncan's séance; her spirit partner reported a Royal Navy warship had just sunk—the next day confirmed that battlecruiser HMS Hood had been struck by Bismarck, with over 1,400 crew killed and only 3 surviving. Six months later in Portsmouth, Duncan relayed a message to a sailor's mother that ‘the ship sank’—HMS Barham had indeed been torpedoed by a German U-boat on November 25, with 850+ of 1,350 crew dead, the entire ship sinking in under 5 minutes. The Admiralty kept this secret for over two months; they did not publicly confirm the loss until January 27, 1942, after German media had already reported it.

19:34

Thousands knew the secret in Portsmouth alone; leak attribution is ambiguous

Portsmouth was HMS Barham's home port and also the headquarters of the Royal Navy command. Beyond the entire ship's crew and all escort vessels that witnessed the sinking, the decision-making chain, and the censorship apparatus, British Pathé newsreel cameramen had even filmed the explosion and sinking. The episode notes that potentially tens of thousands of people knew this secret, many concentrated precisely in the Portsmouth area where Duncan operated—making both ‘she is genuinely clairvoyant’ and ‘someone in the Navy leaked’ equally plausible explanations.

23:50

The Witchcraft Act contained a logical paradox: it abolished witchcraft as real

After the January 1944 raid on her séances, Duncan and three others were initially arrested under the 1824 Vagrancy Act—the standard tool for prosecuting mediums for fraud, with a maximum sentence of three months. Authorities wanted harsher punishment, so they invoked the 1735 Witchcraft Act. This law's actual purpose was to supersede earlier statutes that took witchcraft literally; its text explicitly stated that no person could be prosecuted for witchcraft, sorcery, magic, or spirit communication. What it actually punished was *claiming* to possess such abilities in order to defraud. In other words, using this law to prosecute Duncan required the legal premise that witchcraft does not exist.

30:01

Prosecutor opened trial stating this was fraud, never a question of belief

On March 23, 1944, the Old Bailey Central Criminal Court trial began. Prosecutor John Maude opened by emphasizing this was unrelated to faith—purely ordinary fraud. The defense attorney, C.E. Loseby, was himself a spiritualist and called nearly 50 witnesses to walk through Duncan's ‘manifestations’ from the early 1930s onward—a defense aimed at proving she was a genuine medium that instead documented she had conducted this illegal business for over a decade. The judge instructed the jury to rule only on charges under the Witchcraft Act. The jury unanimously found all four defendants guilty in under half an hour, and Duncan received nine months in prison.

33:05

Churchill wrote a memo condemning the trial after reading news reports

After Duncan's conviction, Prime Minister Churchill apparently knew nothing in advance—he learned of the case from newspaper coverage and promptly wrote a sharp memo to the Home Secretary questioning why a modern court was invoking an 1735 witchcraft statute, and demanding to know how much taxpayer money had been spent bringing witnesses from Portsmouth and housing them in crowded London for a two-week trial. This memo suggests that even if intelligence agencies had engineered the prosecution to suppress Normandy invasion leaks, the conspiracy did not reach the Prime Minister's level.

36:11

1951 Fraudulent Mediums Act reversed the logic: belief became legal, fraud criminalized

Duncan's conviction drove the campaign to repeal the Witchcraft Act—because literally read, the law would criminalize the entire spiritualist industry. The 1951 Fraudulent Mediums Act replaced it, shifting culpability to ‘intent to deceive’ and financial gain; genuine believers who thought they could channel spirits were now legal. Duncan herself was arrested again in 1956 (never charged), and died a few months later from diabetes and heart failure at 59. Her descendants petitioned the Scottish Parliament for a posthumous pardon in 2008 and were refused; the Vagrancy Act used to prosecute her was not formally repealed until 2026.
