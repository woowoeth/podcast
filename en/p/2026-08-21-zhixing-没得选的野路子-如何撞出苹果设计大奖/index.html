No Middle School Diploma, Can't Draw: How He Won an Apple Design Award

A self-taught outsider with a middle school education who can't draw, relying on repeatedly hitting walls and never letting go of the feeling of "discomfort," built China's first non-game app to win an Apple Design Award. Taste isn't a gift — it's a habit trained over years.

4:04

The product metaphor came first; the neuroscience explanation came later

In 2022, when his daughter was just over 2, Qiang Ge took her out and noticed that she would spontaneously read English words for floor tiles, plants, and buildings she saw repeatedly on the road, but had no reaction to words mechanically read out by a translator. He watched her play with stickers — peeling them off, placing them, spacing them all had their own logic — and came up with a metaphor: tear real-world objects off like stickers and attach language information to them. Later a friend gave him a neuroscience book, and only then did he learn this fit the principle of episodic memory — but at the time it was just intuition.

— Qiang Ge
12:19

Speed is the best experience; the animation only serves it

At first, image recognition plus cutout took over ten seconds. Qiang Ge felt no user could tolerate that, but he wasn't from a technical background and couldn't solve the compute problem. He thought of his daughter's complete sticker-peeling motion — peel off, pick up, stick on — and turned this natural process into an animation: the moment the user presses to shoot, recognition has already begun, and by the time the animation finishes, the result appears. He concluded: speed is the best experience; those explosive animations only serve speed.

— Qiang Ge
26:42

The years before he knew the word "design" were the real education

In middle school, Qiang Ge spent every day in internet cafes, drawing QQ Zone skins in Photoshop and adding music, but at the time he had no idea what the word "design" was — he just knew how to make things. After dropping out, he kept posting work on forums and was hired by a shanzhai phone company in Hangzhou based on his portfolio; only after coming to Beijing did he learn that design was a systematic profession, and the colleagues around him made him feel enormous pressure.

— Qiang Ge
34:25

Finding a blue every week trained it into a reflex

At his first company, his boss had him do an exercise every week: this week, find blues, and classify them by feel — professional, mysterious, sci-fi — then make posters and stick them at his workstation; he also had him outline concept car shapes in Photoshop and summarize the visual feeling. Qiang Ge says this training formed a conditioned reflex: now when he sees any object, he instinctively thinks about why it was designed that way.

— Qiang Ge
43:14

Explaining the problem-solving process works better than giving the conclusion

Qiang Ge admits leading a team early on was painful: he was always dissatisfied with proposals but couldn't articulate why, the team didn't understand him, and psychological problems even emerged as a result. Later he deliberately said "I think" less, and instead talked about goals, assumptions, and possible outcomes, laying out his complete thought process. He says that after switching from giving only conclusions to giving the problem-solving process, everyone actually did things better.

— Qiang Ge
52:42

A design bottleneck isn't too little practice — it's incomplete internalization

In 2016, Qiang Ge ran absorption, internalization, and expression training with his team, comparing people to computers: input signal, process, output. He uses fitness plateaus as an analogy for design bottlenecks: a bottleneck isn't insufficient training volume, it's incomplete internalization — the eyes have seen too many good things, but the hands can't keep up. The only way to break through is to keep working with your hands; once the signal is processed, the work naturally moves up a level.

— Qiang Ge
1:04:32

AI has raised the bar for building products, not lowered it

Qiang Ge uses AI heavily to test new features, and results come fast, but he's found things have gotten slower: choices become difficult, attention gets scattered, feedback comes too fast to internalize. He says AI improves efficiency but makes the thing itself slower, and the bar for building products is actually higher, because deep thinking decreases. He's changed his way of working now: give AI a limited set of choices rather than constantly trying new things.

— Qiang Ge
1:08:10

He's good at nothing else, so he doesn't agonize over whether he chose right

Asked whether design was an active choice or no choice at all, Qiang Ge says the latter: he's good at nothing else, and can't even squeeze out time to study vinyl records. He rarely agonizes over whether he chose right; his biggest life choice was leaving the company, and his criterion was asking himself whether he'd regret it in two years — the answer was that not leaving would definitely be a regret. He says design is painful and joyful at the same time, and he's willing to keep at it forever.

— Qiang Ge
