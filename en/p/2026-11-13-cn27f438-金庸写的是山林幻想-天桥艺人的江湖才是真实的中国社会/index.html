Jin Yong wrote mountain fantasy; street performers' jianghu is the real Chinese society

The jianghu Lian Kuangru witnessed was real: a place beyond state reach, where industries self-generated order and reputation kept hierarchy, not titles. Jin Yong's sect-filled jianghu, by contrast, resembles Tao Yuanming's imagined mountain paradise.

Quyi isn't an art form—it's the poor person's speaking trade

The term quyi is recent; old Beijing called it ‘ten kinds of miscellany’. After the 1911 Revolution, dispossessed Manchus drifted to Tianqiao performing—what had been leisure-time amateurism became survival necessity. Rural bankruptcies pushed peasant performers into the city with folk ballads and drum-songs. Street performances without fixed venues couldn't hold audiences without compelling talk, so ‘how well you could speak’ mattered more than singing ability. Nearly all Tianqiao genres relied on verbal charisma.

21:29

Train stations and streetcar terminals created Tianqiao

The railway lines (Beijing-Tianjin and Beijing-Hankou) initially terminated at Majiapu and outside Yongdingmen, later extending to Qianmen. Tianqiao became the required thoroughfare for entering and leaving the city. In the 1920s, the streetcar system established its terminus at Tianqiao, with lines running to Dongdan and the zoo. Where crowds gathered, landless people naturally found earning cracks. Zhabadi wasn't born from nowhere—it was a byproduct of transport infrastructure.

— Yue Yongyi
23:08

Lower status meant living farther from the imperial palace

In the Qing Dynasty, Manchus lived in the inner city and Han Chinese in the outer city. Official rank determined proximity to the imperial palace—the higher your status, the closer you could live. Moving south meant declining social position. The court relocated all entertainment venues from the inner city to the outer city's Daxila district and banned bannermen from learning theater. With Zhushikou as the boundary, the north held the Daxila theaters where famous Peking opera artists appeared; to the south lay Tianqiao. Once a performer's voice failed or their career stalled, drifting south of the boundary made return nearly impossible. Geography and social rank had fused into one hierarchy.

— Yue Yongyi
31:11

The ‘unmanaged zones’ were cracks all three powers ignored

Tianjin's core ‘unmanaged zone’ was the area bounded by Qinghe Street, Rongye Street, Dongxing Street, and Huan'an Street—wedged between the old city proper, the French concession, and the Japanese concession. The Qing and Republican governments couldn't reach the concessions; the Japanese concession didn't want to govern there; the French concession, being farther away, had even less interest. This created unclaimed territory. As merchants and hotel guests accumulated, teahouses, gambling dens, and brothels grew wild in the unpoliced gap.

Tianjin audiences came not to be pleased but to test performers

Meng Qingyan witnessed a stand-up comedy special where the warm-up host was loudly heckled offstage within five minutes. The opening act barely made seven minutes before a woman in the back shouted, ‘Why isn't the main act on yet?’—so loud the performer couldn't continue, so the performer said, ‘I just need to make everyone laugh’. Without saying another word, the entire room erupted. This ecology wasn't just entertainment; it showed that Tianjin quyi performers didn't face simple audience appeasement. They faced cosmopolitan spectators of high amateur skill who would heckle them offstage on the spot.

— Meng Qingyan
54:22

The master-disciple system's flaws masked mutual, reciprocal obligations

Meng Qingyan rejected simply calling the master-disciple system ‘feudal dregs’. Before an industry was formally incorporated into state administration and management structures, forming some order was unavoidable. Disciples did have to cook, clean, walk the dog—a relationship of personal subordination. But this subordination ran both ways. The master had to ensure the disciple could perform, earn money, and secure their future, including marriage and children. This wasn't one-way exploitation but an alternative system for maintaining industry order when contracts and employment bureaucracy didn't exist.

— Meng Qingyan
1:02:46

Cross-talk genealogies were reconstructed backwards by later generations

Yue Yongyi notes that quyi genealogies, like clan genealogies, are constructed retroactively, traced backward by successors—not systematically documented at the time. Most early performers were illiterate. Only after 1949, when identity became an official concern, were genealogies organized, complete with workarounds like posthumous ‘tomb-worship master ceremonies’ when the actual master had died. The genealogies themselves have limited accuracy; even Ma Sanli's memoir had discrepancies noted by audiences. Genealogies are less historical record than retrospective legitimation.

— Yue Yongyi
1:12:34

Jin Yong's jianghu is actually fantasy mountain retreat, not street society

Drawing on Qian Mu's distinction between city, countryside, jianghu, and mountains, Meng Qingyan suggests that Jin Yong's sect-ridden world resembles the imagined Taoist paradise—a fantasy mountain retreat. No real martial jianghu existed; mountain hermitage is the stuff of news stories, not daily life. Meng argues that modern Chinese understanding of ‘jianghu’ actually comes from Hong Kong films like Young and Dangerous, not from Jin Yong. Jianghu isn't violence. It's human relations, the world of state administrative reach's end—where reputation, not title, maintains order. Internet tech companies, academia—they have their own jianghu and slang too.
