Legal ethics rules let partners leave; law firms collapse easier than investment banks

Investment banks lock people in with non-competes and vesting; law-firm partners are protected by legal ethics and cannot be locked down—this is why Weil Gotshal collapsed overnight and investment banks do not.

Prediction markets sell certainty, not accuracy

Matt watched Kalshi on election night as Democratic Senate control climbed from about 73% to about 98%; once it hit 98%, he went to sleep. He admits knowing the result three or four hours early has zero economic value for ordinary people, but says the real value is compressing noisy speculation and contradictory signals on Twitter into a single number—so you don't have to stay up waiting for the vote count. That's underrated social utility, not a trading tool.

80% is not 98%; never conflate the two

Two discuss probability calibration: if an event is tagged at 80% probability, one in five times it won't happen—Brazil's election saw Bolsonaro flip from around 80%. On US Senate election night, odds climbed to 98% before Matt felt safe sleeping. He stresses that well-calibrated probabilities and near-certainty are different things; at 80% on voting day, ‘anything could happen’.

The midterms became a proxy for data-center expansion—many candidates from both parties called for pausing construction; the market read Democratic gains as bad news. Manufacturing progress for AI chips depends on irreplaceable human craft

Matt read a New Yorker piece on chip supply chains and thought: you sneeze in a TSMC factory, you set back AI progress three years. Ultraprecise manufacturing ultimately depends on an old master who taps the equipment by feel—perhaps five people globally know how. Anthropic says Claude can design next-generation chips and fabs, but Claude cannot train this master's hand feel. This reveals overlooked physical fragility in the AI narrative.

— Matt Levine
24:40

Law firms cannot hold onto their moneymaking partners

Weil Gotshal's collapse traces to a partner run. Citing Yale Law professor John Morley's research, collapsing firms are often not in bad financial shape; the problem is top earners get poached by more lucrative private-equity platforms. When they leave, average partner profit drops, so the rest also leave, and the firm hollows out. Investment banks can lock people in with non-competes, garden leave, and vesting schedules; law firms cannot.

— Matt Levine
26:40

Legal ethics block the tools that let investment banks hold partners

Matt explains the key difference: professional legal ethics bars lawyers from being restricted from practicing, so law firms cannot lock partners down with equity vesting like investment banks can—only clawback equity over reasonable periods when they leave. These rules were designed for a traditional gentleman-partnership era and completely fail in today's highly commercialized, high-turnover market. Law firms must compete in a far freer market than investment banks, without investment banks' lockup tools.

— Matt Levine
35:53

Reversible transactions are not a stablecoin flaw; they are a feature

North Korean hackers used social engineering posing as job interviews to steal $6.3 billion in crypto from Binance; roughly half was in centralized stablecoins like Tether and USDC. Because stablecoins are essentially ledger entries in the issuer's system, Tether and Circle simply reversed the stolen transfers. The other half—real on-chain crypto—could not be reversed. Matt sees centralized stablecoins' reversible transactions not as a betrayal of crypto principles but as superior design.

— Matt Levine
39:54

Forty minutes between theft and freeze is the launderer's whole window

From Binance theft to Tether and Circle freeze took about 40 minutes—bridged by direct calls among finance executives, not blockchain. In that window, hackers went wild buying Ethereum on DEXes, pushing the price up 15%, and took bad execution. But a 15% slippage is still cheap for money laundering. More ironic: the market-makers who filled those orders later told Bloomberg they made a killing on the trades.

— Matt Levine
