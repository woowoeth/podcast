An 1859 solar flare nearly destroyed the global grid. We're more fragile now.

Solar-scale geomagnetic storms strike every 150 to 200 years, and we're overdue. The fix is surprisingly simple: shut off equipment a few hours in advance.

4:20

Broken solar magnetic fields hurl plasma toward Earth at 2,500 kilometers per second

NASA's former chief scientist Jim Green used a rubber band analogy to explain the Carrington event's origins. Solar magnetic field lines get twisted and stretched; when they snap, they hurl millions of tons of solar matter at 2,500 kilometers per second into space—this is a coronal mass ejection (CME). This shock wave compresses Earth's magnetosphere on impact, creating electric currents in the upper atmosphere, which then flow through long ground conductors (telegraph lines then, power grids and transmission lines now) and overload them. That's how 1859 telegraph stations caught fire and burned.

— Jim Green
9:31

A geomagnetic storm much weaker than Carrington still blacked out Quebec for nine hours

Fourteen decades later, a geomagnetic storm far weaker than Carrington still let Florida and Cuba see auroras, damaged satellites and GPS, blacked out all of Quebec for nine hours in dead winter, affected six million people, and destroyed a large transformer in New Jersey with smoke and flames. This shows modern equipment is not immune to solar impacts—electrical currents still flow down long conductors, but what's changed is not the physics; it's how utterly dependent we've become on these systems.

11:32

A twenty-year lull in solar activity left critical infrastructure dangerously unprotected

Solar activity follows roughly an 11-year cycle, and each cycle should deliver a major geomagnetic storm. But from the early 2000s to the early 2020s, the sun stayed unusually quiet for about 20 years. Elizabeth Butler of the Space Weather Prediction Center notes that this calm period—a kind of 'earthquake-free zone in a seismic country'—probably led society to cut corners on solar risk protection while building the internet, satellites, and mobile infrastructure.

— Elizabeth Butler
18:46

A history buff's Civil War research accidentally revealed the Carrington storm's true intensity

Jim Green was reconstructing the life of Civil War-era balloon scouts when he combed through 1859 newspapers and stumbled on accounts of the sky turning red that night—he realized he'd found the Carrington event. He had NASA interns read through roughly a thousand news reports from the Congressional Library that night documenting auroras, recording their times, colors, and locations, then animated them into a map. This let him estimate the true strength of the storm—something nobody had systematically done before.

— Jim Green
20:47

The Carrington aurora burning to Colombia proves at least double the 1989 storm's intensity

The animation showed Carrington's blood-red aurora spreading all the way to Colombia in South America. The auroras from 1921, World War II, and the 1989 Quebec event never reached that far south. By modern calculation, Carrington's strength was at least double the 1989 event, possibly even the result of multiple overlapping storms rather than a single hit.

22:50

Records from China, Japan, and Babylon confirm Carrington-scale storms every 150 to 200 years

Jim Green searched for that blood-red low-latitude aurora in Japanese, Chinese, and even five-thousand-year-old Babylonian astronomical records, and found Carrington-scale storms repeating. His conclusion: these events occur roughly every 150 to 200 years—and we're in the window where one is due, having built our entire civilization's operations on dependence on electricity.

— Jim Green
24:52

Custom transformer replacement takes a month, extending any grid blackout to months or years

If we fail to shut down equipment in time, communications satellites, GPS, card payments, undersea internet cables could all go dark. The grid's high-voltage transformers would burn the way 1859 telegraph machines did. Each large transformer is custom-made and takes about a month to replace; transformer manufacturing itself requires electricity. By this logic, wide-scale blackouts could last months or even years.

— Jim Green
29:04

A 2024 government drill found critical gaps, then the worst storm since 1989 struck

In 2024, the U.S. Space Weather Prediction Center, FEMA, and NASA held a tabletop exercise for a geomagnetic storm. It exposed gaps: satellite monitoring had blind spots, the storm warning came late, FEMA and NASA command chains were unclear. More dramatically: halfway through the exercise, the strongest real geomagnetic storm since 1989 actually hit. The team had to run the simulated response steps in real life, and issued a warning roughly six hours in advance—far better than the typical thirty minutes.

— Elizabeth Butler

Glossary

coronal mass ejection (CME)
A burst of charged solar plasma ejected when solar magnetic fields rupture and break.
geomagnetic storm
An electrical surge in Earth's upper atmosphere caused by solar plasma compressing the magnetosphere.
black swan event
An unforeseen, low-probability event with massive consequences if it occurs.
