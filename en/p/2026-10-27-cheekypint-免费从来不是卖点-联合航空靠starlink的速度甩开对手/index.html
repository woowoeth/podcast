Free was never the selling point—United pulls ahead with Starlink's speed

After rebuilding its tech stack, United treats Starlink as a two-to-three-year competitive window, racing to lock in long-term customer brand loyalty.

Rivals contract, United places the largest aircraft order in history

During the pandemic, almost every expert predicted travel demand would stay permanently halved. Every major long-haul carrier except United permanently retired wide-body aircraft. Kirby thought the research methodology itself was broken—people answer surveys while panicked; once panic lifts, travel returns naturally. He placed an order for 200 wide-body aircraft plus hundreds of narrow-body jets. Each wide-body was priced at $400 million. To the board, his logic was: since everyone is contracting, this bet doesn't need to be right, just not catastrophically wrong—the odds are already in his favor.

6:55

Technology investment with no ROI becomes tomorrow's moat

Mid-pandemic, while every airline slashed costs, United launched a multi-billion-dollar project: migrating systems from mainframes to cloud, rebuilding databases. No one would normally fund it—ROI was impossible to calculate. That early investment is now a moat. United can do things competitors can't: ship products in days without research or focus groups, like spinning up a Starlink tracker in response to market demand.

9:08

CEO teaches himself to code, writes a betting-comparison tool in three hours

Kirby didn't use AI on useless tasks. He hired a tutor for three one-hour lessons, then spent two and a half hours building a webpage alone: select sport, date, bet type, then it auto-scrapes odds from four betting platforms—including Kalshi—and auto-executes on the best one. After that session, he called his team and said "We're all-in on AI." That conviction directly led to a critical mainframe-to-cloud system cutover that went zero-failure.

— Scott Kirby
22:06

Air traffic control, not aircraft or airports, is aviation's true bottleneck

Kirby contends air traffic control accounts for 90% of aviation's bottleneck. Government budgeting is glacially inefficient: an annual budget item delays two months for a shutdown, then three months to start up, four months working, three months winding down—no multi-year authorization. Deeper resistance: private jets pay almost nothing for ATC; airlines foot the full FAA bill, and private aviation lobbies hard against reform. ATC facilities are scattered across 450 districts for political reasons instead of being consolidated, tanking efficiency. Result: New York and Chicago airports are now hard-capping flights.

— Scott Kirby
27:02

Chicago hub math: cut feeder flights to open international routes

United flies Milwaukee-Chicago eight times a day in small turboprops. Almost no Milwaukee-to-Chicago passengers stay put; they're Milwaukee pickups for the international flight. These catchment-area flights are a waste of precious takeoff-and-landing slots. United sees American Airlines filling Chicago with redundant feeder flights that generate zero local benefit. Meanwhile, Atlanta and Dallas—smaller cities with one dominant carrier that doesn't waste capacity on redundant feeders—have more international destinations and more economic activity. United estimates American is losing roughly $1.5 billion this year at Chicago alone.

— Scott Kirby
30:26

Starlink keeps customers through speed, not through free WiFi

Starlink now covers 48% of United's fleet; next week it hits 50%. Kirby pushes back on the "free" narrative. Speed is the selling point. He tested the free on-air networks of three competitors on traditional systems—bandwidth so low it's basically dial-up. Passengers are telling him they board regional flights specifically because they have Starlink. He sees a two-to-three-year window before all carriers switch to low-Earth-orbit networks. Now he's converting early-adopter experience into long-term credit-card sign-ups and brand loyalty.

— Scott Kirby
55:51

Budget airlines died because of airport fees, not the model

Spirit's average ticket was $58 before bankruptcy. New York airports charge $52 per passenger. Before you buy jet fuel, before you buy planes, before you pay staff, the fee has already eaten half your revenue. The math breaks. Kirby points out budget airlines' founding thesis was flying secondary airports: old Southwest flew Oakland, not San Francisco; Burbank, not LAX. Ryan Air has never touched Heathrow or Paris. U.S. budget carriers broke that discipline, crammed into Chicago and Newark, and the model collapsed. Ryan Air is the only independent budget airline still standing.

— Scott Kirby
1:07:21

‘Strategic loss routes’are fiction—kill them on first mention

Kirby's advice to new airline CEOs: route networks are the foundation. If your base routes are losing money, excellence elsewhere doesn't matter. Open the route profit-and-loss statement and you'll spot a cluster of chronic losses. Ask executives about them and they say ‘strategic.’His rule: the moment you hear "strategic flying," cut it—no matter which hub, which route. He admits the company often can't follow its own rule. That discipline is everything: knowing who you are, knowing what you're good at, refusing to be both a discount and premium carrier.

Glossary

catchment-area flying
Short feeder flights into a hub whose business is connecting passengers to long-range flights; originating traffic is minimal.
CASM-X
Cost per available seat-mile excluding fuel; Wall Street's standard for airline cost control.
blended wing body
Aircraft where wings and fuselage merge as one design; dramatically more efficient than traditional tube-and-wing.
Value at Risk (VaR)
Statistical risk model assuming normal returns; Kirby argues it dangerously underestimates tail risk.
