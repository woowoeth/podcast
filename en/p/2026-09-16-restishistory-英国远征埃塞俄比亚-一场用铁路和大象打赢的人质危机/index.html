Britain's Expedition to Ethiopia: A Hostage Crisis Won with Railways and Elephants

In 1868 Britain spent £10 million, shipped in 44 elephants and 20 miles of railway track to rescue a few dozen hostages, using breech-loading rifles against spears to produce a massacre — then withdrew immediately, leaving Ethiopia to two decades of civil war.

5:12

Face was worth more than the hostages

The Derby cabinet decided to send troops, and the reason was not humanitarian: Britain could not be humiliated by an African king in front of its subjects in India and Aden. The episode states plainly that if you asked Derby or Disraeli, they cared far more about British prestige than about those few missionaries. Rescuing the hostages was a by-product; prestige was the main product. That judgment determined everything that followed: Napier's orders were to go in, get the hostages, and come out — never occupy, never install a puppet.

11:18

Napier worked the supply line backwards from the endpoint

Napier was an engineer by training, and his planning ran in reverse: to rescue the hostages he needed 5,000 men, those 5,000 men needed hundreds of miles of supply line, so he added another 7,000 to guard the line, and then he needed a coastal base and two sea routes. He was mobilising the resources of an entire empire — Liverpool, Woolwich, Chatham, Bombay, Madras. The episode quotes Philip Marsden's book saying that everyone from quartermasters to cooks to porters went to work to free the hostages held by the ‘gorilla king’. This was an expedition run as an engineering project.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
14:21

The expedition's hard currency was Austrian silver

In East Africa the only universally accepted currency was the Austrian Maria Theresa thaler, first minted in 1741, with the 1780 version the most sought after. Napier first sent men to Central Europe to buy up old coins, then sent men to the official Vienna mint to exchange British silver for 20,000 coins a week struck specifically for the expedition. The episode says this coin was still circulating locally into the 1950s. A British expeditionary force had to rely on Habsburg silver two centuries old to buy food and bribe local warlords.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
18:37

44 elephants and 20 miles of railway track came ashore

By the New Year of 1868 the British had landed 13,000 soldiers, 20 miles of railway track and 44 elephants — the elephants carried across the Indian Ocean in specially reinforced transport ships, seven more than Hannibal had. Engineers blasted paths up cliffs, first climbing to 8,000 feet, then crossing gorges and ravines for 350 miles to Magdala. They also laid copper telegraph wire the whole way, running field telegraph up to the advance guard. The episode says this may have been the most technologically advanced military operation in history.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
21:44

Napier bought the road he had to take with gifts

The road to Magdala ran through the territory of Ras Kassa, king of Tigray. Napier halted to wait for him, drew up his troops in salute, invited him to inspect the artillery, presented Arabian horses, rifles and glassware, promised that his troops would not loot or steal and would pay for every sack of grain, and pledged Queen Victoria's friendship. Kassa gave him a lion-skin cloak in return and promised weekly grain deliveries. The episode points out an understanding that was never written down: Kassa expected Napier to finish Teodros for good, and that was the bargain.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
37:20

Drunk, Teodros threw three hundred people off a cliff

On the Thursday before Easter, Teodros drank too much and went to sleep after lunch. At four in the afternoon he was woken by the noise of prisoners complaining that their chains were being removed too slowly. Enraged, he called for a sword, stabbed two prisoners to death on the spot, then ordered the remaining roughly three hundred thrown off the cliff one by one, with guards sent down to finish off the survivors. The whole process lasted two hours. That night a servant heard him praying to God, admitting he had been drunk when he gave the order for the massacre and asking not to be blamed for it.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
46:49

Breech-loading rifles turned a spear charge into a massacre

The Abyssinians had charged rifle lines before, and they knew riflemen took a long time to reload after firing, so you could rush them in that gap. But this time the British were equipped for the first time with the Snider breech-loading rifle, which could be reloaded in seconds, and the whole line could fire 30 to 40 rounds a second with no break in the fire. The chiefs and captains at the front were cut down first because they wore bright red shirts. The result: roughly a thousand Abyssinians dead and thousands wounded, against two British dead and about twenty wounded.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
56:00

Refusing the cattle meant refusing peace

Teodros sent a thousand cattle and five hundred sheep as a gesture of peace, but Napier refused them. The episode explains why: by local custom, accepting those animals meant accepting peace and letting the matter end there. When Teodros saw Napier driving the cattle and sheep back, he said bitterly to his courtiers that these people had got what they wanted and now wanted his life. That turn locked in the suicidal ending.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
1:00:00

He killed himself with a gun Victoria had given him

On Easter Monday the British shelled Magdala, and soldiers put up ladders to climb the outer wall and used crowbars to force the gate. Teodros shouted his last order to his men: run, I release you from your allegiance, but I will never fall into enemy hands. Then he put the barrel in his mouth and pulled the trigger. When the British came in they saw the gun beside the body and recognised it as the silver duelling pistol Queen Victoria had given him. The episode calls this Chekhov's gun: the weapon introduced in the first act must reappear in the fifth.

— Tom Holland / Dominic Sandbrook
1:05:11

What followed the withdrawal was twenty years of civil war

Napier had no interest in installing a puppet regime; his orders were simply to go in, rescue the hostages, and come out. His message to the troops said that the arduous expedition entrusted to them by the Queen and the British people was complete, and that they were going home. The result was that Abyssinia was left leaderless, sliding into two decades of chaos and civil war, then the spillover of the Sudanese wars, until Menelik II restored order at the end of the century by defeating the Italians at the Battle of Adwa in 1896. The episode says they kicked over an anthill and then left the locals to clean it up.

Glossary

Maria Theresa thaler
A silver coin minted in Austria, circulating in East Africa as a universal currency into the 20th century.
Snider breech-loading rifle
The breech-loading rifle used by the British for the first time, reloadable in seconds, keeping up an unbroken rate of fire.
khaki
The first time the British abandoned red uniforms in combat, switching to khaki to reduce visibility.
Ferenjis
The Abyssinian word for foreigners, in this context referring to the British.
