Shakespeare's History Plays Were Tudor Propaganda

Shakespeare's history plays distort the record constantly and are at bottom political propaganda for the Tudor dynasty: they vilify Richard III and dodge the sensitive subject of Henry VIII, all to shore up the regime's legitimacy.

12:07

Juxtaposition is an old tradition in English literature

Lu Dapeng borrows the view of the English writer Ackroyd: something refined can be placed side by side with something ordinary, vulgar, even obscene. In Shakespeare's plays, the scene of the princess learning English sets a girl's fear and longing toward the king of an enemy nation against a flood of sexual puns wrung from French-English homophones, while getting in a jab at the French. This kind of juxtaposition isn't just dirty jokes — it's a feature carried down through traditional English literature, and today's Yes Minister and Blackadder are the same lineage.

— Lu Dapeng
16:09

The sixteenth-century theatre was no temple of high art

We now think of Shakespeare as high art, but going to the theatre in the Elizabethan age was thoroughly down-to-earth entertainment: the same playhouse packed in nobles, middle-class merchants and street ruffians, with hawkers crying their wares, dogs running on the ground, chickens flapping their wings, and sometimes people brawling. That's why there are so many dirty jokes in Shakespeare — to hold the groundlings. Lu Dapeng stresses that the positioning of Shakespeare's plays then was completely different from today, which also explains those "low" elements in the dramatic structure.

— Lu Dapeng
18:06

Shakespeare's originality lay in working up his material

Almost all of Shakespeare's plots come from ready-made chronicles, legends or earlier texts, but that age did not prize "original plot"; being able to quote widely was what earned respect. What truly made him great was his ability to work up "bad material" — the English chronicles of the time were extremely dry, and only he could make them vivid and alive. Modern literary theory's concept of intertextuality says the same thing: there is no absolute originality in the world, and every text is a re-collage of old texts.

— Lu Dapeng
21:31

Modern English was fixed by Shakespeare and the Bible

Lu Dapeng proposes that the two key texts that make today's English vivid and readable are Shakespeare's plays and the 1611 King James Bible. Shakespeare's plays fixed such now-common phrases as a foregone conclusion, with bated breath and has seen better days; the Bible uses only about 8,000 common words, so that ordinary people could read it. Together the two kept Elizabethan English not far from today's, and lowered the barrier for people now to read Shakespeare.

— Lu Dapeng
38:19

The history plays are political propaganda, not history

Shakespeare's history plays contain a great many deviations from the facts, and Lord Norwich even drew up a word-by-word list of the errors. But Lu Dapeng says that going in with a mind to correct errors misses the point: the purpose of the history plays is political propaganda. The legitimacy of the Tudor dynasty could not withstand scrutiny to begin with — Henry VII was merely the descendant of a bastard and took the throne by usurpation; so the regime needed drama to write Richard III of the House of York as a "spider" and a "hunchbacked toad", using the contrast of "the darkness of the previous dynasty, the radiance of this one" to manufacture orthodoxy.

— Lu Dapeng
41:17

Why Henry VIII steers clear of the beheaded wife

Anne Boleyn was Elizabeth I's mother, and Shakespeare of course knew that Henry VIII had her executed — and so did the queen. So this play about Henry VIII observes the political proprieties to the full: it flatters the queen and the Tudor dynasty on one side, and on the other omits every sensitive episode, ending at Elizabeth's birth. Lu Dapeng holds that Henry VIII is not an outstanding play as drama, but as a text of political propaganda it is very much worth pondering.

— Lu Dapeng
50:24

The 1944 Henry V cut out the truth

In 1944, Laurence Olivier directed and starred in Henry V, and the opening title explicitly dedicates it to the British commandos and airborne troops. London at the time was under V1 and V2 bombardment, and the film was meant to boost morale. To that end, two passages in Shakespeare's play that were bad for "positive energy" were cut: Henry V's order to massacre several hundred French prisoners, and the closing prophecy that his son Henry VI would lose France and make England bleed. Set it against the original and you can see how the context of war once again rewrote Shakespeare into propaganda.

— Lu Dapeng
1:11:43

Kurosawa let Shakespeare shine into Asia

The power of Shakespeare's plays lies in their continuing to spread through intermediaries like Kurosawa: Kurosawa turned King Lear into the warring-states daimyo film Ran, and Macbeth into Throne of Blood, and these in turn influenced Chen Kaige's The Promise and later Hong Kong directors. Many people have never read Shakespeare but have seen The Lion King, behind which is in fact the prince's revenge of Hamlet. Lu Dapeng's metaphor is this: Shakespeare is a luminous body, Kurosawa is the moon, and the audience are the people in the moonlight.

— Lu Dapeng
