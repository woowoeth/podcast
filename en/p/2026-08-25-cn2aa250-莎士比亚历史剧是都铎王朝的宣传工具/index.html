Shakespeare's History Plays Are Tudor Propaganda

Shakespeare's history plays are full of historical distortions, essentially political propaganda for the Tudor dynasty: vilifying Richard III, avoiding sensitive topics about Henry VIII, to legitimize their rule.

Contrast is an old tradition in English literature

Lu Dapeng cites the British writer Ackroyd: something refined can be juxtaposed with something mundane, vulgar, even obscene. In Shakespeare's plays, the scene where a princess learns English mixes a girl's fear and longing for the enemy king with a barrage of sexual puns derived from French-English homophones, while also mocking the French. This 'contrast' is not just dirty jokes but a feature of traditional English literature, continuing into today's Yes Minister and Blackadder.

— Lu Dapeng
16:09

16th-century theaters were not highbrow venues

We now think of Shakespeare as high art, but in Elizabethan times, attending a play was down-to-earth entertainment: nobles, middle-class merchants, and street ruffians crowded the same theater, with vendors hawking, dogs running, chickens flapping, and even fights breaking out. So Shakespeare's plays are full of dirty jokes to capture the groundlings. Lu emphasizes that the positioning of Shakespeare's plays then was completely different from today, which explains the 'lowbrow' elements in the dramatic structure.

— Lu Dapeng
18:06

Shakespeare's originality lay in reworking source material

Almost all of Shakespeare's plots came from existing chronicles, legends, or earlier texts, but the era did not prize 'original plots'; being able to cite widely was what earned respect. What truly made him great was his ability to process 'bad material'—the English chronicles of the time were extremely dull, and only he could make them vivid. The modern literary theory of intertextuality says the same thing: there is no absolute originality; all texts are recombinations of old texts.

— Lu Dapeng
21:31

Modern English was shaped by Shakespeare and the Bible

Lu argues that the two key texts that made today's English lively and readable are Shakespeare's plays and the 1611 King James Version of the Bible. Shakespeare fixed phrases like 'a foregone conclusion,' 'with bated breath,' and 'has seen better days' into common usage; the Bible used only about 8,000 common words, making it accessible to ordinary people. Together, they kept Elizabethan English not far from today's, lowering the barrier for modern readers.

— Lu Dapeng
38:19

History plays are propaganda, not history

Shakespeare's history plays contain numerous factual errors; Lord Norwich even listed them one by one. But Lu says that approaching them with a fact-checking mindset misses the point: the purpose of history plays is political propaganda. The Tudor dynasty's legitimacy was questionable—Henry VII was a descendant of an illegitimate line who seized the throne—so the dynasty needed plays to portray Richard III of York as a 'spider' and 'hunchbacked toad,' using the contrast of 'the dark previous dynasty and the glorious present' to craft legitimacy.

— Lu Dapeng
41:17

Why Henry VIII avoided the wife-killing

Anne Boleyn was Elizabeth I's mother, and Henry VIII had her executed. Shakespeare certainly knew this, and so did the queen. So the play about Henry VIII is carefully calibrated politically: it flatters the queen and the Tudor dynasty while omitting all sensitive episodes, ending with Elizabeth's birth. Lu believes that Henry VIII is not a great play, but as a piece of political propaganda, it is very worth examining.

— Lu Dapeng
50:24

The WWII version of Henry V deleted the truth

In 1944, Laurence Olivier directed and starred in Henry V, with an opening dedication explicitly to the British Commandos and Airborne troops. London was under V1 and V2 bombardment, and the film was meant to boost morale. To that end, two parts of the play that were not 'positive energy' were cut: Henry V's order to slaughter hundreds of French prisoners, and the epilogue predicting that his son Henry VI would lose France and bleed England. Comparing the original reveals how the war context again turned Shakespeare into propaganda.

— Lu Dapeng
1:11:43

Kurosawa let Shakespeare shine on Asia

Shakespeare's power lies in continued transmission through intermediaries like Akira Kurosawa: he turned King Lear into the Sengoku-daimyo epic Ran, and Macbeth into Throne of Blood, which in turn influenced Chen Kaige's The Promise and later Hong Kong directors. Many people have never read Shakespeare but have seen The Lion King, which is actually Hamlet's revenge story. Lu's metaphor: Shakespeare is the luminous body, Kurosawa is the moon, and the audience are people under the moonlight.

— Lu Dapeng
