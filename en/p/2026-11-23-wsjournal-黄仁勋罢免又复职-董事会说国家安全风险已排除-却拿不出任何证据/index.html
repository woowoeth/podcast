Ousted and reinstated in 11 days: board claims national-security risks eliminated, produces no evidence

In ten days, Nvidia's board transformed ‘launch a national-security investigation into Jensen Huang’ into ‘no national-security risks exist’, the only confirmed variable between: Trump's direct involvement. No investigation report, no data, no public evidence.

Only an irreplaceable CEO could regain his throne

After being ousted for 11 days, Huang was reinstated. Reporter Robbie Whelan explains why this fiasco shook the world: Nvidia stock is about 6% of the S&P 500, and Huang himself is seen as an ‘imperial CEO’—his personal image is so bound to the company that Silicon Valley commonly believes ‘without him the company makes no sense.’ This personality-cult governance structure was what made the reversal possible.

— Robbie Whelan
4:09

A definitive 'no' becomes 'yes' in six days, with zero explanation

On the November 17 earnings call, the board stated clearly they had ‘no plan to reinstate Huang’—this came after Huang himself announced he was willing to return. But just six days later, the board suddenly announced unanimously that he was back. The reporter stresses the reversal happened extremely fast, and the company's only explanation was a press release saying it was satisfied with Huang's ‘candor and leadership.’

— Robbie Whelan
5:11

Trump's direct pressure, not evidence, reversed the board's decision

The reporter is direct: within hours of the announcement, one thing was certain—this was largely about Trump. Trump revealed on CNBC that he had discussed Huang's reinstatement with Nvidia's board and said he ‘strongly felt he should come back.’ According to a U.S. official, Trump also asked VP Vance to assist. Trump said Huang had been ‘exonerated.’

— Robbie Whelan
8:21

An alleged spy becomes an ambitious entrepreneur, no new evidence

One person Huang was involved with is Alice Wong, a Chinese national. Her data-center company Megaspeed is under multiple federal investigations, suspected of smuggling high-performance AI chips to China. The government had initially focused on whether she was a trained spy, but this week external descriptions of her softened to ‘possibly just an ambitious tech entrepreneur caught in a complex situation.’ But the reporter stresses: this shift wasn't based on new evidence—the narrative itself changed.

— Robbie Whelan
9:27

The internal affair was the true reason for removal, then forgotten

According to the reporter, when the board first pushed for removal, the relationship with the alleged spy was ‘tolerable’—provided Huang didn't know her identity. But his affair with a subordinate at the company was what truly crossed the line. After the reinstatement announcement, almost no one asked about it again, suggesting the internal investigation was quietly shelved.

— Robbie Whelan
10:28

Markets boom while Congress receives neither answer nor evidence

The afternoon the announcement broke, Nvidia stock rose over 30%, the company's biggest single-day gain in history, with market value returning to about four trillion dollars. Meanwhile, criticism from Washington's center-left intensified, with Senator Warren and Republican Senator Cornyn publicly questioning: on what basis does the board say national-security risks have been eliminated? Where is the evidence?

— Robbie Whelan
12:29

One person's decisions shape how the entire AI industry borrows money

The reporter points out the reinstatement won't change fundamental uncertainty in AI-industry demand, but it will immediately ease short-term lending and financing, because Huang's personal decisions and will shape how the entire industry borrows, raises capital, and invests. During his brief absence, market confidence in the future nearly collapsed; as soon as he returned, ‘the game restarted.’

— Robbie Whelan
13:29

AI-company governance is so weak that outside pressure can override boards

The reporter compares this to OpenAI's previous ouster and swift reinstatement of Sam Altman, arguing it shows that tech, especially AI, has extremely weak corporate governance: board decisions can be ‘steamrolled’ by external forces—if enough people unite against a removal, it can be overturned. He thinks Wall Street worries this governance model is becoming normal.
