The Viking Age is only the finale: a thousand five hundred years of Nordic Iron Age have been severely underestimated

The Viking Age is commonly treated as the starting point of Nordic history, but researchers argue it was actually the product of a thousand five hundred years of Iron Age evolution reaching a pressure point, not a brilliant beginning emerging from nowhere.

If all you know about are Viking raids and longships, this episode rewinds the timeline fifteen hundred years to explain how iron, contact with Rome, and climate catastrophe each step by step laid the groundwork for the Viking Age.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:09

The Viking Age's start date was a story invented by later people

When asked who the first Vikings were, Marianne answered with a slide from her teacher Neil Price's lecture: it showed warriors and a Viking ship with a dialogue box saying ‘Come on, boys, or you'll miss the Viking Age.’ The irony is that Vikings themselves had no idea they were living in a ‘Viking Age.’ She further notes that the traditional origin of 793 and the Lindisfarne raid is likely inaccurate, the true beginning may have been earlier, and the label itself is retroactively imposed by later historians.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
8:16

Iron broke the ancient monopoly on prestige because it could be mined locally

At the end of the Bronze Age, elites controlling long-distance copper and tin trade networks lost power as those networks collapsed: burial mounds vanished, house sizes equalized, great halls disappeared. She explains this followed iron's introduction—bronze required cross-regional networks for raw materials, but iron could be mined and smelted in place, allowing communities to become self-sufficient and no longer dependent on elite-controlled luxury goods. In archaeological terms, iron became known as ‘the great equalizer,’ though full-scale iron production still lay centuries ahead.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
12:25

Settlements were abandoned after each generation because private land ownership did not yet exist

In the pre-Roman Iron Age, houses appear to have been occupied for a single generation, then abandoned while people moved elsewhere to build new homes rather than inheriting and maintaining ancestral plots as they later would. Marianne interprets this as evidence that land was collectively redistributed, suggesting private property and heritable rights did not yet exist. She also notes the anarchist theory now popular in archaeology, questioning whether modern scholars are too eager to locate ‘chieftains’ everywhere, when these societies may actually have been more collective and more egalitarian.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
25:57

A single feast could bind warriors through permanent unpayable social debt

Rome's influence reached far beyond its military borders: Scandinavian elites began importing Roman drinking vessels and feasting culture, building ‘halls’ controlled by elites purely for banquets. Drawing on anthropologist Michael Dietler's theory, Marianne explains that feasting works by making guests internalize the ‘gift’ by consuming it, converting an eternally unpayable social obligation into loyalty. A minor chieftain who could host a lavish feast could bind warriors to himself.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
46:15

A defeated naval army ended up in the bog

The Hjortspring site shows a Norwegian fleet of hundreds to approximately one thousand warriors who sailed to Jutland, were defeated, and had every weapon bent and broken before being sunk in a bog as ritual ‘execution.’ More striking is Alken Enge from the first century CE: the first site where human remains appeared alongside weapon deposits, with bone evidence showing these warriors lay on the battlefield for at least six months without animal gnawing or weathering, then were collected and bundled (pelvic bones even strung on sticks) and deposited into wetland—like a mass funeral delayed half a year to a year after battle.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
56:55

A year of labor by two women is hidden behind every Viking sail

The key technology enabling Viking longship voyages was the mast and sail, but sailmaking required labor vastly underestimated: spinning the raw wool fibers for just one sail took two full-time spinners working continuously for one year. Since such labor was historically assigned to women, the historical record remembers the shipbuilders but almost never asks who was working in dark, smoky sunken houses completing the spinning—almost certainly enslaved people in the Viking Age, the coerced labor force.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
1:01:00

The year 536 broke Northern Europe's economy and kept it broken for generations

At the Migration Period's end came a pivotal event: in 536 CE, almost certainly from a volcanic eruption (confirmed by Greenlandic ice cores), sunlight was blocked for an extended period, crops failed without photosynthesis, and Justinian's plague immediately followed. Archaeological records show settlement and burial counts plummeted, population crashed sharply, and society took decades to recover. This stagnation also created conditions for the later re-concentration of power.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson
1:06:22

Obsession with the Viking Age obscures far richer historical stories

Despite holding the title ‘Professor of Viking Studies,’ Marianne directly criticizes how much scholarly attention the Viking Age receives: it is a compelling narrative—peripheral people conquering their way to Newfoundland and the Abbasid Caliphate—but it has been over-romanticized and widely misunderstood. By contrast, the earlier Iron Age—bodies in bogs with meal still in their mouths and rope around their necks, braids left as sacrifices, that sunless winter of 536—contains equally gripping stories that vanish under the ‘pre-Viking’ label.

— Marianne Hem-Eriksson

Glossary

Pre-Roman Iron Age
Roughly 500 BCE to 1 CE, the period between Bronze Age network collapse and Roman contact with Scandinavia.
Migration Period
The era of large-scale Germanic tribal migrations and pressure against Roman imperial borders.
Vendel Period
The phase after the Migration Period and before the Viking Age, characterized by re-concentration of power; also called the Merovingian period.
bog bodies
Human remains deposited in bogs and preserved in exceptional condition by the anaerobic environment.
wandering settlements
Settlement patterns where houses were abandoned and rebuilt elsewhere after one generation rather than continuously occupied by successive generations.
