Anthropic's Q3 slowdown was compute-constrained, not demand-constrained

The S-1 reveals Q3 had to fill half its compute purchases with a $500M high-cost spot market—long-term capacity was fully booked, not because customer demand for tokens dried up.

Existential risks in passive voice: hedging for both audiences

Zvi notes that Anthropic's S-1 discussion of existential risk uses only passive voice and glosses over it—attempting to tell two incompatible stories at once: ‘we're a highly profitable compute supercomputer company’ to investors, while elsewhere claiming ‘what we built could kill everyone.’ Lawyers instinctively minimize disclosure since S-1 filings become evidence in future litigation. But Zvi argues the silence is itself revealing: if you genuinely believed your stated existential risks, choosing to stay quiet in the document where you should be most transparent says something about who you actually are.

— Zvi Mowshowitz
8:26

Three founders can maintain company control indefinitely if they stay

Anthropic created a Class F shareholder class pooling founder votes with 50.1% voting control on broad corporate matters. The company has roughly seven formal founders, but this super-voting block persists as long as just three remain employed—sufficient, by Zvi's estimate, to outlast ‘the point when the dust settles on the world's fate.’ The company also chartered a long-term giving trust nominally controlling four board seats: the board selects three, the trust selects three, and a seventh seat requires founder approval or else goes to Class E shareholders (employees who've donated stock to charity), who are assumed to align with the founders.

— Zvi Mowshowitz
11:33

Uncapped 3:1 giving match may be capitalism's costliest employee perk

The founders committed to donating 80% of their personal shares to charity, coupled with an employee giving-match program offering 3:1 matching without limit—unprecedented in capitalism's history. Standard companies offer 1:1 matching capped at a few thousand dollars. Anthropic employees can donate $20M to charity and trigger a $60M company match. With thousands of employees holding nine-figure equity stakes, this may become one of capitalism's single most expensive unilateral decisions. The Q3 non-cash charge of ~$24B in unattributed equity compensation hints at what's coming, and Zvi expects it won't be the last.

22:25

Q3's slowdown was compute-constrained, not demand-constrained

Analysts widely interpreted Anthropic's Q3 revenue slowdown as evidence demand has plateaued. Patrick's retrospective reading is opposite: the S-1 makes plain the company faces chronic compute constraints, with procurement requiring 12–18 months lead time—meaning they had to commit in early 2025 to compute volumes for models not yet released. The smoking gun: they spent $5B on spot-market compute that quarter, representing fully half their compute spending, well above normal—signaling long-term capacity was fully booked and customer token demand remained queued.

— Patrick McKenzie
31:56

$141B raised, ~$10B burned, yet carrying ~$800B in compute commitments

Anthropic raised $141B cumulatively and retains $131B in cash and investments; actual cash consumption totals roughly $10B. Hundreds of billions in reported losses stem mainly from mark-to-market losses on convertible debt as stock price climbed—Zvi frames this as pure accounting convention, not real cash hemorrhage. The material number is the ~$800B in forward compute commitments on the balance sheet—effectively spreading years of massive future spending across current-quarter reports, structurally similar to a massive real-estate lease obligation.

— Zvi Mowshowitz
46:49

Wall Street and tech circles price the same company on completely different books

Wall Street values Anthropic as ‘a very impressive software company.’ Tech insiders want to shake Wall Street awake and say: Google's entire commercial history might be merely this business's bootloader. Google would exhaust its entire free cash flow, max out its credit lines, just to own this franchise—not just to defend search from disintermediation, but because this business exceeds every business in capitalist history. Investment banks haven't yet priced that thesis into their DCF models. Zvi thinks that omission is precisely where a credible bear case finds its foundation.

— Patrick McKenzie
59:44

No one taught it Japanese—the model figured it out on its own

Patrick witnessed a research lab demo an unreleased model that had explicitly not been trained on Japanese, with zero in-house Japanese training data involved. Yet the researchers suspected the model had reverse-engineered Japanese grammar and structure from raw English-language internet content alone. Patrick tested it on a CNN news segment the training data couldn't have seen, and—as someone with 20 years' experience as a professional translator—said the model's Japanese might surpass his own. This exemplifies the Bitter Lesson: scale up the model and unexpected capabilities emerge that nobody trained it to have.

— Patrick McKenzie
1:12:02

Binance lost $5B to hackers; the industry's security debt runs $500B

Hours before recording, North Korean hackers breached Binance for over $5B. Patrick argues that the AI-enabled hacking wave won't primarily wield research-lab models themselves, but rather open-source weights distilled from those labs. He estimates the industry's total security technical debt at roughly $500B (priced at pre-AI rates), a sum payable only by betting everything on AI-powered defense. Zvi's outlook is darker: this isn't a one-time invoice because any operator can now parallelize attack probes; vulnerability discovery will compound.
