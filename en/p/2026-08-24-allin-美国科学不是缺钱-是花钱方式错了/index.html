American Science Doesn't Lack Money — It Spends It Wrong

The NIH budget rose from $14 billion in 1998 to $47 billion in 2024, yet output efficiency halves every 9 years. Kratsios's answer: the problem isn't the budget number, it's the funding mechanism itself.

13:45

The NIH budget tripled while output efficiency halved every nine years

Kratsios cites Eroom's Law: the NIH budget was $14 billion in 1998, $27 billion in 2003, and $47 billion in 2024, but breakthrough therapies have not grown proportionally — output efficiency per dollar has fallen roughly 80-fold since 1950 and halves again every 9 years. His explanation is not that "the easy problems have all been solved," but that the funding mechanism has never been innovated — "the answer has always been to keep doing the same thing, just with more money, and then expect output to rise proportionally." His example is flight speed: in the 1990s you could fly Concorde; now it's slower.

— Michael Kratsios
17:08

Golden tickets: let a single reviewer decide unilaterally

NSF's current peer review has three or four reviewers in the same field collectively pick the projects "most worthy of funding," with the result that scientists only propose safe ideas that land in the "strike zone." Kratsios says he wants to pilot golden tickets at NSF: each reviewer gets one to three tickets and can unilaterally select a project without committee approval. The mechanism was first tested in places like Denmark. A side benefit is attracting better reviewers to participate — because they hold that ticket. At the same time he wants to test grants of different durations: NSF grants today are almost all 18 months, but some ideas can be validated in 3 months or 6 months, and some need 5 years.

— Michael Kratsios
20:11

Fund people rather than projects

Kratsios endorses the venture logic: find the right person, give them far more money than they applied for and a very long time horizon, and don't watch how every dollar is spent. He points to NSF's GRFP fellowship — awarded each year to roughly 2,600 of the best prospective PhD students, with the money fully portable, so students can take it to any school and make universities compete for them. The other path is big projects: the Manhattan Project, Apollo, the Human Genome Project. He acknowledges China is very good at big projects under a central-planning system, but argues the U.S. needs to restore that capability, not do only that.

— Michael Kratsios
23:12

A quantum computer by 2028, fusion by 2035

Kratsios lists this administration's big bets: put Americans back on the Moon by 2028, place a nuclear reactor in space, and build the first components of a lunar base by 2030; last month the President signed an executive order launching a new national quantum initiative, with the goal of producing a "scientifically relevant" quantum computer by 2028, directed to the Department of Energy; fusion is targeted for 2035. The flagship program is called the Genesis Mission, whose core is using AI to double America's scientific output. He states explicitly that the government will not compete with private companies on AI compute, but will get scientists doing basic research to think about how to use AI to accelerate their own work.

— Michael Kratsios
27:12

Fragmented agencies are a feature, not a bug

Some argue for merging the science agencies into a single department; Kratsios is opposed. His evidence is China: since the U.S. imposed export controls in 2019, China has been grinding away at EUV lithography without a breakthrough — "nothing is more important to their economy than solving this scientific problem, and they have not been able to do it." He argues that the competition among agencies in the U.S. system and its free-market-style innovation paths are "a feature, not a bug," and that having DARPA people sit inside the Defense Department rather than in some science agency actually makes their work fit their mission better.

— Michael Kratsios
30:13

Vannevar Bush's model is seventy years out of date

Vannevar Bush's 1945 reply to Roosevelt, Science: The Endless Frontier, established the current system: government pays, universities do basic research. Kratsios points out that at the time roughly 70% of R&D was funded by the federal government and 30% by private companies; seventy years later it has completely inverted — now roughly 70% of R&D is done by private companies and 30% funded by the federal government. At the same time, philanthropically funded focused research organizations like Howard Hughes and Max Planck have emerged. His conclusion is that Bush's model "is not as applicable today as it was then, and needs refreshing." The new first principle: government should only pay for what private companies and philanthropy have no incentive to do.

— Michael Kratsios
38:20

Seven in ten STEM PhDs are not American

Kratsios cites a number he has tracked for years: in computer science, thirty years ago about 70% of PhDs were Americans and 30% foreign; now it has completely inverted. He says the figure that "seven in ten STEM PhD candidates are not American" should alarm everyone. He notes that NSF once had programs funding gifted American high school and middle school students, which were later shut down, and he sees this as part of the DEI wave — San Francisco dropping advanced algebra in high school math and the University of California system's fight over the SAT are the same kind of phenomenon. He argues for rewarding America's most talented students again.

— Michael Kratsios
45:27

Fauci has done more damage to science than anyone in modern times

Kratsios traces the collapse of trust in science to the COVID period: telling students to wear masks in school while the pediatric association sent a letter saying they could protest in crowds but not visit a dying grandmother in the hospital — "any median ordinary American hearing that can no longer take science seriously." He believes Fauci "has done more damage to science than anyone in modern history," and says it will take a long time to repair. The solution he keeps returning to is a return to "gold-standard science": science is a process in which everyone participates, constantly experimenting and learning, not dogma handed down from on high.

Drug development efficiency halves every 9 years, the opposite direction from Moore's Law.
