The $300 billion structured notes market is quietly amplifying US stock declines

When worst-of auto-callable notes linked to the S&P, Nasdaq, and Russell indexes stop knocking out on schedule, dealers are forced to dump volatility exposure simultaneously, just as market-makers willing to buy it back are shrinking.

Structured notes are a hidden technical current in the market

The episode opens by positioning structured notes as a technical flow separate from fundamental narratives—whether AI stocks should rise is one question, but what actually drives near-term prices is the forced hedging action when derivatives positions like structured notes expire or knock out. The hosts mention Nvidia's credit default swaps recently ticked slightly higher, a sign of market ‘high-frequency tremors’; this is why they dedicated an entire episode to structured notes.

Essentially, a child of bank deposits and derivatives

Alex defines it this way: you give the issuer your money, forgo interest, and take on their credit risk; the bank then uses the ‘forgone interest’ to buy and sell options, customizing the return shape you want—bullish participation, downside protection, or the more common ‘yield’ structure, where the bank sells you a put option and wraps the premium into coupon payments. Three approaches can be freely combined; the bank is like a chef, cutting the same option ingredients into different products for different clients.

US structured notes hit $300 billion this year

SEC-registered structured notes in the US are on track to reach roughly $300 billion in issuance this year, versus about $200 billion last year. The fastest-growing category is worst-of auto-callable notes linked to the S&P, Nasdaq, and Russell indexes. The reason: Russell has basically flatlined for years, but since last year's ‘liberation day’ it's been climbing, causing notes that should have knocked out repeatedly to keep extending, their size rolling larger.

— Alex Michelizzi
21:50

Falling markets force dealers to dump volatility exposure all at once

When markets climb early in the year, notes knock out frequently and roll forward; dealers' hedge positions self-clear in a virtuous cycle. But once markets turn down, notes that fail to knock out start piling up, dealers' vega exposure (sensitivity to implied volatility) balloons, and just then the off-market counterparties who had been hedging pull back because their own portfolios are shrinking—fewer buyers step up. This is the ‘spot up, vol up’ pattern Charlie McElligot and Nomura colleagues observe playing out in the structured notes market.

— Alex Michelizzi
26:55

Single-stock notes use daily observation, flipping exposure on and off

Tickets linked to individual stocks pursue higher coupons by adding ‘conditional coupons’ (skip payment if the stock falls too far) and daily-observation knockout features, letting the product terminate almost any day after an observation window. The result is that banks' risk exposure becomes extremely binary: once a stock bounces, knockout fires instantly, vega erases; once it falls, the bank instantly holds a full short put position. Markets thus see a ‘either have vega or have none’ on-off pattern instead of smooth change.

— Alex Michelizzi
38:06

Two-year notes can be forced to extend to five

Worst-of notes typical in US markets carry nominal 3-to-5-year terms but dealers hedge on a 2-year expected life. Once indexes fall and notes refuse to knock out, actual duration can stretch to five years; dealers' hedges, built for 2 years, suddenly have to cover an extra three years of vega exposure. Conversely, if an index then rallies back near all-time highs, dealers find they still hold 5-year hedge shorts, but the note knocked out in three months—this structure is called ‘peak vega’ internally.

— Alex Michelizzi
44:17

One stock's early surge knocks out entire basket notes instantly

Alex highlights what he calls the most creative design: a three-stock basket note where if any one stock rises 20% on day one, no matter how the other two perform, the product knocks out immediately. If the leader then crashes while the note already knocked out, that deep-in-the-money put position evaporates—the bank thus holds a position extremely sensitive to ‘vol of vol’ (volatility of volatility): the moment all three stocks rise, risk disappears; the moment one lags, the bank faces a major short put that can vanish any time.

— Alex Michelizzi
45:20

2018's lessons spawned a new generation of product designs

Alex notes that innovation in structure often starts from real past losses. In 2018, a batch of dealers took heavy losses in ‘slow grinding down’ conditions—not a crash or a non-event, but a modest decline that never fully compressed, so hedges kept misfiring. The market then designed new-type notes to handle that middle ground—‘fall a little but not through, knock out even if stopping in a zone’—but new designs bring new second-order problems.

Glossary

Vega
An option's sensitivity to changes in implied volatility.
Gamma
A hedged position's sensitivity to the speed of price changes, determining urgency to chase gains or cut losses.
Auto-callable
A structured note that terminates early and pays a coupon if on an observation date the index exceeds the starting level.
Worst-of
A structure where payoff or loss depends on the worst performer in a basket of underlyings.
Knock-in/Knock-out
Clauses that trigger or terminate a feature only when price hits a preset level.
Uridashi
Japanese offshore fundraising structured notes, a high-yield category that emerged after Japanese interest rates hit zero.
