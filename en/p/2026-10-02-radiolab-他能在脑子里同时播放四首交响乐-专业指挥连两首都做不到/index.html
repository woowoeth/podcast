He can hold four symphonies in mind at once; professional conductors can't manage two

Neuroscientists using fMRI verified: a professional conductor fails trying to hold two pieces simultaneously, while this ragtime pianist reports the exact note of each of four symphonies in real time

7:14

He plays complex pieces while speaking, despite both using the same brain region

Neurologist Kirsten Betterman explains that for most people, the brain regions responsible for speech and complex piano playing are the same, which means theoretically they should interfere with each other. But Bob Milne can play extremely complex ragtime while having normal conversations, even telling jokes—which is precisely what made the neurologist find him unusual and worth deeper study.

— Kirsten Betterman
8:15

He reports holding two complete symphonies simultaneously, with independent volume control

Bob tells Kirsten that he can "play" two entire symphonies in his head, with every instrument's part present, and he can make one louder while the other is quieter—the two run continuously in sync, not switching attention back and forth, but genuinely existing in parallel in his mind.

— Bob Milne
8:15

Four simultaneous symphonies are possible in his brain, skeptics say it's just noise

When the researcher pushed further, Bob said he could even do four, though he's never tried three. Some people on the show immediately objected, saying it's "pure noise, just chaos," that handling four symphonies simultaneously couldn't possibly avoid confusion—this skepticism prompted the team to design an experiment to verify whether his claim was true.

11:22

He can mentally play four symphonies and identify the exact note when stopped

The experiment put Bob and professional conductor Peter Perret in scanners separately. They memorized four symphonies with different keys, tempos, and orchestrations (Schubert, Brahms, Beethoven, Mendelssohn). The test had them close their eyes and "play" mentally while researchers followed the original recording with a stopwatch, calling out "stop" at random and asking the subject to say which phrase the piece had reached, then playing back to verify timing and note accuracy.

— Kirsten Betterman
12:23

Professional conductors fail at two simultaneous pieces; he nails four with single-note precision

On single pieces both performed well: the conductor's timing error was within one second, Bob accurate to one or two notes. But once the "play second piece simultaneously" phase began, the conductor completely failed, describing it as "an impossible task," and lost track of the first piece too. Bob was then asked to add pieces up to four, and when asked afterward which note each symphony was on, he was found to have nailed the exact note on every single piece.

— Kirsten Betterman
15:30

Each musical key carries a fixed emotional tone, enabling his exceptional memory

One explanation Bob offers is related to emotion: for him each key connects to a specific emotional experience. C major is "bland as drinking broth," D major "bright enough to make you want to dance"—even though he doesn't actually dance. The neurologist theorizes that this unusually intense emotional connection is what burns music deeper into his memory, so he doesn't need to laboriously track it but instead directly "lives inside" the music.

— Bob Milne
18:32

He visualizes complete 3D orchestras in his mind, with adjustable perspective and volume

Another explanation involves spatial imagination. Bob says he can "see" two orchestras side-by-side in his head, distinguished by different colors and lighting; when listening he can "fly" like a camera above the orchestra, zoom in on a specific instrument, and the volume changes with his "position"—flying near the bass makes it louder, switching to the violins makes them clearer, and he can even see the creases in the musicians' tuxedos.

— Bob Milne
20:36

Recorded music feels weaker to him than his internal mental performances

When asked if he enjoys listening to recordings, Bob says he completely can't stand CDs because recorded music produces far weaker sensations than when he imagines it in his head. The host joked that hearing his description is like watching someone get high. This confirms what both previous theories point to: for Bob, music's true location is in his own brain, not his ears.

— Bob Milne
