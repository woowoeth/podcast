Pentagon's new command would slow autonomous weapons procurement, not speed it

Critics argue that centralizing autonomous weapons acquisition requires building another four-star command with hundreds of staff—more hierarchy means slower decisions, not faster ones.

3:02

Establishing a new command assumes Congress will maintain long-term budget support

The Secretary of Defense issued a memo requiring a pathway to a four-star combatant command (tentatively named Project Agincourt / Autonomous Warfare Execution) equivalent to Central Command and Strategic Command, with independent budget authority and congressional authorization, specifically for rapid autonomous weapons procurement. The assumption: multiple future Congresses stay aligned. Reality: the House could change hands within months.

— Justin
4:07

More bureaucratic layers slow procurement despite centralized administration

Today regional commanders cannot directly procure equipment but can quickly field new systems to front-line units and report feedback back up through the Pentagon. Placing this process under a new command requires first building an entire four-star staff structure—Central Command at its peak maintained roughly 800 core personnel; it still has over 400 today. Historical record shows deeper hierarchies produce slower military decisions, not faster ones.

— Justin
5:10

Cyber Command's clearinghouse model offers a lower-risk structural alternative

Some propose a gentler approach: the new command need not own equipment and budget. Instead, model it on Cyber Command or Strategic Command—individual services continue producing autonomous systems units, and the new command consolidates these units and distributes them to regional combatant commands to address specific battlefield challenges, functioning as a clearinghouse. This structure carries lower risk. The catch is that once operational, dismantling it becomes politically difficult.

— Brian
10:33

Autonomous weapons command naming could provoke unexpected political backlash

The greatest risk may not be technical or architectural but rhetorical. Autonomous weapons currently occupy relatively quiet political space in the United States—not yet a focal attack point. Policies like Section 3000.09 advanced autonomous systems without triggering controversy in recent years. Formal branding and naming could transform it into social-media meme and ready ammunition for populist politicians seeking targets. The name itself becomes the vulnerability.

26:01

The real goal is recovering discretionary budget power Congress had removed

The true motivation is reconstructing the ‘Overseas Contingency Operations’ (OCO) fund—budget the Secretary of Defense could direct without pre-approved congressional itemization. At its peak, OCO represented roughly 28% of the defense budget. That funding enabled the Pentagon to rush MRAP vehicles into Iraq within months. Congress eliminated OCO after 2022 precisely to deny the executive branch this kind of unilateral spending power. Rebranding the command structure may not recover that authority.

— Justin
34:35

Putting Musk and Luckey in an advisory role raises obvious conflicts of interest

Project Meridian assembled outside experts to assess future warfare. The four-member panel: Emil Michael (Deputy for Research and Engineering), Elon Musk, Palmer Luckey (Anduril founder), and Newt Gingrich. Question: why this group when the Pentagon already operates the Defense Science Board, CAPE, and ONA for identical work? Deeper problem: interest conflicts. If the review concludes future wars require low-orbit reusable rockets for battlefield resupply, observers assume Musk is writing his own contracts. If it mandates Anduril plus Starlink for command-and-control, the same charge follows. Once decision-makers sit at the table, the credibility of their advice is already compromised by their position.

— Arthur
40:47

Strategic advisors with financial stakes in recommendations lose external credibility

One speaker invoked Caesar's wife, who must be above reproach: once you occupy a decision-making seat, your counsel itself becomes suspect. The conflict persists whether or not influence was actually exercised. Outside observers cannot parse genuine strategic insight from self-interested counsel. Even with cleanest intent, the structure poisons trust.

49:53

Pentagon's real lesson from Middle East wars is industrial capacity, not base locations

Responding to analyses claiming Middle East conflicts overturn all U.S. Pacific strategy assumptions: any serious Pentagon planner already assumes theater bases will fall in hot war. What remains untreated is domestic industrial capacity, power grids, ship-loading infrastructure, and ammunition and air-defense gaps exposed in European combat. Ukraine's ‘Spider Web’ operation—striking Russian strategic bomber bases with drones—proved that given sufficient time and resources, no base location survives. This lesson the Pentagon has still not genuinely absorbed.

Glossary

Autonomous Warfare Command (AutoWarCom)
Proposed four-star unified command to coordinate autonomous weapons procurement and deployment.
Overseas Contingency Operations funds (OCO)
Defense budget authority the Secretary of Defense could direct without prior congressional approval, available during counterterrorism wars.
MRAP (Mine-Resistant Ambush-Protected vehicle)
Heavy armored vehicle rapidly produced in Iraq using OCO funds for emergency deployment against roadside bombs.
Airborne Moving Target Indicator (AMTI)
Radar technology detecting ground and airborne moving targets, potentially defeating stealth aircraft.
Functional combatant command
Unified command like Cyber Command integrating forces across military branches for specific missions without geographic territorial control.
Project Meridian
Pentagon's internal strategic review inviting business leaders to assess future warfare.
