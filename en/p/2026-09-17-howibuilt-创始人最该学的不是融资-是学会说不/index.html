The thing founders should learn isn't fundraising, it's how to say no

Beyond Yoga founder Michelle Wahler says you don't have to raise money or plan an exit — just build a business that sustains itself. Her husband stopped her from making swimwear and forced her to perfect one pair of leggings first.

All three callers' problems are concrete: conversion for adult study-abroad trips, a craftsman winning back old clients, a small brand selling emotion online. The answers are concrete too, not chicken soup.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:06

You don't have to raise, and you don't have to plan an exit

Michelle says the advice entrepreneurs hear most often is ‘you have to raise’, and she thinks that's BS. Her logic: once you bring in investors, the exit becomes the company's focus and the company's trajectory changes with it. She suggests first figuring out what drives you and what you want the business to become, then building a sustainable business instead of staring at an exit. She adds: you don't have to be the next Anthropic, and you probably won't be, but there is nothing wrong with a smaller business that supports you and gives you freedom in your life.

— Michelle Wahler
9:17

Screen people first, then let them book a call

Carrie's adult study-abroad courses average $6,200. Plenty of people express interest, open the emails, book calls, but they just don't enroll — deadlines and discounts don't help. Michelle's suggestion is to add a few screening questions before ‘book a call’: Are you ready for a seven-day immersive experience? Are you willing to spend money on this once-in-a-lifetime investment in yourself? Are you a lifelong learner? If every answer is an enthusiastic yes, then we'll talk. That filters out the calls that were never going to convert.

— Michelle Wahler
10:20

No Eiffel Tower, and she loses people instead

Carrie explains why the site doesn't show the Eiffel Tower: the travel market is crowded, and she wants to emphasize that this is an educational experience, a community of lifelong learners, not sightseeing. Michelle pushes back: the first two images on the site are a group of people looking at a map and a group of people in a classroom — images that could be anywhere, when you should show people at a glance the thing that exists only here. Guy adds another layer: what actually stops people from hitting the enroll button is the nervousness of ‘I won't know a single person’, so sell the cohort — eight days with ten equally curious people — and put up testimonials like ‘I was nervous at first too, I almost didn't go’.

— Michelle Wahler
13:31

Sign up four or five at a time at retirement communities

Michelle offers a channel idea: partner with high-end bespoke travel agencies, or with high-end independent senior living communities. She uses her own 79-year-old mother as an example: her mother lives in an independent living community, surrounded by retired people who are all looking for something exciting to do, but who are afraid to go alone. Partner with communities like that and you might get four or five people signing up together at once — a great top of funnel. Guy also suggests counterintuitively trying print media, like the New York Review of Books or the London Review of Books: spend a thousand dollars on a small ad, and the readers are exactly people over fifty with obvious intellectual curiosity.

— Michelle Wahler
22:53

Fix the process first, then win back old clients one by one

Grace makes custom upholstered furniture. When she started in 2021 she was flooded with demand, promised timelines too fast, and left some interior designers embarrassed in front of their clients. Michelle says the relationships can be rebuilt, but only if you get operations in order first — if you fail to deliver a second time, the relationship is finished for good. The method is to make a list and go one at a time, starting with the smallest client, learning as you go and working upward, and not contacting the second until you've won back the first. Guy calls it the ‘second first impression’: don't open by asking someone to trust you again, but let them see what you're making now and earn a small project first.

— Michelle Wahler
27:02

Build buffer into your quotes and deliver early

Michelle's concrete approach for Grace: first time your own process like an assembly line to learn your real capacity, then deliberately add buffer to the delivery expectations you give designers — enough buffer, but not so much that it scares them off. That way you work toward a target of two weeks early; if you really do finish two weeks early, call the designer and say ‘we can deliver early, or we can hold it for you’, which is a huge plus. And if the budget was miscalculated and you really need a few extra days, that two-week buffer absorbs it. Either way you win.

— Michelle Wahler
33:15

What the site lacks isn't design, it's story

Angel makes handmade soap with a stone hidden inside that corresponds to an emotion, paired with an affirmation. At markets everyone says it's great, but online it doesn't sell. Michelle says she opened the site and couldn't find the story — even the about page was empty when you clicked in — and that's the first thing to fix today. She also points out that every product is called a ‘self-care kit’, when it should just say rose quartz, tiger eye, with a note next to it saying whether that stone stands for love or energy, instead of making people click into each one. And this is a perfect gifting scenario: the copy can extend from ‘a moment for yourself’ to ‘for someone who deserves a moment more’.

— Michelle Wahler
35:23

You're selling a ritual, not soap

Guy uses the Calgon ad he remembers from childhood as an analogy: a woman washing her hair in the bathtub, then the shot cuts to the Caribbean. He says what Angel is really selling is that ritual — wherever you are, these five minutes of product can take you away — and that costs nothing, it just requires changing how you say things on the site and then using that to tell the story across every channel. Michelle adds the conversion formula: explain the ingredients and build trust, tell the story, show the product — all three have to be true at once for the sale to happen, because this is something you put on your skin.

Glossary

A group of people who take a course or travel together in the same session; here, the group of ten traveling together for eight days.

Guy's phrase for re-approaching an old client not with an apology but by showing new work and rebuilding the impression.

The rule Michelle keeps stressing: leave room when you promise, exceed expectations when you deliver.
