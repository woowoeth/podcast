Respectful Parenting: Janet Lansbury Unruffled 2026-10-27

When children bargain during transitions, they're asking you to take charge, not challenge authority

Repeated negotiation, hiding, and stalling at transition time isn't defiance—it's a child overwhelmed by the moment, calling for parental rescue. The answer is to take control, not open debate.

Labels like ‘terrible twos’ and ‘three-nager’ create hostility toward children

These labels plant images of wild beasts or rebellious teenagers in parents' minds, making them treat the child as a scheming opponent rather than a small child doing age-appropriate things. Once parents see clearly what's actually happening, they can feel compassion for where the child is stuck instead of treating them like an enemy more frightening and mature than they actually are.

— Janet Lansbury
10:13

Resistance to transitions like ‘time to leave’ shows struggle, not defiance

When the child resists ‘time to leave’ or ‘time to get dressed’, Janet explains this is classic transition difficulty. Around age three, children are in a huge developmental transition and naturally struggle to stay balanced. At this age, the healthy default is to say no first—even to something they actually want. The mother is asking for cooperation at the exact moment the child cannot give it, and separation anxiety may make this small morning transition the flash point for all other emotions.

— Janet Lansbury
13:27

Negotiating for ‘four more minutes’ means parents surrendered their leadership

When the child asks to ‘tell Alexa for four more minutes’ and the mother agrees, she has essentially handed leadership to the child. Yet transitions are exactly when children most need parents to move them from A to B with confident momentum—not space for negotiation. Negotiation belongs in calm conversation the night before about how tomorrow could go better, never during the transition itself. The child's behavior is telling you clearly: he can't handle it, so he cannot be in charge.

— Janet Lansbury
16:37

Stop asking ‘want to go to the changing table’; just lead him there

When the child hides in response to choices, stop offering them. Instead, place your hand on his back and confidently say ‘now we're changing your diaper’, with your other hand ready to hold his arm or shoulder if he tries to run. This isn't force—it's the parent playing the hero role with certainty, directly refusing his requests to choose or delay. This is actually more loving than letting him be defeated over and over, exhausting you both.

— Janet Lansbury
18:49

Repeated embraces back-and-forth exhaust everyone; learn to decline once

Before leaving, the child resists hugs and kisses, then cries for them in the car. Each time the mother goes back, and after three or four rounds the departure is badly delayed. Janet explains the mother fears the child won't feel loved if she doesn't go back, but this isn't logical. Better: when it's time and the child says no, say ‘okay, sweetie, I'll hug you when I pick you up’ and let him be angry and release his feelings. He'll feel relieved because mom took on the leadership role he actually needed.

— Janet Lansbury
21:58

Separation sensitivity isn't healed by avoidance but by facing it directly

The mother worries the child doesn't feel secure about separation, but Janet says this is common, especially in adopted children—even those adopted at birth have experienced separation. Healing doesn't come from tiptoeing around it or feeling guilty about necessary separations. It comes from facing separation with confidence in yourself and your child, while leaving space for them to express any emotion. Children are actually very skilled at self-healing—each separation occasion brings a little more recovery.

— Janet Lansbury
22:58

When the child tells his stuffed monkey ‘look, I'm back’, he's self-healing

When the child greets the mother with ‘mommy, you came back’ or tells his stuffed monkey ‘look, I came back’, Janet sees this not as insecurity but as self-healing. The child is processing separation through play—which is how children naturally and beautifully heal themselves. This is what therapists use games to do, yet it's rare to see it this clearly.

— Janet Lansbury
23:59

Toilet training is the child's domain; parents' boundary is no pull-up option

Bowel control itself is the child's domain—something parents cannot and should not control—yet the child still needs boundaries. The boundary is that the parent must see the child isn't ready yet, so shouldn't offer underwear as an option. This prevents turning it into another battlefield where both become frustrated. Janet also cites listener feedback that breaks toilet learning into three dimensions—physical (bladder flow), emotional (feeling part of yourself flushed away: separation), and cognitive (understanding how they intertwine)—stressing that the emotional dimension is separation itself.

— Janet Lansbury
