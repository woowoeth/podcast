Said brought the question of Palestine into American mainstream discourse, but never saw the reconciliation he hoped for

Said spent his life trying to make the West understand Palestinian existence through the language of literature and music, but in his final years he witnessed increasing death after the Oslo Accord was signed, and concluded that the two-state solution had been rendered moot by reality.

The Six Day War transformed an apolitical literary scholar into a political actor

Said's students and biographers agree that before the 1967 Six Day War he was essentially an apolitical literary professor. Those around him knew he was Palestinian, but he had not yet truly expressed it publicly. After Israel occupied the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, Sinai, and the Golan Heights in six days, Said described his world as shattered, and began traveling throughout the Middle East to understand the perspectives of Jordanians, Lebanese, and Israelis. This defeat marked his transformation from literary scholar to public political spokesperson.

18:23

His overnight translation of Arafat's speech won sympathy from the United Nations

In 1974, 48 hours before Arafat's first UN speech, organizers found Said to translate Mahmoud Darwish's Arabic speech into English. Said and an editor worked through the night, with Said doing the typing himself (because, as he said, 'nobody types but me'). They finished at three in the morning, and the speech reached the assembly on time. It was this English translation that made 'Do not let the olive branch fall from my hand' into a sentence quoted worldwide, and marked Said's first systematic engagement in the work of 'explaining Palestine to the West.'

21:28

Western scholarship's representations created an imaginary East that never existed

Said's 1978 Orientalism argues that 19th-century French and British scholarship on 'the East' constituted an organized system of representation, repeatedly reproducing the same images—exotic yet passive women, mysterious and dangerous foreign lands. His point was not that these descriptions were inaccurate, but that 'the act of representation itself was laden with manipulation,' capable of creating a 'reality' with no correspondence to the actual world. Ultimately this system of imagination served the question of who had the power to control whose resources.

— Edward Said
23:32

Palestinians were imagined as non-existent people in this Western narrative

Orientalism specifically points out how Palestine was imagined as 'a vast empty desert waiting to bloom,' with its inhabitants described as 'insignificant nomads with no stable claim to the land, and thus no cultural continuity.' From this, Said drew a more direct conclusion: the state of Israel itself, in a sense, was created and sustained by these Western fantasies, dreams, and ambitions. This reframed the Israeli-Palestinian conflict not merely as a territorial dispute, but as a narrative war over 'who counts as fully human.'

— Edward Said
37:45

He exposed the Oslo Accord as empty far earlier than most

In 1993, when the Oslo Accord was signed, most of the world's media celebrated. But Said was angry at Arafat for 'making commitments on behalf of Palestinians without ever telling them what he had committed to,' and directly stated that the agreement contained 'not a single word about ending the occupation.' According to the episode, even those who drafted the accord with Arafat took a year to realize 'he had not actually gained a state.' The episode notes that Palestinian death tolls subsequently rose rather than fell, and Said was thus described as being proven right 'like a prophet.'

40:46

Late in life he abandoned the two-state solution for coexistence in a single state

By the late 1990s, Said's view had changed. He believed Israel had 'too successfully fragmented occupied territories into separate pieces,' establishing a pass system similar to South African apartheid, which 'completely negated' the two-state solution on the ground. The only remaining option, he argued, was a 'one-state solution' allowing Jews and Arabs to coexist within a single country. This required what he called a capacity for 'creation and invention,' which became central to his late-life vision of coexistence.

— Edward Said
44:47

After his cancer diagnosis he practiced his vision through a multi-ethnic orchestra

After his cancer diagnosis in 1991, Said and Israeli conductor Daniel Barenboim co-founded the West-Eastern Divan Orchestra, which brought together Palestinian, Arab, and Israeli musicians. It held its first performance in Weimar, Germany—a city just kilometers from the Buchenwald concentration camp memorial. Some criticized this as 'normalizing dangerous ground reality with a harmonious small example,' but Said called it 'the most important thing I have ever done.'

— Edward Said / Daniel Barenboim

The 1993 peace agreement between the PLO and Israel that recognized each other's legitimacy but did not explicitly end the occupation.
