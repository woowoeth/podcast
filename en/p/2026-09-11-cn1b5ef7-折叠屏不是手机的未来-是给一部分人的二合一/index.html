The foldable isn't the future of the phone — it's a two-in-one for a subset of people

Foldables have sold into the tens of millions, but the inner screen gets opened less than 30% of the time. They won't replace the slab phone; for people who want a phone-plus-iPad two-in-one they're a plus, and for everyone else they're a minus that costs you grip and performance.

The foldable's home turf is China and nowhere else

In 2025, China's full-year foldable shipments broke ten million units, with Huawei taking 70% — a crushing share. But globally, China is the single largest market, accounting for more than 40% of shipments. In other words, this category isn't evenly distributed the way other phone form factors are; its core users are the Chinese bosses holding Huawei foldables in first class and at social occasions. From the big picture, foldables are only 2.5% of global phone shipments, and only 15% of Huawei's own phone shipments.

— Liu Fei
20:23

The inner screen gets opened less than 30% of the time

Liu Fei used foldables as his daily driver for the better part of a month, and in the end he didn't want to keep them. The biggest problem wasn't the crease, wasn't the camera, wasn't the battery — it was the scenario. The inner screen doesn't get opened much: it's unstable in one hand and prone to shaking, and it's more for situations where you're holding it with both hands — but in those situations he'd already be bringing a 10-inch iPad. A Vivo vice president said at a launch event that foldables keep getting better, but the core way you use them is no different from seven years ago, and many users use the inner screen less than 30% of the time. His analogy: it's like carrying a phone case with a built-in power bank — your phone's weight goes up permanently, but most of the time you don't need to charge, so you'd be better off carrying a separate external power bank.

— Liu Fei
23:25

Once it gets wider, scrolling feeds actually gets worse

To accommodate the outer screen, wide-expanding foldables like the Huawei Pure X Max and the Apple iPhone Door got wider, and the experience of scrolling feeds becomes very bad. Xiaohongshu is fine, because it's been adapted; but when you're scrolling Meituan to filter restaurants or Taobao to filter shops, the amount of information each screen can hold suddenly shrinks. This isn't purely an adaptation problem, it's an aspect-ratio problem: list feeds run vertically, so widening is meaningless — the height shrinks, and you can see less at once. He recalls that ordering takeout on Meituan, he could only see three restaurants at a time.

— Liu Fei
29:33

76% more area, yet widescreen video looks smaller

A large foldable is almost square when unfolded, which is a disaster for watching video and looking at photos. By screen area, opening the inner screen adds 76%, nearly double; but if you're watching a widescreen movie, the effective area of a foldable's inner screen actually shrinks by 10%, because it can't fit it. Watching 16:9 content gets bigger, but only by 20%, which is fairly limited. Wide-expanding foldables have more sensible dimensions and higher effective area, but a 16:10 wide-expanding foldable playing 16:9 video still leaves 10% black bars top and bottom. The point isn't that area naturally increases — it's what you actually want to do with the inner screen.

— Liu Fei
37:38

Who the two-in-one is a plus for

Liu Fei says the most important decision logic for whether to buy a foldable is: does it add to or subtract from your existing experience. He has a full set of devices himself — a Windows PC for gaming, a MacBook for work, an iPad for shows — so a foldable is pointless for him. But for many young people, the phone is their only computing device, and a lot of work gets done on the phone, offline, so a bigger screen makes the experience much better. Salespeople who travel constantly and need to present a PPT to a client in a café also get a much better result from the inner screen than the outer one. Flip it around: what you're buying isn't a slab phone that turns into a tablet, it's a foldable iPad mini.

— Liu Fei
45:47

Without AirPods there'd be no Chinese podcasting

Liu Fei says both the Apple Watch and AirPods developed during Cook's tenure are world-changing products, and AirPods especially so: without AirPods ushering in the era of Bluetooth noise-cancelling headphones, you could say there'd be no podcast market — especially no Chinese podcast market. Chinese podcasting took off in sync with Bluetooth noise-cancelling headphones — because you can only hear clearly what someone is saying on your commute, at the gym, or doing chores if the noise is cancelled. Before that, those scenarios were usually for listening to music, or to content with very low information density.

— Liu Fei
57:04

Apple's kill shot is migrating the iPad ecosystem

The iPhone Door doesn't have such an overwhelming hardware advantage; it uses materials from mature supply chains, weighs 254 grams, and doesn't feel dramatically different from Android flagship foldables. The core advantage is that Apple has the iPad: since the iPad launched in 2010, the 4:3 ratio has accumulated an enormous amount of content ecosystem, and a lot of software was already optimized well enough for that ratio before foldable phones even existed, so it can be ported straight to the iPhone Door. For Android, that's a crushing level of ecosystem migration. Apple also did a lot of optimization on one-handed interaction, putting frequently used buttons on the dominant-hand side — that's a system-level design, and it's relatively easy for developers to follow and adapt to.

— Liu Fei
1:08:09

How you define success decides whether it succeeds

Whether the iPhone Door succeeds depends on how you define success. If you define it as replacing the slab phone, it's nearly impossible; if you define it as becoming a product line that keeps selling, it's very possible. A few million units in the first generation is no problem, and it might even reach ten million; JD reservations have already passed 500,000. Liu Fei says phone form factors won't converge, they'll diverge: the slab phone is the only form factor the whole world would vote for, but other form factors will keep existing and keep differentiating, at every price point and in every shape. Foldables won't replace slab phones, even if performance, experience and price were exactly the same, because a substantial number of people simply have no need to open the inner screen.

— Liu Fei
