Looney Tunes animator Chuck Jones: A character isn't drawn, it's thought up

Chuck Jones said the key to animation isn't what a character looks like or how it's drawn, but "who he is" — he understood the character first, which is how Daffy could be cowardly and overbearing at once, and how Bugs Bunny had to be provoked before he fought back.

Bugs Bunny's violence wouldn't pass review today

Comedian Dustin Nickerson tried showing classic cartoons to his young daughter and found they hadn't become harmless with time: plenty of characters have speech impediments, and the behavior is highly aggressive. Pepe Le Pew physically holds a female character so she can't leave, while other characters murder each other — the Coyote wants to kill the bird. This runs counter to the cartoon impression in viewers' memories that "everyone is friends."

A shelved Warner film was saved by an independent distributor

After Coyote vs. Acme was finished, Warner Bros. Discovery shelved it and wrote it off for financial purposes. This year an independent distributor bought the rights for $50 million, and last month it finally opened. The film played in relatively few theaters across the US, but it has already recouped almost its entire cost domestically, with another $30 million overseas. The movie itself is about the Coyote suing the Acme Corporation, inspired by Ian Frazier's 1990 parody piece in The New Yorker.

— David Bianculli
7:07

Daffy is cowardly and overbearing at the same time

Chuck Jones said what makes Daffy funny is that he rushes in and fears to take a step at the same time, is sycophantic and domineering at the same time — like when we go into the boss's office to ask for a raise, domineering at the door and sycophantic once inside, except with Daffy both happen at once. He admitted he couldn't understand "crazy" characters, so he always had to "pasteurize" a character and figure out who he is. He stressed that the point of character animation isn't what the character looks like, nor how it's drawn, but "who he is."

— Chuck Jones
9:11

Bugs Bunny is a comedy hero, not a comedy loser

Jones first did Duck Amuck with Daffy, then later did Rabbit Rampage with Bugs Bunny, and the same setup didn't hold, because Bugs isn't that kind of character. He classed Bugs as a comedy hero, whereas the line of comedians running through Chaplin and Buster Keaton are half-losers, more like the rest of us. He liked Daffy because Daffy keeps going forward, to the point where Jones himself would stop for fear of what others would think.

— Chuck Jones
10:14

A two-hour "yes session" where no one may say anything negative

After 1946, Warner's cartoons were directed by three men: Fritz Freeling, Bob McKimpson and Jones. Whoever had an idea for a film called the other directors and the writers into a meeting they called a "yes session": for two hours no one was allowed to say anything negative, and if you had nothing to contribute you shut up. The result was that everyone worked on everyone else's films, but each director had absolute control over the content of his own, and there was no infighting among them. Jones also admitted that if a good gag he'd given was used by Freeling and the audience laughed, he naturally wanted to go throw up — that's human nature, and it was the Daffy in him.

— Chuck Jones
13:25

The narrower the rules, the better the comedy

Jones believed that in comedy, the more you narrow and restrict a character, the better it works: once Chaplin put on the Little Tramp outfit, what he could do was locked down. Once Roadrunner and Coyote were fixed in the American Southwest desert, the rules followed — Roadrunner has to stay on the road, can never harm Coyote, and Coyote can only harm himself by buying those unlucky Acme products, with no sound in the whole film but "beep-beep." Bugs Bunny is too strong and easily becomes a bully, so every film first lets him live his own life in his natural rabbit environment until someone comes to take his hide or his life, and only then does he fight back.

— Chuck Jones
16:30

Hiding one dirty frame in a twenty-fourth of a second

Jones admitted that Warner's animators all drew that kind of pornographic cartoon on the sly. He himself would sometimes slip a single frame — a twenty-fourth of a second — of something dirty into a Pepe Le Pew film, then call the office manager over and say there seemed to be a little glitch here that he couldn't figure out. He knew no one could see it, but the manager would be shocked on the spot. He always kept a replacement frame on hand just in case, but he enjoyed doing it, in his words, to "reduce him to moccasins."

— Chuck Jones
17:31

The networks cut films until they were incomprehensible

Jones said his scenes and lines were never censored; that was left to the "brilliant people" at the networks, and what they cut made the whole film incomprehensible. Both CBS and ABC did it. What he found absurd was that the Roadrunner series, the most complained-about for violence, actually has no violence — Roadrunner never harms Coyote, and Coyote is simply the victim of his own incompetence.

— Chuck Jones
22:33

She sang jazz into something that couldn't be categorized

Cassandra Wilson first drew notice in the 1980s singing difficult pieces by Henry Threadgill and Steve Coleman, then recorded her own albums and became a breakthrough star of jazz. Kevin Whitehead traced her thirty-year recording career: early on she already bore the marks of a mature style — a preference for lazy tempos, smoky timbres, the dramatic low notes a contralto can reach, and the occasional swallowed syllable. After working with producer Craig Street in the 1990s, she made music that deliberately resisted "jazz" or any category.

— Kevin Whitehead
39:06

She refused to call her improvisation scatting

Wilson said she doesn't like the word "scatting," because it's a product of the bebop era, when singers tended to imitate horns. What she does isn't imitating instruments, it's just improvising, creating sounds that belong to the voice; sometimes it sounds like an instrument, but that isn't the starting point. She'd rather make a "sound." She also spoke about the historical reasons musicians look down on singers: singers often enter music lacking depth in theory, and function in a band not as musicians but merely as singers. Her own positioning was "a musician who sings."

— Cassandra Wilson
