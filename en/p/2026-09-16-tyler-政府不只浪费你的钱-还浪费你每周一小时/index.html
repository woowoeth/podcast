Government Doesn't Just Waste Your Money — It Wastes an Hour of Your Week

Plenty of countries file taxes for their citizens; the IRS is legally barred from doing it for you. The time tax hits the poor, immigrants and the formerly incarcerated hardest — and AI may not save them.

0:58

The IRS is legally barred from filing your taxes for you

Lowrey's central example: in Japan, salaried workers never touch the tax process at all; many countries send you a pre-filled return and you just correct the errors. The US could easily do the same — nine in ten households take the standard deduction, so their tax situations are fairly simple — but the IRS is not legally permitted to. This is not a technology problem, it's an institutional choice. She defines the "time tax" as the portion of cost the government shifts onto individuals as administrative labor, and unlike a monetary tax it works differently: you can earn more money, you cannot earn more time.

— Annie Lowrey
1:02

The worst program is TANF, not the IRS

Lowrey names TANF (cash welfare) the single worst program in government: it nominally exists to address child poverty, in practice it does not change the child poverty rate, its compliance burden is extremely heavy and punitive, and its $16.5 billion a year is essentially wasted. She offers a rule — programs aimed at high-income Americans are a far better experience, safety-net programs are generally bad, and welfare is the worst. This yields a counterintuitive judgment: the quality of government service is inversely proportional to the political power of the beneficiary.

— Annie Lowrey
6:08

Pandemic fraud is not something the time tax can prevent

Cowen raises a common objection: fraud exploded after deregulation in 2020, so maybe the time tax is the necessary cost of fraud prevention. Lowrey rejects this outright: that fraud came mainly from criminal rings and fake small businesses, plus the fact that UI systems themselves run on ancient COBOL infrastructure. Her mechanism is misaligned incentives — governors have no incentive to upgrade UI systems when unemployment is low, so the same conversation repeats every recession. The conclusion: building state capacity and agile digital systems prevents fraud; pushing the work onto individuals does not.

— Annie Lowrey
11:19

Large-scale fraud is almost entirely on the supply side

Lowrey points out that Medicare and Medicaid fraud is almost entirely on the provider side: nursing homes, clinics and hospital systems defrauding the government. The ceiling on individual fraud is low, because the amount you can extract from the government is limited; to commit large-scale fraud you have to process a lot of transactions. So her policy ordering is: reduce administrative overhead in other programs, concentrate enforcement resources on criminal organizations and legitimate providers, rather than loading burdens onto the 99.9% who don't commit fraud. She adds a piece of political economy: fraud narratives are often used to make programs look illegitimate, so voters tolerate cuts.

— Annie Lowrey
13:25

Fifty hours per person per year, and highly unequal

Lowrey's quantitative estimate comes from the American Time Use Survey: total bureaucratic time is about 50 hours per person per year, close to an hour a week. She also concedes the number is understated — ATUS cannot capture multitasking (cooking while on hold only gets recorded as one thing), and time taxes are scattered across categories and never aggregated. Who pays the most? People entangled with the criminal justice system, immigrants, the poor. The rich can "skip" it with subordinates and accountants — with one exception: permits to renovate a house in San Francisco, where no amount of money spares you a 500-day wait and multiple rounds of public review.

— Annie Lowrey
22:57

AI can fill out forms, but it can't fill a urine test

Cowen asks whether AI agents could handle all of this within five years. Lowrey says probably, but raises two caveats: first, the users of the most burdensome programs are low-income, and whether they know about or trust these agents is unknown; second, much of the burden isn't paperwork at all — it's mandatory interviews, urine screening, data that is hard to collect. She gives SNAP as an example: benefits are calculated by household, and the government has no stable definition of "household," so you have to explain how you split staple food costs with the people you live with — a question AI would struggle to answer for you too. She notes she was originally skeptical, but a civic technologist who has worked in this field for years firmly believes AI will help a great deal.

— Annie Lowrey
31:18

Disability insurance is already hard enough to apply for

Cowen applies pressure with data: the number of people on disability insurance rose roughly fourfold between 1990 and 2014, while workplace deaths kept falling — shouldn't the threshold be raised? Lowrey doesn't accept the inference. She offers two alternative explanations: population aging and rising obesity-related chronic disease. Then she offers counterevidence: 5% of applicants must submit more than 1,000 pages of medical material; 11,000 Americans die each year while waiting for a disability determination. She concedes she worries about both directions — about paying people who don't need it, and about denying people who genuinely do — so she doesn't think this is the moment to add more paperwork.

— Annie Lowrey
38:28

The research moved her position on UBI

Lowrey's previous book, Give People Money, was pro-UBI; this time she says plainly that the research findings are "sobering," and that she has updated her priors: the side benefits she expected cash to deliver did not materialize. But her defense is that the point of giving people money is to give people money to buy things, and that rationale still holds; life improving less than expected is not an argument against the policy. On AI and UBI, she admits she was extremely dismissive back then of the claim that "AI will force UBI," and she won't go that far now — but if GDP and productivity surge while unemployment rises at the same time, that would be very strange, and we would have to start considering UBI-type policies.

Glossary

time tax
The time cost the government imposes by shifting administrative labor onto individuals; unlike a monetary tax, it cannot be offset by earning more.
TANF
The US cash welfare program, which Lowrey considers the most administratively burdensome and least effective program in government.
submerged benefits
Benefits delivered through the tax code, where recipients often don't know they are receiving them, such as the child tax credit.
Jevons paradox
Efficiency gains increase total consumption rather than reducing it; Lowrey uses it to explain how labor-saving tools like email ended up adding to the task load.
improper payment
Erroneous disbursements caused by complex rules, distinct from deliberate fraud, and reducible simply by simplifying the rules.
