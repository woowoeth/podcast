Cao Cao's real aim at Red Cliffs: pursue Liu Bei, not destroy Eastern Wu

Cao Cao's letter to Sun Quan was a threat, not a surrender ultimatum. His real aim going south was to chase the fleeing Liu Bei. He stumbled into the Battle of Red Cliffs only when Sun Quan chose to fight, and the fire burned at Wulin, not Red Cliffs.

7:57

Liu Bei was still a wandering warlord before the Battle of Red Cliffs

After his defeat at Changban, Liu Bei had reached his lowest point: homeless, with only one or two million troops from various sources, after bouncing between employers like Yuan Shao, Cao Cao, Tao Qian, and Liu Biao, each wary of him. Yet this paradoxical low point was where his life actually turned. In just a few years, he went from being entirely dependent on others to ruling both Jingzhou and Yizhou and establishing his own state.

— Tan Musheng
20:17

Lü Su's strategic vision came before and surpassed the Longzhong Discourse

In 197 AD, Lü Su already proposed to Sun Quan the strategy of controlling the entire Yangtze from mouth to source—seven years before Zhuge Liang's Longzhong Discourse. This was why Eastern Wu fought so hard over Jingzhou. Tan argues that Lü Su is severely underestimated: in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms he appears manipulated by Zhou Yu and Zhuge Liang, but his strategic vision actually surpassed Zhuge Liang's. The Longzhong Discourse had a fatal flaw: the strategy of controlling both Jingzhou and Yizhou was ultimately impossible to achieve.

— Tan Musheng
26:39

Zhuge Liang's Longzhong Discourse advised Liu Bei to seize his allies' territory

When Zhuge Liang advised Liu Bei in the Longzhong Discourse to ‘control Jingzhou and Yizhou’, Liu Biao had not yet shown signs of imminent death, and Liu Zhang had actually hired Liu Bei as a military defender on the border. Tan directly calls this advice ‘immoral’. Liu Bei had used the same technique before in Xuzhou—not because Tao Qian entrusted him, but because local gentry proactively contacted Liu Bei to displace the outside administrators. Zhuge Liang taught Liu Bei to use this same maneuver again, and Liu Bei never asked how to rightfully obtain these territories.

— Tan Musheng
29:36

Cao Cao came south to catch Liu Bei, not to conquer Eastern Wu

Cao Cao's real objective was not to destroy Eastern Wu but to pursue the fleeing Liu Bei to Xiakou where he had joined Liu Qi. His letter to Sun Quan was a threat: do not interfere, or I will deal with you as well. Real battle commenced when Sun Quan took the initiative to fight Cao Cao's forward units, who then withdrew 160 li to Wulin—the fires burned at Wulin, not Red Cliffs. The famed 800,000-strong army was later estimated at about 300,000; by physics, burning winter reeds spreads only 10-plus kilometers at a time, so a single fire could never burn through hundreds of li of connected encampments.

— Tan Musheng
45:09

Three-way rule meant three powers had to divide Jingzhou three ways

In the second struggle for Jingzhou, Sun Quan brought 30,000 troops and Liu Bei hastily mobilized 50,000 from Yizhou to oppose him. When Cao Cao moved in Hanzhong, both sides rushed to compromise at the Xiang River: west bank to Liu Bei, east bank to Sun Quan, Nanzhun remaining with Guan Yu. This bloodless resolution let all three powers see each other's actual strength for the first time—in Yangzhou it was Cao Wei against Eastern Wu, in Hanzhong-Qinling it was Cao Wei against Liu Bei, but only in Jingzhou did all three interfere. ‘Jingzhou was like the biggest chip on a gambling table when three people are playing. Nobody dared reach for it. The moment you tried to grab it, the other two would immediately gang up on you.’

— Tan Musheng
53:30

Three conspiracy theories competed to explain Guan Yu's death

During the Xiangfan campaign when Guan Yu flooded seven armies and terrified the empire, Cao Cao rushed thirteen relief forces. Yet Liu Feng and Meng Da, who had just conquered Shangyong, refused to send troops, claiming ‘this place isn't yet stable’—they didn't even secure Jiangling. Meanwhile in Chengdu, Liu Bei, Zhuge Liang, Zhao Yun, Ma Chao, Huang Zhong, and Zhang Fei all gathered, yet none moved. This vast historical vacuum spawned three conspiracy theories: Wang Fuzhi argued Zhuge Liang held a grudge against Guan Yu for disrespecting civilians and killed him by proxy; Zhang Taiyan claimed Zhuge Liang feared Guan Yu's power growing too great; Fang Shiming suspected Liu Bei himself, with Mi Fang and Shi Ren (garrisoning Gong'an and Jiangling) staying put.

— Tan Musheng
59:51

Liu Bei spent a hundred thousand soldiers' lives on self-therapy at Yiling

Before the Yiling campaign, ministers opposed it, Zhao Yun plainly said they should attack the usurper Cao Pi, not Sun Quan. Zhang Fei died before the army even departed, and Sun Quan proactively offered peace. By any rational measure, this was a war that could and should have been avoided. Tan judges that Liu Bei in his late years could no longer reason clearly: after a lifetime of warfare seeing Cao Cao and Sun Quan as peers and juniors, he could not accept that his dream of ruling Jingzhou and Yizhou would die with the loss of Jingzhou, and he could not swallow defeat by Lu Xun, a ‘child’. Thus this war became ‘Liu Bei giving himself psychological therapy with hundreds of thousands of lives. He needed a therapist. Those hundreds of thousands of lives were his therapist. And the medical bill was indeed steep.’

— Tan Musheng
1:09:20

Jingzhou remained the north-south gateway for four hundred years after

In the four centuries of North-South division following the Three Kingdoms, Jingzhou was always the geopolitical lifepoint: to hold the Yangtze one must hold the Han, and southern regimes had to hold Xiangyang and Fancheng to block invasion from the north—once Xiangyang fell, southern dynasties typically collapsed soon after (the thirty-year Xiangyang siege in the Yuan-Mongol conquest traces back to this geography). Yet Jingzhou was a double-edged sword for southern regimes: it had to be strong enough to defend against the north, yet once strong enough, the commander there could move downriver and threaten the capital at Jiankang. Supplies sent upstream cost three times more than supplies sent downstream, so downstream forces could never credibly threaten upstream—the downstream court could never control its own frontier.

— Tan Musheng
