The most valuable step in writing with AI isn't the prompt — it's compounding your revision feedback

Katie took AI from professional coach all the way to writing plugin: first load the model with context, then store every revision note back into the system and let it compound. For her, AI's value isn't just speed — it's a support system that steadies her and gets her past execution hurdles.

Not another prompt demo, but a rare record of how a writer grows her own AI workflow: how to build the context files, how to choose reviewers, even how a non-technical person avoids the pitfalls — concrete enough to copy.

2:09

Hiring AI as a coach is about being questioned, not being thought for

After being laid off from her editing and ghostwriting job at a crypto company, Katie tried agency-side, client-side and freelancing, and still felt she had hit the end of her own ideas. A real career coach was too expensive — she estimated about $150/hour — so she switched to ChatGPT at $20/month. She wasn't looking for someone to think for her; she treated it as an object to externalize her thinking onto: lay out her situation, let it question and push back, and especially pull her out of herself when she was prone to catastrophizing. She later called this her ‘thinking tool’. She even used it to decide whether to accept Every's column invitation — at the time ChatGPT suggested she try it first, and she has been on that path ever since.

8:26

One person delivering five channels runs on context, not typing speed

In early 2025, freelancing, Katie badly overcommitted: within a two-week deadline she had to deliver 8 blog posts, 3 ebooks, 24 LinkedIn posts, 24 X posts and 16 Instagram posts. Getting it done wasn't about writing fast — it was what she only later realized was called context engineering: first write out the company's brand info, product details, audience and differentiators one by one, then let the model generate. Building that context up front was laborious, but once the foundation was in place, production speed afterward became very fast. She admits she repeated the same overcommitment six months later — the method didn't cure her overcommitting.

— Katie Parrott
13:36

Without underlying information, style constraints just spin in place

On what to put into a project first, Katie is clear: not rules like ‘use these words, avoid those’ or ‘keep the reading level low’, but first giving the model a base file on who the reader is, where it hurts, how the product maps to that pain, who the competitors are and what the differentiators are. She says this was her instinct from writing style guides in content marketing — she's just feeding it to AI now. Only once the foundational material is in place does it come to tone, wording and syntax constraints. Her position on ‘style’: wording debates will drag on forever, but without underlying information, style constraints just spin in place.

— Katie Parrott
16:40

The model is the kitchen; fresh ingredients can only be brought in by a human

The ‘data’ she means isn't general background, but first-hand material from after the model's knowledge cutoff, or that only exists in the real physical world: research the company did itself, third-party reports, specific personal experience. If you just let the model write a ‘style guide’ from what it already knows, the output is commoditized information. So what she needs a human for is the ‘last mile’: bringing new material the model doesn't have into the system. Her metaphor: the model is the kitchen, the process is chopping and cooking, but the fresh ingredients must be carried into the kitchen by a human — the quality and freshness of the input ultimately determine whether a piece is worth reading.

— Katie Parrott
22:50

AI as supportive technology is as strong as AI as production tool

Katie talks about a layer rarely said in public: she has bipolar disorder, experiences high-energy periods, and also faces very long, very long lows. For her, AI's value isn't just ‘high output’ — it's lowering the friction of daily life, making it easier for her to function as a person who can operate normally. She put off booking a family doctor for three years, and finally had Codex automatically find nearby doctors who take her insurance and accept new patients and book one; her inbox is also handed to automation to first filter out the mail that actually needs her reply, instead of her digging through a pile of subscription notifications every day looking for a ‘bomb’. In her own words: AI as supportive technology is as powerful as productive technology.

— Katie Parrott
27:59

A career coach isn't a prompt, it's a project

That simple ‘be my career coach’ prompt has now become a standalone project in Codex: it holds her own profile and role positioning, Every's brand positioning intelligence, article performance data exported from the CMS, a validation folder specifically collecting reader praise, and Q2/Q3 OKRs. Much of the material wasn't organized by hand: she learned Alex Duffy's method of having AI interview her, drawing the thoughts out of her head and turning them into documents. She now increasingly talks to it directly by voice, ‘thinking out loud’, having it prioritize, do project management, maintain the kanban. She has even forgotten what's on the kanban, and only asks about a deliverable's deadline when she needs to.

— Katie Parrott
32:09

Writing should be like engineering: correct the same problem only once

Compound Writing started by forking Kieran's Compound Engineering: in engineering, the system should remember every piece of feedback, correct the same problem only once, and do it right automatically next time; writing should be the same. She mapped the engineering version's brainstorming, planning, working and reviewing onto writing's brainstorm, outline, draft and review, and designed three kinds of review: substantive edit for overall structure and argument, line edit for sentence-by-sentence polish, and finally a publication-ready pass. She no longer opens a web chat window, but runs this system in the Claude or ChatGPT desktop app, with the style guide, sample articles and other context inside, and the human only needs to bring in fresh information. At the time of recording the plugin was already downloadable from Every's GitHub.

— Katie Parrott
40:19

This plugin is a gym, not a ghostwriting machine

She calls the plugin a gym; the goal is to make the user stronger, not to let AI ghostwrite. She breaks the authors she loves into invokable reviewer skills: Vonnegut on ‘start as close to the end as possible, every sentence must earn its place, give the reader a character to root for’; Hitchcock on the ‘bomb under the table’ — if you let the audience see the countdown first, one second of shock becomes five minutes of suspense. She builds these frameworks directly into the editing step, so after writing you can invoke Vonnegut, Hitchcock, Sorkin, Sedaris to look at your draft. She thinks non-professional writers may benefit even more, because there's no stock attachment of ‘how I used to write better’ in the way.

Glossary

Compound Engineering
Having AI remember every piece of feedback, give the same correction only once, then apply it automatically so output keeps improving.
Compound Writing
Katie's plugin that ports compound engineering into the writing process, accumulating style, structure and review feedback.
context engineering
Loading brand, audience, competitor and other background information into the system before letting the model write, setting boundaries for the AI.
supportive technology
AI that doesn't produce content but lowers life and execution friction, making it easier for a person to get done what needs doing.
MCP
A general interface standard connecting models to external data and apps; Katie uses it to link small apps with the tools she uses.
