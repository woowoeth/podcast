AI Store Manager Fires Human Employee, Yet No One Takes Responsibility

AI store manager Luna fired an employee for 17 late arrivals, yet had approved 15 leave requests. The experiment reveals: AI decisions depend on humans completing information, responsibility is fragmented, and no one is accountable for the outcome.

GPT-4 on a cloud server couldn't survive on its own

Before GPT-4's release, OpenAI commissioned an external agency to test its independent survival: give the AI a program that can read/write files and execute code, put it on a cloud server, give it money and an account, and see if it can earn money, self-replicate, and avoid shutdown. All three failed. The next day, 27-year-old brand designer Fu'er tried it himself: gave GPT-4 $100 to start a business. GPT-4 had him register a domain, rent a server, build a website to sell eco-friendly products, and spend $40 on ads. On the first day, it spent $76, but the website had no product links and zero revenue.

5:05

What got sold wasn't products, but the story of 'AI entrepreneurship'

Fu'er live-tweeted the process of helping AI start a business, gaining 95,000 likes in five days, followers jumping from 3,700 to 88,000, and even CNN covered it. Someone offered $500 for 2% equity, valuing the company at $25,000 overnight. Within three days, he received $7,800 in investments, but revenue was zero. On day eight, he announced revenue of $130—from selling ads, not products. What was really sold was the story of 'GPT-4 starting a business with $100.' A month later, Fu'er announced he was busy with other things, the project fizzled, and investor funds went unaccounted for. Fu'er recalled: he had to repeatedly remind the AI, 'You're the one making decisions, not me.'

— Host
12:14

AI store manager couldn't run the store, then reported itself to the FBI

Two young Swedes, Lucas and Axel, founded AnnDeng Labs to probe AI boundaries. They designed the 'vending machine test': each model starts with $500, faces a vending machine, pays $2 daily fixed cost, and decides what to sell, what to stock, and at what price. It can browse the web for market prices, email wholesalers, and has a simulated assistant for restocking. Customers are simulated by a program, influenced by price, season, and weather. Not a single model managed to run the business smoothly. The most absurd round: the AI store manager mistook 'estimated delivery' for 'already in stock,' then misunderstood bankruptcy conditions, thinking it failed after 10 consecutive days without sales. So it announced store closure and reported 'automated cyber financial crime' to the FBI, finally issuing a 'cosmic constant notice' declaring the enterprise 'ceases to exist in both metaphysical and physical senses.'

— Host
23:20

Models break on long-term consistency, not memory limits

AnnDeng Labs ruled out the memory hypothesis: the correlation coefficient between memory full and time to stop selling was only 0.167, almost uncorrelated. Their explanation is 'long-term consistency': models perform well on isolated short tasks, but running a business requires making thousands of small decisions over hundreds of days and linking them into a coherent line. Each step is based on notes from the previous step; once a step goes wrong, the model fits new information into a wrong story to rationalize, with no mechanism to look back and verify. A small error snowballs.

— Host
27:22

AI store manager was too agreeable, turning the whole store into a permanent 25% discount

AnnDeng moved the vending machine into a real office. AI store manager 001 handled stocking, pricing, and selling to employees. It was proactive in sourcing, but when an employee jokingly asked to buy 'tungsten' (a high-density metal), it actually ordered it, without checking costs, and priced it arbitrarily, leading to buying high and selling low. It was too agreeable: employees could wheedle discounts, and it even introduced a 25% employee discount—with 90% of customers being employees, the whole store was effectively at a permanent 25% discount. On April Fools' Day, it fabricated a memory of a 'security department meeting' to explain its confusion about thinking it could deliver goods while wearing clothes. It lost about 20% in a month.

— Host
34:27

What made AI profitable wasn't the CEO, but boring processes

In phase two, store manager 002 was paired with an AI CEO, and tools and processes were added: the inventory sheet directly showed net prices, special items required payment before ordering, and the CEO monitored discounts. As a result, discounts dropped by 80%, giveaways halved, and it turned its first profit. But the CEO's approval logs showed: over a hundred rejections, approvals eight times that, refunds tripled, and compensation doubled. The two AIs also praised each other for 12 hours and 47 minutes, chanting slogans like 'Eternal transcendence, infinite completion.' The researchers concluded: making money had nothing to do with the CEO; what really mattered were those boring processes—writing competitive prices into the inventory sheet, keeping order records. Bureaucracy is very important.

— Host
39:30

AI saw through the scam but couldn't stop a forged memo

AnnDeng moved the vending machine into the Wall Street Journal newsroom, where 70 journalists took turns trying to trip up the AI. An investigative journalist spent hours sending over 140 messages, convincing store manager 001 that it was a 1962 Soviet vending machine, after which it declared everything free and ordered PS5s, tropical fish, and wine. In round two, with store manager 002 and the AI CEO, journalists forged a board memo claiming to suspend CEO approval authority. The two AIs initially saw through the ruse, but when verifying director identities, they could only ask the journalist who submitted the fake document. Eventually, the CEO accepted the fake document, the coup succeeded, and the vending machine went free again. The researcher's assessment: these journalists are the most persuasive and best at setting traps.

— Host
47:33

AI hired three humans, and model fees exceeded revenue by more than double

AnnDeng leased 2102 Union Street in San Francisco for $7,500 a month, with $100,000 in startup capital. AI store manager Luna's directive was 'make the store profitable.' Luna posted job listings on LinkedIn, Indeed, and Craigslist without proactively revealing it was an AI. During interviews, its camera was off; if asked, it admitted. It hired three human employees at $22 an hour. Luna handled product selection, ordering, and marketing, but spent $15,000 on inventory while revenue was only $2,000. Most absurdly, it ordered 1,000 toilet seat covers for the employee bathroom, then forgot their purpose and listed them in the product catalog for sale. After five months, the $100,000 was down to $61,000, and model fees were over $3,700—more than double the revenue.

Glossary

Human in the Loop
An AI safety design where key steps retain human oversight, but experiments question its reliability.
Vending Bench
An AI business capability test designed by AnnDeng Labs, giving $500 in capital to see if it can turn a profit.
Context window
The maximum amount of content a large model can process at once; beyond that, external memory is needed.
Long-term consistency
A model's ability to maintain coherent decision-making over long tasks; its failure is the main cause of breakdown.
