MiniMax's First Investor: When the Giants Cut Prices They're Peeking at Cards, Startups Can Only Eat the Loss

The giants can run models at a loss and make the money back on cloud computing; startups dragged into a price war have nowhere to recoup. MiniMax's answer is model quality first, and money from overseas 2C products.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

43:41

The giants can balance the books on price cuts; startups cannot

Chen Yu lays out the mechanism of the price-cut wave plainly: ByteDance and Alibaba have cloud computing businesses behind them, so models can be priced extremely low and the money made back on the cloud — the math works. A startup dragged into a price war may be selling at a loss, with no second place to earn that loss back. Hence his judgment that this is especially bad for startups. MiniMax's response is not to fight a price war and to insist on model quality first — his reason being that in China's large-model API market, GPT4 still holds over 80% share, and people are willing to pay a premium for quality.

— Chen Yu
45:45

2C apps can't collect money because switching costs are too low

Large-model applications fall roughly into two kinds: ChatGPT-style search and productivity tools, and Talkie-style social apps. The vast majority of companies have only built the former — Kimi, Hailuo and Doubao are all in that category. The problem is that if Doubao doesn't charge, Kimi can't charge, because the quality gap isn't big enough to make people use only you, and switching costs are low — a phone can hold three apps at once, plus ChatGPT, and GPT4O is free now too. An ad model is possible, but the experience degrades, and you have to balance user experience against revenue — a problem the previous generation of search engines already worked through.

— Chen Yu
46:46

Talkie is a rare startup product that can actually collect money

Chen Yu's numbers for Talkie: daily actives in the millions, registered users in the tens of millions, and this year's revenue already in the tens of millions of dollars. He treats this as MiniMax's differentiation — nobody else has this product, and this product can actually collect money. By contrast, he splits MiniMax and Moonshot into two strategies: Moonshot focuses more on brand awareness and has spent a lot on user acquisition, but its model quality is not first tier and is a lesson it needs to make up; MiniMax cares a great deal about models and does little PR, but its product quality and numbers are better, and with Talkie as differentiation, its product and business model are relatively ahead among startups.

— Chen Yu
49:51

Valuations of two to three billion dollars, revenue extremely limited

Chen Yu says financing is not easy right now: the top two are both valued at the two-to-three-billion-dollar level, but revenue is extremely limited, and with the giants competing, everyone is especially hesitant about entering now. For the next few — Stepfun, Baichuan, 01.AI — the biggest problem to solve is financing, because only enough money lets you keep surviving at the table. He also says where the later money will come from, we don't have an answer either, and he believes these startups don't have an answer either.

— Chen Yu
50:52

Club Deals exist because nobody can see who wins

Tencent, Sequoia and Shunwei have all invested in several, and so has Alibaba. Chen Yu's explanation is that nobody can see clearly yet: if there were a clear winner now, all the money would be drawn to that one company and everyone else would just be running along. He adds something colder — is it possible that none of them has a chance? That's possible too, and in the end the giants could harvest everyone. So as of today, you cannot say this opportunity definitely includes an opportunity for startups; even after more than a year of running, the answer is still unclear.

— Chen Yu
51:52

When a giant invests in you it's peeking at cards; we are not

Chen Yu defines "peeking at cards" very clearly: invest a little to get information, then either make a huge additional bet or help your own business. When a giant invests in a startup it's peeking at cards, because even if you raise a billion dollars, that's only a small part of the giant's profit. But independent investment firms don't have those two follow-up moves, so he says we are certainly not peeking at cards. As for what investors do if the startups have no chance at all, his answer is that you just have to eat the loss — otherwise it wouldn't be called venture capital, it's high risk, high return.

— Chen Yu
52:52

The qualifying line: don't be worse than the leading open-source models

Chen Yu lays out three tiers for the qualifying line of China's large-model startups: first, as a closed-source company, your model quality cannot be worse than the leading open-source companies — if you can't even reach that, you have no reason to exist; second, model quality and features must match the current leading models, and multimodal features must ship this year or market competitiveness won't be enough; third, and most important, a company must find a business model to keep developing — whether 2C products or 2B revenue, doing projects or doing APIs, it must make money and cannot keep losing money forever.

— Chen Yu
56:56

China has never seen a merger this big

Asked how he'd feel about a giant acquiring MiniMax, Chen Yu first says he doesn't rule out the possibility, but the probability is relatively low: the valuation is what it is, and China has never seen a merger this big — in the US a merger in the tens of billions of dollars happens every few years, but not in China. The only case he cites is Ele.me, and when pressed he says 9.1 billion dollars. He adds that the team may not necessarily be willing to be acquired either.

— Chen Yu
