Rome Won With a Crow: The Key to the First Punic War

Rome turned sea battles into land battles with a boarding bridge called the corvus, cancelling Carthage's advantage in seamanship; but Carthage brought in a Spartan to rebuild its army and captured a Roman consul in return.

2:03

This was not a war between two equal powers

The host opens by calling this a clash of ‘the two great powers of the western Mediterranean,’ and Eve MacDonald corrects him on the spot: at the war's outbreak, Carthage was not necessarily a military power of that rank. Carthage had money, controlled the sea routes, and had centuries of alliance networks in western Sicily, but it had no strong standing army — it relied on allies and mercenaries. What actually made Carthage into a power capable of fighting Rome as an equal was this war itself. So the story Roman sources tell over and over, of ‘Roman adaptability,’ is only half the story; the other half is Carthaginian resilience and adaptability, without which there is no explaining why this war lasted more than twenty years.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
15:29

Mercenaries at Messana dragged both powers into war

The trigger was not the ambition of Rome or Carthage but a group of Campanian mercenaries called the Mamertines. They had seized Messana, and after being defeated by Hiero II, king of Syracuse, they split in two: one faction asked Carthage for help, the other asked Rome. Carthage acted first from its base in the Lipari Islands, and Rome followed — and not with a small intervention, but a full invasion with two consular armies. Eve points out that the Roman sources' claim of an ‘accidental empire’ is highly suspect, because the Romans showed up at Messana at the scale of an invading force. Polybius treats the Romans' first crossing of the sea into Sicily as the starting point of Roman imperial expansion.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
28:14

The corvus turned sea battles into land battles

Carthage was the naval power: better oarsmen, ships built fast and well — the Romans even built their warships to Carthaginian standards. But the Romans added one thing: the corvus, meaning ‘crow,’ a boarding bridge that could hook an enemy ship. Once hooked, the Carthaginian ship could not move, and Roman marines simply crossed over and fought a land battle. At the Battle of Mylae, the Carthaginians clearly did not expect this, and took heavy losses. Eve also cautions that the details of the corvus leave many unanswerable questions in the sources, but ancient historians all treat it as decisive. At the Battle of Cape Ecnomus, the corvus is again credited as the chief factor, and Rome thereby opened the sea route for its invasion of Africa.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
38:40

Carthage hired a Spartan to rebuild its army

After the Roman landing in Africa, Rome advanced steadily and Carthage looked finished. Carthage's response was to recruit a new army in the eastern Mediterranean and to bring in a Spartan commander, Xanthippus. He reorganised the Carthaginian army and reworked how the war elephants were used. Eve notes in particular that the Carthaginians had not used war elephants before; it was only after seeing the Indian elephants Pyrrhus brought that they realised they had a smaller African elephant of their own they could use. Xanthippus's reforms worked: the Roman army was destroyed and the consul Regulus was captured. This was Rome's first devastating defeat of the war, and it became a key event in later Roman memory defining the relationship between Rome and Carthage.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
43:11

The captured consul went back to Rome to argue against peace

Carthage sent the captured consul Regulus back to Rome to negotiate peace. In the Senate, Regulus said not to surrender, that Carthage was within reach. By agreement, if peace was not made, he had to return to Carthage and remain a prisoner. He went back, and died in captivity. Eve says the various horrifying versions of how he died are not actually mentioned in the credible sources, so it is hard to judge how much is later embellishment; but what is certain is that he died in captivity in Carthage, peace was refused, and Rome fought on. This passage is a textbook moment of Roman self-congratulatory rhetoric.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
46:27

The sacred chickens would not eat, and Rome's fleet was finished

In 249 BC, the Roman consul Tiberius Claudius Pulcher took his fleet to fight Carthage off Drepana. By Roman ritual, the sacred chickens on his ship had to eat for the battle to proceed; if not, it was a bad omen. The chickens would not eat, so he threw them into the sea, saying that if they would not eat, then let them drink. Roman sources record this as the most serious act of sacrilege, and the Roman fleet was then defeated at Drepana. Eve says that once Roman sources start emphasising a commander's arrogance and contempt for tradition, it usually means something bad happened. This defeat kept Rome from sending out a fleet for eight years.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
50:48

Hannibal's father held Rome down with guerrilla war

After Drepana, Rome vanished from the sea and Carthage gained freedom of action on the western coast. In 247 BC, Carthage sent a new commander, Hamilcar Barca — Hannibal's father. He brought a force Polybius calls the Mix-Hellenes, made up of soldiers of Greek, Libyan and Phoenician descent from North Africa, and waged guerrilla war against Rome, occupying the high ground above Drepana and Panormus, with considerable success. Eve says this was the first time in the war that Carthage's army fought Rome this well. He also seized the temple complex at Eryx, dedicated to the goddess the Carthaginians called Astarte, and both sides fought fiercely over this sacred site.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald
57:20

The rams from this war still lie on the seabed

The Battle of the Aegates Islands in 241 BC was the last battle. The Roman fleet hid behind the islands and ambushed a Carthaginian fleet heavy with supplies meant for Hamilcar. Eve says that if you climb Eryx today, on a clear day you can see the Aegates Islands perfectly, and Hamilcar very likely watched this disaster unfold from land. More remarkably, the site of this sea battle has been preserved underwater: over the past twenty years, underwater archaeologists have found large bronze rams on the seabed near the island of Levanzo, some inscribed with the names of Roman consuls, some bearing Punic inscriptions and carvings, along with Monte Fortino helmets and amphorae.

— Dr. Eve MacDonald

Glossary

corvus
A boarding bridge fitted to Roman warships that could hook an enemy vessel so marines could cross over and fight.
Mamertines
Campanian mercenaries who seized Messana, named after their worship of the war god Mamers.
Mix-Hellenes
Polybius's term for the soldiers of Greek, Libyan and Phoenician descent from North Africa under Hamilcar's command.
Monte Fortino
A Celtic-style helmet used by both Romans and Carthaginians, recovered from the Aegates battle site.
