Good work sleeps for centuries from bias, not poor quality

David Byrne finds that science and art history are full of ‘Sleeping Beauty’ ideas: once recognized, then forgotten for decades or centuries due to gender, racial, and disciplinary bias, until finally rediscovered.

‘Sleeping Beauty’ ideas were recognized once, then forgotten for decades or centuries

David Byrne says the term comes from a scientific paper, and he borrowed it to describe a phenomenon: an idea or work that once achieved success and recognition, then faded from view and was forgotten—perhaps for years, decades, or even centuries—before being rediscovered and appreciated again. He emphasizes this is not limited to art; science has the same, and he believes the mechanisms are not as different as imagined.

— David Byrne
4:09

Gender bias and intuition bias hide in science history too

Byrne points out that the scientific community has the same biases: ideas not trusted because ‘she is a woman’, or ideas rejected outright for being too counterintuitive, until later proved right. This breaks the assumption that ‘science only cares about evidence’—bias can put correct ideas to sleep just as easily. He then challenges the common explanation ‘ahead of its time’, arguing it actually masks the harder question: ‘why would an idea accepted at the time later be forgotten’.

— David Byrne
6:15

van Eyck's stolen panel sparked art history as a discipline

Emma Capron recounts how van Eyck's central altarpiece panel was looted by Napoleon's armies to the Louvre—not from love of it, but to build an ‘encyclopedia museum’, displaying it as a ‘rough origin’ of painting. The result unexpectedly caught German attention to these Flemish painters, sparking the birth of art history as a discipline—a looting became a rediscovery.

— Emma Capron
10:30

A Black composer won the race, then was erased from history

Vincente Lusitano was a mixed-race Black composer who wrote complex polyphonic works, was invited to an Italian composition competition and won. But a Black man defeating local Italians was unwelcome at the time, so he was pushed out of the historical record. This is racial bias directly causing ideas to ‘sleep’, parallel to Maria Tharp's discoveries being dismissed as ‘girls chatting’.

— David Byrne
17:44

van Eyck painted the first portraits that looked like actual moments

Emma Capron explains that earlier portraits were mostly profiles or formulaic ‘types’, requiring inscriptions or coats of arms to identify. van Eyck transformed this entirely: he painted a record of a specific moment—hair blown by wind, stubble as if growing before your eyes, one sitter's eyes bloodshot—and signed it with the exact date, claiming ‘this person's eyes were red that day’.

— Emma Capron
19:48

van Eyck claims realism while painting optical illusions everywhere

van Eyck used inscriptions on frames to claim ‘faithful record’, yet painted fake marble backing, faux-metal sculptural frames, inscriptions as if carved into gold or bronze—using paint to create perfect illusions. Edmund de Waal asks whether the stubble and bloodshot eyes might also be designed ‘tricks’ rather than true records; Capron concedes this is the central contradiction the exhibition argues: van Eyck pursued realism while obsessively playing ‘illusion’ games.

— Emma Capron
27:05

Edison shut down early solar panels because they inconvenienced him

Byrne mentions that solar panels were actually invented in the early twentieth century, but Edison shelved and closed them because they were inconvenient for him. The technology took a long time to be revived—an example of a good idea put to sleep by commercial interest rather than technical defect.

— David Byrne
36:41

Work first, perfection later—what Rilke learned from Rodin

Edmund de Waal explains that Rilke served as Rodin's secretary not to become a sculptor, but because Rodin taught him ‘work, work, work’: write first, make first, even if what comes out is garbage, you can revise it later—but only if you have something to revise. de Waal distills this into one insight: revelation happens in the work, not while waiting for work to arrive.

— Edmund de Waal
