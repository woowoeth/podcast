The Oldest Manuscript of The Song of Roland Is Actually an English Performance Copy

The Oxford Digby 23 manuscript is written in Anglo-Norman, and scholars date it to between 1140 and 1170 by tracking dialect drift — the most complete version of this French epic was performed for the English.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:03

The most complete version is an English manuscript

The Song of Roland is a French epic that took shape around the 12th century, belonging to the genre Chanson de Geste (songs of deeds), and it spread with 12th-century French cultural hegemony to regions including Britain. But its most complete and oldest surviving version is not from France itself — it is an English manuscript, catalogued as Oxford Digby 23. This manuscript was originally bound together with a completely unrelated book by someone later, which is why it is called Digby 23 B, and A is that "other book." Nobody knows who bound them together; possibly some monk felt both were his favourite books.

3:05

Every stanza is a camera position

The poem is organised in laisses (stanzas) of varying length, and each laisse is like a camera focus: when the scene or the mood shifts, you change laisse. The whole poem is in decasyllabic meter, cut into four plus six in performance: the first four syllables rise, then a dramatic pause, then the last six syllables fall, overall close to iambic turning into trochaic. Recited over and over this becomes hypnotic, and the poem runs to four thousand lines — hours of recitation.

7:27

At peak emotion, the same action is performed three times

This kind of epic has a convention that is hard for any reteller to handle: when the action is especially intense or the emotion especially strong, it uses consecutive laisses to tell the same action from three angles, a kind of poetic slow motion. For example, Roland's refusal to blow the oliphant is told three times, with almost no gap between them. This is also why it reads like an action film or a comic: scenes cut hard, with no softening transition — for instance, "Roland dies, his soul ascends to heaven" is followed directly by "the Emperor arrives at Roncevaux."

9:29

The performer's name is left at the end of the manuscript

Digby 23 is very likely a real minstrel's working copy. Line 4002 of the poem, its final line, says roughly: "here ends the song of deeds; Turoldus will not tell more." This line appears before Charlemagne's lament, so it reads more like the performer saying: this is too sad, I cannot go on. The name Turoldus does not appear in the poem, and he is not a known scribe, so scholars lean towards him being the owner of this manuscript, the performer of this performance.

10:31

Its pocket-book size gives away that it was for work

The manuscript is only about six and a half inches by four and a half inches, a pocket book you could carry on your person, not a wealthy man's finely bound volume. It is itself heavily worn and not refined, and it contains some small annotations of unclear meaning. Together these features point to a professional performer on the move: he might have travelled alone with an instrument, or been the lead singer or narrator in a troupe, with someone in front of him doing the physical performance. Such people were jongleurs — the origin of the later word juggler.

12:31

The dialect pins this performer to England

The manuscript is not in the French of central France, nor continental French, but in Anglo-Norman, which suggests this performer was probably Anglo-Norman, performing in Britain or at least in Normandy. The dialect features are very specific: continental French O and OU are replaced by U, so Roland becomes Rulant, l'or becomes l'ur; Anglo-Norman French was also losing final soft e. These changes even begin to break the originally strict decasyllabic meter, leaving some lines with only nine syllables or swelling to eleven.

14:34

Accent drift alone can date a manuscript

Scholars' grasp of this dialect is fine enough that they can date the manuscript to between 1140 and 1170 based on the degree of accent drift. The method is similar to scholars reconstructing Shakespearean pronunciation, tracking regular changes in syllables and sounds. Poetry is especially suited to this kind of reconstruction, because meter and rhyme expose which sounds were dropped and which were added. For many jongleurs, French was a second language anyway, spoken with a dialect accent, and they spelled by sound.

17:40

A French epic, an English artefact

Although this is a French poem about an old French king, this manuscript is an English thing, performed for Britons at the time. There is no equally complete other manuscript to compare line by line, but there are abundant records of The Song of Roland being sung before and after this manuscript, and scholars can use them to judge where this performer departed from what the genre should look like. Glossary

Chanson de Geste
A 12th-century French epic genre; literally "song of deeds."
laisse
The stanza unit of The Song of Roland, of varying length, one laisse to a scene or a mood.
decasyllabic meter
Ten syllables per line, cut into four plus six in performance, with a pause in the middle.
oliphant
The horn Roland refuses to blow in the poem, meant to call Charlemagne for help.
jongleur
A medieval professional travelling performer; the word juggler comes from it.
Anglo-Norman
The French dialect used in Britain after the Norman Conquest, already markedly different from continental French.
