18:17

Artisanal foods can't scale—they lack economies of scale

Luo once ran a braised-meat stall called Wangfu Braised Goods (王府卤味), using superior meat, but the flavor turned bitter because the stock simmered constantly and he wouldn't refresh it; customers also photographed and exposed unplugged hairs on pig ears. His guest analyzed the mechanism: small artisanal workshops can't source factory-preprocessed ingredients, kitchens aren't allowed open flames, so everything requires painstaking manual labor. Result: the operation can't scale and the taste can't improve—a shared trap for all artisanal food and handicraft businesses.

42:43

Standup comedy has developed unspeakable fan-culture taboos

Luo served as a festival judge for several years, but quit after last year's taping. His observation: the industry started as desperate young people with nowhere else to go, and his presence actually helped; now it's mainstream entertainment, and the more successful a performer, the less critique you can offer them—even well-intentioned criticism gets attacked by fans if you qualify it with ‘although’. He says he ‘can't be bothered’; showing up just gets him accused of chasing clout.

— Luo Yonghao
1:06:56

His delays before big events stem from ADHD, not disorganization

For years Luo thought his suffering came from unfinished speeches, only to realize the root was ADHD—the more important the task, the more he delays it. Once tickets sold and pressure mounted, he faced 100+ items on his to-do list and started with the lowest-priority ones—even compulsively deep-cleaning his office days before the event, which hadn't been touched in six months. His previous record was a 47-minute delay before a product launch; he broke it this time with a 50+-minute delay, and the event descended into chaos.

— Luo Yonghao
1:13:00

He plans to donate millions annually to ADHD charities

Luo is discussing with doctors at Peking University Hospital (北医六院) to set up an ADHD-related charity fund, planning to donate 1–2 million yuan annually from his product launches indefinitely. His goal is to help children misdiagnosed as ‘lazy’ or ‘lacking willpower’. He urges the audience: if your child has trouble concentrating, a three-minute attention span, or starts strong each semester then quickly reverts, don't rush to scold them—get tested for ADHD first. It's a medical condition requiring treatment, not an attitude problem.

— Luo Yonghao
1:31:20

Frequent moves are his unconscious trick for resetting his life

Luo's love of moving isn't about hardship but an accidental discovery: each move unleashed 6+ months of sustained excitement. Later he started pursuing it intentionally. Guest Song Fangjin (宋方金) summed up the mechanism as ‘the minimal unit of life restart’—like a computer accumulating glitches, one full reboot is more effective than debugging each issue individually. Both agree: the joy of moving far outweighs the exhaustion of the move itself.

— Luo Yonghao
1:52:35

AI filmmaking will be fully viable within one to two years

Luo pushes back on the idea that filmmaking necessarily costs money: art films and low-budget films don't burn cash; what burns money is genre films like sci-fi and blockbusters. He mentions chatting with director Wen Muye (文牧野), who shot nine short films for as little as 1,000 yuan—because phones can make movies. He judges AI filmmaking already ‘pretty scary’, and within one to two years (optimistic: one to two years; pessimistic: two to three years) will be fully viable. At that point, you can combine real performances with AI, delegating expensive, complex sequences to machines, with costs only decreasing further.

— Luo Yonghao
2:05:42

99% of wealthy friends can't last six months before diving back in

Luo's view is that people should work for life; retiring too early makes you age and die faster. His evidence comes from a decade in mobile internet: many friends achieved financial freedom, all claiming they'd live in total decadence. But his observation: 99% lasted only three to six months before starting something new. His explanation: humans lack the genetic capacity for a life of complete leisure; excessive comfort is what people call ‘degeneration’.

— Luo Yonghao
2:08:43

He nearly changed his name to something more literary and refined

When young, Luo thought his name sounded too rustic and wanted to change it to something literary, like ‘Shu Huai’ (舒淮). He asked a law-student friend to help with the paperwork but was talked out of it—the friend said the name, though rustic, ‘sounds like someone who does big things, an entrepreneur's name’, the aura of a CEO with tens of thousands of employees. Convinced, and with his friend unwilling to deal with the bureaucracy, the name change never happened.

— Luo Yonghao
