Penguins ruled the Southern Ocean before whales, once grew as large as gorillas

After the asteroid extinction wiped out dinosaurs, penguins reached the Southern Ocean's food chain apex before whales and sharks, dominated for about ten million years before ceding the throne, transforming into today's endearing small creatures.

Penguins are dinosaurs—birds are the flying branch of dinosaurs

Guest Steve Brusatte explains that dinosaurs were once as diverse as mammals—one dinosaur lineage shrank, grew wings, learned to fly, and became birds, equivalent in status to bats among mammals. The difference is that all other dinosaurs went extinct, leaving only birds to survive. When you see penguins, sparrows, and crows, you're looking at real dinosaurs, not metaphorically ‘dinosaur-like’ creatures.

— Steve Brusatte
7:04

The earliest penguin fossils had already lost the ability to fly

The oldest known penguin fossils are 61-63 million years old, appearing shortly after the asteroid extinction of dinosaurs. These fossils had already abandoned aerial flight, with wings evolved into paddle-like flippers specialized for underwater swimming. Penguins completed the evolutionary ‘trade of flight for swimming’ as soon as they entered the fossil record, rather than gradually degenerating over time.

— Steve Brusatte
9:06

Penguins' homeland may have been a sunken continent

All known oldest penguin fossils come from New Zealand, which is merely the remaining portion above water of an ancient continent mostly sunken underwater—Zealandia. Zealandia was once connected to Australia and Antarctica, with granite bedrock and distinct boundaries consistent with continental geology, but plate tectonics submerged most of it beneath the sea. After the asteroid impact, land still protruded from Zealandia, and with mammals nearly absent, birds became the apex of the food chain there; penguins likely originated here.

— Steve Brusatte
16:42

Penguins dominated the Southern Ocean before whales and sharks

When the asteroid extinction eliminated dinosaurs, marine apex predators like mosasaurs and plesiosaurs also vanished, leaving a gap at the top of the Southern Ocean food chain. This gap wasn't filled by the later orcas and great sharks—at that time, whale ancestors were still small even-toed ungulates living on land, requiring another ten million years or so before entering the sea. The first to truly claim the throne were penguins; only the fossil record can tell us this, something genetic analysis of modern animals alone cannot reveal.

— Steve Brusatte
19:50

Giant penguins were roughly the size of gorillas

The giant penguin nicknamed ‘Colossus’ may have weighed about 300 kilograms, equivalent to a gorilla. From its fossil, the creature is still recognizable at a glance as a penguin—upright stance, long beak for catching fish and squid—just magnified many times over. Penguins were not originally the endearing small creatures of today; the small penguins we see now are actually the result of a ‘second reduction in size’.

— Steve Brusatte
23:57

The penguin reign was actually brief, and they eventually ceded power

Penguins' time at the apex of the food chain was not long, roughly ten million years, after which they were replaced by evolved orcas and large sharks; the reasons remain unclear. But Brusatte emphasizes this was not a devastating defeat—penguins were not ‘conquered’ but rather transitioned into a new lifestyle, becoming the endearing small creatures we see today, symbols of polar ice. He compares this history to an empire that flourished briefly but brilliantly.

— Steve Brusatte
34:29

Gastornis was herbivorous, not the top predator everyone believed

Gastornis, with its fierce appearance and robust hooked beak, was long thought to be an apex meat-eating predator, similar to terror birds. But about ten years ago, paleontologists conducted oxygen and carbon isotope chemical analysis of its bones—the principle being ‘you are what you eat,’ with herbivores and carnivores leaving different chemical signatures. The results showed Gastornis was actually herbivorous, a conclusion that shocked many but also confirmed the suspicions of some scholars. What exactly it ate with that enormous skull remains a mystery.

— Steve Brusatte
38:38

Flying giant birds filled the pterosaur niche, but it remains empty today

Pterosaurs in the dinosaur age were giant yet could fly, once thought to be beyond what birds could achieve, since flight requires active chest muscle wing-beating, which becomes impossible at enormous size. But fossils show that a group called pelagornithids actually did this, with wingspans reaching about 20 feet, double that of today's largest flying bird, the albatross. They relied more on soaring than flapping, gliding on the wind like pterosaurs. These giant birds survived for over 60 million years until extinction about 2.5 million years ago—before humans appeared. Since then, no species has evolved to fill this ecological niche of ‘soaring giant birds,’ and it remains empty.

— Steve Brusatte
