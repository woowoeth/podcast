Chinese People Embrace AI Not Out of Optimism, But Because They Have No Choice

Surveys show 83% of Chinese people believe AI will improve their life and work, far higher than America's 38%, but the reporter says this optimism largely stems from having no choice rather than genuine confidence.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:05

China is running a full-stack race, not chasing frontier models

Afra Wong notes the Chinese government frames AI almost exclusively through ‘AI+’ and ‘high-quality productivity’; Xi Jinping's public speeches never emphasize building the strongest model, but rather plug AI into manufacturing, supply chains, agriculture—‘thousands of industries’. This echoes the ‘internet+’ era. The US and China are competing in different races: America runs the frontier-model competition, China runs the full-stack race including power grids and supply chains. Neither has fully entered the other's competition.

— Afra Wong
13:18

83% of Chinese are optimistic about AI, but resignation is mixed in

Surveys show 83% of Chinese believe AI will improve their lives and work, against only 38% in America. Afra's first explanation isn't confidence—it's ‘no choice’. China lacks organized public channels to effectively say no; the historical narrative convinces people the state is walking a path, and AI waits at the end of it. Participation becomes nearly Darwinian necessity. The second explanation is more concrete: past rounds of industrial upgrading genuinely improved ordinary life. Her 81-year-old grandmother once walked five hours to buy a clock; now she speaks commands to smart lightbulbs and chats with Doubao (豆包).

— Afra Wong
16:22

Liangzhu is a vibe-coder town built from escapees

Liangzhu is a small town called Meilishuixiang (美丽水乡) on Hangzhou's edge—cheap rent, leisurely life. It draws engineers laid off from mega-apps or fleeing 996 culture, plus young graduates who moved straight in. They don't build WeChat or Alipay; they make odd, niche products: fortune-telling apps, apps for people with ADHD or dyslexia, focus apps. These people are voting with their feet, deliberately opting out of China's mainstream tech races.

— Afra Wong
25:23

AI training courses in China have become an MLM-style industry

Afra visited Shengcaiyoushu (生财有术), which claims to be one of China's largest AI education companies. Its office sits beside Alibaba HQ. It has 80,000 paying members at roughly 3000-4000 yuan each—about half a Hangzhou person's monthly salary. The membership system is designed like ‘dragon pearl’ upgrades: ‘navigator’ to ‘deep sea circle’—the top is a CEO club. She divides payers into three: college grads and non-elite school graduates with no good path, small factory owners wanting to overhaul supply chains or write Amazon listings, and solo vibe coders needing office space and peer community.

— Afra Wong
31:31

In Liangzhu's fortune-telling app cluster, only one actually profits

Liangzhu has five to ten identical astrology apps running at once; most have single-digit users. Many entrepreneurs claiming to ‘do something meaningful’ actually serve three, seven, eight users. The FateTell she wrote into the New Yorker is the only profitable one. Its founder once considered becoming a Daoist monk; he believes his body is ‘water-dominant’ while Afra's is ‘fire-dominant’, so he had her attend the opening ceremony to balance the office's energy.

— Afra Wong
38:37

Since the pandemic, China is sliding toward low-trust society

Asked if China remains a high-trust society, Afra directly says no. Post-pandemic Chinese society has become more zero-sum: civil service exams are textbook—once someone secures a slot, they risk being reported or envied; people now describe daily life using Liu Cixin's ‘dark forest’ logic—opportunities shrink, suspicion and wariness between people grow.

— Afra Wong
44:42

Local governments use AI junk-water to prove relevance; 90% of companies lose money

Afra noticed many cities untouched by tech—Qingdao, Wuhan, Zhengzhou—rushed into AIGC fastest. Local governments are risk-averse, eager to prove to Beijing they're investing in ‘strategic industries’; AI-generated short video is the lowest-barrier entry. Result: extreme head-concentration in short drama. Only about 0.16% of content becomes viral hits bringing revenue; 90% of AIGC companies actually lose money. The industry shows ‘ghost-city effect’—next year an estimated 5 million AI short dramas will be produced, but most will find no audience.

— Afra Wong
52:56

The AI boom is replaying China's production-surplus cycle

Tracy uses pig farming as analogy: China heavily subsidized pig farms after swine fever, then faced severe oversupply within a year or two, sending prices into vicious boom-bust cycles unbroken to this day. Same logic applies to AI—central command says prioritize AI, provinces pile in; some develop competitive tech (like EVs), others dump AIGC junk-water to meet quotas. Neither she nor the host figured out how this junk-water model actually monetizes.

— Joe Weisenthal/Tracy Alloway

Glossary

RSI (Recursive Self-Improvement)
AI becomes continuously stronger through self-iteration, a theoretical path toward exponential progress.
dark forest
Liu Cixin's sci-fi concept describing mutually wary, zero-sum competitive survival logic.
OPC (one-person company)
A startup venture run independently by a single person, common form among Liangzhu vibe coders.
Bazi
Chinese traditional destiny calculation based on birth time, also called Four Pillars.
AIGC
AI-generated content, referring in this episode mostly to low-quality junk-water short dramas and videos.
