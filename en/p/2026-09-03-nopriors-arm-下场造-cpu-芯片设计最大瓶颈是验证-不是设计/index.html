Arm Builds CPUs: The Biggest Bottleneck in Chip Design Is Verification, Not Design

The real time sink in the chip cycle is verification, debugging, and documentation, not RTL; Arm already has 80%-90% of engineers using AI daily and has broken its 98.5% gross margin licensing model to build CPUs itself.

Arm builds CPUs itself because customers are pushing for it

Arm originally only sold IP licenses with a 98.5% gross margin, never touching inventory or wafers. The step forward to compute subsystems already had customers grabbing the pre-integrated 'subsystems' of CPU, GPU, and memory as modules. Going further, because 'customers wanting general-purpose CPUs for the large-model era couldn't find a supplier,' they came directly to Arm to place orders. Before the announcement, Arm asked nearly all licensing customers; opposition was minimal: a larger software ecosystem benefits every customer, so companies making Arm server chips—Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, Google—all publicly supported it.

— Rene Haas
8:07

Banning AI tools is like sending engineers back to the library

The total chip design cycle is 24-36 months; the most time-consuming part is not RTL generation or architecture mapping, but verification, validation, debugging, and documentation. AI is especially suited to these tasks. Internally, about 80%-90% of Arm engineers already use AI daily; turning off the tools would be like in the 1990s letting people use the internet only two hours a day and sending them back to the library downstairs for the rest. Currently AI's weakest areas are RTL generation and physical design, because models rely on public corpora while the industry's most critical details are private data—exactly the gap that companies like Arm can fill.

— Rene Haas
10:35

From idea to tapeout takes five years, not two or three

When asked whether AI, by eating the most time-consuming verification step, could shrink the 24-36 month cycle to 6-12 months, Rene Haas gave a measured answer: 2-3 years is unrealistic; beyond five years, for designs with less complex constraints, going from idea all the way to GDSII (the tapeout file handed to the foundry) is possible. If you feed the tool 'make a chip that is 10% faster, 20% cheaper, and 30% more power-efficient than a given reference,' don't expect a one-click completion. His judgment is that in 5-10 years, the industry's design methodology will change significantly.

— Rene Haas
15:00

AI supply and demand are far apart; the bottleneck is at the construction site, not the wafer

Discussing the AI bubble, Rene Haas distinguished between stock-level valuation froth and the industry chain itself; he stated clearly that supply 'is still far from' demand. Transformer inference and training consume both compute and memory, so demand won't stop. The real hard constraint will shift to data center construction: many projects are being delayed and need more manpower, and there is growing resistance to new data centers in various places. In the next 3-5 years, the constraint may not be wafer capacity or memory, but the persistently tight physical construction link.

— Rene Haas
17:14

SoftBank's cloud move gives chip companies a customer

For chip/AI entrepreneurs in heavy-asset, high-capex businesses: capital availability itself is a gate, and strategic partnerships should reach the three levels of supply chain, PE, and banks early. Behind Arm is SoftBank, not just as a shareholder; SoftBank just announced it will build a 'neo cloud'-type cloud service, allowing young chip companies to avoid having to only knock on Microsoft's or Google's door for design wins, and instead form a direct customer relationship with the Arm/SoftBank ecosystem—an extra path to market.

— Rene Haas
21:25

Humanoids won't dominate; specialized robots will coexist long-term

Rene Haas believes Robot 1.0 is purpose-built: both mechanics and software are optimized for a single action, and changing a production line means almost starting over. New-generation robots that can redefine tasks through training and have a general-purpose mechanical body will first eat repetitive labor in construction, infrastructure, and security. Form factors won't converge on a single direction: factories, warehouses, and tools are all designed to human dimensions, so humanoids have an advantage; other tasks are better suited to specialized forms. Costs must come down first, and delivery and warehousing will be the earliest automated scenarios.

— Rene Haas
27:13

Curbing chip exports to China is an unwinnable infinite game

He is direct on U.S. manufacturing: America should build more fabs domestically, for both national security and supply chain diversification. On export controls, he speaks of an 'infinite game'—restricting chip exports to China cannot produce a victory with an end, and may instead drive key technologies out of the U.S. He also denies that data centers are 'big warehouses with no jobs': the ecosystem chain of energy, liquid cooling, etc., can all create jobs in America.

— Rene Haas
36:00

In the token world, every path goes through the CPU

Using the token factory metaphor for accelerators, Rene Haas thinks the real system problem is: who orchestrates and arbitrates where tokens go, and delivers them to users? He believes every path will go through the CPU: beyond training, there is inference scheduling, system orchestration, context and tool invocation. At the edge, on devices that can't fit a 50W GPU, Arm's power advantage is more pronounced. The CPU hasn't disappeared; instead, it creates sustained demand alongside accelerators.

— Rene Haas

Glossary

IP licensing
A business model where a chip company sells design blueprints rather than physical chips.
compute subsystem
A pre-integrated semi-finished design from Arm that bundles CPU, memory, and other components, leaving customers less room for modification.
RTL
Register-transfer level: a code-level description of a chip's logic, sitting between architecture and physical layout.
GDSII
The physical layout file format that chip designs are finally handed to the foundry for manufacturing.
