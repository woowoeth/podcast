The brain is a computer, not philosophy — and that's the commercial route around drugs

Max Hodak's bet: wiring the brain as a computer gets you effect sizes and iterability that small-molecule drugs can't — Prima's retinal prosthesis is already approved for sale in Europe, but the field of view is like looking through a straw, and it's black and white.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:03

Restoring sight isn't the mission, it's the cash flow that funds the mission

Science was founded with two tracks: a biohybrid neural interface — no metal electrodes, no genetic modification, instead transplanting living neurons and letting them grow new biological connections; that's a big research project, and it has to be paired with a near-term business that makes money. They asked themselves ‘with the resources we have, and the state of the field in early 2021, what is the most valuable thing we could do’, and the answer was restoring sight to the blind. That structure determines every trade-off that follows: vision isn't the mission itself, it's the cash flow that funds the mission.

— Max Hodak
4:03

If the optic nerve is still there, you should do it in the retina

There are three injection points for restoring vision: the retina, the lateral geniculate nucleus in the thalamus, or V1, that half-billion cells at the back of the brain. Hodak says there's a pile of scientific and technical reasons all pointing to the same conclusion: as long as the patient still has an optic nerve, you should do it in the retina. They tried every variant — they built their own gene therapy targeting the retina (possibly entering humans next year), and they surveyed both electrical stimulators and ultrasound. The conclusion is that on the electrical stimulation path, the world's strongest work isn't in their hands.

— Max Hodak
5:03

The breakthrough in vision came from an acquisition, not a lab

The best electrical retinal stimulation approach belonged to a small French company, Pixium: the technology came from an inventor at Stanford, was licensed to it, and by the end of 2022 was already in clinical trials. Science had known them for several years, and then bought the company at a moment when ‘they saw something that at the time basically no one else saw’. After the acquisition it took about two more years to push it into an approvable state; approval for sale in Europe came in July, with first sales in the following weeks. This is the most operational takeaway in the episode: the breakthrough came from valuation arbitrage plus two years of engineering cleanup, not from a flash of insight in a lab.

— Max Hodak
8:04

Devices aren't better than drugs, they just let you keep fixing things after you fail

Hodak's argument isn't ‘devices are better than drugs’, it's that the failure structure is different: drug development runs ten years, finishes the clinic, and when you turn over the card it may be a no, and then everyone goes home; whereas on the brain-computer path ‘we have a clear sense of how to make the thing better’. He even brings in the highly engineered patient-specific CAR-T as a comparison — you might trade that for a massive immune overreaction. At the same time he doesn't sugarcoat Prima's current state: this is the first time anyone has restored form vision in a blind person's mind's eye, but the field of view is very small, like looking through a straw, and it's black and white. The next step is adding grayscale depth; red and green have a path, blue is harder.

— Max Hodak
11:05

This company looks more like a tech company than a biotech company

Hodak admits their position runs against the current in the industry: BCI is being broadened beyond motor decoding, and the seemingly pedantic question of whether a retinal prosthesis counts as BCI, once the answer is yes, opens up a whole swath of medicine that no one previously thought of this way. He attributes this to culture: biotech has always had an East Coast／West Coast split, and Science looks more like a tech company than a traditional biotech company; looking at historical biology problems through a device lens pushes it toward tech. The result is the financing structure — the money comes mainly from tech investors, and the only biotech VC he actively went after in the A round was Bob Nelson.

— Max Hodak
20:10

Models and brains have similar representations, and it's already usable as a tool

He hangs the reasonableness of investors flooding into BCI on the platonic representation hypothesis: look inside large models and those mathematical objects look a lot like what you see in neuroscience — the way a model represents concepts and the places in the brain that represent concepts have very similar geometric structure. Crucially, he says this isn't an observer's observation; Science is using it constructively: they can align neural recordings from animal brains with the internal representations of AI models. For him the significance goes beyond neuroscience — it's evidence that ‘AI isn't hitting a wall, there's something deeper and real here’. He also names the controversy: whether this structure is global or only holds locally isn't clear within the field, and there's a faction that simply doesn't want it to be true.

— Max Hodak
24:14

A prosthesis that can only see spots of light still sells for $150,000 a patient

Pricing isn't set yet, and he deliberately doesn't say much, but he lays out the anchors: about a decade ago Second Sight's retinal prosthesis could only produce flickering spots of light, patients had to piece together the meaning themselves, it couldn't deliver form vision, and each patient still paid about $150,000; a gene therapy that only matters to about 5% of patients in one narrow indication, delivers only a 0.1-line improvement, and basically just slows the rate of degeneration, reimburses at close to $500,000 per eye. His explanation is twofold: partly these therapies are expensive to develop and have a high historical failure rate, and partly vision matters so much that losing it is total disability. On addressable population, the current version is on the order of hundreds of thousands of patients in the US and Europe, and the next generation (in animal trials now, possibly entering humans next year) expands to millions.

— Max Hodak
26:15

Apart from the brain, every other organ is just a supporting player

The core of his worldview: the only organ that even in principle can't be transplanted is the brain; the heart, pancreas, liver and lungs are, in his view, supporting players whose reason for existing is to keep the brain's activity going and interesting. The skull is the vat — the brain connects to the environment through very few lines, the cranial nerves and spinal nerves; the optic nerve is the second pair, and the vestibulocochlear nerve, which carries hearing and balance, is the eighth pair. So as long as you can get vision, hearing, balance, somatosensation and motor signals in and out of the brain, that itself is the endpoint. He points out that the two biggest causes of death are cardiovascular disease and cancer that has metastasized to the brain, and thinks both can be attacked this way, while neurodegeneration remains very hard. And if humans really are going to the stars, they'll have to adapt to hard vacuum — preserving yourself and updating yourself turn out to be the same project: substrate independence.

— Max Hodak
29:16

Direct brain-to-AI won't be faster; the bottleneck is 10 bit/s

He explicitly rejects the easiest BCI startup story to tell right now — non-invasive, high-bandwidth conversation with AI models. There are two layers to his reasoning. First, speaking or writing is itself thinking; the intuition that ‘there's a fully formed thing in your head and it would just be faster if BCI could read it out’ is misleading — you only find out it isn't formed when you actually sit down to write. Second, cognitive bandwidth already has a deeply evolved bottleneck of about 10 bit/s — have someone with an excellent memory fly over Manhattan in a helicopter and then draw what they saw, and over an hour or two it comes out to that order of magnitude, and there are multiple independent lines of evidence. He concedes that wearing a hat and walking around asking AI questions via inner monologue might be feasible (some combination of EEG plus MEG), but that might be like AR glasses: attention is already 100% occupied, and moving it onto your face doesn't change anything.

Glossary

form vision
Being able to make out a coherent face or paragraph and read with your eyes, rather than scattered spots of light.
retinal prosthesis
A chip implanted under the retina that bypasses dead rods and cones and stimulates the retina directly.
biohybrid neural interface
An interface route that transplants living neurons to grow new biological connections instead of using metal electrodes.
platonic representation hypothesis
Different models and brains converge during learning on the same underlying representational structure.
connectomics
Mapping all the neural connections in a brain; mice are close, humans are still far off.
substrate independence
Mind doesn't depend on a particular physical substrate and can be migrated to run on another one.
