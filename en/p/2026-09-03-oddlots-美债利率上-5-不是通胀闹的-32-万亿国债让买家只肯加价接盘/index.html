The 5% Treasury Yield Isn't About Inflation: $32 Trillion of Debt Means Buyers Will Only Take More

Inflation can't explain a 30-year above 5%: even with no inflation and no default risk, a buyer already full up on Treasuries at 5.3% won't budge for another $10 billion. What's really steepening the curve is $2 trillion of new supply a year, and Treasury buybacks can't hold it down.

The mainstream narrative pins the 30-year at 5% on inflation; this episode offers a genuinely additive alternative framework, folding the limits of Treasury intervention, the mechanics of Fed balance-sheet runoff, and the term premium debate into one logic.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:04

5% is first a Treasury interest bill, not a macro signal

Host Joe asks Daryl what he sees when the 30-year Treasury yield is above 5% — even after it ticked down slightly that day following the Fed chair's remarks. Daryl's answer isn't a macro model but Treasury's predicament: if I were the Treasury secretary, what I'd see is that US government interest expense is extraordinarily high, and I'd have to find a way to push yields down. That line sets the frame for the whole episode: the official side has a strong incentive to push down long-end rates, but whether it can and whether it should are the premise for everything that follows.

— Daryl Duffy
5:07

Treasury wants to sell another $10 billion, it can only pay more

Daryl gives the single most important thought experiment of the segment: suppose there's no inflation risk and no sovereign default, and you're a macro hedge fund that already holds $20 billion of 10-year Treasuries. The Treasury secretary calls and asks you to buy another $10 billion — why won't you? The answer: at a 5.3% yield you've already made the allocation you want, and to get you to add, you'd have to be paid a higher interest rate as compensation. This logic incidentally explains the structural shift on the demand side — foreign central banks are already full up and no longer buying, so new Treasury supply can only land with domestic free discretionary buyers: banks, pensions, insurers, hedge funds, and that money is highly sensitive to yield.

— Daryl Duffy
8:10

The logic didn't change; the debt stock hit a tipping point

Joe raises the objection that has to be faced: aging populations, a political system with no appetite for spending cuts, unsustainable deficits — this bearish logic existed in 2018 and 2019, and Japan was talked about this way for decades before that, so why did yields keep falling then and why are they rising now? Daryl's answer is a scale effect: the 60% debt/GDP red line the IMF drew back then is far behind us, France is already at 100%; the US Treasury market has grown from about $18 trillion to $31 trillion and keeps expanding. There's no new logic — the quantity of debt itself has accumulated to a tipping point.

— Daryl Duffy
10:13

The long end's 5% is mainly issuance, not inflation expectations

Since the short end is still pinned by high inflation and the Fed, why not attribute the high 30-year yield directly to inflation expectations? Daryl concedes inflation over the past five years has indeed been significantly above the Fed's target and the job of getting back to target isn't done, but points out: implied inflation expectations backed out of real versus nominal bonds aren't flashing alarm; the investors actually buying and selling 10-, 20-, and 30-year Treasuries aren't processing inflation first in their heads — they're processing supply relative to demand. He mentions John Cochrane's fiscal theory of the price level, but that's a long-run framework — the main driver of the term premium right now is issuance, not prices.

— Daryl Duffy
13:18

Buybacks to cap yields are just a signal, with far too little ammunition

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced an expansion of Treasury buybacks, with the official rationale being liquidity. Daryl reads the subtext directly: from the wording of his public remarks, he doesn't think the problem is market liquidity, he thinks yields are ‘too high’, and he says outright that he's sending a signal to the market. The problem is that the size of Treasury's operations is a drop in the bucket relative to the whole Treasury market, and the market moved briefly on the news before quickly reversing. Trying to use the government's own resources to suppress the path of yields has a precedent in the 1992 sterling crisis — and Bessent was the man at Soros's fund who shorted the pound.

— Daryl Duffy
19:22

Buybacks' real job is sweeping up old bonds, not managing rates

The original design of the buyback program wasn't rate management: as Treasury keeps issuing new securities, a large stock of illiquid off-the-run old bonds is left in the market, clogging dealer balance sheets and trading away from a smooth yield curve. A paper Daryl is working on with two New York Fed economists is testing whether sweeping up the fragments on a ‘regular, predictable’ schedule and swapping in new issues can improve market liquidity and earn taxpayers the gain from buying low and selling high. The phrase ‘odd lots’ is exactly where the show's name comes from. He thinks this routine operation works; Treasury stepping in during an emergency to defend its own bond market is a different matter.

— Daryl Duffy
25:30

The constraint on runoff is reserves, not the asset side

A new Fed chair wants to shrink the balance sheet, but most people only stare at the asset side. Daryl points to the arithmetic constraint: selling assets requires extinguishing an equal amount of liabilities. Of the three liability buckets, TGA can't have Treasury pull its deposits out, currency in circulation can't be recalled, and the only thing that can be squeezed is commercial banks' reserves at the Fed. But today's reserves are the ‘Swiss Army knife of the financial industry’: they pay interest at market rates, satisfy liquidity regulation, and are usable for payments at any time, so banks have no incentive to give them up. The ratchet effect Acharya and Rajan laid out at Jackson Hole in 2017 already showed this: every time the Fed expands its balance sheet, banks get addicted to reserves, and runoff necessarily causes market volatility.

— Daryl Duffy
28:33

The task force will swap duration, not actually shrink the size

Daryl states he has no inside information on the task force, but makes two predictions about the group led by Jeremy Stein: first, the real problem isn't size but composition, and the sensible move is to gradually replace the Fed's long-dated Treasuries with short-term bills, so that reserve interest expense on the liability side offsets bill interest income on the asset side in the same direction, reducing the hit to the Fed's own P&L from swings in the policy rate; second, keep no mortgage-backed securities on the asset side and let them roll off naturally. His own conclusion is blunter: the Fed doesn't necessarily have to shrink the balance sheet, but it must have the tools in hand to shrink it on its own, or it will be backed into a corner when Congress applies pressure.

— Daryl Duffy
30:00

The term premium is a window on the deficit, not noise

The episode closes with a rare live confrontation. Joe confesses his ‘amateur’ framework: the 30-year yield is just 30 overnight rates added together, and the so-called term premium can't be measured accurately, its models contradict each other, and it has no analytical value. Daryl counters that the term premium itself is an easily measurable concept — it tells you the value of long-term money relative to short-term money; what's hard is splitting it into the expected path of short rates and a risk premium, and John Cochrane and Monika Piazzesi did the best work on that decomposition. He also assigns Joe reading homework: when Treasury supply rises, the whole yield curve gets pushed up, and the term premium is a window on the path of the fiscal deficit.

Glossary

term premium
The portion of a long-term Treasury yield that exceeds the market's expected path of future short-term rates — the compensation for holding long-duration government debt.
fiscal dominance
When government debt is so large that the central bank's monetary policy space is constrained by absorbing that debt and by political pressure.
off-the-run Treasuries
The batch of Treasuries the US Treasury no longer issues, which are less liquid and often sit on dealer balance sheets.
ratchet effect
Once Fed balance-sheet expansion leads banks to hold more reserves, banks are unwilling to give them back, making runoff hard to push through.
Treasury General Account (TGA)
The deposit account the US Treasury holds at the Fed, from which the government's day-to-day spending is disbursed.
