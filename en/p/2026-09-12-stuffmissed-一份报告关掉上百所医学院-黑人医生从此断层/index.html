One Report Closed Over a Hundred Medical Schools, and Black Doctors Were Cut Off for a Generation

The Flexner Report is usually written up as the starting point of modern medical education, but it also closed five of seven Black medical schools, and for nearly forty years no new Black medical school appeared.

6:09

The report's author was not a doctor but an education reformer

Abraham Flexner was not a member of the medical establishment. In 1890 he ran an experimental school with no fixed curriculum, no exams and no grades, and its students still got into good schools; in 1908 he wrote The American College, A Criticism, attacking American higher education, which is why Henry Smith Pritchett of the Carnegie Foundation came to him and asked him to turn the same critical eye on the medical schools of the United States and Canada. He spent two years travelling to 155 medical schools, recording admissions requirements, faculty, finances, laboratories and hospital affiliations school by school, and in 1910 delivered Medical Education in the United States and Canada. It was precisely because he was an outsider rather than a member of the medical community that the assessment dared to be so unsparing.

14:23

Medical schools were still teaching obsolete knowledge

In the fifty years before the report, bacteriology, diagnostics and surgery were all changing fast, but medical schools had no uniform standards. At many schools the faculty were practising physicians who taught the way they themselves had been taught and did not update the content. The result was that the care the doctors they produced gave patients was, at best, outdated and, at worst, life-threatening. Admissions standards were just as chaotic: when the report was published, 74% of medical schools required only a high school diploma, some accepted even a high school equivalency certificate, only one in five required more than two years of college coursework, and many schools were essentially for-profit businesses.

— Tracy V. Wilson
18:31

He recommended closing 120 of the 155 schools

Flexner set standards first and then graded school by school: admission should require college-level biology, chemistry and physics; the curriculum should include classroom and laboratory work in anatomy, pathology, bacteriology and pharmacology; there should be a teaching hospital for hands-on practice; and teachers should be salaried, full-time teaching and research staff rather than practising physicians moonlighting as instructors. He published the results by state and by school, naming dirty laboratories and lax standards, and criticising curricula that left students sitting for hours listening to dull lectures by part-time teachers. In the end he recommended closing 120 of the 155 schools. Within a decade the number of American medical schools was down to half, the surviving schools reformed their standards and curricula, and laboratory and clinical teaching were substantially modernised. In Canada only one school made the closure recommendation list, and in the end it did not close.

— Tracy V. Wilson
23:43

He never did the books on Black medical schools

The report was equally unsparing toward Black medical schools, writing that five of the seven "can make no valuable contribution at this moment," and endorsing only Howard and Meharry as worth developing. But the assessment did not take into account that Black medical schools charged far lower tuition than white schools, had smaller endowments, and were already running on extremely thin margins. He wrote that "the Negro needs good schools rather than many schools," and said that one of the most important duties of Black doctors and Black nurses was to teach hygiene rather than life-saving skills — a statement that is itself racist. Every Black medical school he named as inadequate closed: the first four closed before 1914, the fifth in 1915, and the last held on until 1923.

— Tracy V. Wilson
25:48

The next Black medical school would not come until 1966

Howard and Meharry survived and are still there today. But the next medical school aimed primarily at Black students was Charles R. Drew Medical School in Los Angeles, which did not open until 1966, and then in response to the health-access needs of the Black community; the one after that was Morehouse School of Medicine in Atlanta in 1975. The direct consequence of narrowing the options was a huge care gap: many potential doctors were shut out of educational opportunity by geography, and Black patients were often cared for by white doctors who, even at best, carried inherent racial bias. An expert panel convened by the AMA's Institute for Ethics in 2005 concluded that Flexner's "good schools rather than many schools" ensured that medicine remained segregated, and that the communities that most needed doctors were the ones left without them.

— Tracy V. Wilson
26:51

The AMA was paying behind Carnegie's project

One fact not disclosed at the time: the Carnegie Foundation hired Flexner for the project, and the AMA was also a sponsor. There are also unresolved questions — whether someone connected to the AMA accompanied him on some of the school visits. His accounts differed over time; sometimes he said the person went with him, and later he changed his story to say they merely met there, so whether the report was subject to more direct directional influence cannot be determined to this day.

— Tracy V. Wilson
28:53

Did he know he was restricting Black medicine?

He knew. The report contains an idealistic passage saying that building up Howard and Meharry would benefit the nation far more than barely sustaining more schools. In 1933 he wrote to the board of the institute he had founded, arguing that hiring and admissions "shall not directly or indirectly take account of race, religion or sex." One explanation comes from his biographer Thomas Neville Bonner: when Flexner was growing up in Louisville, the local Black community had more services and opportunities than in many places, with a dozen or so Black schools and vocational training in medicine and law for Black students; the Flexners were not well off, there were Black families among their neighbours, and he and his siblings often played with Black children in the community. This may have left him overly idealistic about the reality of Black family life, and he later admitted that it was not until he began studying educational institutions that he realised how disadvantaged those Black classmates of his had been.

— Tracy V. Wilson
35:01

The hygiene line may not have been his own

The claim that hygiene was the main duty of Black doctors is one of the key pieces of evidence for judging Flexner a racist. But the leaders of Meharry and Howard had said similar things in 1907 and 1908 — two or three years before the report — and those leaders were also white. This suggests Flexner's formulation may have been a restatement of the schools' established mission rather than a judgement he arrived at independently. In 2011 Lynn E. Miller and Richard M. Weiss, writing in the Journal of the History of Medicine and Allied Sciences, proposed another possibility: that this language was in fact opening up a public health role for the doctors at these schools in order to win support across racial divides — because those statements about hygiene being the most urgent need were immediately followed by the conclusion that these institutions obviously ought to be supported. In 2008 the AMA issued a public apology for its history of racial inequality.

Glossary

Flexner Report
The 1910 publication Medical Education in the United States and Canada, which reshaped North American medical education standards.
AAMC
The Association of American Medical Colleges, founded in 1876 with 22 member schools, which had itself agreed on uniform standards for the medical curriculum.
The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge
A 1921 memorandum Flexner wrote for the Rockefeller Foundation board, later published as an essay, arguing that the pursuit of pure curiosity yields unexpected uses.
