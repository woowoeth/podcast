Cook's Death Mystery: Killed as a God or as a Fraud?

Two anthropologists have debated for decades whether Hawaiians treated Cook as a divine incarnation, with neither convincing the other. What is certain: the collapse of this sacred aura led to him being stabbed to death on the beach.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

26:46

Cook's Route Matched Lono's Sacred Procession, Making Hawaiians See Him as Divine

When Cook returned to Hawaii, it coincided with Makahiki, the annual harvest festival during which Hawaiians process an idol of the deity Lono (Orono) along the coast, eventually carrying it to the temple at Kealakekua Bay. Anthropologist Marshall Sahlins argues that Cook's sails, shipping route, and landing site entirely mirrored the idol's processional path—sheer coincidence that led Hawaiians to treat him as Lono's incarnation. Cook unknowingly ‘played’ the role: priests anointed him with piglet fat and crowds prostrated before him, all while he stood impassive, allowing the ceremony to proceed.

29:56

Sahlins' Divine Cook Theory Was Attacked as Colonialist Projection, Then Defended

Princeton anthropologist Gananath Obeyesekere fiercely attacks Sahlins' theory, arguing that ‘Orono’ was a term Hawaiians also used for their own kings—so Cook was treated as merely a liminal figure between human and divine, not a true god. Sahlins counters immediately that Obeyesekere is imposing a 20th-century American rational framework onto 18th-century Hawaiian thought, which is itself a form of arrogance. The two reach no consensus even now when interpreting the same historical evidence. The host admits they cannot truly reconstruct how Hawaiians actually thought.

34:59

The King's Grand Parting Gift Was Probably a Ritual to Send the Deity Away

Before Cook departed, King Terriaboo presented him with large quantities of pork, vegetables, and elaborate feather cloaks, with great respect. Crew members later speculated that this grand gift-giving was actually a ritual ‘sending off’ the deity—if Cook was truly a divine incarnation, he should depart after manifesting, not linger. This interpretation proved prophetic: a few days later, storm damage forced Cook's ship to return to Kealakekua Bay, violating the expectation that the god should have already left. This may have prompted a fundamental recalculation among Hawaiians about who Cook truly was.

— Dominic
43:11

Cook's Return to the Bay Shattered the Entire Atmosphere of Reverence

Cook's ship suffered mast damage in a storm and was forced at the last moment to turn back to Kealakekua Bay instead of continuing to Maui to find another anchorage. What greeted them on return was no longer enthusiastic canoes and white cloth, but only a few scattered vessels and a dead-silent coastline. Hawaiians were no longer interested in axes and chisels—they wanted only daggers. Theft became brazen, and attitudes shifted from reverence to mockery and provocation. The host offers one explanation: if Cook was truly treated as a deity, he should not reappear after the festival ended. This ‘return’ itself disrupted the calendrical and ritual balance and might have made people suspect that he was not a god at all but a fraud exposed.

— Dominic/Tom
54:30

Cook's Attempt to Seize the King as a Hostage Sparked the Massacre

After a small canoe is stolen, Cook opts for his usual tactic—the method he has used in Tonga and Tahiti—taking King Terriaboo hostage to retrieve the vessel. The operation proceeds smoothly at first; the king even agrees to board the ship. But as they cross the beach, the king's wife rushes forward pleading on her knees while several chiefs restrain him physically. The king sits down on the sand. The crowd swells rapidly to hundreds, possibly thousands. Cook realizes that forcing the king's departure ‘cannot be done without killing people.’ He orders the marines to line up along shore on alert. The situation spins entirely out of control.

— Dominic
57:40

One Officer's Misinterpretation of Cook's Order Left Him Without Escape

In the chaos, Cook waves toward the support boats. Later oil paintings interpret this as Cook ordering the marines to cease fire and demonstrate his humanitarian restraint, but the host suggests this is almost certainly a misreading—Cook was actually signaling the boats to draw closer for evacuation. However, Lieutenant Williamson, commanding the support boats (the same officer who had previously fired without authorization during the initial landing), misunderstands the signal. He interprets Cook's wave as an order to evacuate and withdraws the boats, completely abandoning Cook's group, and even threatens to shoot any subordinate who refuses to obey. This one misunderstanding leaves Cook and his marines with zero naval support.

— Dominic
1:00:55

The Priest's Question Hinted That Hawaiians Might Not Believe Cook Was Dead

After a heavy blow to the head, Cook falls and is stabbed in the neck. He dies in the shallows. Afterward, Hawaiians return his remains piecemeal, wrapped in cloth with black and white feathered cloaks—first a charred piece of thigh, then days later his hands (identified by their distinctive scars), skull, jaw, and feet. Much of his torso never arrives. Most strikingly, a priest asks second officer King a question he finds ‘highly unusual’: when will Cook return to the ship to resume his former position, and how will he treat the crew left behind? This hints that Hawaiians might not have actually believed Cook was dead at all.

— Dominic
1:10:13

Cook's Historical Image Pivoted From Enlightenment Saint to Colonial Destroyer

Oil paintings from the 1780s–90s typically portrayed Cook standing alone on the beach, raising his hand to stop soldiers from shooting—the image of a ‘victim of his own humanity,’ depicted as a martyr to Enlightenment reason and compassion. But in contemporary times, especially in New Zealand, Hawaii, and Australia, the evaluation has completely reversed. Writer Anna Della Subin in 2022 called Cook a man who, ‘before becoming a god, was already burning entire villages and carving crosses on indigenous bodies’—characterizing him as an incarnation of colonial violence. The host reflects that they once defended Cook in 2023 but now recognizes that his loss of emotional control and behavioral contradictions during the third voyage make the ‘saint’ image untenable.

— Dominic/Tom
