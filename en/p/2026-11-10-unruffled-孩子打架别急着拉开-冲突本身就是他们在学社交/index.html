Stop Rushing to Pull Kids Apart: Conflict Itself Is How They Learn Social Skills

Frequent yelling, biting, even slapping you—often doesn't signal a broken child, but rather what happens when parents intervene: conflict becomes a competition for ‘Mom's attention’.

7:17

Both stay in the conflict by choice: it's not one-sided victimhood

The letter writer believed her two-and-a-half-year-old son upset his four-and-a-half-year-old sister through unreasonableness. But Janet notes that conflict almost always goes both ways: if the sister truly didn't want to engage, she could walk away entirely. Both children kept participating in the argument, which reveals they both wanted it on some level—this is precisely how children learn and get to know each other through conflict, not through victim-and-perpetrator relationships.

— Janet Lansbury
8:24

When parents intervene, conflict becomes a power struggle with them

What often escalates conflict isn't the children themselves, but parental discomfort with what's happening. Once parents step in, what unfolds between the kids quietly becomes a game of ‘who can get Mom to come’—which explains why these squabbles almost always occur within earshot, not when parents are absent. The more parents resist the urge to intervene, the more the conflict stays with the children to resolve themselves.

— Janet Lansbury
12:44

Younger children's provocative moves are usually bids for connection

Janet challenges the assumption that younger children are always innocent victims. Frequently, it's the younger child who deliberately starts the conflict—even infants who can only crawl know which toy to reach for to provoke older siblings. Because they prefer any interaction to complete disconnection, they'd rather be yelled at than ignored. Understanding this, parents stop reducing conflict to ‘who started it’.

— Janet Lansbury
13:44

Set the agreement before conflict: resolve it yourselves, I come if you call

Janet suggests having a conversation during a calm moment, not during conflict. Tell your children that you've noticed them yelling, biting, and hitting; you've been rushing in to make decisions for them, but now you want to give that back—you'll only step in if they actively call for ‘Mom, help me’. This advance conversation establishes their capacity to handle conflicts far better than intervening in the moment.

— Janet Lansbury
17:55

If you do intervene, there's only one rule: neutrality, always

If the children actually call you in, Janet's core principle is absolute neutrality: don't speak for either side, don't judge who's right or wrong, don't take a team. She stresses this is harder than simply deciding who's at fault, but this kind of impartiality lets them feel you love both equally, rather than becoming one more judge in their lives.

— Janet Lansbury
18:57

Stop the hit without removing the child: less intervention is better

When physical intervention is needed, Janet's advice is ‘do the smallest, most effective thing’—block with your hand, hold their wrist—not pick them up and carry them away. Picking up and removing appears more decisive, but it sends the message ‘this is a serious problem that must be stopped immediately’, leaving the child feeling cast as either the perpetrator or the victim. In the letter, when the mother picked up her son, he slapped her face—exactly the kind of backlash that comes from overintervening.

— Janet Lansbury
23:59

Explaining one child to another isn't reassurance, it's insecurity

Citing Siblings Without Rivalry, Janet explains why the mother's comment—‘he's only two’—fails to console the older sister. That kind of remark frames the sister as more mature and capable, seeming to side with her, but it actually reveals that she too could be dismissed or compared based on age at any time. It plants anxiety, not real safety or connection.

— Janet Lansbury
26:01

Light-touch parenting isn't the same as tolerating abuse

To the common pushback—‘I was beaten by my older brother and my parents never did anything’—Janet separates ‘trusting kids and intervening when necessary’ from ‘total abandonment’. She points out that the child who habitually bullies siblings is usually the one who has absorbed the deepest parental rejection and judgment; that displaced pain gets redirected at siblings. Parents maintaining neutrality with both children—genuine impartiality rather than indifference—is what breaks the cycle.
