Damp explosives might have kept World War One from ending in 1915

One night a fortress gate failed to blow because the charges were damp. Austria-Hungary narrowly avoided immediate collapse—and the program argues that this and similar contingencies explain why World War One continued past 1915 until 1918.

A fortress's job is buying time, not winning battles

Przemyśl's real value was not whether it could stop the Russians, but that it cut off rail lines and supply routes, giving the routed, demoralized Austro-Hungarian field army a chance to breathe. After the front collapsed, the fortress positioned the troops close enough—within tens of miles and weeks of respite—to rebuild discipline and morale, preventing total collapse within the first month and a half of war. From the start, the fortress existed to give an entire army a chance to recover.

19:18

Brusilov's actual objective was Berlin, not Przemyśl

Russian commander Brusilov insisted on assaulting the fortress not for the city itself but for the Galician rail network it controlled. Capturing it would let him command all transport lines across Galicia, push north, and drive into Germany's industrial heartland of Silesia, reaching Berlin. For him, Przemyśl was only a gate to a larger prize. He believed this supposedly obsolete fortress would fall in days.

— Alexander Watson
25:20

Damp explosives saved an entire defensive line

On the night of October 6, during a major assault, Russian soldiers reached the top of redoubt number 11 through darkness, set up ladders, and prepared guncotton to blast open the gate while containing the Austro-Hungarian garrison inside. But it had rained that night. The explosives were wet. They ignited with a hiss and smoke but did not detonate. One technical failure—that single gate did not open. Both analysts believe that if the explosives had fired that night, the entire line would likely have collapsed, and the war's course might have been completely different.

— Alexander Watson
30:22

An unnecessary withdrawal turned the fortress into a trap

By November, when the Austro-Hungarian forces made their second ordered withdrawal, the fortress was no longer strategically necessary. It lacked food for a long siege—the garrison should have evacuated with the army, leaving an empty city. But Przemyśl had become a household name through its September stand, and Austria-Hungary could not bear to abandon the prestige it had already invested. They kept the garrison inside to hold on, turning it into the spark that ignited a series of brutal, futile relief offensives.

— Alexander Watson
31:23

A winter bloodbath in the Carpathians has been forgotten

To rescue the trapped garrison, Austro-Hungarian forces spent the entire winter fighting the Russians in the Carpathian Mountains. Temperatures dropped to minus 20 degrees Celsius, and neither side had prepared for winter warfare. Combined casualties reached between one and a half and two million—on the scale of the Somme or Verdun. Yet because the Eastern Front is marginalized in historical memory, and because this region endured an even worse war after, this bloodbath has been nearly erased.

— Alexander Watson
36:29

When supplies ran out, horses became rations

As the siege dragged on, food itself became a weapon. The city held about thirty thousand civilians, one hundred and thirty thousand soldiers, and twenty-one thousand horses. One-fifth of the garrison lay in hospital from malnutrition. At first, horses hauled ammunition and supplies. As the siege lengthened, their fodder was diverted to humans, and the horses themselves were slaughtered for meat. By March 1915, when the siege ended, nearly 100 percent of the garrison's horses were dead—a reminder that industrial war consumed not just men.

— Alexander Watson
38:29

The commander chose a suicidal charge to preserve honor

With ammunition spent and food gone, the garrison could not actually break out. But commander Kusmanek ordered a final assault—not westward toward home but eastward, toward Kiev and Moscow. Terrain-wise the ground was flatter, but more fundamentally, this direction preserved a legend, a sense of honor, over any real chance of escape. The charge collapsed in a single night. The garrison then ordered all redoubts and bridges destroyed and the remaining horses shot.

— Alexander Watson
45:31

After the siege, three nations faced separate forms of ethnic cleansing

When Russian forces occupied the city, they applied different methods of cleansing to local Poles, Ukrainians, and Jews. Polish nobility were arrested and stripped of power. Russia denied Ukrainians were an independent nation, calling them "little Russians," closed Ukrainian-language schools and papers, and arrested intellectuals. About one hundred thousand Jews were forcibly expelled; seventeen thousand received notices to leave in late April 1915. Scholars emphasize that this large-scale forced migration of Jews predates Hitler's genocide.

Glossary

Galicia
A pre-war Austro-Hungarian border province, today divided between Poland and Ukraine, which Russia regarded as part of its rightful territory.
little Russians
The Russian Empire's label for Ukrainians, used to deny that Ukrainians constituted an independent nation.
ring of steel
A concept introduced by this episode's guest referring to the Entente Powers' total economic blockade of the Central Powers.
guncotton
An early high-explosive compound used to breach fortifications and bunker gates.
Battle of Gorlice-Tarnów
A major 1915 offensive led by German forces that routed the Russian army and recaptured Przemyśl.
