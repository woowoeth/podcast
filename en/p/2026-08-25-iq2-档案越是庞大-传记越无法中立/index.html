The Fuller the Berger Archive, the More You Should Doubt: Home Is Where Two Axes Cross

What Berger left behind is not conclusions but a viewing mechanism: the fuller the archive, the more you should doubt; home is where two axes cross; his anti-colonial stance never changed.

9:59

The archive was donated, but the 1960s were still in an air-raid shelter

Berger donated his archive to the British Library in 2009. When Tom took it on, he found the material incomplete: its strength is the manuscripts from the 1970s onward, when Berger and Beverly were living in the Alps; the 1960s are almost blank. Tom asked Berger directly why there was nothing from the 1960s, and the answer was hidden in an air-raid shelter in a Geneva apartment — after his partner died, the early material had been piled up there. Organizing the archive is therefore not mere cataloguing but a journey of filling gaps one by one.

— Tom Overton
14:00

What migrants lose is the intersection of place and time

The through-line Tom found is Berger's relationship with the dead. Berger wrote about peasant culture and migration: human society exists on two axes — place is the vertical line, time the horizontal line, and home is where the two coincide; those driven away by migration lose this intersection. His remedy is art: language is the only human home, and reproduced artworks can also become places where people re-experience home and connect with generations. Tom says he was not entirely convinced at first, until he later realized this may be connected to the genocide he was witnessing in real time — in his later years Berger was extremely committed to the Palestinian cause.

— Tom Overton
17:10

He was not a party member, but anti-imperialism never wavered

Berger considered himself a romantic Marxist. He never joined the party: early on he wanted to join but was considered too heretical; after he became famous the party invited him and he refused; during the 1956 Hungarian events he could articulate the party line better than actual members, and in 1968 he sided with the dissidents. The real constant appeared earlier — in the 1950s the editor of the New Statesman showed Berger Britain's role as an empire. From then on he stood unconditionally with decolonization struggles, and in his later years firmly opposed the US-led world order; the empire changed form throughout his life, but his opposition to empire never changed.

— Tom Overton
23:52

The most crucial author of Ways of Seeing was uncredited

Ways of Seeing is often seen as the opposite of Kenneth Clark's Civilisation, but when Berger was young he always wanted to invite Clark to see his painting exhibitions, and Clark's wartime art affairs also shaped the understanding that "art has a social role." The program itself was a collaboration: Mike Dibb did the visuals, Richard Hollis the graphic design (he also designed the cover of G), Chris Fox the research, and the most crucial uncredited person was Ana Berger — she provided Berger with different translations of Walter Benjamin's theory of mechanical reproduction and brought in the perspective of the women's liberation movement. Berger effectively updated Benjamin's argument from the age of photography to the age of television.

— Tom Overton
28:14

Women can make these books, and they can burn them too

Tom deals with the "womanizer" reputation by talking about responsibility: he is almost equally familiar with the people in Berger's life, must answer to each of them, and cannot write the book as someone else's biography. Ana Berger is the most important example — she shaped Berger's intellectual horizons, and the ideas of women's liberation entered Ways of Seeing through her; without her, Berger could not have brought these ideas to television. His first wife helped him finish his first novel, and they broke up immediately after it was done; she burned a large pile of Berger's manuscripts. Stories can be created by women, and they can be erased by women.

— Tom Overton
32:33

The biographer has already become part of the archive

Faced with piles of archives, one can mistakenly believe one has all the facts; Tom says that returning to the work made him understand that he must acknowledge his own perspective. He cannot even be neutral anymore — he interviewed people around Berger, spoke with Berger himself, and has himself become part of the archive. He mentions Berger's almost Montaigne-like ending in Ways of Seeing: "Please doubt what I say" — this both disarms you and acknowledges his own limits. Traditional biography pretends the author is invisible; this book puts the author back on the table.

— Tom Overton
34:36

Who is paying for this — that question has not expired

Tom breaks down four reasons: first, the openness and generosity in all of Berger's work, leaving a place for the reader; second, luck — it caught the moment when color television was just emerging; third, collaborators like Mike Dibb and Richard Hollis were themselves geniuses; fourth, it teaches people to question the media environment they are in. Berger's starting question back then was simple: who is paying for this. Today, when you hear "this episode is sponsored by...", that question has not expired.

— Tom Overton
38:09

The living standards of rich countries rest on migrant labor

Tom says if you read only one Berger today, he would choose The Seventh Man from 1975: it is about Turkish guest workers in Germany and argues that the living standards of rich countries as a whole rest on the exploitation of migrant labor; at the time some people already drew the same structural analogy to different groups in Britain. Tom believes this questioning of "where does your living standard come from" is absent from current discourse and is a necessary correction. Paired with photographer Jean Mohr's graphic book, it also demonstrates the irreplaceable power of images and words respectively.

— Tom Overton

Glossary

Ways of Seeing
Berger's 1972 television series and book of the same name: seeing is never a neutral act.
romantic Marxist
Berger's self-described position: it wants artistic imagination, and also class and anti-colonial politics.
The Seventh Man
A collaboration with photographer Jean Mohr: Europe's affluent life rests on the exploitation of migrant labor.
guest workers
Foreign temporary laborers recruited by West Germany after World War II; in the book, specifically Turkish workers.
fourth wall
The imagined boundary between stage and audience; Berger breaks it directly in the program to speak with viewers.
