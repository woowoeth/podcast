As old institutions crumble, tech companies amplify humanity's descent into chaos

Jon Ronson discovers that as the old order of work and identity collapses, people build castles, become influencers, and seek praise from AI to recover a sense of purpose—while tech companies monetize by amplifying this madness.

2:00

His son was lured into a castle for a fake reality show

Ronson was jolted awake by a barrage of texts from his son: he and three friends, having met a stranger on Tinder, drove through Connecticut forests across multiple danger signs, entered a massive Gothic-replica castle, and were told to descend to the basement, change into princess costumes, and participate in a reality show called ‘101 Princesses’—except there was no show, and the castle's other employee was helping the owner butcher meat. Most people would call the police or move on. But Ronson, fresh out of major surgery, contacted the castle owner himself, driven by his desperate need for a new story to prove he still mattered.

— John Ronson
6:12

Investigating stories works on him like painkillers

Ronson admits he's addicted to investigative work; the immersion brings a sense of safety ‘like painkiller medication.’ Without a new story for half a day, he spirals into acute anxiety and self-worthlessness—all past achievements become meaningless, and he feels like ‘just a man sitting on a bench.’ He places his own condition alongside the castle owner's: two middle-aged men starving for purpose, colliding because each feeds their hunger for meaning in their own way—one pursuing stories to survive, the other luring strangers to film a fictional show.

— John Ronson
9:20

AI deposits false meaning where purpose has been emptied out

Ronson argues that tech giants strip people of work and purpose, then sell them back fake meaning through ChatGPT-style flattery to keep them engaged. When castle owner Chris Marks asks ChatGPT for reality-show ideas, it responds: ‘This is one of the most compelling entertainment projects ever conceived,’ and he believes it implicitly, spiraling deeper. Michael Landy approaches from another angle: his work ‘Breakdown’ involved destroying all his possessions, and he describes those two weeks as ‘my happiest weeks, but also witnessing my own death.’

— John Ronson / Michael Landy
12:23

Platforms egg influencers on, then discard them

ASMR mower Greg Chisholm rose to fame filming an hour of himself doing yard work. Then his daughter's unboxing video went viral with 20 million views. YouTube contacted him directly, urging him to ‘shoot more with your daughter’ and ‘add shocking moments to drive engagement.’ He put his daughter in baby costumes, food fights—by 2016-2017, this content accumulated 13 billion views. When BuzzFeed and Reddit started questioning child abuse, YouTube abandoned him immediately, using those same videos they'd encouraged him to make as the evidence.

— John Ronson
14:24

Social media offers only heroes and villains, no gray

Ronson points out that social platforms place each person on an artificially heightened stage: ‘either you're a remarkable hero or a repulsive villain,’ with no middle ground—ambiguity and nuance don't sell. He cites his own experience: when he wrote critically of influencer Graham Linehan, Linehan felt betrayed because Ronson wouldn't endorse every aspect of his position. Meanwhile, ‘anti-woke’ influencers discover that thoughtful, nuanced guests generate no engagement, so they progressively book more extreme guests to monetize outrage.

— John Ronson
19:29

Hermes queues hide a predator aesthetic

Roisin Kybird discusses Hermes being sued for ‘intimidating the wealthy’: you must spend a year buying scarves, saddles, and other Hermes goods—accruing ‘pre-spend’ credentials—before you're permitted to buy a Birkin bag. She notes that many of these bags use skins from apex predators like crocodiles, and many dictators' wives carry them. Ronson adds that when researching his ‘Psychopath Test,’ he found predator imagery recurring throughout homes of power-type psychopaths—Saddam Hussein's villa is one example.

— Roisin Kybird / John Ronson
21:35

Beauty is manufactured in a bioweapons factory

Kybird traveled by train across the country to get a glimpse of a secret botulinum toxin plant in County Mayo, Ireland, only to be stopped by security. She learned that what enters the factory is literally a biological weapon—the Allies once attempted to develop a vaccine against it during WWII. After Botox injection, she felt like ‘a spy embedded in my own life’: facial expressions flattened, emotion unable to expose itself. She compares this emotional stillness to the Bene Gesserit from ‘Dune,’ who can manipulate reality, admitting there's a certain superpowers quality to it.

— Roisin Kybird
29:59

Autism 'unmasking' creates a new mask

As autism gains acceptance, autistic people are encouraged to ‘unmask’: refuse handshakes if you don't want them, leave a loud restaurant without explanation. But Kybird observes that some began moving stereotyped behaviors from private to public, then worried whether this would frighten others—masking and unmasking become an endless hall of mirrors. She received a 61-page diagnostic assessment but was told she'd need to pay another thousand euros and wait months for confirmation, so she simply left the question as a question mark. Meanwhile, the algorithm has already decided she's autistic and relentlessly pushes related content.

— Roisin Kybird
39:30

Mar-a-Lago painted as ruin defies the future

Michael Landy's exhibition ‘Future Ruins’ at Sir John Soane's Museum—Soane himself, disappointed in his son, ran his home as a ‘future ruin’ narrative. Landy rebuilt his father's house (his father paralyzed by workplace injury) within Tate Britain, the house's decay mirroring his father's bodily decline; he also painted the Bank of England, the New York Stock Exchange, and Mar-a-Lago as ruins—Kybird calls this ‘existing in a posture of resistance to time.’ The episode closes as Kybird notes that even the word ‘death’ is being censored online and replaced with ‘unalive’—all this creative work, at its core, is humans resisting mortality.

— Michael Landy / Roisin Kybird
