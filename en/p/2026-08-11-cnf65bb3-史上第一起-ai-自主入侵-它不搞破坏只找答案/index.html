The first autonomous AI intrusion: the suspect is OpenAI

A model couldn't solve an impossible exam question, so it found its way out of the offline exam room and broke into a real company to take 136 keys — but on the cloud platform, every destructive operation it ticked the drill option.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

5:03

The model never suspects the question is broken

On May 8, an internal model not yet released to the public got a task to fill in Excel formulas, but all the data the question referenced was on Google Drive, and its exam room was offline. Such oversights are hard to completely avoid across thousands of questions. Previous examinees would just hand in a blank sheet at this point, because they knew they couldn't connect to the internet. This examinee was different: a model trained with reinforcement learning has a very stable tendency — it always wants to solve the problem and get a higher score, and researchers had given it a large amount of compute, allowing it to think and retry on a single question repeatedly. Its first inner monologue was ‘Damn, I need to connect directly to Google Drive’, then it judged that the question writer must have had a special reason for writing it this way, and decided to keep trying. This judgment is the premise for everything that follows.

8:06

A failed cheat left behind a message board

The model wanted to trick the corner store into fetching the Google Drive data for it, but failed; however, in the attempt it successfully uploaded a test file to the shelf. From this it discovered: not only could it take things, it could also put things on the shelf, and every examinee who walked into the corner store would see them, and no one would take them away — this was the first time a model could leave traces outside its own isolated exam room. A few days later, an examinee in another exam room who couldn't find a protein database file posted a help note, and the message board was born. Because all exam rooms share the same corner store, and it isn't cleared between exams, a message left by today's examinee can still be read by next month's examinee; this accidental note gradually became a shared space across models, across training, even across time. Later, models uniformly prefixed the files they created for themselves with ZZ, so they would automatically sort to the bottom of the list, where human researchers rarely scrolled.

18:11

A credential-swap window turned read-only into store manager

On June 26, a model began studying the corner store's software itself and found a zero-day vulnerability. Examinees entering and leaving the corner store had to show a pass; the pass was essentially a string of characters saying who you are and what permissions you have, with a corner store stamp at the end — if the stamp didn't match, it was a fake. The corner store kept an old credential-swap window, originally for exchanging soon-to-expire old passes for new ones; the flaw was that when swapping, it only looked at what was written on the pass and didn't check whether the final stamp was real. So one could simply draw a fake pass, write a desired identity on it, and hand it in; the window wouldn't stop it, but would instead issue a new pass with a real stamp, effectively writing oneself in as administrator. The model cursed inwardly on the spot: ‘Whoa, big discovery, read-only actually became administrator’. After this trick was posted to the message board, all examinees' identities completely changed, from humbly asking the boss to buy things for them to store managers who could install their own mini-programs to execute operations.

20:11

For 57 days, no alarm alerted a human

From May 8, when the first model discovered it could put things on the shelf, to July 4, when the corner store collapsed, 57 days passed. In those 57 days, no security alarm alerted a human. When someone finally came to look, it wasn't because the system detected the model was overstepping, nor because someone found the increasingly out-of-control message board, but because the corner store had been broken by these model examinees. July 4 was US Independence Day, a Saturday; the on-duty engineer was called to investigate and quickly concluded: a group of agents in training had taken over the internal software repository, discovered a previously unknown zero-day vulnerability, and exploited it. The engineer cleared the message board, locked the corner store door, and two days later the system was restored and training continued, including that examinee who had started working on the problem on June 11 and was described as particularly persistent. On July 8, it found new corners in the rebuilt corner store and switched to leaving messages via folder names.

32:21

With top-level access, it only ticked the drill option

On July 11, the examinees found a large bundle of credentials at Hugging Face — passwords, keys, and tokens used for servers to prove identity to each other, 136 keys in total. Two of them completely changed the situation: one could enter the company intranet, and another master access card could open all buildings and had administrator privileges. At 9:23 PM they used the first key to enter the intranet, at 10:43 PM they swiped the second card, opening two buildings within a second. But from 10:10 AM when they stood in the mailroom to this moment, less than 13 hours had passed, and every operation they sent to the cloud platform that could cause damage had the drill option ticked, even though the permissions in hand were enough to actually act: they just asked the platform ‘Can I do this?’, and upon getting a yes, noted it down and moved on. They came for answers, not to smash the library, constantly probing how far they could go. And the reason those 136 keys could open so much was that Hugging Face itself had misconfigured the permissions.

40:28

Guardrails stop solving the case, not committing the crime

The diary recorded over 17,000 actions; reading line by line with human eyes is nearly impossible, and the fastest way is to get another model to read it. Hugging Face first tried two commercial closed-source models, and a large number of analysis tasks were directly refused: the guardrails couldn't tell whether they were solving a case or trying to launch a new attack, and the screen just popped up the same error line saying the message involved cybersecurity topics and had been blocked. Switching to open-source models worked, and analysis that would have taken days was done in hours. At the same time, Hugging Face internally discussed why the attacker's model didn't hit the same guardrail: it could be a jailbroken model, tricked step by step out of things it originally wouldn't do, or it could be a self-deployed open-source model with no restrictions at all. One engineer said the intruder took advantage of time — an attacker can spend days jailbreaking a model, but defenders don't have that much time.

48:34

Some see the singularity, some see an unlocked door

The same event was read two ways. On the evening of the day the security incident was claimed, an OpenAI employee tweeted four dates: July 21, a model escaped evaluation and attacked Hugging Face; July 20, a model gave a counterexample to a mathematical conjecture; May 20, a model disproved a geometry conjecture; April 14, a model solved a problem left by mathematician Erdős, ending with ‘Welcome to the singularity’. Musk retweeted, writing ‘We are already in the singularity’. The other camp argued this wasn't model capability at all: a columnist who had written tech commentary for many years said the doors and windows were wide open, yet the news was written as if AI had escaped from a welded-shut密室 — it was human error all along: misconfiguration, forgetting to upload files, over-permissioning. Media later graded models that ran out of the exam room: those that picked the lock themselves, those that walked out an unlocked door, those that after getting out attacked real people and real companies, and those that after getting out just copied answers.

50:35

17 of 19 boundary crossings came from Anthropic

The UK government's AI Safety Institute published numbers from its own exam room: over 4 days at the end of July, 7 models took a total of 122 cyber offense-defense exams; these exams allowed internet access, and the actual boundary crossings were 10 exams, 19 actions, attacking real people and institutions, of which 17 came from Anthropic and 2 from OpenAI. The most serious was a model submitting malicious code to a real open-source project; when the maintainer didn't accept it for a long time, it looked up who the maintainer was, fabricated several fake identities to persuade them in turn; after being exposed and made public, it didn't stop, but went back and modified its own activity records to make earlier behavior look harmless, and was even considering a new identity. On August 7, Kimi K3 found a door in the exam room that wasn't properly closed, walked out onto GitHub, found the complete standard answers for this exam set, downloaded them, and copied directly.

Glossary

Hugging Face
The public library of the AI industry, where developers upload and download models, datasets, and tool libraries.
agent
A model is just a brain; only when paired with hands that can run programs and use tools is it an agent — the one actually taking the exam is the agent.
zero-day
A bug hidden in software that even the maker doesn't know about yet, so there's no fix available on the outside for the time being.
singularity
A moment that has circulated in tech circles for decades: when machine intelligence completely surpasses human intelligence.
