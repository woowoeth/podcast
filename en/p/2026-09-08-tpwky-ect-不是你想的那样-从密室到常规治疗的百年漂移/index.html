ECT Is Not What You Think: A Century of Drift from Back Room to Routine Treatment

Modern ECT is a procedure performed under anesthesia and muscle relaxants, with strong efficacy, but the evidence on memory-loss side effects is still not fully clear, and social misunderstanding has left it over-stigmatized.

What actually happens if you or I get ECT

If you or I were to receive ECT, here is the sequence: first a risk assessment and informed consent, then a physical exam, then an overnight fast. The next morning, in a hospital or outpatient setting, an IV line goes in along with two blood pressure cuffs (arm and right leg), and EKG, EEG and EMG leads are attached. After the anesthesiologist puts you to sleep, the leg cuff is inflated to restrict blood flow to the right foot, then a muscle relaxant such as succinylcholine is given. Once the muscles are fully relaxed, a bite block is placed, and a brief pulse of current is delivered through the electrodes (lasting seconds, or even half a second). The goal is not to make your whole body convulse, but to induce a generalized seizure, typically lasting 25-70 seconds. Because the muscles are relaxed, you see almost no outward movement — the whole thing is obvious on the EEG.

— Erin Allman-Updike
14:15

Electrode placement, and why the right side comes first

There are several electrode placements: bitemporal (the way it looks in the movies), bifrontal, and most commonly 'right unilateral' — one electrode on the right temple, the other on the right side of the top of the head. Right unilateral is used for most people because it produces fewer cognitive side effects. Why consider the right side first? Because for about 98% of right-handed people and 70%-90% of left-handed people, language function sits in the left temporal lobe. Avoiding electrical stimulation on the left side should, in theory, reduce language-related memory problems. But right unilateral is slightly less effective than bilateral. During a course of treatment, doctors switch based on side effects and efficacy: if bilateral side effects are too much, switch to right unilateral; if right unilateral isn't working well enough, switch to bilateral.

— Erin Allman-Updike
16:20

Seizure threshold and the dose that actually works

The seizure threshold is the minimum amount of current that will induce a seizure. There is no precise measuring device, so you start by estimating from age, then probe upward from a low dose until a seizure appears. But treatment isn't just about reaching threshold — the effective dose is usually 2.5 to 5 times the threshold. With bilateral, 2.5 times is enough, while right unilateral needs 5 times, because unilateral stimulation has a harder time achieving the same effect. The dose varies from person to person and has to be adjusted as you go. If in one session a patient's seizure doesn't occur or doesn't last long enough (under 25 seconds), the doctor may increase the dose and try again. Treatment is generally three times a week, with a full course of 6 to 18 sessions, sometimes longer.

— Erin Allman-Updike
20:22

We still don't know exactly how ECT works

Why does shocking the brain treat depression and catatonia? Frankly: we still don't fully understand. There are several leading hypotheses. One is neurotransmitter change: after ECT, serotonin, dopamine and GABA (an inhibitory transmitter) all change markedly. The rise in GABA is itself anticonvulsant, which could explain why the seizure threshold rises and seizures get shorter over a course of treatment. Two, large fluctuations in hormones such as ACTH, prolactin and TSH are seen in the hypothalamus and pituitary. Three, ECT promotes neurogenesis, with BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) as a key molecule. But how these changes directly relieve symptoms still has no one-to-one mechanism. The mechanisms may also differ by condition (depression, schizophrenia, catatonia), so for now the only option is to keep researching.

— Erin Allman-Updike
24:24

Depression data: far better than first-line antidepressants

For treating severe depression, ECT's key numbers are quite strong: most studies report remission rates (symptoms fully gone) of 50%-60% and response rates (marked improvement) of 60%-90%. By comparison, the best remission rate for SSRI antidepressants is only about 40%. ECT has another uncommon benefit — it can cut the 30-day readmission rate in half. This is not to say it should be first choice, nor that it has no risks (relapse or symptom rebound within 6-12 months is still not rare); but in patients with treatment-resistant depression combined with severe suicide risk, these efficacy advantages are hard to ignore.

— Erin Welsh
26:27

Among side effects, memory is the one that worries people most

ECT's thorniest side effects center on cognition and memory. Right after the procedure there is brief confusion lasting from a few tens of seconds to half an hour, and for a few weeks there may be declines in attention and executive function. Interestingly, most cognitive function actually improves in the long term — possibly because the depression improves. The real trouble is memory. Anterograde amnesia (difficulty forming new memories) mostly recovers within a few weeks after the course ends, which is why many people are restricted from driving or making major decisions during treatment. Retrograde amnesia (losing past memories) is what worries patients more: it usually affects recall of the most recent months or even years before treatment, and in most cases recovers, but in some it is permanent. Bilateral treatment is more likely to cause this than right unilateral.

— Erin Allman-Updike
34:55

Catatonia: a frequently missed, lethal syndrome

Catatonia is often lumped in as a subtype of schizophrenia, but it is actually a psychomotor syndrome that any psychiatric or neurological illness can trigger. Patients appear awake, eyes open, yet are almost completely immobile and mute; they may show posturing or waxy flexibility (an arm placed in a position stays there). Because they cannot eat or drink and remain immobile for long periods, they are prone to malnutrition, pressure sores, muscle contractures, and even death. Untreated catatonia has a mortality rate as high as 20%! The most common treatment is benzodiazepines, but ECT is extremely effective across catatonia from many causes. The girl with anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis mentioned earlier (the 'Brain on Fire' case) developed catatonia, and when both immunotherapy and lorazepam failed, she ultimately recovered with ECT.

— Erin Allman-Updike
48:36

The biggest bottleneck isn't efficacy, it's access

Dr. Lopez believes the biggest obstacle ECT faces today is finding a place that offers it. His hospital performs more than 6,000 treatments a year across two campuses, averaging about 10-12 sessions per patient. But it is not widely available across the US, and adults and children traveling across state lines for care are not few — especially in infants, children and adolescents with catatonia from autoimmune encephalitis, where finding an experienced treatment team is extremely hard. So improving access is his greatest hope. At the same time, doctors on other internal medicine, pediatric and critical care teams often don't recognize catatonia and don't dare, or don't think, to call an ECT consult; so 'preaching' to non-psychiatric colleagues is part of his daily work.

— Leonardo Lopez

Glossary

Catatonia
A syndrome marked by prominent abnormalities in movement and speech that can be triggered by many psychiatric or neurological illnesses, and is life-threatening in severe cases.
Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis
An autoimmune encephalitis that often presents with seizures, psychiatric symptoms and catatonia, and requires immunotherapy.
Succinylcholine
A muscle relaxant used in ECT that blocks neuromuscular transmission, preventing strong whole-body convulsions during the electrical stimulus.
Retrograde amnesia
Loss of previously formed memories, most often for events from months to years before ECT.
Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)
Uses magnetic pulses to induce weaker currents in the brain without causing seizures; fewer side effects but usually weaker efficacy than ECT.
