Obeying Incentives Is Loser Behavior: You Should Decide Right and Wrong for Yourself

Economists say fix the incentives and you fix the world. Mastroianni says that misses half of it: even if you have a reason to do wrong, you still shouldn't do it. Outsourcing your value judgments to an incentive structure is abandoning the core task of being a person.

8:07

Having a reason to do wrong doesn't mean you should

Mastroianni first concedes that incentive analysis beats ‘people are bad, so they do bad things’: at least it admits people have reasons, and changing the reasons might change the behavior. But he adds a premise economists often miss — if you do it because an external reward structure tells you it's good, because it will pat you on the head or give you a little treat, that is loser behavior. His exact words: even if you have a reason to do a bad thing, you still shouldn't do it. He jokes that this is an obvious point, but some points are so obvious that no one thinks to say them.

12:21

The real Omelas is a Rube Goldberg machine

In Le Guin's Omelas, the whole city's happiness rests on a tortured child in the basement, and some people choose to walk away. Mastroianni says that story was in his head when he wrote the piece, but he cut a section: in the story you have some metaphysical connection to the child, whereas in reality the connection is more like a Rube Goldberg machine — you aren't the one torturing the child, you're three warehouses away, just the hammer that swings down, hits the ball, the ball rolls down the slope, lights the match. The bad thing is far from you, you didn't do it with your own hands, but you're in the causal chain. That makes ‘walking away’ much harder and far less heroic: it doesn't look like brushing the dust of Omelas off your feet, it looks like ‘I'm not going to be VP of brand partnerships for Philip Morris.’

— Adam Mastroianni
17:27

Outsourcing value judgment means being conscripted by the wicked

Russ asks: are you saying we should encourage people to be more moral? Mastroianni says he doesn't think that can be centrally organized, demanded, mandated or cultivated by government — when government tries, it tends to go bad fast. His goal is to produce the kind of person who does the right thing even when it's inconvenient, even when it's costly. He offers a chain: if you only do what's rewarded, you can start out amoral and end up deputized for the immoral. His judgment: the wicked are few; what's plentiful is people without a strong sense of right and wrong, and those people can be turned into an army by the wicked.

— Adam Mastroianni
20:33

The big fish will find you; you decide whether to look

The ‘big fish’ comes from Lovecraft's Dagon: a man sees a fish god, is never right again for the rest of his life, and finally jumps out a window. Mastroianni uses it as a metaphor — everyone has a fish inside them, and if you really take it seriously you have to overturn your entire life. His own fish was the 2020 Society for Personality and Social Psychology conference in San Francisco: the venue was in the Tenderloin, and inside people ate canapés and talked about reducing implicit bias a tiny bit, possibly just noise, while outside people were curled up with fentanyl in their veins. He specifically mentions the 1970s seminary student experiment: randomly assigned to speak on the Good Samaritan or on career opportunities, then randomly told they were late or on time, and the only thing that determined whether they stopped for the man lying by the road was whether they were in a hurry. He says one of the most famous findings in the field, known to everyone in that room, and no one felt any conflict about it — that was the fish he saw.

— Adam Mastroianni

Sumner was caned and went back to provoking

Charles Sumner is the man whose statue Mastroianni walked past every day in grad school. He came from a poor Boston family, his father was illegitimate; the place where he grew up was home to large numbers of formerly enslaved people. He went to France, and at the Sorbonne he saw Black and white students studying together as equals, and wrote in his diary: the difference between people is obviously not caused by nature, it can only be caused by education. He became a radical abolitionist, arguing not just for abolition but for racially integrated schools and Black suffrage — things that only became common sense more than a century later. As a senator he gave a fiery anti-slavery speech, and days later a Southern congressman walked up to his desk and beat him nearly to death with a cane; he was trapped in the desk, soaked in his own blood, and took more than a year to recover. After recovering he went on being a provocateur, and pushed Lincoln to issue the Emancipation Proclamation two years early. His new biographer says Sumner is cited 40 times in the brief for Brown v. Board.

40:20

Desperate to join the club of people you don't respect

Russ cites Adam Smith: we want to be loved and to be lovely, so we should surround ourselves with good people whose approval we want. Mastroianni goes harder: he and the people close to him have spent their whole lives desperately seeking opportunities from people they don't respect, landing them in an absurd position — ‘I think they're bad people, I think they're completely wrong about what's good and bad, but I really want them to let me into their club.’ Into it for what? You will inevitably become more like them, and you can't control it. He says the moment you can say ‘I distrust this person's judgment so much that even though he could give me success, I'll go do something else’ — that's the moment of freedom he described earlier: that person has no power over you anymore.

— Adam Mastroianni
50:15

High pay doesn't buy you better professors

Russ says his own salary when he graduated was about $18,600 a year, and he's glad he didn't go into finance or a big company's chief economist office. He asks: economics professors can make $500,000 or even $750,000 a year — with more money, isn't it harder to maintain the norms of honesty and truth-seeking that founded the discipline? Mastroianni says this looks more like a selection effect than a corruption effect: the people attracted by $750,000 often aren't the people who can be good professors; the consequence of high pay isn't corrupting people who might have been good, it's attracting people who shouldn't be there and making them compete with each other. Russ presses: doesn't paying $750,000 get you the best professors? Mastroianni answers: what you want is a professor who puts truth above everything, and the more money there is, the more the people who come are the ones who don't put truth first — and our selection procedures can't tell who's in it for truth and who's in it for the salary, especially since many of the people doing the selecting came for the wrong reasons themselves.

— Adam Mastroianni
57:37

Status is harder to separate from knowledge than money is

Mastroianni says that in most places it isn't money that attracts the wrong people, it's status: being called professor feels good, so many people want it, so few get it. And status markers are harder to distinguish from knowledge markers than money markers are, because they claim to be knowledge markers — ‘you published in a good journal, so it must be good work.’ He says we've now reached the point where people talk about writing a specific paper for a specific journal, and call papers pubs or A-pubs, and he finds it vile and disgusting, like defacing a cross. He says this is why he isn't in academia: he was willing to earn less to study what he thought mattered, to tolerate job insecurity, to move all over the world, but if after all that it's still ‘why don't you publish a bunch of junk papers’ and ‘why don't you massage the statistics a little’, then it isn't worth it.

Glossary

Rube Goldberg machine
A contraption that performs a simple action through a long chain of complicated, indirect steps; a metaphor for a causal chain that pushes responsibility far away.
big fish
Mastroianni's metaphor: an unexamined belief that, once you face it squarely, forces you to overturn your entire life.
obedience to the unenforceable
Lord Moulton's phrase for the territory between law and freedom, governed by people's own voluntary compliance.
blackpilled
Internet slang for having completely given up hope on something and concluding it is doomed to fail.
