Paying Hip-Hop's Pioneers What They're Owed — and Some Thought It Was a Scam

Some didn't even show up to collect, because they didn't believe it was real. Handing out cash doesn't work in hip-hop — as Nas put it, you have to give recognition and dignity first, and only then can the money be accepted.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:04

A $10 album owed $5 million

The starting point is a debt Ben Horowitz owed himself: he once paid $10 for the Paid in Full album, and over his career he got at least $5 million of value out of that record, with no channel to pay it back — and he felt he had to. He called Steve Stoute first, and Steve said the idea wasn't just good but timely — everyone was saying these old rappers were broke and dusty, but nobody was actually doing anything. The second call went to Nas, who said Quincy Jones's Jazz Foundation model wouldn't work in hip-hop: you can't just give a person money, it has to be recognition of what he did and who he is, and it has to be more than money. So the form became an awards show and a Grandmaster Award, with Scarface and Rakim in the first class of honorees.

— Ben Horowitz
5:05

Some thought it was a scam and didn't show up

The foundation's first obstacle wasn't money, it was that nobody believed it. Ben says the early disbelief was beyond what you'd imagine, and for a while they had real trouble actually getting money into people's hands — some didn't even come to collect the award because they thought it was a scam, and others said "I don't need anyone giving me money." He treats that disbelief as evidence the design was right: it can't look like reparations, it has to be celebration and tribute, shining the light they're owed on them, and only then will it be accepted. Caz later put it more bluntly: the industry has shortchanged these people for so long that the first reaction can only be "why would anyone be helping us now."

— Ben Horowitz
10:07

The first hip-hop hit was stolen from him

Grandmaster Caz wrote the rhyme that became Rapper's Delight — his own name is even in the rhyme — and the first hip-hop hit was taken out of his hands just like that, and he never got what he was owed. Ben says everyone in the music industry knew about it, but nobody ever did anything, and nobody ever said anything. The first time he met Caz in New York he was stunned: this is a man who changes the energy of any room he walks into today, and he carries no bitterness at all, still thinking of himself as a star. Steve added: it's not that nobody knew, it's that not enough people knew — the industry knew, the public didn't.

— Ben Horowitz
17:10

Dr. Dre asked for an introduction to his idol

Steve Stoute tells a detail: Dr. Dre heard about Paid in Full and decided to come, then asked whether Steve could introduce him to Kool Moe Dee — Dre had never met him. Another time, in Las Vegas, Dre asked whether he could be introduced to Slick Rick, whom he had also never met. A Dre who has made it has to ask for an introduction just to meet these legends, which shows how fragmented the scene is — and getting these people into one room was something only this foundation managed to do. Caz puts it more directly: the industry separates people by success, status, and who's hot right now; for years he only ran into Nas by chance, and now they see each other once a year and can just call.

— Steve Stoute
32:20

The Grammys botched it, so they did it themselves

Quincy Jones was the legend of legends to this group, but when he died the Grammy tribute was called out flatly as botched. The Quincy Jones Award wasn't conceived to fill a slot — it came from seeing the Grammys get it wrong, and Quincy's son QD3 saying fine, we'll do it ourselves, which is how it became a fixed annual award from the foundation. This year's recipient is Nile Rodgers. Steve mentions that George Clinton showed up last year with his "mothership" prop and, at 83, recited the lyrics to Rakim's Follow the Leader on stage — a man who changed music, paying tribute to another man who changed music.

— Steve Stoute
39:24

Hip-hop has no social security

Caz explained what the money actually means: it's a grant paid out over five years, and he bought a house and moved out of the projects. He lived in the Bronx his whole life, and for the past ten to fifteen years his last thought at night was always "I don't want to die in the projects"; now he and his wife live in a house in Pennsylvania. He says hip-hop has no social security and no health insurance, you have to keep working, which is why you still see him doing radio, playing parks, and running hip-hop tours — because he has to. The money gave him something he'd never had: a buffer. He also made a point of saying money is only part of it; the real thing is being put in the light.

— Grandmaster Caz
44:26

Brands came back from the dead on hip-hop

Steve Stoute uses business data to argue why "paying the debt" makes sense: Adidas had only 2% of the U.S. market at the time, three-quarters of it from the Northeast, and the company just saw a bunch of people frantically buying the same shoe and throwing the laces away, with no idea why. Grand Puba's line "First things first, obey your thirst" later became Sprite's ad slogan. Tommy Hilfiger was selling ties that wouldn't move, drove through Harlem, saw everyone wearing their pants low with the Calvin Klein waistband showing, went back and made the underwear waistband wider and thicker with his own name on it, and only then did the brand take off. These brands exist because of what these people contributed.

— Steve Stoute
48:31

Love isn't writing a check, it's flying to Houston

Ben tells a story about his wife, who is also the foundation's chair, Felicia, to show how this organization differs from other charities: Scarface had a heart problem and needed open-heart surgery, but he came from a place where people barely even go to the doctor, he was scared, and on the phone he said he wasn't going. Felicia flew to Houston and said no, you're going now. Just as they walked into the exam room, his aorta ruptured, and the doctors got him in in time and saved his life. Steve adds the everyday version: she'll suddenly call to ask how you and your family are doing, and if someone in your family gets sick, flowers show up the next day, or whatever was agreed shows up.

— Ben Horowitz

Glossary

Paid in Full
The album name that comes up again and again in this episode, and also the name of the foundation; Ben says he bought it for $10 back then.
Grandmaster Award
The award the foundation gives each year to hip-hop's true originators; recipients join a committee that helps pick future honorees.
Jazz Foundation
The foundation Quincy Jones ran that sends money to old jazz musicians — the model Ben initially wanted to copy.
dusty
Hip-hop slang; Steve says people used the word to describe these old rappers, but nobody actually did anything.
the projects
Public housing developments in American cities; Grandmaster Caz lived in the Bronx projects for most of his life.
