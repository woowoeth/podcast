Doing Social Media in the AI Era, Your Boss Is More Hopeless Than the Algorithm

The real AI shock to marketing teams comes from the boss: outsourcing intuition to Claude, approving only what Copilot generates, so employees disguise their own ideas as Copilot screens to sneak them past approval.

4:06

Bosses have outsourced their intuition to Claude

Rachel interviewed roughly a hundred marketers and found that the main path AI takes into a team isn't tool procurement, it's the boss personally. A social media manager carefully makes a funny meme, sends it to the boss, and the boss drops it into Claude to ask "is this funny?" Even more absurd: some marketers discovered their boss only approves ideas that come from Copilot, so they first mock their own ideas up to look like the Copilot interface, then send that to the boss for approval. Rachel says what's behind this is the boss outsourcing their entire intuitive judgment to AI, while the team has no training or guardrails at all on how to use AI — she contrasts it with her own experience spending months learning Asana at Condé Nast.

7:14

Consumers don't hate AI, they hate being fooled

Taylor points out an overlooked fact: plenty of YouTubers now read AI-generated scripts straight to camera, and the comments still praise the "brilliant analysis, well-written script." In other words, when people don't know they're consuming AI content, they actually like it. What triggers rage isn't AI itself, it's the feeling of being played. Rachel adds the explanation for this double standard: an AI fruit romance meme like Fruit Love Island is low-risk meme territory, nobody cares about the craft of a meme; but when a brand you pay money to uses AI to generate a menu, you wonder how much else it's cutting corners on in the product. The same person can curse brands for using AI while forwarding an AI fruit meme to a friend.

— Taylor Lorenz
9:16

How-to creators are the first to be replaced by AI

Rachel splits creators into two categories. The ones who built their following on how-to content are most at risk — from the earliest YouTube beauty tutorials onward, the democratization of media ran along this path, but for questions like "how do I fix the dishwasher," AI is the better authority, and people will just ask AI directly. Taylor gives the example of her own mother, who recently proudly fixed a toilet with ChatGPT, which would have been unthinkable before. The other category of creator is actually safe: people doing deep parasocial content, doing commentary, doing things that aren't easily replicable can use these tools to make better content. The test is whether the content itself is replicable.

— Rachel Carton
11:28

Anti-data-center and anti-extinction people never allied

Taylor observes that people opposed to data centers and people worried about AI wiping out humanity are two groups with completely different motivations, and they still haven't formed any coherent alliance; she even half-jokingly says they should team up, but their interests diverge too much. Her own position is clear: AI won't kill us, "it's actually really hard to kill all humans on Earth, even if we wanted to." What she thinks genuinely deserves scrutiny is how people anthropomorphize AI and grant these products agency — especially dangerous in policy debates about assigning responsibility, because they are computer programs, not living things. She also quotes a former FTC commissioner: we survived the Black Death, and that only killed 50% of people.

— Taylor Lorenz
19:35

AI accounts are pulling Instagram's slot machine

Taylor thinks Meta is in a tough spot, because it is essentially a 2010s social network whose influence peaked in the 2010s, while Google is better positioned than Meta. In the short term, AI's impact on platforms is about scale and speed: one AI account can post 50 times a day, which is like constantly pulling Instagram's slot machine — eventually one hits. She checked the 20 highest-viewed reels at the time, and two or three of them were AI-generated, and all of them were very new. One used a "behind-the-scenes reveal" framing to show how a film crew shoots ocean waves, how the waves crash down a house — essentially using "proof of authenticity" as a shell to manufacture confusion. Her judgment: this is monetizing confusion, with short-term gains and a very bad long-term outcome.

— Taylor Lorenz
21:35

Threads is the most toxic social platform

Taylor says she recently posted a hot take calling Threads the most toxic social platform to date, far more toxic than Twitter — "Twitter compared to Threads is a beautiful wonderland." The mechanism is that the algorithm prioritizes replies extremely heavily, so you get dropped straight into someone's angry reply about something, which in turn feeds the algorithm and quickly escalates into a groupthink pile-on. She also points out that Threads' AI-generated news summaries "are just lying, making things up outright." Asked who uses Threads, her answer is her mom.

— Taylor Lorenz
22:36

The growth secret is turning your account into a series

Asked how to grow a following in the AI era, Rachel says the accounts growing now all have a clear throughline of familiarity in their content, like they're making their own TV show. Her example is a restaurant chef wearing Meta glasses filming himself cooking, who grew to about 500,000 followers in two or three months, under the account name Gil Holy Cooks, posting one episode a day. Taylor adds that content is becoming serialized across the board, and you really have to think about how to entertain people; the era of "share your life" is clearly declining, and in 2026 you should not become a lifestyle influencer — people have a lot of anger toward that type.

— Rachel Carton
24:42

Instagram handed out an official division-of-labor chart for formats

Rachel says Instagram once shared a very useful chart with her readers explaining what each content format is for: carousels and reels are for reaching new audiences and get pushed to non-followers, and what the current algorithm really feeds on is non-follower views — if your content is only intelligible to your own audience, the platform judges it "not entertaining enough" and withholds main-feed traffic. Single-image posts serve only your existing audience. Stories and DMs are treated by the platform as where community happens. She also quotes Adam Mosseri on Bloomberg Screen Time: the "social" in social media is no longer sharing family photos, it's DMing friends the thing that reminded you of them. She finds that pretty sad, but it does clarify what "social" now means on social media.

— Rachel Carton

Glossary

carousel
An Instagram multi-image swipeable post; the current algorithm uses it to reach new non-follower audiences.
reel
Instagram's short-video format, pushed mainly to non-followers just like carousels.
slop
Mass-generated, low-quality, quantity-over-quality AI content, such as "Fruit Love Island."
BTS
Behind the scenes; video showing the production process, used by AI to fake authenticity.
