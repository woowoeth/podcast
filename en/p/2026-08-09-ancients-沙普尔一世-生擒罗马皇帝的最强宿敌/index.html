The Persian King Who Captured a Roman Emperor Was a Enemy Rome Raised Itself

Shapur I twice destroyed Roman armies and took the emperor Valerian captive, making him Rome's most dangerous enemy in the East — but his victories were more a consequence of the third-century crisis than its cause.

5:15

The Sasanian dynasty was forged by Rome itself

Starting with Trajan, Rome repeatedly invaded Parthia, established provinces beyond the Euphrates, and steadily weakened the Arsacid dynasty over decades. The Sasanian family was originally a local governor family in Fars, and watched Parthian kings fail again and again to stop Rome, until in 224 they defeated and overthrew Artabanus IV and founded a new dynasty. The guest states explicitly that the Sasanian rise was directly tied to Rome's defeat of the Arsacid dynasty — a "blowback" from sustained Roman military pressure.

9:30

Shapur's bloodline was stitched together, not born

Shapur's origins are wrapped in a mass of legends: he may have been the son of Ardashir and a Parthian princess, or adopted, and there are even versions in which his mother was executed and he was hidden as a child. The guest notes that these contradictions are precisely evidence of "manufactured legitimacy." The scholar Khodad Rezakhani argues the Sasanian family did not come from the Persian core but from Sakastan, and therefore needed myth and inscriptions to stitch the old Arsacid royal line together with the new Zoroastrian monarchy. To understand Shapur, one must first understand this legitimacy project.

34:08

Shapur says he killed the emperor; no Roman source agrees

At the Battle of Misiche in 244, Shapur's inscription claims he fought a great Roman army, utterly destroyed it and killed the emperor Gordian III. But while Roman sources acknowledge Gordian died in this event, not one says he fell in battle: some say he was killed by his own troops, others that he died of wounds. The guest points out that a single battle leaving two sides' narratives is almost unique. This divergence itself reminds us that all of Shapur's "battle records" must be read within a framework of propaganda and counter-propaganda.

40:00

Taking 37 cities was no longer a border skirmish

After Gordian's defeat, Philip was forced to sign a treaty, but Rome violated it in the Armenian direction, and Shapur immediately launched a second large-scale offensive. After winning at Barbalissos he entered Syria, and his inscription lists the fallen cities one by one: Antioch, one of the richest cities in the entire eastern Mediterranean, was also taken. In total he captured 37 towns in the region. This was no longer a border skirmish but the Sasanians' first systematic sweep into Rome's heartland, directly setting the stage for Valerian's military gamble.

44:03

A Roman emperor was captured alive for the first time, and never returned

In the third campaign of 260, Shapur claims that while besieging Carrhae and Edessa, Valerian came with a 70,000-strong army drawn from Germania, Noricum, Pannonia, Spain, Africa, Thrace, Bithynia, Asia and elsewhere. Shapur's inscription says "we captured Valerian Caesar with our own hands" — the first time in Roman history an emperor was taken alive. Valerian never left Persia for the rest of his life; later tales of him being used as a footstool or flayed are unverifiable. Tens of thousands of captured Roman soldiers were settled in a new city, influencing its street grid and mosaics, and even bringing early Christian transmission.

47:37

What stopped the Sasanians was not the Roman army but Palmyra

After Valerian's capture, the one who actually pushed the Sasanians back was not the regular Roman army but the Palmyrene king Odaenathus — Zenobia's husband, originally fighting as a Roman ally. He pursued the Sasanians all the way, driving them back west of the Euphrates and allowing the Roman East to stabilise after 260. The guest specifically notes that in surviving sources this history often does not intersect with Shapur's victory narrative, yet it was this "third party" that determined Shapur's offensive ended here.

50:00

Shapur was not the cause of the third-century crisis but its result

The guest gives a clear judgment on whether Shapur worsened the third-century crisis: he did not create the crisis; his victories were a product of the crisis becoming embedded in the Roman state. But these victories provided moral "proof" for the preceding decades of campaigns against Parthia, and opened three centuries of border tug-of-war. In the later Sasanian period the two sides called each other brothers, writing in letters that they were "the two eyes of the world"; earlier still, Galerius had captured Narseh's whole family, completing Rome's "revenge." Shapur set the tone for this balance of power.

56:38

Apart from Hannibal, only Shapur counts as a long-term defeat

Discussing "Rome's greatest enemy," the guest proposes a ranking criterion: look at how Rome defined itself. Hannibal is the eternal answer — as late as the 7th century, Roman writers still held Scipio up as the ideal commander, and the man who defeated him thereby became the greatest enemy. Shapur can stand alongside Hannibal because what he inflicted was a long-term defeat; Arminius was more a psychological shock, and Boudica and Vercingetorix were ultimately suppressed. Mithridates is also nominated, but Shapur ranks higher on the dimension of "sustained damage."

Glossary

Shahanshah
Persian for "King of Kings," the title Sasanian monarchs claimed for themselves, signifying rule over other vassal kings.
Ērānšahr
The Sasanians' own name for their realm, meaning "land of the Iranians," as opposed to "non-Iranian lands."
Arsacid dynasty
The dynasty that ruled the Parthian Empire and was overthrown by the Sasanian family; its noble families still held real power in the Sasanian era.
