A disabled theatre troupe makes the able-bodied the discriminated minority — and it isn't asking for your sympathy

Yaoyao flips the whole social order: after a nuclear war most people are augmented, and the few unmodified able-bodied are the ones born in sin, the ones who have to fight for their rights. This isn't a lament — it's a power fantasy written for disabled people.

7:15

Disabled women being unable to leave the house is the norm

Yaoyao's earliest work was the documentary Hanyā (寒鸦), about three disabled women's struggles with family education and employment. One of them, a woman she calls jiejie, had not left her home in 36 years; when her parents took her out to visit relatives at New Year, what she felt was ‘like being walked like a little dog or cat’. Later she got the first computer of her life, went online, and found a job as a telephone operator — no degree required, only a sweet voice. Once she had the job she bought herself a wheelchair and rolled out of the house. Another woman, a visually impaired mother, was allowed to breastfeed her baby for only five minutes before her mother-in-law took the child away, on the grounds that ‘you can't see, you can't raise a child’; for years afterward she fought her husband's family for custody. These accounts made Yaoyao realise that being unable to leave the house was not an isolated case.

— Yaoyao
11:19

Disabled people's friends are all disabled people

A former colleague of Yaoyao's uses a wheelchair, and when she moved she could only ask friends who also use wheelchairs; they hung the chairs front and back with belongings and hauled load after load. Yaoyao instinctively asked why she didn't call able-bodied friends who can walk upright to help — it would have been done in half a day. The colleague paused, then said, I don't know any able-bodied people — she did not have a single able-bodied friend, and even her friends all used wheelchairs. Yaoyao says she didn't know how to continue the conversation, and realised only one thing: if disabled and able-bodied people never even get the chance to meet, ‘you have no chance at integration to speak of’. That was the direct starting point for her later search for a drama teacher and her work in integrated theatre.

— Yaoyao
14:19

Writing the able-bodied as the discriminated ones born in sin

Leyi Theatre's first play, Huí Zhuǎn Wèi Lái (“逆转未来”), does a straight identity reversal: a nuclear war turns most people disabled, and they become new humans through technological modification — electronic eyes, cochlear implants and the like; the surviving minority of able-bodied people, guilty of ‘the crime of starting the nuclear war’, become the ones born in sin, marginalised by society. Yaoyao's logic: since she herself is a situs inversus person, she might as well flip the whole society too, turning every right disabled people now have to fight for into a right the able-bodied have to fight for. She calls this writing a power fantasy for disabled people. The plot's core: an original-sin father, to spare his son from suffering for his able body, gets drunk, picks up a chainsaw and tries to saw off his son's legs, but ends up in a pool of blood himself; the son is charged with patricide, and the courtroom scenes open up the debate about original-sin people and modified people.

— Yaoyao
22:25

How the one who can't see and the one who can't hear partner up

Visually impaired actor Wang Xiaomin was pulled in as a partner by deaf actor Li Ning during an audition, and his first reaction was blank: you can't hear, I can't see, how are we supposed to act. Li Ning's solution was to put his hand on the back of Wang's hand and trace, tapping once when it was Wang's line. Director Ma Yan says blind and deaf performers sharing a stage already exists abroad: the deaf person's hand rests on the visually impaired person's hand, and the visually impaired person can feel the movements. Wang Xiaomin says deaf people had been around him since childhood, but back then he saw them as two kinds of people born with a communication barrier, ‘absolutely no way they could ever communicate’, a barrier that was almost insurmountable — after this collaboration he and Li Ning became very good friends. The troupe also had to solve sign language interpretation, real-time captioning on a big screen, and assigning a volunteer to each visually impaired actor for audio description.

— Wang Xiaomin
25:28

Visually impaired actors cheat on stage with earphones

Only later in rehearsals did the director discover that the visually impaired actors were in fact ‘cheating’: they wore earphones, listened to their lines at several times normal speed, and delivered them live on stage without any trouble, so the director had no way to assess them and could only marvel at how fast the visually impaired actors got their lines down. Conversely, the director doesn't know sign language and could only watch everyone signing while grinning on the sidelines, when in fact the actors might be complaining about him in sign. Once they got to know each other, everyone's little tricks became known.

— Yaoyao
30:36

She received the best production award from the ICU

In 2024 Huí Zhuǎn Wèi Lái was selected for the Nanjing New Theatre Festival initiated by Stan Lai. That year Yaoyao's heart gave out and she went into the ICU; the troupe nearly fell apart, and it was a friend who stepped in to gather everyone for rehearsals and take them to Nanjing. At the closing buffet, while the other groups' tables sat empty and their members dressed up waiting for the awards, only their table kept going back for more after finishing, thinking ‘how could it possibly be us’. In the end they won Best Production, the festival's top prize, and jury chairman Stan Lai stood up and led the applause. The news reached the ICU at the same moment as the award, and Yaoyao lay in her hospital bed in tears. That October the play was invited to the Wuzhen Theatre Festival, the first integrated disability production, and the only one in the ‘Nothing Is Impossible’ section.

— Yaoyao
35:45

After writing this play, she changed her answer and chose not to be born

The second play, Wǒ Xuǎnzé Chūshēng (“我选择出生”), is set in a world where every country legislates that parents must use brainwaves to inform the fetus of its health status and family environment once it reaches eight months, and the fetus decides whether to be born. Among five families: deaf parents who must tell their child truthfully that you will be deaf too; a husband who deceives his wife into having a child; a family that favours boys over girls; and a final story adapted from Yaoyao's own experience. The troupe's actors couldn't get through the script, because their life experiences were eerily similar — only the illness differed. Asked whether they would still choose to be born if they had it to do over, most of the troupe still said they would choose birth, because they still love this world; Yaoyao changed her answer: she used to say she would choose birth too, but now she might choose not to be born, because disabled people are forced to be born into this society, with no choice at all.

— Yaoyao
36:45

The same question, and Cai Cong gives the opposite answer

The episode sets the deaf couple in Wǒ Xuǎnzé Chūshēng against the real-life Cai Cong (蔡聪): Cai Cong and his wife are both visually impaired, and when his wife was pregnant many people urged them to get tests to see whether the child would inherit the visual impairment, but Cai Cong did not want to presume, merely because the child might also be visually impaired, that such a life was not worth starting; they believe they can give a child a loving family. The deaf couple in the play decide to let the child be born. Yaoyao and Cai Cong give very different answers, and the argument over this choice persists. The question the episode poses: when a family makes a choice, how much choice do they actually have.

Glossary

original-sin people (原罪人)
The setting of Huí Zhuǎn Wèi Lái: able-bodied people who underwent no bodily modification after the nuclear war, marginalised by society for the crime of war
situs inversus (镜面人)
A congenital condition in which the internal organs are completely reversed from the normal position; Yaoyao has it
audio description (口述影像)
A volunteer describes in real time, in the visually impaired person's ear, the movements and images happening on stage
