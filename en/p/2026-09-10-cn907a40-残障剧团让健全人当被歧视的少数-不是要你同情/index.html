A disabled theatre troupe makes the able-bodied the discriminated minority — and it isn't asking for your sympathy

Yaoyao flips the whole social order: after a nuclear war most people are augmented, and the few unmodified able-bodied are the "original sinners" who have to fight for their rights. This isn't a lament — it's a power fantasy written for disabled people.

7:15

Disabled women being unable to leave the house is the norm

Yaoyao's earliest work was the documentary Hanyā (寒鸦), about three disabled women's struggles with family education and employment. One of them, a woman she calls jiejie, had not left her home in 36 years; when her parents took her out to visit relatives at holidays, she felt "like a little dog or cat being walked." Later she got her first computer, went online, and found a job as a call operator — no degree required, only a sweet voice. Once she had the job she bought herself a wheelchair and rolled out the door. Another woman, a visually impaired mother, was allowed to nurse her baby for only five minutes before her mother-in-law took the child away, saying "you can't see, you can't raise a child"; for years afterward she fought her husband's family for custody. These accounts made Yaoyao realise that being unable to leave the house was not an isolated case.

— Yaoyao
11:19

Disabled people's friends are all disabled people

A former colleague of Yaoyao's uses a wheelchair, and when she moved she could only ask friends who also use wheelchairs; they hung the chairs front and back with belongings and hauled load after load. Yaoyao instinctively asked why she didn't call able-bodied friends who can walk upright to help — it would have been done in half a day. The colleague paused, then said, I don't know any able-bodied people. She had not a single able-bodied friend; even her friends all used wheelchairs. Yaoyao says she didn't know how to continue the conversation, and realised only one thing: if disabled and able-bodied people never even get the chance to meet, "you have no chance at integration to speak of." That was the direct starting point for her later search for a drama teacher and her work in integrated theatre.

— Yaoyao
14:19

Writing able-bodied people as the discriminated original sinners

Leyi Theatre's first play, Ni Zhuan Wei Lai (逆转未来), does a straight identity reversal: a nuclear war turns most people disabled, and they become new humans through technological modification like electronic eyes and cochlear implants; the remaining few able-bodied people are branded original sinners for "the crime of starting the nuclear war" and pushed to the margins of society. Yaoyao's logic: since she herself is a situs inversus person, why not flip the whole society too, turning the rights disabled people now have to fight for into rights the able-bodied have to fight for — what she calls writing a power fantasy for disabled people. The plot's core: an original-sinner father, to spare his son from suffering for his able body, gets drunk, picks up a chainsaw to cut off his son's legs, and ends up in a pool of blood himself; the son is charged with patricide, and the courtroom scenes open up the debate about original sinners and modified humans.

— Yaoyao
22:25

How the unseen and the unheard partner up

Visually impaired actor Wang Xiaomin was pulled into a pairing by deaf actor Li Ning during an audition; his first reaction was blank: you can't hear, I can't see, how do we act. Li Ning's solution was to put his hand on the back of Wang's hand and trace, tapping once when it was Wang's line. Director Ma Yan says blind and deaf performers sharing a stage already exists abroad: the deaf person's hand rests on the visually impaired person's hand, and the visually impaired person can feel the movements. Wang Xiaomin says deaf people had been around him since childhood, but back then he saw them as two kinds of people born with a communication barrier, for whom "communication was absolutely impossible" — a barrier that was almost insurmountable. After this collaboration he and Li Ning became close friends. The troupe also had to solve sign language interpretation, live captioning on a big screen, and a volunteer doing audio description for each visually impaired actor.

— Wang Xiaomin
25:28

Visually impaired actors cheat on stage with earphones

Only later in rehearsals did the director discover the visually impaired actors were in fact "cheating": they wore earphones, listened to their lines at several times normal speed, and delivered them on stage seamlessly — the director had no way to assess it, and only wondered how the visually impaired actors got their lines down so fast. Conversely, the director doesn't know sign language and could only watch everyone signing while laughing along, when in fact the actors might be complaining about him in sign. Once they got familiar, everyone knew each other's little tricks.

— Yaoyao
30:36

She got the best play award from the ICU

In 2024 Ni Zhuan Wei Lai was selected for the Nanjing New Theatre Festival initiated by Stan Lai. That year Yaoyao had heart problems and was admitted to the ICU; the troupe nearly fell apart, and a friend stepped in to gather everyone for rehearsals and take them to Nanjing. At the closing buffet, while other groups' tables sat empty and their members dressed up waiting for the awards, only their table kept going back for more food, thinking "how could it possibly be us." In the end they won Best Play, the festival's highest award, and jury chairman Stan Lai stood and led the applause. The news reached the ICU at the same moment as the award, and Yaoyao lay in her hospital bed in tears. That October the play was invited to the Wuzhen Theatre Festival as the first integrated disability production, the only piece in the "Nothing Is Impossible" section.

— Yaoyao
35:45

After writing this play, she changed her answer on being born

The second play, Wo Xuan Ze Chu Sheng (我选择出生), is set in a world where every country legislates that parents must use brainwaves to inform the fetus of its health and family circumstances at eight months, and the fetus decides whether to be born. Among five families: deaf parents who must honestly tell their child you'll be deaf too; a husband who deceives his wife into having a child; a family that favours boys over girls; and a final story adapted from Yaoyao's own experience. The troupe's actors couldn't get through the script, because their life experiences were eerily similar — only the conditions differed. Asked whether they would still choose to be born given another chance, most of the troupe still said they would, because they still love this world; Yaoyao changed her answer: she used to say she would choose to be born, but now she might choose not to be, because disabled people are forced to be born into this society with no choice.

— Yaoyao
36:45

Cai Cong and she give opposite answers to the same question

The episode sets the deaf couple in Wo Xuan Ze Chu Sheng against the real Cai Cong: Cai Cong and his wife are both visually impaired, and when his wife was pregnant many people urged them to get tests to see whether the child would inherit the visual impairment, but Cai Cong did not want to presume that such a life was not worth starting merely because the child might also be visually impaired; they believe they can give a child a loving family. The deaf couple in the play decide to let the child be born. Yaoyao and Cai Cong give very different answers, and the argument over this choice persists. The episode's question is: when a family makes a choice, how much capacity to choose do they actually have.

Glossary

Original sinner (原罪人)
The setting of Ni Zhuan Wei Lai: able-bodied people who were not physically modified after the nuclear war, marginalised by society for the crime of war
Situs inversus (镜面人)
A congenital condition in which the internal organs are completely reversed from the normal position; Yaoyao has it
Audio description (口述影像)
A volunteer describes in real time, in the ear of a visually impaired person, the movements and images happening on stage
