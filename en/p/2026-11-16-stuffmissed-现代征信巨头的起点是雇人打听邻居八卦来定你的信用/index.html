How Today's Credit Giants Started Hiring People to Gather Gossip About Your Neighbors

Today's Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion all descended from companies that made money gathering reports by prying into neighbors' private lives; racial and religious bias was baked into credit records from the industry's first day.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:13

Investigators scored merchants by gathering gossip from their neighbors

Lewis Tappan founded Mercantile Agency in 1841, hiring people to gather information about merchants by asking around — questions like whether the person was of good character, whether they had capable friends, and whether they had a bankruptcy history. Investigators compiled these answers into reports submitted twice yearly, then sold them as subscriptions to lenders. This intelligence, cobbled together from acquaintances' casual remarks, was the earliest form of the modern credit report.

11:20

Racial bias was written into credit records from day one

Historian David A. Gerber points out that investigators gathering information were almost exclusively American Protestant lawyers with homogeneous backgrounds and values, and their assessments of merchants from different ethnic backgrounds naturally carried bias. Reports often contained contradictory information — the same person might have sound finances but be marked unreliable because of ethnic or religious background — and this chaos directly created later demand to reduce credit to a single number.

14:28

Two rivals merged merely to eliminate wasteful duplicate work

Dun's company and Bradstreet's company each maintained nearly identical investigative networks, sending different investigators to ask the same people the same questions. When the two merged into Dun & Bradstreet in 1933, Time magazine directly stated the reason was to eliminate these duplicate costs, not to improve report quality or protect consumers.

19:36

A guide listing neighbors' romantic partners sold for $25

Retail Credit Company, founded in 1899, went door-to-door gathering information about neighbors and filing it away — content that went far beyond financials, including someone's political activities, social circle, and who their romantic partner was. This gossip was compiled into a book called the Merchants' Guide, selling for $25 a copy at the time. The company later changed its name to what is now Equifax.

22:36

Unverified rumors could directly destroy someone's credit

The industry's early universal problem was that information was never verified. A single negative comment from a neighbor would be recorded verbatim in a file, with almost no one checking whether it was true. This meant the entire industry of 'assessing whether you're trustworthy' was built on unverified gossip, yet it had real power to determine whether someone could borrow money or buy things.

22:36

Two men made credit scoring scientific with mathematics

In the 1950s, engineer William Fair and mathematician Earl Isaac collaborated, using statistical analysis to judge whether someone's financial history warranted a loan. They founded the consulting firm Fair Isaac. In 1958 they released a credit application scoring algorithm; in 1960, the Inform product formally transformed credit scoring into a quantifiable number — the origin of today's FICO score.

25:39

One phone call could trick out someone's complete credit file

In 1968, Columbia University professor Alan Westin had an employee pose as a recruiter and call Greater New York Credit Bureau; the manager, with no identity verification, read a woman's complete credit report over the phone and later sent a written copy. The woman herself worked at that bureau but was unaware of the experiment — which directly spurred subsequent federal legislation.

30:45

Companies targeted by Congress thrived after simply changing their name

After the Fair Credit Reporting Act came in 1970, the Federal Trade Commission briefly targeted Retail Credit Company, long criticized for crude collection practices, but it ultimately faced little real penalty. In 1976 it simply changed its name to Equifax. By 1989, the FICO score became the unified standard across all American credit agencies, and today's three credit giants are actually more powerful than before the regulatory storm.

Glossary

Mercantile Agency
The first credit reporting company, founded in 1841 to gather information about merchants through neighborhood inquiries.
FICO score
A statistical credit scoring algorithm developed in the 1950s by William Fair and Earl Isaac; it became the industry standard.
Fair Credit Reporting Act
1970 federal legislation establishing consumer rights to access and dispute their credit reports.
D&B credit rating
A letter-and-number corporate credit rating system developed by Dun & Bradstreet that continues to this day.
