Microsoft's Ex-CEO Tried to Route Around the NBA Salary Cap — and Lost: A Star Got $48M for Doing Nothing

What really sinks Steve Ballmer isn't the $48 million funneled to Kawhi — it's that the money bought no real deliverable. The clause that says "get paid, do nothing" is what made the endorsement story collapse under investigation.

4:09

In the NBA, rich people's money can't buy everything

To understand why Ballmer went to such lengths, first accept the counterintuitive fact of sports: no matter how rich you are, you can't just buy a top player. NBA rules cap total league payroll, meant to keep big and small markets on a superficially even footing. Pablo calls it "sports is a funny mix of socialism and capitalism," and that mix is usually convenient for owners, because every time an owner reads the news he finds himself richer. The problem is that what Ballmer wanted was Kawhi in his prime; if he only went through team salary, he couldn't just throw money at him. So his only option was to keep it off the team's books and have outside companies funnel benefits to the player, routing around the salary cap.

6:05

Not crossing the line once, but making the same mistake four times

What surprised Pablo in the investigation wasn't that Ballmer crossed a line, but that he made the same mistake four times. According to the NBA's investigation and Pablo's own reporting, besides Aspiration (the tree-planting/carbon-credit company), there was also scoreboard maker Daktronics, Locked In Insurance which did insurance for Intuit Dome, and Boingo Wireless which provided Wi-Fi to the Clippers — all used to pay Kawhi Leonard. In every case, Kawhi didn't have to shoot an ad or attend a meeting. The result was four sets of documents, four batches of employees, four sets of money flows. Pablo says this is exactly what's most damning — once the volume stacks up, the "I didn't know anything" defense stops working, because all four companies were carrying out the same inexplicable task: paying a star who did nothing.

— Pablo Torre
7:20

Rich people make mistakes too, because not being able to buy something makes them desperate

Many people's first reaction is: Ballmer is such a shrewd businessman, he can't be this stupid. Pablo says that's precisely the most dangerous assumption. In investigating financial violations he's seen too many "extremely successful people" who, desperate for something money can't directly buy, do things they think will never be discovered. In this case, what Ballmer couldn't buy wasn't the player himself, but the fact that the rules didn't allow him to poach Kawhi directly from another team; the Lakers were also competing, and Toronto might not let go either. Ballmer's wealth let him win in almost every market — except in front of the salary cap rules. So he believed the multi-layered structure of four companies was hidden enough. The problem is he trusted people he shouldn't have, and every step left a paper trail. Being smart and being crazy aren't mutually exclusive.

— Pablo Torre
9:30

Kawhi's insistence on doing nothing is exactly the sham contract's fatal flaw

The truth of the whole arrangement can be seen from Kawhi's own attitude. Pablo recounts that Kawhi was very clear externally: for this off-the-books money, he didn't plan to do anything; he wouldn't work for the payer, but he wanted the money. Ballmer's answer was: no problem, we'll handle it. There is no business deliverable here that could be defended as an "endorsement fee" — no ads, no appearances, no social content, not even a formally announced contract. When a real audit chases money, the first question it asks is: what did you buy with this payment? As long as the answer is "nothing needed to be done," the whole multi-layer company structure collapses. A payment with no deliverable is always the most glaring item in the paper evidence.

— Pablo Torre
11:30

The heaviest owner fine ever, and its real purpose is to close off arbitration

The NBA's punishment of Ballmer is the heaviest ever at the owner level: 5 first-round picks, a $30 million fine, another $50 million to cover the league's legal fees, plus a one-year ban from entering the Intuit Dome he built for $2 billion — a arena he was so hands-on with that he personally measured the bathrooms. To Ballmer the numbers are just "rounding error," and the real design is at the procedural level: the NBA let Kawhi off with a relatively light penalty, so Kawhi accepted a settlement, and the arbitration path was legally cut off. Ballmer's only route to a comeback was civil litigation, and litigation means more document disclosure — and the last thing he wants is more details exposed to public view. On the surface it's a heavy fine; underneath it's sealing off the opponent's only safe exit.

— Pablo Torre
15:05

Five first-round picks gone, and the team's valuation still rises

The impact on the Clippers' competitive prospects is indeed large: the next first-rounder they're eligible to pick, in Pablo's words, hasn't hit puberty yet. But from another angle, the punishment didn't change the team's financial value. Pablo judges that if the Clippers really went up for sale, they wouldn't get the Lakers' $12.5 billion price, but they'd sell for more than most people expect; the reason is that sports assets have decoupled from revenue — scarcity is what it is, and valuation runs on a different system. The host puts it more bluntly: sports and the private markets are now "nakedly tied together." The mapping for founders: once an asset becomes a scarce license, no matter how ugly the fundamentals, the price can still detach from common sense.

— Pablo Torre
20:40

Ballmer wants back into Seattle, but first he has to get past 29 owners

What Ballmer really wants may be Seattle: for his hometown to have an NBA team again. But this is also the most easily overlooked chain in the whole affair — for any new team to be born, it must be approved by the existing 29 owners, and those 29 owners are exactly the group that pushed the NBA to issue its heaviest fine ever. The longer Ballmer chooses to "go to war," the worse his standing in that club gets; even if he wins in court, buying a team back in the future becomes nearly impossible. Pablo half-jokingly warns: if you still want to buy a new team, don't make enemies of the 29 owners whose approval you need. In a power structure, the most expensive thing is never the fine — it's your peers' votes.

Glossary

salary cap
The league's ceiling on the total amount a team can pay its players, so owners can't just outspend everyone to grab talent.
jersey patch sponsor
A sponsor whose small ad appears on the chest of a team's jersey; the fee goes to the team and is a major NBA revenue source in recent years.
SPAC
Special purpose acquisition company, a financing method that lists a shell company and then merges with a target; very popular around 2021.
carbon credits
Metrics companies buy to claim emissions reductions and green credentials; Aspiration used this kind of ESG concept to package itself and attract celebrity endorsements.
