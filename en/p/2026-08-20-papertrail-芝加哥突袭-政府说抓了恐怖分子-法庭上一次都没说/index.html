The Chicago Raid: The Government Said It Caught Terrorists; In Court It Never Said It Once

A counterterror-style raid with Black Hawk helicopters, BORTAC and the FBI ended with zero criminal charges, and the word Tren de Aragua never appears in the arrest records.

After the raid, the government suddenly went quiet

Normally, after an operation like this, law enforcement holds a press conference, lays the seized guns, drugs and explosives out on a long table for the media to photograph, puts up posters with the names of the arrested, and announces "we made America safer." This time there was nothing. Melissa says that if you're going to tell the public you deployed Black Hawk helicopters, fast-roped from the sky, kicked in every door, took away both U.S. citizens and immigrants, and paraded them in front of cameras like terrorists, you have to produce something to back it up. But days passed, weeks passed, and there was nothing.

8:34

The breakthrough was a single TikTok comment

Melissa searched TikTok for Spanish-language coverage of the raid, and the comment sections were mostly hate speech: "these people should get out," "Venezuelans are the worst." But one was different: "Not everyone is like that, a lot of people in that building are hardworking, decent people." She judged that this person knew the detainees, sent a direct message, and the person turned out to be the aunt of a detained woman, Norelli. She then found the mother of another detainee, Gabriel, through Facebook comments, typed his full name into Google, and pulled up his booking photo — intake date September 30, Clay County, Indiana jail.

— Melissa Sanchez
10:42

Clicking the next photo found all of them

At the bottom of the booking photo page were "previous/next" arrows. Melissa clicked next and found another person booked into the same jail the same day; she clicked again, and again, and started recognising the names and faces — they were all people who had appeared in videos DHS had released. Sitting on her bed, she said, "Oh my God, they were all booked together, it's them." By that weekend she had assembled a spreadsheet of more than twenty names. The site had nothing but names and photos, no information at all about any Tren de Aragua affiliation.

— Melissa Sanchez
17:01

No search warrant, and no one explained

Melissa found most of the adults detained that night. They said consistently that no search warrant was ever shown — and in an immigration raid, forcing a door open requires a warrant. She also found that about a dozen children had been detained along with their parents, including infants. Five people separately described the same thing to her: a police dog biting into a Nigerian resident's leg. Giancarlos said agents smashed the door, threw him to the ground, and struck his head with the butt of a rifle, in front of his six- or seven-year-old nephew. He kept repeating, "They treated us like criminals, I'm not a criminal."

20:06

Of more than twenty people, only three had records

Research reporter Mariam Elba pulled the criminal records one by one: apart from three, none of the detained men had any criminal conviction in the United States. The three convictions were domestic violence, shoplifting and marijuana possession; one person had a conviction for assaulting a police officer and a pending theft charge, another had an illegal firearms charge. Giancarlos had essentially no criminal record, just a traffic ticket. Melissa also found an independent Venezuelan journalist and compared the names against the Tren de Aragua membership list she had compiled from Venezuelan law enforcement and Interpol data — none of these names were on it.

— Melissa Sanchez
26:20

BORTAC entering the city was itself an anomaly

Sebastian's law enforcement sources said BORTAC, the elite Border Patrol SWAT unit, does not normally operate in a city like Chicago, and bringing BORTAC into a city is "extraordinary." Fast roping from a helicopter — sliding down a rope with no safety tether at all — had been requested by federal agents for urban use before, and was rejected when it went to Washington for approval, on the grounds that the urban environment is too dangerous. That tactic is generally used only in a "hostage on the top floor of a building" scenario. At the same time, seasoned FBI and HSI investigators were pulled in to do the most basic immigration raid work, and morale was extremely low, with a great deal of internal resistance.

— Sebastian Rotella
30:30

In court, zero mentions of a gang

Melissa and Jody went to immigration court to observe the detainees' hearings. The first was Johnny Caicedo's: no mention of the raid, no mention of Tren de Aragua, no mention of any crime — a total contrast with the show of force that night weeks earlier, and he was quietly sent away. They sat through a string of hearings, and the government had countless opportunities to say "this person is a criminal," "this person is a gang member," and it never said it once. In the entire so-called counterterror operation, not a single criminal charge was filed against anyone. At least two people were granted voluntary departure — which is different from deportation, and allows them to apply to re-enter the country in the future.

— Melissa Sanchez
33:31

The arrest records say the landlord agreed

The team obtained the immigration arrest records of several detainees. Documents like these normally spell out what the person did and why the government decided to arrest him. But in these documents, the term Tren de Aragua never appears once, and "gang member" never appears once. What the documents say is: agents received information that undocumented immigrants were living in the building and illegally occupying some units, and that they had obtained written and verbal consent from the property owner and the property manager to enter the building. In other words, the government's own documents show they went in with the landlord's permission to arrest "squatters." The team contacted the landlord and property manager multiple times, and they all refused to respond.

— Melissa Sanchez
36:43

The sources' conclusion: this was for show

Sebastian's sources — serving and retired law enforcement officials — said the operation was mainly for spectacle, to send a signal: we've gotten tough, and we'll use fear to deter people from coming or to make them leave the country. Sebastian says his reaction was sadness, because he had expected to dig something up: maybe there really was a notorious gang leader in the building with a squad of armed men, and the government hadn't caught him. But the more he looked, the more there was nothing. He says he has deep affection for that community, and if the federal government were genuinely going after crime he would welcome it, but this was a misuse of energy and resources.
