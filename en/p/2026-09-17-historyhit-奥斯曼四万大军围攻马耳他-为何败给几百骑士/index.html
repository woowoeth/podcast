40,000 Ottomans Lay Siege to Malta — and Lose to a Few Hundred Knights

In 1565 an Ottoman fleet of 180 ships and 40,000 men besieged Malta, defended by roughly 500 knights and a few thousand Maltese. Fort St Elmo held out for a month before falling, but the delay lasted until Sicilian reinforcements landed in September and the Ottomans withdrew.

3:02

Malta's value is its position, not its resources

Malta sits almost exactly in the middle of the Mediterranean: 80 km north of Sicily, right next to North Africa to the south, and roughly equidistant from Gibraltar and the Suez Canal. Whoever controls Malta controls the trade routes. The Grand Harbour where Valletta now stands is a rare natural deep-water port, safe to anchor in no matter which direction the wind blows. The Knights did not initially want Malta, because it had no existing fortifications and no resources; it was the first Grand Master who insisted on a foothold and took it.

7:10

What the Knights brought was the Italian bastion

The Knights brought Italian-style military architecture (Traccia Italiana) to Malta: no longer the tall medieval castle, but low, sloped, outward-projecting bastions with thick walls packed with earth to absorb the impact of gunpowder artillery. Fort St Angelo got the first bastion. By 1552 the Knights had realised how dangerous their position was, and Grand Master Domades pushed hard for fortification. An underwater iron chain was also strung between St Angelo and Fort St Michael at Senglea, functioning like a WWII anti-submarine net to block ships.

— Kirsty Grek
11:26

The defenders were waiting for a fleet on the horizon

On 18 May 1565, the garrison of Fort St Elmo saw a vast fleet approaching on the horizon, signalled at once, and the whole island went to war footing. The defenders numbered about 10,000 in total, including knights, Maltese civilians and mercenaries hired from Italy. This was not a war you could surrender in: the defeated would be killed or sold as slaves, so for many it was a fight to the death. The Ottomans, for their part, arrived thinking they would take the place in a few days and move on to the next target, and set their first objective on Fort St Elmo.

— Kirsty Grek
13:33

Fort St Elmo held only a month, but it tied the Ottomans down

The Ottoman fleet of about 190 ships and an estimated 35,000 soldiers arrived at the Grand Harbour and did not attack the Knights' headquarters at St Angelo directly, but first assaulted Fort St Elmo, which guarded the entrances to both harbours. Fire opened on 27 May, with hundreds of guns bombarding almost without pause and reducing the walls to rubble; every night the Knights patched the breaches, hauled ammunition and treated the wounded, then fought again the next day. Fort St Elmo had been thrown up in only about six months, its works were not good enough, and the Ottomans found it relatively easy to scale and dismantle. The Grand Master's strategy was simply to keep the Ottomans attacking and keep them bleeding. By the night of 22 June the fort was a ruin, and the surviving garrison said Mass in the chapel, preparing not for victory but for death. At noon on 23 June the last knights died fighting, and Fort St Elmo fell.

— Kirsty Grek
16:46

Both sides used corpses to send each other a message

This was a war with no room for surrender. After the last defenders of Fort St Elmo were killed, the Ottomans cut off their heads and lashed the bodies to rafts in the shape of a cross, casting them into the sea as a warning to the Grand Master of what was coming. The Grand Master is said to have fired the Ottomans' heads back from the guns of St Angelo in reply. On 15 July the Ottomans tried to attack Senglea, but below St Angelo at sea level there was a small battery the Ottomans had not spotted; when it opened fire they were caught completely off guard, and nearly all the attacking boats were sunk.

— Kirsty Grek
18:51

Scorched earth broke the Ottomans first

By July the Knights' side still had reasonable supplies; it was the Ottomans who had the problem. The Knights and the Maltese practised scorched earth, burning everything as they withdrew, so the Ottomans could find no food at all. It was the height of summer, the heat was unbearable, and Ottoman morale began to collapse into panic. The Grand Master's tactic was to wear down the enemy's manpower and morale as much as possible and hold out until they gave up on their own. An Ottoman leader is said to have remarked that if St Elmo had taken this long, what would St Angelo cost. Maltese civilians also fought in large numbers, including women who took part in nursing and in combat.

— Kirsty Grek
22:03

A tiny cavalry force scared off an Ottomans army on the verge of winning

In early August the Ottomans attacked Birgu and Senglea at the same time, at one point breaking through the walls and fighting a bloody melee in the streets, and they looked to have the upper hand. The Knights countered with a ruse: a commander at Mdina, the fortified city in the Maltese interior, sent out a very small force of Maltese cavalry, which raided the Ottoman field hospital and supply camp. Rumour spread rapidly through the Ottoman army that a huge relief force had arrived. The Ottoman commander Mustafa Pasha broke off the assault, retreating on the edge of victory. By late August both sides were locked in a war of attrition, with disease, food shortages and casualties mounting, and the Ottomans had also lost experienced commanders such as Dragut.

— Kirsty Grek
23:03

The relief force arrived, but the Ottoman Empire was not broken

On 7 September 1565, 8,000 Spanish, Italian and Sicilian troops dispatched by Don Garcia in the name of Philip II of Spain landed in Malta from Sicily and advanced on the exhausted Ottoman army. The Knights had been begging for help for weeks, and the relief came late but it came. The Ottomans formally surrendered. But Kirsty stresses that this did not weaken the Ottoman Empire's power or legitimacy; it continued to exist as an empire for a long time afterwards, and today people tend to inflate the significance of this victory. After the siege the Knights immediately set about building the new city of Valletta and repairing Fort St Elmo; Grand Master La Valette died before he saw the city finished.

Glossary

Traccia Italiana
16th-century Italian military architectural style, using low, sloped bastions to resist artillery.
scorched earth
Burning all supplies during a retreat so the enemy cannot live off the land.
Grand Master
The supreme head of the Knights; during the siege this was Jean de La Valette.
corsair
An armed raider in the 16th-century Mediterranean, such as Dragut.
