Time Is Not an Illusion: Irreversibility Is More Fundamental Than Symmetric Equations

Jim Al-Khalili argues that time is real and directed, that irreversibility is more fundamental than time-symmetric equations, and proposes replacing thermodynamic entropy with quantum entanglement entropy as the basis for the past hypothesis.

Time's direction is built in, not emergent

Jim stakes out a hard position: time is real, tangible, part of four-dimensional spacetime, not merely something a clock measures. He goes further, saying that time's directionality is not emergent from directionless fundamental laws but is built into the universe. Sean reminds the audience that this is in fact a major controversy — many physicists hold that time is just a tool or that its direction is a product of coarse-graining. Jim's intuition is that if we look from inside the universe, irreversibility is the more fundamental thing.

17:38

No system is isolated, so irreversibility is the norm

The traditional narrative runs: the fundamental equations of Newton, Schrödinger and the rest are invariant under time reversal, so the arrow of time must emerge from entropy increase or coarse-graining. Jim inverts this. Time-symmetric equations are only an idealization, he argues, because no system is truly isolated — even quantum systems are constantly entangling with their environment. Time-symmetric equations apply only to isolated systems and are a special case of the master equation; in reality everything exchanges energy and information with its environment, and irreversibility is the norm. He concedes this is a minority position, but holds that it is the foundation closer to the real world.

— JA
27:50

The special moment can only go at the Big Bang, not last week

If you treat the entire universe as an isolated system, and it also obeys time-symmetric laws, then by the second law yesterday's entropy should be higher than today's, not lower. The only way out is to push that special moment back to the very beginning: the Big Bang. This is the past hypothesis. Jim stresses that it is not an extra assumption but part of the universe's initial conditions, sitting alongside the fine-tuning of constants. He agrees with Sean: if you must pick a special moment, the Big Bang is far more reasonable than last week.

— JA
31:03

The laws contain no flow of time, yet we feel one

Even if low-entropy initial conditions explain the arrow of time, Sean presses: where do psychological experiences like ‘now’ and the sense of passing come from? Jim honestly admits that the laws of physics contain no flow of time, yet we feel time flowing. He calls this the gulf between physical time and manifest time, and says he is not even sure how to state it clearly. That candour is more valuable than pretending to have solved it, and it shows the problem of time is far from closed.

— JA
45:51

A DNA mutation may come from one proton's quantum tunnelling

Jim, from the perspective of a nuclear physicist, gives the most concrete case in quantum biology: the two strands of the DNA double helix are joined by hydrogen bonds, and a hydrogen atom is essentially a proton. Löwdin proposed in the early 1960s that a proton might tunnel through the energy barrier to the opposite strand, causing a mutation when the DNA replicates. Jim's team has run increasingly refined calculations to pin down the ratio of tunnelling to thermal excitation. He points out that quantum biology is not the old line that ‘atoms are themselves quantum’, but the question of whether life has evolved tricks that exploit long-lived coherence and entanglement.

— JA
1:03:12

A more fundamental version of the past hypothesis is entanglement entropy

The thermodynamic past hypothesis requires specifying a low-entropy microstate. Jim and the philosopher Eddy Chen tried a more quantum version: assume the universe began in a pure state with extremely low entanglement entropy, with subsystems barely entangled with one another. As it evolves, subsystems gradually entangle, and the entanglement entropy of the whole universe rises monotonically. Conceptually this is more fundamental than thermodynamic entropy, but Jim admits there are still many holes — such as how finely the subsystems should be divided. This direction is not finished.

— JA
1:12:29

After the heat death of the universe, time still passes

Many people say time may disappear at extreme scales. Jim pushes back: even if tens of trillions of years from now the universe reaches thermal equilibrium and no change can be used to measure time, time is still passing. He concedes this is not time that holds in every reference frame in the relativistic sense, but it brings time almost back to Newton's absolute clock — a real happening of events. For him, even if time emerges from something more fundamental, that does not make it unreal.

Glossary

past hypothesis
The assumption that the universe was in a special, extremely low-entropy state at the Big Bang, invoked to reconcile time-symmetric laws with rising entropy.
entanglement entropy
Entropy arising from quantum entanglement between subsystems, measuring the quantum correlations hidden in an overall pure state.
master equation
An equation describing the evolution of open quantum systems; the Schrödinger equation is only the special case of an isolated system.
decoherence
Entanglement of a quantum system with its environment causing interference terms to vanish, which shows up as the emergence of classicality.
non-Markovian
When the environment's evolution depends on the system's history, so information can flow back rather than being lost monotonically.
many-worlds interpretation
The view that measurement does not collapse the wave function and that all outcomes are branching parallel universes.
