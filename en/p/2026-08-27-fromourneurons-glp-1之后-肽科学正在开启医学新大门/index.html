After GLP-1: Injections Are No Longer a Barrier, Peptide Drugs Enter a New Era

Injectable drugs are no longer a barrier; peptide drugs are expanding from weight loss to exercise mimetics, neural regulation, and beyond. Scientists caution: outside GLP, data is still sparse, and approval cycles could take up to 20 years.

The industry consensus that 'nobody wants injections' has been overturned

The pharmaceutical industry used to believe that, aside from life-or-death drugs like insulin, nobody would accept injectable medications. The success of GLP-1 drugs has completely overturned this assumption, and some patients even prefer injections over oral forms. As a result, the entire field of peptide therapeutics has been reopened, and the question becomes: besides GLP-1, what else is worth injecting into your body?

9:22

The peptide market is like subprime mortgages: good and bad are packaged together

The peptide label covers two completely different categories: one is peptide hormones that truly exist in the body with clear receptors, such as insulin and growth hormone, with high levels of evidence; the other is synthetic sequences like BPC-157, with unclear origins, lacking receptors and clinical trial evidence, yet widely sold in the gray market. Long compares this to the subprime crisis: mixing high-quality assets with junk and packaging them together, leaving investors unable to distinguish good from bad.

— Jonathan Long
14:48

The ~50 textbook peptides are just the tip of the iceberg

Textbooks list only about 50 well-defined peptide hormones. Modern mass spectrometry detects between 500 and 50,000 peptides in body fluids—at least an order of magnitude more than previously known—originating from organs and cells not previously known to produce peptides. But detection does not equal function: many may be low-abundance flotsam, but some could hide the next insulin. The discovery window is wide open, but going from existence to validated function is still a long process.

— Jonathan Long
21:46

Peptides are like LEGO; multi-target drugs are built piece by piece

Peptides are linear chains of amino acids, like LEGO bricks, and can be modified one amino acid at a time. Ozempic is a hyper-stable version of natural GLP-1 with added lipids and amino acid substitutions. Further, Tirzepatide fuses GLP and GIP hormones into a single-molecule multi-target agonist, known as a unimolecular polyagonist. The industry is already discussing a 'five-peptide super metabolic drug' that fuses five peptides, as well as conjugating peptides to antibodies to reduce injection frequency to once every six months.

— Jonathan Long
32:12

Exercise-induced molecules can directly switch off the brain's appetite switch

Lac-Phe is produced by intestinal epithelial cells in response to exercise or metformin, enters the hypothalamus, inhibits appetite-promoting AGRP neurons, and activates appetite-suppressing POMC neurons, forming a complete 'gut-brain' feeding regulation circuit. Long emphasizes that 'anorexigenic' is just the academic term for 'appetite-suppressing' and is unrelated to anorexia; also, Lac-Phe may be just one of many effects of a molecule, just as GLP-1's initial discovered role was not weight loss.

— Jonathan Long
33:48

Metformin's weight loss may be mediated by Lac-Phe

Metformin, a century-old drug, significantly raises Lac-Phe levels, partially explaining its weight-loss effects. A Danish team has completed a Phase 1 human trial with intravenous Lac-Phe infusion in about 30 healthy volunteers; results have not yet been published. This is the first step for Lac-Phe toward clinical use. Long cautions that Lac-Phe is currently in a position similar to GLP in 1980, and it may take decades before a drug is approved.

— Jonathan Long
41:11

Exercise effects are being broken down into druggable molecules

Beyond Lac-Phe, exercise-related molecules include Apelin—an exercise-induced peptide hormone that in preclinical studies reduces body weight and food intake while preserving muscle. Bay Area startup BioAge is developing it as an exercise mimetic therapy. Stanford's Helen Blau team focuses on the role of prostaglandin signaling in muscle regeneration. The national project MOTRPAC is systematically mapping the exercise-induced molecular atlas in animals and humans, with human data beginning to be released.

— Jonathan Long
46:13

No agency regulates synthetic peptides in the gray market

GLP was discovered in the 1980s, and the first drug, Byetta, was not approved until the early 2000s—an interval of about 20 years. For new molecules like Lac-Phe, data accumulation must follow the same long path. Meanwhile, current FDA and USDA regulatory frameworks do not cover gray-market synthetic peptides. The historical lesson is dinitrophenol: an unregulated weight-loss drug discovered at Stanford in the 1930s caused multiple deaths and eventually led to the creation of the FDA. If peptide products cause serious harm, an entirely new federal regulatory infrastructure may be needed.

Glossary

GLP-1
Glucagon-like peptide-1: a gut-secreted peptide hormone that stimulates insulin secretion and suppresses appetite; the target of weight-loss drugs like Ozempic.
Lac-Phe
Lactate-phenylalanine: a peptide-like molecule induced by exercise or metformin that suppresses food intake via a gut-brain circuit.
Unimolecular polyagonist
A single molecule that fuses multiple peptide hormones to act on multiple receptors, such as Tirzepatide.
AGRP neurons
Agouti-related peptide neurons: appetite-promoting neurons in the hypothalamus that Lac-Phe inhibits.
POMC neurons
Proopiomelanocortin neurons: appetite-suppressing neurons in the hypothalamus that Lac-Phe activates.
MOTRPAC
Molecular Transducers of Physical Activity Consortium: an NIH-led national project systematically mapping exercise-induced molecular changes.
