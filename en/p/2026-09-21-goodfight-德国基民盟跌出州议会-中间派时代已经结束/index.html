Germany's CDU Falls Out of a State Parliament: The Era of the Centre Is Over

The CDU won under 5% in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, shut out of a state parliament for the first time since the Federal Republic was founded. Merz has lost his authority, and the populist far left and far right command an overwhelming majority among voters under 35.

The argument · timestamps estimated from transcript position

0:00

The CDU is shut out of a state parliament for the first time

In the Mecklenburg-Vorpommern state election, the CDU won under 5% of the vote and therefore holds no seats in that state parliament — the first time this has happened in the history of the Federal Republic. In the same election the AfD took around 38% of the vote, once again becoming the state's largest party. The author calls this the "final nail in the coffin" for Merz: he may hang on as chancellor for a few more weeks or months, but his authority is gone, his capacity to shape the country is completely exhausted — in political language, "a dead man walking."

0:00

The CDU's decline came later than the SPD's but is just as fatal

The CDU is the most important party in postwar German history, governing the Federal Republic for 53 of the past 77 years, and from Adenauer to Kohl to Merkel it left its mark on every aspect of the country. The author notes that in the early 2000s everyone was talking about "the crisis of social democracy," as the SPD fell from over 40% support to record lows; in hindsight, the fate of the centre-left big party was only a leading indicator of a broader crisis of the entire political centre, and the CDU then walked the same road. The era of what Germans call the Volksparteien — catch-all parties capable of mobilising most of the vote — is effectively over.

0:00

Populists are not a fringe phenomenon but the most important political force

The author says this is the most-asked question of the past decade: "Will the populist wave last, or is it about to peak?" When Trump became the Republican nominee in the summer of 2016 and Britain voted for Brexit, the answer seemed obvious to him; now the answer is undeniable — the populists are here to stay. What looked abnormal a few years ago has, like it or not, become the mainstream. Populist politicians now hold power in the US, India, Turkey and much of the democratic world, and have a very high probability of taking office in France and Brazil in the coming months. Even where they remain in opposition, they are already strong enough to destroy any sense of business as usual.

0:00

The rise of one extremist party is feeding another

In the Berlin state election, Die Linke took 26% of the vote and emerged the big winner. The party descends from the SED, which ruled East Germany with an iron fist, and after reunification initially appealed mainly to older voters nostalgic for the security of the communist system; in recent years it has remade itself in the image of the global illiberal left, which the author considers roughly comparable to Zack Polanski's Greens in Britain or the more extreme parts of the DSA in the US. During the campaign, some of its supporters were filmed chanting "Yalla, Yalla Intifada!"; on election night, the head of a notorious crime family turned up at the party's election party and cheerfully told the media he felt an affinity with it. The party's leader will now very likely become the next mayor of Berlin — the first time an heir to the SED takes power in the capital.

0:00

Voters under 35 have swung to the two extremes

Another notable feature of the two elections is the age distribution. The SPD and CDU, which once dominated Germany's political landscape, draw most of their support from pensioners. Among voters under 35, Die Linke and the AfD hold unassailable majorities in both Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern. From this the author concludes that the tilt toward the extremes will only grow larger in the coming years. It also explains why the collapse of the centre is not a one-off election accident: the structure of the electorate itself is pushing the scales toward the two ends.

0:00

Mecklenburg-Vorpommern's left coalition is just an election accident

In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, because a batch of other parties all failed to clear the 5% threshold for entering parliament, a broad left coalition just barely won enough votes to govern. But the author stresses this is an election accident unlikely to repeat — a few thousand votes either way and the state would be completely ungovernable. The point matters: when centrist parties fall below the threshold, they don't just lose their own seats, they push the governability of the whole state to the edge of a cliff.

0:00

Don't surrender, and don't deny the problem is real

The author explicitly rejects two responses. The first is to urge the centre to surrender: if the political middle can't hold, moderates might as well defect to the extremists on "their own side." He thinks this is wrong — neither Die Linke nor the AfD can solve the real problems Germany faces, and despite their enormous differences they have conspicuous similarities, from their ambivalence toward Moscow to their persistent inability to purge antisemites from their own ranks. The second is to keep denying reality: for a decade the centre has attributed the extremists' success to foreign interference, social media algorithms, a candidate's tactical blunder, or the stupidity, sexism or racism of voters. The author says these ways of blinding yourself to the facts in front of you may be comforting, but they are just as dangerous.
