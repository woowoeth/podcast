Living human brain tissue reveals neurons are faster and more reliable than rodents

Brain tissue from surgery survives only about a day, but it's the only material that directly measures how neurons actually fire in living human brains—revealing that human neurons differ from mice in speed, connection density, and signal reliability.

Recording must start within 30 minutes of obtaining tissue

Lucy Palmer's team obtains peanut-sized brain tissue samples from surgery at the Royal Melbourne Hospital, keeping them alive in containers filled with CO2 and oxygen, and begins recording neural firing patterns within 30 minutes. These samples survive only about a day before degrading, since dead brain tissue preserves structure but loses activity. Live tissue is essential because animal brains may function differently, so Palmer wants to understand human brains specifically, not rodent brains.

— Lucy Palmer
4:03

A decade of research finally revealed what makes human brains distinctly different

Researchers have worked with live human brain tissue since the 1970s, but only in the past decade has understanding of mammalian brains advanced enough to identify what truly differs between human and animal neurons. Scott Owen and colleagues compared cortex samples from 24 children and adolescents (ages 9 months to 18 years) with mice at equivalent developmental stages. They found human neurons fire faster as they mature—teenagers faster than children—while mouse neurons show the opposite pattern. This corresponds to ion channel genes expressed only with age in humans, whose mutations are linked to various developmental disorders.

7:05

The human brain is not simply an enlarged mouse brain

Peter Jonas challenges the assumption that human brains are simply enlarged rodent brains. His lab found that dendritic spine counts and other neural properties scale differently in humans than in mice. Human cortex has denser neuron connections, yet fewer connections in the hippocampus. A 2025 Cell study found human synapses successfully trigger the receiving neuron 90% of the time, compared to 62% in mice; computational modeling suggests this more reliable signal transmission may be a key distinction of human brains.

— Peter Jonas
9:08

Thicker dendrites enable larger, more complex neural networks

Palmer observes that human and rodent brain cells differ in excitability, possibly because human dendrites are approximately twice as wide. Larger neurons have longer, denser branches that form large-scale networks, while smaller neurons confine networks to neighboring cells. This demonstrates how size directly determines information processing capacity.

— Lucy Palmer
10:08

Brain tumor cells are unique in humans; rodent models can't match this

Rodent brain tumors typically contain only a few cell types, whereas human brain tumors contain highly diverse individual cells. Most mouse brain cancer research focuses solely on glioblastoma. Using human brain tissue slices, Palmer's team found that cancer cells from aggressive tumors are far more active than those from slow-growing tumors, increasing signal transmission and forming more synapses. These patients' neurons overall showed higher activity levels, and this heightened activity drives broader cancer spread—confirming findings from animal models.

11:09

Patch clamping takes a year to learn and years to master

Patch clamping is a 1970s electrophysiology technique using glass micropipettes on neuron cell membranes to measure electrical activity. Unlike optical imaging—which records thousands of neurons simultaneously but requires genetic modification or viral labeling, making it unsuitable for live human tissue—patch clamping has an extremely steep learning curve. Trained practitioners are now fewer than 10-15 years ago. People's eyes light up at the word "optical," while patch clamping is dismissed as old-fashioned.

— Lucy Palmer
14:13

Scarce samples force most teams to rely primarily on animal experiments

Owen notes that at best they receive several human brain samples per month, so most research teams rely primarily on animal experiments and use the scarce human samples to verify whether structures and mechanisms observed in mice also apply to humans. He aims to use human samples to test potential mechanisms in OCD and other psychiatric conditions, since while animal models exist for these diseases, it remains unclear whether the underlying mechanisms match what occurs in humans.

— Scott Owen
15:13

90% of CNS drugs fail in human trials; live tissue might explain why

Roughly 90% of central nervous system drugs fail when entering human trials. Kari Lamsa believes testing with live human brain tissue helps explain these failures—the same drug concentration might benefit mice but harm humans. Researchers can also test whether adjusting drug formulas alters how human tissue responds.

— Kari Lamsa
