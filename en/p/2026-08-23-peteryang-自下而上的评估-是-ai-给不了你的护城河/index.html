The eval moat isn't AI, it's human taste

Evals can't just be auto-generated by AI: top-down criteria are what it's good at, but the bottom-up evals that actually decide whether a product is good — the taste distilled from error data — have to come from a human, then be scaled up with Claude Code.

Eval systemsClaude CodeError analysisLLM productsHuman review
This episode doesn't fire off abstractions; it spends 15 minutes live-demoing error discovery as a workflow and gives benchmark data on automated tools. The second half on tool precision is especially valuable.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:03

The model can write your criteria, not your taste

Shreya splits evals into two kinds: top-down is set in advance based on the nature of the task, such as length or actionability; bottom-up is browsing a large number of samples and distilling intuitive dissatisfaction into rules. Claude is very good at top-down, but bottom-up has to come from a human, because the model cannot know your taste out of thin air. This is also why evals must be iterative: every time you find a new failure mode, add a rule — but be careful not to overfit to a single interview.

— Shreya
9:05

Long eval criteria get quietly skipped by the model

Shreya suggests splitting eval criteria into two groups and having sub-agents evaluate them in groups, so the model doesn't get lazy and ignore items when facing a long checklist. At the same time, have the skill automatically generate a spreadsheet (or pivot table) listing pass and fail for each criterion, so you can quickly judge which criteria matter more for the current task, or even weight them manually, rather than blindly following uniform weights.

— Shreya
17:08

15 minutes to build an interface beats staring at a table

Shreya demos her error discovery skill: step one, read the data and determine the semantic type (is it an article, code, or a trace); step two, design visual encodings, using color, spacing, and opacity to highlight differences in the data so it's easier to see; step three, build the review interface with HTML and Python; step four, have AI cluster and pick diverse samples; step five, enter the interaction loop — you give open-ended feedback in the interface, and Claude Code reads it in real time and integrates the feedback into new samples or eval criteria. The whole process takes about 15 minutes, but what you get back is readability far beyond a table.

— Shreya
27:19

Humans just say they don't like it; AI does the categorizing

Hamel explains the mechanism behind this interaction: as a person reads samples they casually leave open-ended comments, like "I don't like the negative contrast" or "this is annoying but I can't say why," and the Claude monitor tool in the background continuously digests these comments, automatically starts summarizing themes once about ten have accumulated, and proactively scans all samples to automatically flag similar problems. The core philosophy is: humans are responsible for taste and judgment, AI is responsible for discovering and categorizing at scale — not for inventing criteria on people's behalf.

— Hamel
35:23

The output of error analysis is your eval criteria

Shreya points out that error analysis produces more than a list of problems — these failure modes can directly become your rubric, i.e. your eval criteria. Among the 361 suggestions automatically generated in her demo, the most common was staccato fragments of fewer than four words (249). Write these criteria into the skill, and from then on every generation can be judged item by item with an LLM judge; and because you already have a human-labeled dataset, you can also reverse-validate whether these eval criteria themselves are reliable.

— Shreya
44:28

What automated eval tools miss is exactly product judgment

Hamel shares their benchmark test of automated eval tools like Brain Trust, Arize, and LangSmith: they can catch obvious errors like tool-call failures or users expressing dissatisfaction, but they generally miss implicit errors that require product judgment — for example, a real-estate sales bot facing the objection "you don't have this listing" only says "sorry, it's gone" instead of recommending an alternative. More notable still, coding agents like Claude Code and Codex perform the same as these tools, because the underlying models and prompts are essentially identical.

— Hamel
50:33

90% precision isn't enough — false positives distort direction

Shreya adds that automated eval tools hit at best 80%-90% precision, meaning 10%-20% of reported errors are actually false positives, and blindly following them will distort the product direction. So the genuinely effective approach is: use tools to quickly find obvious errors, then manually review the data, verify the tools' findings, fill in what they missed, and make sure your taste is injected into the product. That's what separates you from competitors.

— Shreya
52:35

Trade evals for cheaper models and cut cost 100x

On the depth of the course, Shreya says that beyond error analysis and productionization, it also covers how to wire evals into CI/CD, monitor long-term drift, and adversarial evals (to prevent tenant data leakage). The most practical part is the improvement module: using evals to guide model switching — when switching to a cheaper model, optimize its accuracy through evals; they have actually helped students and clients cut cost by 100x. That's engineering capability beyond pure eval skills.

— Shreya

Glossary

bottom-up evals
Eval criteria formed by a human distilling failure modes from a large volume of real outputs.
top-down evals
Eval criteria set in advance based on the nature of the task and domain knowledge, independent of the data.
LLM judge
Having a model make a pass/fail judgment on an output according to explicit criteria.
error discovery
The workflow of finding failure modes by reviewing data and hardening them into eval criteria.
staccato fragments
Broken sentences of fewer than four words, a common AI writing style of padding word count.
slop
Hollow, formulaic, information-poor filler in AI generation.
