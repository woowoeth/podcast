There Is No Universal Recipe for Admission: The Anti-Anxiety Truth About Applying to U.S. Colleges

Low admit rates are the narrative of only about 80 elite schools nationwide; for most families there is no formula for admission, and the most useful thing a parent can do is hold a fixed weekly meeting and control what is controllable.

7:36

No single application path can be copied

Rick says the admissions world has long used the word "process," making people think there is an optimal formula where x+y=z. But take Georgia Tech's roughly 4,000 freshmen: some stand out because they focused intensely on a single field, some stand out because they did too many things at once, and some stand out because they are from North Dakota and want to study public policy. The conclusion: there is no copyable application path, and someone else's successful path may not apply to you.

— Rick Clark
11:09

"Extremely hard to get into" is an illusion created by 80 schools

Rick first uses Georgia Tech to illustrate the change: 20 years ago it received fewer than 10,000 applications and admitted about two-thirds; in the most recent year it received 60,000 applications and its admit rate fell to 15%. But he stresses that the Stanford-style low admit rates the media focuses on are not the whole picture — only about 80 colleges in the entire country have admit rates below 25%; after the pandemic, birth rates fell, schools are hungrier for students than in the past, and the national average admit rate for four-year colleges is actually rising from 65%. So "extremely hard to get into" is a cognitive bias manufactured by a small number of elite schools.

— Rick Clark
14:37

The essay is not about writing quality, it is about information gain

Rick believes the essay makes people anxious because grades, activities and course choices are basically locked in before application season, leaving the essay as the only thing the student can still control. But what is truly valuable is not the prose or the topic — it is information gain: don't repeat in an essay the clues already in the application, but write a "next chapter" the admissions officer did not expect, so that the picture of the applicant already forming in their head gets color and a frame. He gives the example of Lisa's daughter writing about a difficult customer at a retail store — the kind of thing that would never show up on a transcript.

— Rick Clark
20:00

Parents should be perspective coaches, not invisible

Rick rejects the idea that "the student should completely own the process and the parent is best kept invisible," calling it unrealistic and a wasted chance to upgrade the parent-child relationship. What he proposes is a fixed weekly time for an application meeting, boxing off essays, deadlines and other issues into one time slot, so you avoid suddenly discussing the Notre Dame essay late at night, right after practice, or over a snack. The parent's core function is "perspective coach": let the child know there are many roads to happiness and success, and this will not become the only topic in your relationship.

— Rick Clark
25:00

Last year's middle 50% no longer counts

Test-optional makes students who can submit or withhold scores more anxious. Rick says the most common advice is "submit if your score falls in the school's middle 50% from past years," but that math is now distorted: because some lower-scoring students choose not to send scores, the average of admitted students is artificially inflated, and past middle 50% ranges cannot be applied directly. He calls on colleges to publish admit rates and middle 50% separately for those who submit and those who don't, otherwise no one can say clearly what "optional" actually means for any given person.

— Rick Clark
32:26

The athletic scholarship funnel keeps narrowing

Rick says only about 7% of high school varsity athletes go on to play on a college varsity team; even at the D1 level, the vast majority of scholarships are not full rides — full rides are common only in revenue sports like football and basketball, while many so-called Olympic/non-revenue sports give partial scholarships. Add the transfer portal, which lets colleges poach players who have already proven themselves at the college level, and the funnel from high school straight onto a college varsity team keeps narrowing. Parents are sometimes swept up by the commercial motives of showcases and camps, and are best off first asking parents whose kids are already in college.

— Rick Clark
41:13

A consultant can buy you process, not admission

Rick's attitude toward admissions consultants is "it depends on the goal": if a family needs someone to stand in the middle, watch deadlines and keep the parent-child relationship healthy, there is value in that; but any talk of "guaranteed admission" should not be believed, because predictability is declining at many schools. The same goes for whether SAT/ACT tutoring is worth it: some public universities tie scholarship amounts directly to test scores/GPA, and he gives the example of spending $2,000 on prep that could return an $8,000-per-year, $30,000-total scholarship advantage. But a self-disciplined kid can do well enough with free resources like Khan Academy.

— Rick Clark
44:55

Don't apply to a school you wouldn't attend

Rick says the most common problem among students is "smart people doing dumb things": applying to a school they would never actually attend. The right posture for building a list is to put together a set of schools "I would genuinely be happy to attend" — there are 4,000 colleges nationwide, and if you are only applying to 10, you are already pickier than any single school. Change "I have to write this essay, I have to do this interview" into "I get to write it, I get to go," and the whole experience changes. This is also the way of thinking colleges themselves teach students: start with a hypothesis, then stay open and curious about different outcomes.

— Rick Clark

Glossary

holistic review
Admissions officers evaluate grades, activities, essays and background together, rather than simply ranking by score.
test-optional
Students decide for themselves whether to submit SAT/ACT scores; not submitting does not make the application incomplete.
Common App
A shared platform that lets one application be submitted to multiple U.S. colleges at once.
FAFSA
The U.S. federal government's financial aid application form, which determines most federal aid amounts.
net price calculator
A tool on a school's website that estimates the actual cost after you enter your family's financial information.
