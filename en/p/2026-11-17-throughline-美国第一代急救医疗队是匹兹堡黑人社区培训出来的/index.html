America's first paramedics trained by Pittsburgh's Black community

America's first properly trained paramedics came not from hospitals but from Pittsburgh's Hill District—a Black community organization that converted police cars into ambulances and built the prototype of the nation's EMS system on community trust.

No one had defined what made a qualified paramedic

When John Moon first applied to Freedom House, they asked him to draw the heart's circulation system and the lungs' respiratory system—he failed. The term "paramedic" itself wasn't yet in common use, meaning both the job and its evaluation standards were being created on the spot. Moon refused to accept rejection, enrolled in a medical training program on his own, then returned to apply again. This refusal to accept rejection characterized his later work pushing for systemic change.

— John Moon
9:59

Before this, emergency care was just police cars and speed

Before the 1960s, emergency calls went to police. Patients were placed on canvas stretchers suspended two inches off the ground in the back of a paddy wagon while officers sat up front and ignored them. The vehicle raced to the emergency room at top speed—a practice called "swoop and scoop." A 1966 National Research Council report highlighted the problem: an American shot in Vietnam had a better survival rate than an American shot at home. The system itself was lethal.

13:54

Peter Safar trained only Black men to be paramedics

When Freedom House recruited Dr. Peter Safar, the CPR pioneer, the community organization imposed a strict requirement: everyone on the project must be from the Hill District and must be Black. Safar agreed. He selected 25 Black men and put them through the most intensive medical training in America at that time—covering anatomy, physiology, childbirth, hemorrhage control, and spinal injury management. The training exceeded even military trauma-care standards.

— John Moon
16:58

The community stopped accepting police and started demanding Freedom House

When the first Black paramedics with afros arrived in the neighborhood carrying equipment, residents grew nervous and rushed people to the hospital. Freedom House had to teach the community: "We're bringing the emergency room to your home." As word spread about successful rescues, the community's attitude flipped completely. In emergencies, residents now specifically demanded: "Don't call the police. Call Freedom House."

— John Moon
18:59

Street intubation was an operating-room procedure until they did it

John took a call for a patient with congestive heart failure. His supervisor radioed instructions: perform intubation and start an IV right there on the street. This was a procedure previously done only in hospital operating rooms by doctors or anesthesiologists in a controlled environment with the patient unconscious and not breathing on their own. John had learned the procedure just weeks before. He doubted he'd heard right and asked the supervisor to repeat the order. Then he did it. The patient's chest rose with ventilation. This was the moment when the concept of "bringing the emergency room to the street" actually happened.

— John Moon
22:00

Naloxone saved the Black community and enraged the mayor's White voters

In the early 1970s, Pittsburgh was in the grip of a heroin crisis. People noticed something: heroin deaths were rising in the White community but falling in the Black community—because Freedom House was already using naloxone to reverse overdoses. Angry White residents called the mayor with a complaint: Why do "those people" living in decrepit public housing have better medical care than we do? The mayor did not expand the system to the whole city. Instead, he systematically squeezed Freedom House.

— John Moon
23:00

The city spent twice as much catching dogs as funding them

Mayor Peter Flaherty couldn't directly control Freedom House and couldn't easily shut them down. So he systematically cut their budget. In 1972, the city spent twice as much money catching dogs as it spent on Freedom House. Meanwhile, the city government created its own competing system: new vehicles, new equipment, all-White staff. All this happened while Freedom House was setting the national standard—their federal project was becoming the template for paramedic training across America.

— John Moon
26:03

The experienced paramedics were sidelined once the city took over

In 1975, with funding dried up, Freedom House reached an agreement with Pittsburgh to transfer all vehicles, equipment, and staff. The transition was supposed to be smooth. Instead, it became what survivors called "an extraordinarily painful experience." The Black paramedics with the most experience in the nation were demoted to "observers." They couldn't drive, couldn't use the radio, couldn't examine patients. They were forced to retake classes and reexams. The goal was clear: make them miserable until they quit.

— John Moon
