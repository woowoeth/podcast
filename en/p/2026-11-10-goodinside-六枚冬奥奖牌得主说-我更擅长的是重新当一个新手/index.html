Six-time Winter Olympic medalist: I excel at becoming a beginner again

Her softball dreams ended after a failed tryout. She switched to the unfamiliar sport of bobsled—not through inherited talent, but by repeatedly surrendering the identity of "I'm exceptional" and resetting to zero.

Staking your entire life on a single identity means one tryout can collapse everything

Alana decided at age 9 that she would become an Olympic softball player, and played all the way through to setting records at GW University. But she describes that Olympic tryout as "the worst tryout of my life"—on her first at-bat, she swung and missed, and the ball sailed over the backstop. Because she had staked her entire identity on "I am a softball player," this single failure amounted to the complete collapse of her life plan; she sat in the car and cried with her father. Later, softball was dropped from the Olympic Games, cutting off any second chance entirely. What this reveals is: when an activity becomes a child's entire identity, its failure is no longer a setback—it becomes an existential crisis.

17:37

Before switching sports, let your old identity die completely

From softball to bobsled, she didn't transplant her "softball player" identity directly onto the new sport; instead, she acknowledged that "this identity had to die," and redefined who she was. During this period she had no training goals but instead read books, considered attending medical school, and spent time with family—deliberately placing herself in a state of "identity void." She says that precisely because she knew nothing about bobsled (her only reference was the film Cool Runnings), she didn't carry the old pressure of "I must perform perfectly" into the new sport; instead, she could enter with the mindset of "let's see what happens."

20:38

Price failure in advance, and you dare to charge ahead

The logic she used entering bobsled was this: I've already experienced the worst outcome—failing to make the Olympic team. So even if bobsled fails, it's just a repetition of known pain, not a fresh catastrophe. This kind of psychological accounting—"pre-paying" the worst-case scenario—let her approach a completely unfamiliar sport with the ease of "I have nothing left to lose." After her first ride in a bobsled, with all the violent shaking and roar throughout, her reaction wasn't to assess her talent; it was "I think I can do this again."

— Alana Meyers Taylor
25:51

Childhood excellence blocks adult willingness to start over as a beginner

The host makes a counterintuitive observation: letting a child excel early at something seems good, but it can make them unwilling as adults to put themselves back in the "novice" position to try unfamiliar things. Alana confirms this has data support in sports—many athletes who won first place in their teens can't reach professional or Olympic levels as adults because they never learned how to adapt and grow; they were always naturally the best. By contrast, among Olympic medalists, the proportion who played multiple sports in childhood is unusually high, precisely because switching sports repeatedly forced them to cycle through being beginners.

— Alana Meyers Taylor
27:56

Entering a field "white as snow" became an unexpected mission

She mentions that one reason she entered bobsled was that in 2002 Vanessa (pronounced Vanetta) Flowers became the first Black American to win gold, and because her own background was remarkably similar to Flowers', "I can do this too" became concrete and credible. But after joining the team, she discovered she was almost the only one—the bobsled community jokes to itself that it's "as white as snow," and she was even the first Black person some white teammates had ever met. This section places her personal athletic story and the sport's racial structure directly alongside each other, without dodging the tension.

— Alana Meyers Taylor
32:03

Becoming the mother of a disabled child redefined what "achievement" means

She mentions that her older son has Down syndrome, and at every pediatric visit she's asked to check which developmental milestones he's reached, only to have to answer "not yet" repeatedly. This experience led her to completely transplant the athlete's definition of achievement (medals) onto parenting: success is no longer the result, but "whether I gave my best effort in preparation and whether I'm proud of the effort I invested"—regardless of where my child ultimately ends up or how things ultimately turn out.

— Alana Meyers Taylor
39:15

The real wager in "challenge accepted" is not winning, but having a story

She redefines "challenge accepted": not a bet on winning or losing, but a bet on "is it worth having a story?" This framework comes from her husband—faced with an opportunity, you either do it, and worst case you get a good story, or you don't do it, and you have nothing. Using this logic, she decided that after having her second child (both children are Deaf; her older son has Down syndrome), she would still pursue making the fifth Olympic Games, knowing she might not make the team again, but "I'll have a good story."
