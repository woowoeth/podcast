One pot of wheat and the refusal to experiment: how the Soviet agronomist starved his peers and the nation

Lysenko said "hunger does not wait" and refused field trials, declaring success from a single wheat plant in a flowerpot; Borlaug faced the same famine but fed Mexico with more rigorous control experiments.

10:07

Two and a half years to breed new wheat—with only a flowerpot

Stalin demanded agricultural experts compress the breeding cycle from ten to twelve years to four or five. Scientists said it was impossible. Lysenko claimed he had done it in two and a half years. His method: skip the laborious field trials and arrange a few flowerpots in a greenhouse corner, relying on his invented "plant development stage theory" to predict crossing results. His justification: people were starving, and scientists had no right to waste a year on field trials.

— Tim Harford
12:11

From 500 surveys, published 50—and claimed victory anyway

Lysenko asked 500 farms to test his summer potato method and submit reports. Because they knew he was Stalin's favorite, the responses were likely biased favorably. He published only 50 of them anyway, claiming the results "completely proved the possibility of doubled yields." The show notes the logical error: try any new method across 500 random locations and some will succeed by coincidence. You cannot ignore the many failures. But Lysenko reasoned otherwise: farms that failed must have been lazy, incompetent, or deliberately sabotaging.

— Tim Harford
14:20

Vavilov begged Stalin face-to-face and heard he was just "tinkering with plants"

Vavilov was an internationally renowned geneticist who tried to persuade the authorities to stop ideological attacks on scientists. He even obtained a solo meeting with Stalin. Face-to-face, Stalin told him he only "tinkered with plants" and spouted "botanical nonsense," unlike Lysenko who truly helped agriculture. Vavilov understood he was in danger. In 1940, during a wheat field inspection, secret police arrested him. A month later he was forced to "confess" to being a "saboteur," sentenced to twenty years hard labor, and died in prison from hunger.

— Tim Harford
18:34

Borlaug broke the textbook rules for site selection—and moved to a rat-infested ruin

The Rockefeller Foundation's agricultural textbook required new varieties to be developed near where farmers actually planted. Borlaug wanted to establish a second base in northwest Mexico with a different climate—he could grow two crops a year there, accelerating trials. His supervisor refused at first because the Foundation had no work permits in that region. Borlaug insisted on taking the risk anyway. He took over an abandoned research station alone: fields overgrown with weeds, windows broken, no sanitation, rats running at night. He swept dead rats off the floor to make his bed, built fires from corn cobs to cook canned beans, and rebuilt the station from scratch.

— Tim Harford
20:39

Rabbits could become chickens: Lysenko declared all of genetics bourgeois pseudoscience

Lysenko did not stop at agriculture. He declared the entire field of genetics to be a bourgeois doctrine, mocking Mendel and Morgan's theory of "hereditary particles" as nonsense. He believed acquired characteristics could directly pass to the next generation, and claimed he could transform wheat into rye, hornbeam into hazel, even rabbits into chickens by adjusting conditions. At a 1948 conference, he produced a report personally approved by Stalin. After that, Soviet biology textbooks were rewritten, research institutes dissolved, researchers fired, and genetics research was completely banned in the USSR.

— Tim Harford
32:08

The bird-chasing boy whose pollination method saved Borlaug years of labor

Borlaug hired local boys including Reyes Vega to chase birds away from wheat. One boy asked if they could try extracting pollen sacs from wheat spikes using tweezers for hand pollination. Borlaug agreed. Reyes quickly became more skilled than Borlaug himself and invented a new method: one cut to open the spike and extract all pollen sacs at once. Borlaug later said this uneducated boy's discovery saved them years of labor.

— Tim Harford
34:16

Lysenko could not set up a control plot—yet he was Soviet biology's highest authority

After Stalin died, a visitor toured Lysenko's experimental fields. He saw a plot with the new fertilizer formula growing well and asked to see a control plot with the same seed but different fertilizer. Lysenko said there was none. The visitor then found another plot labeled "new formula applied" growing poorly. Lysenko explained that the fertilizer worker must have made a mistake. The episode's point: as the USSR's highest biological authority, Lysenko never grasped the most basic principle of scientific method—the control group.

— Tim Harford
40:38

Lysenko never repented, yet he identified a real problem in science

In 1971, an American writer dined with elderly Lysenko. Lysenko remained angry and indignant, insisting his knowledge came from field work while Vavilov's people relied on books and laboratories. The show concedes Lysenko's diagnosis of the "problem" was not entirely wrong: academic researchers do sometimes work too comfortably in ivory towers, disconnected from real problems they should solve, and intelligent young people without formal education can discover things scholars miss. But the crucial difference: facing the same problem, Borlaug chose to use better scientific tools, while Lysenko chose to abandon scientific tools altogether.

— Tim Harford

Agricultural transformation of the mid-to-late 20th century that vastly increased grain yields through high-yield varieties, fertilizers, and irrigation.
