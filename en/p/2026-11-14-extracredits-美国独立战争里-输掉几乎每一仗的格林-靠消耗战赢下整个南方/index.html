ExtraCredits 2026-11-14

Greene lost almost every battle but won the entire American South through war of attrition

Greene ended nearly every direct confrontation in retreat, but each time inflicted higher casualties on Cornwallis; through this dynamic of gains and losses, he ultimately exhausted British forces across the entire Southern theater.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:05

Washington selected Greene not for combat talent

Cornwallis could defeat any force that met him in direct confrontation, yet was constantly harassed by militia guerrillas. Washington needed someone who could both rebuild the broken Continental Army, coordinate with guerrilla leaders, and resist the urge to rush into pitched battles. Greene happened to possess both military command experience and logistics expertise from his prior role as quartermaster general, and was appointed commander of the Southern Department in October 1780. As Greene himself wrote, whether this appointment proved fortunate or not would depend on events yet to unfold.

3:11

Refusing a second Camden, Greene pivots to attrition

The force Greene inherited had poor supplies, threadbare uniforms, and worn-out shoes. He recognized Cornwallis's weakness: limited numbers but responsibility for holding vast territory, leaving supply lines vulnerable. Greene thus abandoned pitched battle for what he called ‘fugitive war’—whittling away British forces through harassment and small-scale engagements while coordinating with guerrilla leaders like Daniel Morgan, Thomas Sumter, and Francis Marion to pin down the enemy.

5:14

Wheeled boats strand pursuers across the river

Cornwallis, seeking to avenge Camden, burned his own supply wagons for a lighter chase, swearing to pursue Greene to the ends of the earth. Greene, however, had pre-positioned supplies along his route, scouted crossing points, and even towed wheeled boats like wagons behind him. After crossing the Yadkin River, he left all boats on the far bank, forcing Cornwallis to detour in search of alternate crossings, and ultimately crossed the Dan River first.

6:14

Guilford Courthouse: lost the field, won the casualty count

Now outnumbering the British two to one, Greene established three defensive lines at Guilford Courthouse to bleed the enemy. The British attacked frontally. Militia in the first line held against volleys and withdrew after inflicting heavy casualties; the second line in the woods dissolved into close combat, with Cornwallis himself nearly captured. Finally British elite guards broke a Continental regiment and took the cannon; Greene ordered an orderly retreat. The field nominally belonged to the British, but Cornwallis's casualties exceeded Greene's.

9:15

Cornwallis abandons Greene's pursuit, turns toward Yorktown

Cornwallis acknowledged he could not catch Greene and could no longer absorb the attrition. He turned north into Virginia, a move that would ultimately lead him to Yorktown and surrender. Greene, however, did not follow north; he remained in the South, systematically dismantling British strongholds across the Carolinas, forcing the British to abandon strongpoints like Camden.

10:19

‘We fight, we are beaten, we rise and fight again’

At Hobkirk's Hill, confused command and several officers killed caused one unit to retreat, partially collapsing the Continental line—another tactical defeat for Greene. Yet his army remained intact and outnumbered the enemy. After the battle, he wrote the war's most famous line. Following this, the siege of Fort 96 ended the same way after four weeks—another tactical withdrawal, but the pattern held: Greene no longer needed to win individual battles because he had made victory itself costly enough that the British had no choice but to leave.

— Green
12:20

Denouncing slavery as indefensible while using enslaved labor to repay debts

After the war, Southern states granted Greene confiscated Loyalist land and enslaved people, but Continental Army finances had collapsed. Greene had personally fronted military supplies and fell deeply into debt. Quaker relatives condemned his transformation into a plantation owner; he acknowledged slavery as ‘indefensible in every way’ yet still used hundreds of enslaved people's labor to pay his debts—a contradiction that defined his final years.

— Green
13:21

War did not kill Greene; heatstroke claimed him at 43

Greene once quarreled with a Georgia officer over a horse and an enslaved person, an exchange the officer sought to settle through duel—Greene refused but afterward carried a gun while traveling in Georgia for self-defense. What actually took his life was different: three years after the war ended, he collapsed from heatstroke while inspecting a neighbor's rice field, dying at his plantation on June 19, 1786, at age 43.
