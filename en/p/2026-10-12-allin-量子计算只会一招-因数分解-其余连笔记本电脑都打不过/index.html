Quantum computing's only killer app is factorization

Shkreli says quantum computing is commonly equated with ‘faster computation,’ but globally there's only one algorithm that quantum computers can compress from exponential time to polynomial time—integer factorization and elliptic curve discrete log problems, the mathematics for breaking encryption. For everyday algorithms like sorting and merging, quantum machines are actually slower: existing quantum computers run at only hundreds to thousands of hertz, single-core single-threaded, nowhere near GPU performance. Unless your business is factoring large numbers, quantum computing has nothing commercially exciting to offer.

— Martin Shkreli
4:03

Without quantum memory, he's betting on optical computing

His argument goes further: quantum computers lack ‘quantum memory’—input size must be tiny, can't fit real-world data. He says whenever someone pitches quantum computing to OpenAI, the response is always ‘not interested.’ He and his wife tested every quantum computer in the world; they couldn't even compute 300 times 200. He's been shorting quantum computing companies, losing millions of dollars, yet says he got ‘billions of dollars' worth of insight’ in return. What he really backs is optical computing: a Microsoft paper mentioned matrix multiplication at near-zero latency in light. He thinks in twenty years today's H100, B100, B200 will all be e-waste.

— Martin Shkreli
8:11

Pharma treats billion-dollar drugs as waste

At 16 he worked at Jim Cramer's hedge fund, which returned 23% annually after fees. Later he discovered the ‘molecular market’ is far less efficient than the public market: he bought a drug from Bristol Myers for $2 million; that company is now valued at $6 billion. Pfizer too has been willing to dump billion-dollar drug rights for millions. With Sparsentan, his play was to take a drug originally destined for a ‘moribund’ hypertension market and redirect it toward FSGS kidney disease, for which no drug had ever been approved. FDA approved it, and thirty thousand patients got their first treatment option.

— Martin Shkreli
13:27

He co-founded EAC as effective altruism's counter-movement

He says he co-founded the EAC (effective accelerationism) movement as a counter to EA (effective altruism). He calls Dario Amodei a ‘fear-monger’ and questions whether OpenAI's breakaway to found Anthropic was legally defensible—comparing it to stealing trade secrets (distinct from the Waymo engineer who went to prison for stealing physical documents). He believes Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence* ‘scared the hell out of’ Elon Musk and seeded this wave of AI doomsaying, while Meta, the President, and Jensen Huang ‘have the backbone’ to call it what it is: computer science, nothing to lose sleep over.

— Martin Shkreli
15:28

AI will deliver nothing to drug development

His claim is stark: ‘I don't believe any AI-related drug will exist in the next decade. AI doesn't help drug development.’ He knows this from having done it—built Cray clusters to analyze drugs, ran an NLP company in 2015 that fed Watson tens of millions of papers. The hard part of drug development isn't compute; it's biology. He also takes a swipe at something Dario Amodei said two years ago: AI would make humans ‘completely unemployed’ in one to five years and ‘cure all diseases.’ He says none of that happened, and none of it will.

— Martin Shkreli
16:29

Don't set the AI market ceiling at white-collar work

Faced with the claim that ‘$400 billion in venture capital is flowing to AI,’ he doesn't think that's a valuation trap. He pushes back against capping AI demand at ‘knowledge workers’—that severely undercounts total addressable market because demand for intelligence is unlimited. His example: as long as intelligence isn't ‘asymptotic’—i.e., more tokens still bring better answers—McDonald's could easily spend $10 billion renting ten million GPUs from OpenAI just to compute ‘how should the next burger be designed?’ The NPV math works out.

— Martin Shkreli
18:30

This AI wave mirrors the internet: consumers and Meta are the winners

His analogy for the AI market is the internet: the real winners aren't infrastructure providers but consumers and companies getting ‘near-tax-free’ intelligence windfalls, just as Cisco only got a sliver of the pie. He singles out Meta as most likely to ‘make too much’ because Meta has both distribution and has shown product chops (he mentions Muse). His read is we're still on ‘day one’ of AI; in hindsight, today's chatbots will look childishly primitive.

— Martin Shkreli
21:36

Refusing to admit he wasn't the best cost him his freedom

On Sam Bankman-Fried: he lumps himself in the same category—people branded ‘genius’ as kids who carry the burden of having to prove they deserve the label, an obsession that pushes you toward disaster. His real lesson, he says, wasn't the facts of what he did but ‘refusing to accept I wasn't the best hedge fund manager.’ That unwillingness to fold is what sent him to prison. Prison showed him that regardless of background or fortune, everyone at heart wants roughly the same things—like being a good father.

— Martin Shkreli

Glossary

ECDLP (elliptic curve discrete log problem)
The mathematical problem underlying modern encryption that only quantum computers can quickly solve.
Jevons paradox
When efficiency improvements increase total demand, used to counter the idea that computing power will become scarce.
EAC (effective accelerationism)
A movement countering effective altruism that advocates rapid advancement of transformative technologies like AI.
NPV positive
When discounted future cash flows exceed initial costs, making an investment economically justified.
