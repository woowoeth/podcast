The Good Fight 2026-11-10

The more secular politics becomes, the more it resembles a holy war

Two authors argue that when people stop deriving identity and ultimate meaning from religion, this longing does not disappear but instead transfers into politics—which is the root cause of American politics becoming ‘holy war-like’.

Politics is filling the spiritual void left by religion

Kaemingk's argument is that democratic theorists long believed secularization would make people more rational and unified, but American reality contradicts this: as religious identity declines, political polarization rises instead. The reason is that people no longer derive identity and meaning from religion, instead seeking them from political tribal identity—who are good and who are bad, the ultimate meaning of life, all concentrated in elections and parties. This produces political language imbued with apocalyptic sense like ‘this is the most important election of our lifetime,’ with politics itself beginning to perform functions that should belong to religion.

— Kaemingk
0:00

Monotheism's true function is pulling politics off the pedestal

The three believe monotheism's greatest contribution to democracy is not moral doctrine but ‘desacralizing politics’—pulling presidents, parties, and particular elections down from ultimate status. Kaemingk raises the ancient concept of ‘idolatry,’ which actually carries urgent contemporary political significance: placing ordinary things in life (an election, a political figure) on a sacred pedestal is itself a political danger. Judaism, Islam, and Christianity share a common legacy of reminding people never to treat any secular thing as the source of ultimate salvation.

— Kaemingk
0:00

Belief in the afterlife paradoxically relaxes political intensity

The book has a dedicated chapter called ‘In Defense of Hell,’ arguing that belief in the afterlife's judgment brings a kind of political relief—you need not make final reckoning with every dictator and injustice in this life, because there will eventually be a final accounting. Hamid argues this is not defending political passivity, but rather that it allows people to avoid the obsession with ‘must completely win in this lifetime,’ and thus handle politics more lightly. Conversely, the 20th century's most destructive communist movements were precisely secular ideologies attempting to realize paradise on earth.

— Hamid
0:00

Secular ideology killed more people in the 20th century than religious war did

Against the common accusation that ‘religion is the cause of violence,’ Hamid counters that the 20th century itself was a century when secular ideologies killed tens of millions—Mao's Cultural Revolution and the anti-Christian core of Nazism are examples. Both acknowledge that religious extremism has existed historically (from Christian religious wars to Islamic extremist organizations), but they argue that secular ideology in the modern era often escapes the same scrutiny, and Mounk agrees this has been underestimated.

— Hamid
0:00

European religious wars were power struggles, not conflicts over faith

Kaemingk cites William Cavanaugh's “The Myth of Religious Violence” to point out that conflicts historically called ‘religious wars’ in European history were largely different power brokers exploiting tensions between Catholicism and Protestantism to serve their political and economic ends, with religion being more a manipulated tool than the root of conflict. This stands opposite the historical narrative often invoked by secularists to argue that ‘religion cannot enter public life.’

— Kaemingk
0:00

Party identity is now replacing race as the most dangerous dividing line

Mounk raises that the most dangerous division today is no longer primarily race or class, but party identity—polls show American parents are more likely to be unhappy if their child marries someone from the opposite party than if they marry someone of a different race. Religion could originally have been a ‘bridging identity’ across parties: Catholicism, some Protestant churches, and Islam in America are all highly multiethnic and could reasonably connect people in different parties. But in reality religion has become highly mapped onto party division—observant white people are likely Republican, secular white progressives are likely Democratic.

— Mounk
0:00

Democratic Muslim candidates are selected to be too smooth

Kaemingk criticizes Democratic Muslim candidates like Zohran: they align perfectly with Democratic positions on gender and sexual orientation, with no genuinely ‘strange’ Muslim perspectives. What he hopes to see is a Muslim elected with openly orthodox Islamic positions (such as diverging from party mainstream on gender issues) rather than being treated as symbolic packaging. Hamid partly agrees, suggesting Abdul El-Sayed may privately hold more conservative views but chooses to avoid them for fear of angering the progressive base; he also notes that the Democratic Party was not inherently meant to be secular—four or five decades ago in the Jimmy Carter era, it still accommodated large numbers of white evangelicals.
