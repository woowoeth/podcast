Shared Phones in India: Apps Downloaded and Deleted, Trust That Installs and Stays

In India, phones are shared household devices; women reclaim privacy by downloading and immediately deleting apps. The true site of trust is their agency to adapt technology to their needs.

2:09

The community layer keeps getting left out of digital trust research

Researchers identify how Euro-American digital trust conversations center on the relationship between the individual user and technology, but in India, women are deeply embedded in social and family contexts. A fifteen-year-old girl has completely different technology capacities than a mother of two—not just different, but differently constrained. Moreover, device access doesn't equal use, and use doesn't equal trust. Trust actually includes accepting risk: acknowledging that expectations may fail and being willing to face that uncertainty. This distinction is typically omitted from trust conversations.

— Aditi
7:18

India wants its AI supply chain to start and end at home

The Indian government is pushing the construction of homegrown large language models driven by digital sovereignty—not merely training models on Indian data, but building an AI supply chain that begins in India and ends in India. Previously, India exported vast amounts of data annotation labor to companies in the Global North. Now the government seeks to convert existing domain knowledge in agriculture, health, and nutrition into local model capabilities using Indian languages, then return that capacity to Indian users.

— Aditi
9:18

Language adaptation brings resonance with AI, but not yet real trust

Interviewees hold that AI's current expansion in India runs on ‘resonance’and curiosity about AI's capacities, not on trust. They believe India is at the stage of building trust—mistrust typically emerges only after users experience something that breaks badly, and the history of large-scale AI use in India is not yet long enough for that to have happened.

— Antara
14:26

On a shared phone, every conversation becomes a negotiation with others

In the research, many women don't own phones; they share devices with fathers, mothers, or brothers. A mother's phone is called the household phone. A girl might have only two hours a day to use her phone. She downloads an app at the beginning of those two hours, deletes it before returning the phone, and in that entire process negotiates with the technology, with herself, and with everyone around her.

— Aditi
16:32

When a feature doesn't give control, women find an app that does

WhatsApp's disappearing messages feature was designed to increase trust, but some women discovered that the other person can unilaterally enable it—and once they've said what they want to say, the messages vanish completely. The women have no copy. So they switched to downloading clone apps that claim to preserve disappearing messages. Researchers see in this that trust largely depends on how much ‘control over how you use it’platforms give users, not on how perfect the feature itself is.

— Aditi
19:35

One identical icon in every app creates safety through pure consistency

Many of the women interviewed are not literate, but they discovered that nearly every app contains a visually identical home icon. If you can find that icon, you know you can return to the beginning and start over. This consistent visual symbol across apps gives them a sense of safety when switching between new apps. Design consistency itself is a mechanism for building trust.

— Aditi
24:40

Saying AI is unstoppable erases the humans who chose to build it

Responding to the idea that regulation always lags behind innovation, interviewees push back on the narrative that AI is unstoppable. Behind AI are real people building, governing, funding, labeling data. Calling AI a ‘wild beast’acting on its own means we are voluntarily surrendering our agency as users and data contributors. This assumption itself needs re-examination, not acceptance.

— Aditi
25:42

Friction keeps you aware; that's why seamlessness is the actual risk

Interviewees invoke the concept of ‘risks of seamlessness’—deliberately preserving friction has value. A phone unlock requires a password and takes time, yes, but that's the point. Similarly, letting AI regulation move slowly—thinking through the risks, harms, and opportunities—is the goal, not a failure. ‘Faster’doesn't automatically mean ‘better outcomes’.

— Antara

Glossary

household phone
A shared household device managed by one member (often the mother), used by multiple people but not individually owned.
digital sovereignty
A nation's claim to autonomous control over its own data, computation, and AI models.
risks of seamlessness
When an interface is too smooth, it masks the risks you should perceive; friction creates necessary awareness.
