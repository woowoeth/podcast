The Thai Flood That Wrecked the Hard Disk Drive Industry: The Biggest Factory Fell, the Number Two Took the Throne

One flood in Thailand redrew the hard drive rankings: the largest maker, Western Digital, saw shipments fall 51% quarter over quarter, while Seagate fell only 8% and overtook it to take the top spot.

What are the risks of geographic concentration in manufacturing supply chains? This flood is a complete case study — industrial estate siting, zero inventory and a single point of failure all detonating at once.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

6:13

Flood walls gave the hard drive makers a false sense of security

The industrial estates were built on river floodplain converted from rice paddies north of Bangkok. For flood protection, developers installed ring levees up to two metres high, secondary wave walls, pumps and drainage systems; the parks quickly grew into small cities with their own housing and shops. But this engineering may have misled everyone. When the real flood season arrived in 2011, seven industrial parks were inundated within two weeks. Research later suspected that precisely this kind of seemingly complete infrastructure created a false confidence — the belief that even if it rained, everything would be fine. In other words, what the manufacturers bought was not safety, but an illusion about risk.

8:24

Reservoir releases were not the culprit; local rainfall was

After the flood, dam managers were blamed for releasing water too late, with criticism centring on favouritism toward farmers and rice-corruption allegations. But the research does not support that blame. A 2023 simulation ran 17 scenarios swapping the release strategies of two dams, and concluded that even with perfect foresight of subsequent rainfall, the flooded area could have been reduced by at most 3.68%. Another 2020 study examined hydrological station data along the Chao Phraya River and found that in one low-lying disaster area, less than 30% of the floodwater came from the river, while 73.7% came from local rainfall. In other words, the water mainly fell directly on the disaster zone; reservoir decisions cannot carry the blame.

9:25

Why Seagate stalled even though it wasn't flooded

Seagate's factories in the north at Korat were basically fine, but production stopped anyway — because seven of its top ten suppliers were underwater. The typical case was Japan's Nidec, which makes spindle motors for hard drives and shut down production outright after two plants flooded. The hard drive supply chain interlocks layer by layer: however safe the assembly line, if the motors don't arrive, the whole line stops. Seagate held its quarterly shipment decline to 8% by quickly qualifying new suppliers, modifying designs to reduce complexity, and negotiating payment terms with suppliers. In the same year, Western Digital's core plant took on water 1.8 metres deep, and its quarterly shipments fell 51%. The difference between the two was not whose building was taller, but whose supply chain could route around the break point faster.

12:34

Zero inventory amplified the flood into a global shortage

Before the flood hit, the hard drive industry had just completed a round of consolidation: Western Digital announced in March 2011 that it would acquire Hitachi's hard drive business, and a month later Seagate announced it would acquire Samsung, barely bringing supply and demand back into balance. Precisely because there was no surplus inventory to draw down, the shock was multiplied. Tom Coughlin estimated that in the fourth quarter of 2011, hard drive demand was about 180 million units while actual production was only 110 million to 120 million; and the flood landed right on the Christmas shopping season. Retail immediately raised spot prices by 20% to 40%; at Taipei's Guanghua Market, a 1TB hard drive went from NT$1,750 to over NT$3,000. Even Intel admitted in December that Q4 revenue would be about $1 billion lower than expected because of the hard drive shortage. A local flood, transmitted through zero inventory, became a global shortage just like that.

15:41

The water receded, but hard drive prices didn't fall for two years

The water receded, but the shortage did not end quickly. Hard drive availability took about nine months to return to pre-disaster levels; retail prices fell back more slowly — hard drive prices on Newegg did not drop back to pre-disaster levels until April 2013. Backblaze did the arithmetic with its own purchasing records: in September 2011 the cost per GB was $0.044, and returning to that level again had to wait until September 2013. Why is it easy to raise prices and hard to cut them? The author lists several interpretations: the ecosystem recovered more slowly than expected, the two giants' 2011 acquisitions brought extra costs, tablets caused PC hard drive demand to fall permanently, and a more cynical guess — after Seagate and Western Digital acquired their second-tier rivals, only three major manufacturers remained in the whole industry, making prices easier to ‘stabilise’, or in his own word: cartelize. As for which one is the truth, he does not conclude.

18:47

The flooded giant chose to stay and keep building walls

Western Digital's post-disaster choice is telling: it did not retreat, it doubled down on defences. WD and the Bang Pa-in estate owner built an 11-kilometre flood wall along the estate, and WD added a 6-metre-high inner wall around its factory; to add redundancy to its supply chain, in December 2011 it announced a new slider fab in Malaysia. A survey of 50 directly affected Japanese companies showed that nearly 80% decided to stay in Thailand. The reasons are not hard to understand: ready-made estates, supporting infrastructure and skilled workers are hard to pack up and take away. Thailand has since suffered several more rounds of major flooding, especially in the 2020s when it happened almost every year; in November of the most recent year it even rained in what was billed as a ‘once in 300 years’ event, but these estates have held out so far. Whether stronger defences will be needed in future can only be tested by the next big flood.

Glossary

industrial estate
A large industrial zone developed by the government with water, power and road infrastructure, often growing into a small city with its own housing and commerce.
slider
The ceramic aerodynamic component that connects the read/write head and keeps the head from touching the platter; without it a hard drive cannot work.
La Niña
A climate phenomenon of abnormally cool sea surface temperatures in the eastern equatorial Pacific, which tends to bring above-average rainfall to Southeast Asia.
cartelize
A few manufacturers reaching a tacit understanding to keep profits high by controlling output and prices.
