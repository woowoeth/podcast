A Bank Holiday Isn't Inefficiency, It's a Fraud Control

Financial systems going dark on weekends and holidays looks like technical backwardness, but it's a deliberately designed control: it keeps conspirators from all sitting at the keyboard at the same time, and that exposes internal fraud.

Holidays are a Schelling point both sides default to

A holiday isn't an authoritative list; it's a Schelling point you and your counterparty default to together. Nobody questions that Christmas is a holiday in the US, so nobody has to ask. But a holiday can also be the product of explicit negotiation: one reason government payroll modernization projects keep failing is that you need historians doing a lot of bespoke research just to determine which document governs the overtime rate for a Chicago parking enforcement officer on Good Friday. A holiday is both a fact ‘every child knows’ and a negotiable object you can litigate.

6:00

The Bank of Japan's Company Foundation Day is on no official list

The Bank of Japan is also a Japanese company, so some of its human and computer systems rest on days when most office workers are at work, because that day is the bank's own Company Foundation Day. Institutions that interact with it inherit the counterparty's company founding anniversary for certain operations. This holiday appears on no list in government publications, Bloomberg, or Google Calendar, yet it carries all the consequences of a holiday. Once you trip over it, the engineering team has to urgently redefine how the system understands global holidays — because when the system asks ‘is today a holiday’, it usually gets passed a jurisdiction, but not a counterparty.

— Patrick McKenzie
10:33

Black Friday falls inside the four days when money doesn't move

Black Friday is the day after Thanksgiving, Cyber Monday follows right behind, and transaction volume on those two days spikes as predictably as the sun rising — and they land inside a four-day window when the banking system basically doesn't move money between companies. If you promise a customer ‘we'll pay out the next business day’ while the underlying rails take two business days to get the money to you, you have a one-day mismatch, and that mismatch consumes float. Normally you only have to front one day; on a weekend you front three; during BFCM you front about five, and that includes the biggest sales day of the year. So hundreds of people spend weeks ahead of time walking through BFCM scenarios, writing contingency plans, and modeling float requirements with finance.

— Patrick McKenzie
21:29

Crypto tried 24/7, and two banks closing at once broke it

Heavily online technical people hate systems that go down on a predictable schedule, and the crypto crowd especially loves always-on distributed systems with no single point of failure. One idea is to have every market participant bank at the same bank, get internal APIs for book transfers, and money can flow 24/7, with a second bank for redundancy. Crypto did try this path, and then in 2023 discovered just how questionable the design was when two highly correlated banks closed on adjacent weekends at the same time. The lesson: systems that mediate between cultures are useful, and mediating systems often have to bridge gaps in operating schedules — a gap you can't eliminate by declaring ‘the only operating schedule is 24/7’.

— Patrick McKenzie
23:30

Forced vacation exists so conspirators can't assemble a full set

Banks deliberately create misaligned operating schedules; it's an important tool for detecting and containing certain kinds of fraud. The most terrifying fraud comes from insiders who know the back-office processes: a single insider who understands the back office is enough to bring down a bank or cause billions in losses — Barings and Daiwa in '95, Société Générale in '08, UBS in '11 are all examples. A very old control is forced vacation, and its purpose is to keep the group involved in a conspiracy from assembling enough co-conspirators, in the right place on the right day, all sitting at the keyboard at once. So a finance CEO sleeping on a beanbag chair in the office may be very dedicated, but the company and he himself had both better not allow it.

— Patrick McKenzie
26:31

Expecting financial systems to be always-on isn't even self-consistent

In a world that's increasingly interconnected, where decision-makers care more and more about nines, it's reasonable to expect financial systems to partially narrow the gap between historical practice and contemporary practice. Culture creates systems, and systems create culture; both change, and infrastructure changes far more slowly than fashion, but it does change. Still, expecting financial systems to run continuously isn't just something you shouldn't expect — it isn't even a self-consistent expectation: the financial system is a nested network of organizations, systems, and subsystems, and at the bottom it depends on individuals who have been made complex sociocultural promises and who have a physiological need for sleep. These people will keep resting and celebrating periodically, just as they keep working for the society the financial system supports.

Glossary

float
Legitimately holding someone else's money temporarily because of business flows; positive float means you're using their money, negative float means they're using yours.
Schelling point
A default coordination point both sides can land on without communicating, which is how holidays usually hold up.
five nines
99.999% availability, roughly five minutes of downtime per year.
chaos monkey
A mechanism that deliberately injects random failures to expose system weaknesses.
