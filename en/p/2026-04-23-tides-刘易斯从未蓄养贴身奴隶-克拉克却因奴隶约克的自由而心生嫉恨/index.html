Historians from different eras projected their own times onto the same expedition

Craig Fehrman observes that Bernard DeVoto, writing in the Cold War era, saw imperial shadows everywhere in the journals, while Stephen Ambrose, writing during the Clinton presidency, emphasized efficiency and commerce with a neoliberal frame. Fehrman admits his new book will similarly bear the imprint of his own moment.

— Craig Fehrman
6:20

Ordway's New Hampshire family farm was too poor to divide among brothers

Fehrman grounds Ordway's enlistment in the concrete poverty of a small farm that couldn't sustain multiple siblings, then contrasts it with the Missouri River valleys the expedition encountered—some of the richest farmland they'd ever seen. A period farm-sale listing lets him recover what "land" meant to a poor farm boy: not Jefferson's grand nation-building, but survival.

— Craig Fehrman
13:31

Ordway quietly skipped Clark's single complaint about exhausted soldiers

During the grueling portage—sixteen to eighteen hour days hauling canoes—Clark wrote that the men were ‘疲沓懈怠’. Ordway, who normally copied Clark verbatim, conspicuously omitted this line: neither repeating it nor refuting it. Fehrman sees this as the defining signature of Ordway's role—absorbing all the emotional pressure between enlisted resentment and officers' authority.

— Craig Fehrman
17:36

At least four enlisted men kept parallel diaries, creating competing accounts

Jefferson's obsession with duplicate copies means we have at least four complete private journals beyond Lewis and Clark. The same portage looks entirely different in different accounts; even Lewis and Clark disagreed on terminology. Lewis preferred ‘野蛮人’ (savage), Clark favored ‘印第安人’ (Indian), and each sometimes deleted phrases they found objectionable.

— Craig Fehrman
23:48

The Lakota encounter dismissed as failure was likely their greatest diplomatic success

The Native American towns near Mandan held populations four times larger than St. Louis—denser than Washington D.C.—making this not empty frontier but a complex political world. Historians have called their Lakota encounter the expedition's worst diplomatic setback, but Fehrman found a rare 1807 diplomatic record telling another story. Viewed through Black Buffalo's lens, the captains weren't architects of failure but leverage in a power struggle Black Buffalo was already fighting.

— Craig Fehrman
36:19

York's bear-capture game was resistance to slavery, not innocent play

Earlier accounts treat York's popularity with Native peoples as the moment he ‘意识到自己值得平等对待’ (discovered his own worth). Fehrman argues York always knew his equality—what changed was having space where he didn't have to defer. His game of "the bear captured by Clark" wasn't innocent amusement but processing his own bondage. When Clark tried to stop him, York pushed back, because outside American society those rules no longer fully applied.

— Craig Fehrman
43:30

Calling historical figures 'creatures of their time' obscures the actual choices they made

Fehrman and interviewer both reject the reflexive defense that ‘不该用今天标准评判古人’ (we shouldn't judge ancients by today's standards). Saying someone was a ‘时代中人’ (creature of their time) falsely assumes only one way to live in 1804—but people then, like now, held diverse views. Lewis never owned enslaved servants and hired a free man even in the White House; Clark wrote resenting York's claim to have made a ‘巨大贡献’ (tremendous contribution). The richness of sources lets Fehrman pin down precisely who Lewis and Clark chose to be, not dismiss them as inevitable products of their era.

— Craig Fehrman

Official name for the Lewis and Clark Expedition, composed of around thirty-plus members

Overland hauling of canoes and cargo around waterfalls and rapids to avoid impassable water
NCO
Non-commissioned officer; an enlisted man with supervisory rank, such as Sergeant Ordway
