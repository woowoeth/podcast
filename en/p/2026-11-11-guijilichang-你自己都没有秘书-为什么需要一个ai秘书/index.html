You don't even have a secretary, so why do you need an AI one?

Zhu Xiaohu believes that 99.9% of daily work doesn't require frontier models, and open-weight models will eventually consume more tokens than closed-source ones; but Personal Agents lack high-frequency use cases and may repeat the failed subsidy playbook from ride-hailing a decade ago.

2:00

Frontier models are wasteful for everyday work

Zhu Xiaohu says you fundamentally don't need frontier models for 99.9% of daily work. He canceled his Claude subscription months ago because the combination of domestic Workbody and DeepSeek already takes his work "beyond expectations." He judges that open-weight models will eventually consume more tokens than closed-source models—the reason is straightforward: most people's everyday needs simply don't require such powerful models.

— Zhu Xiaohu
4:01

Foundation model margins far exceed cloud computing

Zhu Xiaohu gives a counterintuitive figure: a single GPU running inference for one hour generates $38 in revenue with costs of around $5, yielding gross margins exceeding 80%—higher than AWS cloud computing's 40%-50%. He also mentions Anthropic's staggering projected annual loss of $78 billion, but emphasizes that the company has actually turned profitable in the past two quarters—including both R&D and inference costs—showing that foundation model companies aren't margins-less but continuously investing in next-generation research.

— Zhu Xiaohu
9:03

Personal Agents lack real high-frequency use cases

Facing the Personal Agent craze, Zhu Xiaohu counters: ‘Do you have a secretary?’ Most consumers lack sufficiently quantified routines and simply don't need a secretary. A decade ago, Laiyi (莱野) ran a similar business but ultimately survived only through ride-hailing subsidies and food-delivery subsidies. He observed that even users of American muse treat it like chatting with Doubao (豆包)—casual conversation rather than actual scheduling or report generation. Without high-frequency use cases, the business doesn't hold up; China's app silos and WeChat ecosystem only make it harder.

— Zhu Xiaohu
12:03

Speed is just the entry fee; retention is the real moat

Over the past year, the startup ecosystem debated whether an AI product's moat is speed or retention. Zhu Xiaohu's conclusion: speed is just the entry fee, retention is the real moat. With Vibe Coding and better product development tools, almost every company can now drive iteration velocity—meaning speed itself no longer creates differentiation. The American investor benchmark is: for companies with under $2K in annual revenue, growth below 5x won't get looked at; 5x is the pass line, 10x is excellence. The two Haidian companies he invested in roughly double every two months.

— Zhu Xiaohu
14:05

Companion businesses are more profitable than time-saving tools

Zhu Xiaohu believes that emotional companionship products are better suited for startups than ‘help you save time’ productivity tools, because companion users are price-insensitive while productivity tools get price-warred into irrelevance by big players. His example: Pax Historia, a YC-backed game that uses AI to interpret historical narratives, reportedly hit $100 million in ARR with a $300 million valuation—he was skeptical until he realized he personally spent $2,000 a month on it. Female users crave romantic fantasy, male users crave conquest fantasy—these time-killing needs are actually more valuable than time-saving ones.

— Zhu Xiaohu
17:06

The best founders combine engineering excellence with business and human nature

Zhu Xiaohu summarizes the shared profile of successful founders over the past 20 years: excellent engineers who also understand business and human nature, with Zhang Yiming as a one-in-a-hundred example. He points out that China has no shortage of excellent engineers; what's scarce is the combination of "engineer + understands business + understands human nature." This is a reminder for ecosystems like Haidian, where engineering and research culture is thick but product and operations are weak: the AI era's demands on founder core competencies are as high or higher than the mobile internet era, not lower just because technical barriers have shifted.

— Zhu Xiaohu
18:06

He passed on Chinese foundation models, and he's not sorry

Asked whether he regrets not investing in any Chinese foundation models, Zhu Xiaohu shifts the answer from judgment to liquidity: primary and secondary markets differ—in secondary markets you can dump a bad call the same evening, but primary market exits require waiting for someone else to buy. Over the past 12 months the trend has been consistent: post-lockup, prices are 80% off the peak, sometimes 90% off, and many projects are essentially write-offs even after IPO. So he prefers small positions to test, then adding after confirmation, rather than all-in betting on hot tracks—that style of investing would stress him out and "give him gray hair."

— Zhu Xiaohu
21:08

He predicts 20-30 Chinese AI apps will hit $500M ARR next year

For next year, Zhu Xiaohu gives a specific forecast: China will see at least 20 to 30 AI application companies hit $500 million in ARR—lower and more achievable than the $1 billion threshold he mentioned previously. The prerequisite is going global; the domestic market alone can't support that threshold. He specifically mentions that Manus has already exceeded $500 million in ARR and is attempting to transform into a truly global-serving, Haidian-headquartered Chinese AI startup—he thinks it would be interesting if that happens.

— Zhu Xiaohu
