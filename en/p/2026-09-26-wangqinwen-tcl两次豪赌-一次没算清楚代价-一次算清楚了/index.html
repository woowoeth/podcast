The difference between TCL's ruin and its survival: seeing the downside in advance

When Li Dongsheng pursued the Thomson acquisition, the allure of becoming 'number one' clouded his judgment about his own management capabilities; when he later bet everything on building a display factory, he had calculated in advance that he could survive the worst case—the fundamental difference between the two gambles.

16:19

Half-reading a balance sheet taught this lesson at enormous cost

At TCL's telecom division, Li Dongsheng invented a wildly popular free-dial telephone—but a good product doesn't guarantee it will sell. Inventory piled up, the company ran out of cash for payroll, and nine months in he resigned. His postmortem was brutal: he had only understood the profit-and-loss statement's bottom line, completely missed the cash flow statement—the one thing that determines whether a company survives—and didn't grasp the balance sheet, which determines operational health. He spent the next years systematically studying industrial accounting and legal courses. The negotiation and management skills he developed then became the foundation for everything that followed.

— Li Dongsheng
24:33

A single factory tour sets a 30-year mission

At thirty, Li visited Philips and saw something that changed everything: a joint research lab with Sony housing over two thousand scientists researching technologies that wouldn't ship for three to five years—while TCL could see only as far as next quarter. The gap was so vast it felt like 'worship from far away; can't see the end.' On the spot, he made a commitment: use thirty years to catch global leaders. Thirty-nine years later, he still hasn't fully achieved it—'still on the way'—but that promise became the deciding anchor for his entire career.

— Li Dongsheng
40:57

Why 'number one in the world' blinded him to the risks

In 2004, TCL acquired Thomson's TV business. Both sides hired elite advisors (First Boston, McKinsey, EY, PwC), and the risks were clearly flagged. Yet the allure of becoming 'world number one' proved seductive enough for Li to acknowledge later that he selectively ignored the warnings and convinced the board to follow him. His postmortem was sobering: the strategy itself wasn't wrong, but TCL had drastically overestimated its ability to manage a global operation. The company had only practiced in Vietnam; it had zero experience in Western markets. Thomson's management team, retained for a transitional year, departed almost entirely within twelve months.

— Li Dongsheng
49:07

Three months from catastrophe

In 2005 and 2006, TCL bled over twenty billion yuan per year. Banks began calling in loans; the company couldn't borrow internationally to fund its global operations. Li knew that if the cash flow ran out by late 2005, there would be no money to pay salaries in the first quarter of 2006. The lifeline came from an unexpected source: Li orchestrated the sale of the company's profitable International Electrical Business. A three-month sprint to close the deal before year-end—finishing December 31st—brought in over twenty billion in cash and restored the balance sheet. 'That money saved our lives,' Li said later. 'Without it, TCL might not exist today.'

— Li Dongsheng
58:15

Betting 80% of your company's net worth on one factory

In 2009, TCL had just stabilized after the Thomson catastrophe. Total company assets: under 250 billion yuan. The decision: invest 200+ billion to build its first LCD panel line. The original plan was to partner with Sharp. The day before signing, Sharp backed out to join Nanjing instead. Li pivoted: he assembled a Taiwanese technical team, pulled in the Shenzhen government and Samsung as cross-investors, and somehow raised the capital. His logic was surgical: Korean TV makers dominated because they controlled upstream panel supply and could move fast; Japanese makers fell behind because they depended on outside suppliers. 'If I don't make this decision, we are definitely going to lose in the future,' he said. Over the next decade-plus, TCL would invest over three trillion yuan in panel line construction—a bet-the-company move that paid off.

— Li Dongsheng
1:05:23

The discipline: only bet on where you can lead

When asked whether he regretted not diversifying into refrigerators and washing machines, Li said no. The word 'leading' shows up constantly in TCL's budgets—'global leading,' 'Chinese leading,' 'industry leading'—and TCL has to be top-ranked in at least one of those categories. On television, he set the goal early: global top three. He achieved it. His reasoning was unforgiving: a CEO's time is finite. The bigger the business portfolio, the more resources each division requires; management bandwidth gets thin, and the result is usually that nothing gets done well. Better to own one category completely.

— Li Dongsheng
1:21:49

Six billion reasons why AI is already working

For four years, TCL has been deploying AI across production, supply chain, and R&D. At its Huaxing display subsidiary, it built a vertical AI model. Li offered a specific number: last year, AI-driven optimization created approximately six billion yuan in additional value. Huaxing's total profit was over eighty billion, so AI accounted for roughly seven or eight percent. At the same time, Li was candid: 'We can't fully see how AI will transform industry operating rules.' But he had a pragmatic answer: take what you do understand, deploy it first, and let the future clarify itself.

— Li Dongsheng
1:23:51

Supply chains have new rules: safety, roots, and contribution

Li's reading: the fundamentals of global economics have shifted. The WTO era optimized for efficiency, cost, and speed. The new order prioritizes supply chain security, stability, and asking what each operation contributes to its local economy. TCL's response is 'Globalization 3.0.' Where the old model managed overseas sales from a China-centric center, the new model creates independent local operations that plant deep roots in their markets and create genuine local value. The structural change: instead of using foreign operations as profit-extraction channels, embed value creation locally. Profits still return to China, but the economic value generated stays rooted where the company operates.

— Li Dongsheng
