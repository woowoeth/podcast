Shooting days halved in a decade; the real culprit is tax subsidies, not AI

Los Angeles' shooting days plummeted from roughly 40,000 in 2016 to under 20,000, as production crews flee to South Carolina, Vancouver, and Dublin to save on taxes. AI is actually squeezing below-the-line work like lighting and makeup—screenwriters' turn hasn't come yet.

Shooting days halved in a decade, driven by tax flight not collapse

The metric for measuring Hollywood's real output is ‘shooting days’—covering film, TV, ads, and reality TV. Los Angeles had roughly 40,000 shooting days in 2016, dropping to around 37,000 by 2019 as streaming squeezed cable. A brief post-pandemic rebound brought it back to 37,000; the 2023 writers' and actors' strikes crashed it to 25,000, spurring talk of ‘survive till '25.’ Instead, 2024 saw a drop to 23,000, with no rebound—now under 20,000, exactly half 2016's peak.

9:15

State tax wars, not production quality, are moving sets to Dublin

Production flight is a race to the bottom across states and countries. Hayes's Eastbound and Down crew, based in North Carolina, moved to South Carolina when the state canceled tax credits, filming Righteous Gemstones there too. More extreme: Fox's reality shows and sitcoms now fly hosts like Rob Lowe and casting contestants to Dublin because Ireland's taxes and labor are cheaper. Meanwhile, Los Angeles' large soundstage complexes built during the 2016 peak face weak demand, and soundstage real estate is beginning to suffer.

— Hayes Davenport
11:19

Writers' rooms have structurally shrunk from thirty to one person per season

Writers' room sizes are shrinking structurally. Hayes saw 30 screenwriters in a Family Guy room; today a ‘mini room’ of seven is already considered large. His recent project could afford just one writer to spend weeks mapping the season outline on a two-showrunner budget; the showrunners then wrote it themselves. Part of the reason: post-strike contract rules mandate that once a room opens, it must hit a minimum headcount and tenure, so production companies now skip opening rooms altogether—they prefer the Mike White model, one person solo, like The White Lotus.

— Hayes Davenport
15:30

AI is hitting below-the-line work first, not screenwriters yet

Los Angeles is one of the least AI-friendly cities in America, possibly the least. Generative AI is deployed as a search engine, but using it in production invites peer backlash—a podcast guest faced questions just for a small AI element in a project. The real precipice is ‘below the line’: lighting, makeup, set design, production assistants. Once AI replaces them, job losses cascade through the local economy via City Hall's familiar supply chain. The new WGA contract added AI protections, but no produced show has been written by AI yet.

— Hayes Davenport
18:32

Comedy is hardest for AI to replace—and hardest to sell

Horror is Hollywood's most active genre—mid-budget films commercialize reliably, so studios invest, knowing the math works. Blumhouse spent tens of dollars on Paranormal Activity and banked millions; comedies have no such floor. This explains AI's path into content: AI can outline, can handle other genres partly, but comedy is hardest because you need a room reading lines live, voicing characters to feel if it lands—and comedy's production volume is shrinking in lockstep.

— Hayes Davenport
20:32

Screenwriters have been fighting free content for years; AI is just cheaper

Hayes thinks AI fears in scripted content are overstated: for years, screenwriters and directors have battled ‘the entire internet, free, algorithm-fed straight to your face.’ AI can't frighten an industry already worn down by that. He compares news: a field battered by Google News for two decades, where AI brings cheaper more than upheaval—and AI, no matter how cheap, loses to free.

— Hayes Davenport
23:37

Production's collapse is starving the restaurants that fed writers' rooms

Hayes served Los Angeles City Hall from 2021 to 2024, witnessing production decline's economic ripple. Some restaurants, already pushed to the edge by pandemic closures, were finished off by lower production volume: writers' rooms order lunch and dinner daily; active sets mean hundreds of crew spending on-site and around the lot, and those meals sustained neighboring businesses. Declining production also warped tourism and city culture: Insecure drew sightseers to certain blocks; Venice's Instagram-lifestyle scene in Ingrid Goes West now reads like a time capsule.

— Hayes Davenport
28:52

Los Angeles stopped making national-scale stars after the 1990s

Mainstream ‘star-making’ essentially halted after the 1990s. Millions-strong internet celebrities exist now, but no new George Clooney—the kind everyone recognizes—has emerged; the rare exception is Taylor Swift. Stars who broke in the '90s, Clooney among them, mostly stay in Los Angeles and keep acting, but how the public reaches them has shifted: not Troubadour nightlife scenes or Saddle Ranch, but bumping into Usher at the Hollywood Farmers Market.

Glossary

below the line
Production-level crew roles—lighting, makeup, set design, production assistants—in film and TV shoots; collectively, everyone on set below the director.
mini room
A small writers' room with far fewer staff and shorter tenure than traditional story departments; often implies a lean operation and tighter budget.
WGA
Writers Guild of America; the union representing screenwriters in labor negotiations, recently adding AI-use protections to contracts.
