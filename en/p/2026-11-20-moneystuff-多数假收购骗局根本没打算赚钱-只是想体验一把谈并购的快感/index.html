7x IPO oversubscription proves investors are systematically padding orders

Before Anthropic's IPO pricing, demand was reported at $500 billion, with 7x oversubscription. The hosts explain how such figures are self-reinforcing fictions: investors deliberately submit oversized orders, expecting pro-rata cuts, but if $500 billion were actually fulfilled, no one has that money. The subtler risk: if oversubscription stems from everyone padding their orders and actual demand is lower, then full fulfillment signals no real secondary-market demand, making next-day trading difficult.

— Matt
5:12

Perpetual markets on decentralized exchanges pre-price IPO opening performance

Matt coins 'perp walk' for how perpetual contract prices on platforms like Hyperliquid presage IPO opening performance. Anthropic's pricing range was $70-$78, but perp markets hovered near $97, suggesting roughly 20% opening pop. But the hosts note perpetual-contract samples for IPOs are tiny (fewer than ten cases), and pricing logic might just be 'IPOs average 20% pops, so always price 20% above'—containing no real signal. Whether the stock soars 300% or crashes, the perp market would still show the same 20% premium.

— Matt
14:23

CFTC's Trump-only mention-market ban reveals political considerations over principle

The CFTC blocked Kalshi's market betting whether Trump would say a given nickname (e.g., 'Slopidopoulos'), but left untouched similar markets on what the White House press secretary or other political figures might say. Hosts debate the logic: perhaps regulators fear Trump's remarks move markets and invite manipulation, or worry the bets would embarrass the president. Kalshi's CEO defended the market as hedging real financial risk from presidential speech, prompting the hosts to joke that even betting on a specific nickname now gets reframed as serious financial protection.

— Matt
17:29

Baiting sandwich traders with fake orders doesn't constitute wire fraud

Two brothers discovered a hole in Ethereum's block-building: sandwich traders insert transactions before and after yours to capture spread. These brothers deployed bait orders to trick sandwich traders into attacking them, then used technical manipulation to execute different blocks, extracting $25 million from the sandwich traders. Prosecuted for wire fraud, the jury deadlocked and mistrial was declared. This week, the judge ruled them not guilty: wire fraud requires proof of false statements, and posting bait orders isn't lying to anyone.

— Matt
23:35

Fake acquirers want the dealmaking role-play, not any actual proceeds

A British man was convicted this week of forgery-based fraud: he impersonated 'Simon Jones' in emails to company executives, then posed as business partner 'Greg Thomas' in face-to-face negotiations using a Boston accent, forging documents to extend the scheme. He bought stock ahead of the deal announcement, but forgot to issue the press release, pocketing nothing. Court documents cite a psychologist's finding: he simply enjoyed playing a different character and got lost in the role. Matt contends this is the true motive behind nearly every fake-acquisition scheme—dealmaking role-play, not money.

— Matt
25:37

Insurance companies offer the best fake-acquisition targets in a fakeover club

Inspired by the case, the hosts half-jokingly propose a 'fakeover club' where people can enjoy negotiating mock acquisitions. Matt says he'd target an insurer, since equity value typically lags the premium capital they control—buy a $100 million insurer, access a $1 billion premium pool to redeploy. Mary suggests specialized mining companies as targets. They sketch out recruiting people to pose as private-credit lenders for 'financing' the fictitious deal over drinks.

Glossary

perp walk
A show-created term for how perpetual contract prices on decentralized platforms presage an IPO's opening-day performance.
mention market
A prediction market betting whether a public figure will say a specific word or phrase at a given event.
sandwich attack
Inserting transactions before and after a target trade to capture the price difference and profit from the spread.
fakeover
Impersonating a buyer to stage mock acquisition negotiations while having no real funds or intent to close.
