Neuralink 2025-01-01

Needle cost drops from $350 to $15 as implantation becomes mass production

Over the past year, Neuralink compressed electrode insertion from 17 seconds to 1.5 seconds and cut needle costs from $350 to $15—these metrics show real progress far better than any animated demo ever could.

6:39

Speech and typing limit human output to below one bit per second

Elon offers a frame of reference: 86,400 seconds in a day, and most people struggle to output more than 86,400 bits of information—speech and typing are the bottleneck. Neuralink's target isn't incremental improvement; it's first the megabit range, then gigabit-per-second bandwidth (what he calls ‘concept-level telepathy’). Visual input, already in the megabit range, could also scale to gigabits and beyond.

— Elon Musk
17:21

Early implant users sustain 50-plus hours of weekly independent use

Seven participants have received implants for the telepathy product—four with spinal injuries, three with ALS—with two surgeries just a week apart. The key metric the team reports is at-home independent use time: averaging 50 hours weekly, with some users hitting over 100 hours per week (essentially every waking moment). The team sees this usage intensity as evidence of both safety and real-world value, not just lab-bench demonstrations.

— DJ
21:26

Electrode count will triple annually for three years, reaching 25,000

Current devices have 1,000 electrodes implanted in the hand knob, a small region of motor cortex, enabling cursor and game-controller control. The next quarter brings a speech-cortex implant to decode intended speech directly into text. By 2026, electrode count triples to 3,000 and the first blind vision implant launches. By 2027, another tripling to 10,000 channels enables simultaneous multimodal implants in a single patient (motor, speech, and vision at once). The 2028 target: a single implant exceeding 25,000 channels, covering any brain region to treat psychiatric illness and pain, with initial AI integration testing.

— DJ
28:44

Brain implants unlock outdoor freedom that assistive devices cannot provide

ALS patient Brad couldn't speak and spent six years post-diagnosis relying on eye-tracking for communication, but eye-trackers fail outdoors, meaning he barely left home. After his Neuralink implant, the team captured video of him with his kids in a park. His own words: ‘I was Batman (housebound) for a long time, but now I go outside—that's huge for me.’

— Sahej
33:15

Bridging severed spinal cords could restore walking to paralyzed patients

Elon's next step: ‘bridging’ spinal injuries by placing implants both in the brain and at the lesion site, relaying motor commands around the damage to restore walking and full-body function, even after cervical fractures. His phrasing: ‘I'm quite confident on this point.’ But this remains a future goal—none of the current participants has achieved it.

— Elon Musk
42:24

Cursor control now takes 15 minutes to calibrate instead of hours

New user RJ went from zero cursor control to fluent computer operation in 15 minutes, with minimal engineer intervention. Compare that to 18 months earlier, when first participant P1 needed several hours to reach the same control level, with engineers clustered around frantically optimizing. RJ hit 7 BPS on day one, showing that calibration itself is being engineered into something approaching plug-and-play.

— Harrison
49:32

Next-generation robot inserts electrodes 11 times faster than the current model

The current R1 robot completed implants for all seven early participants, but inserting a single electrode took 17 seconds even in ideal conditions; external disruptions meant re-gripping and re-inserting. The next-generation robot mounts the implant directly on the arm itself instead of a separate staging platform, compressing single-electrode insertion to 1.5 seconds—11 times faster. It can also reach depths exceeding 50 millimeters, making it compatible with over 99% of human head anatomy.

— John
51:36

Needle manufacturing shifts from $350 handmade to $15 through mass production

Current needle-assembly manufacturing cycles take 24 hours and cost $350 per unit, all handmade: technicians glue 150-micrometer tubing to a metal base, electropolish 40-micrometer tungsten wire into a sharp cone, hand-thread it through a 60-micrometer orifice, then laser-weld everything together. Next-generation design uses injection-molded bases (eliminating adhesive and wire-cutting steps), refined needle geometry (cutting electropolishing), and press-fit joints instead of laser welding, compressing the cycle to 30 minutes at $15 per unit.

— John
