OpenAI President: Cybersecurity Spending Should Exceed Software Development Costs

AI has made software extremely cheap; reliability and safety are the truly scarce goods—OpenAI once pulled a quarter of its engineers off projects to focus entirely on patching vulnerabilities.

2:06

AI can now generate genuinely new knowledge

Greg Brockman calls this week's math results a watershed moment—not just a machine computing an answer, but proof that century-old unsolved math problems can be cracked. He recalls a long-standing question: can AI actually produce genuinely new knowledge? The answer is now definitively yes. While we're not yet at the stage of AI proposing entirely new grand theories, it can solve specific math problems that have stumped humans for over a century. OpenAI publicly released nearly 400 of these important solved open problems for the mathematics community to examine the insights behind them, not just stare at a proof no one can follow.

— Greg Brockman
7:14

Humans can still oversee AI beyond their direct comprehension

When AI solves problems beyond what humans can directly verify—like judging if a book summary is good without reading the whole book—OpenAI has since 2017 been researching debate and iterative amplification: another AI finds the flaws in the summary, and a human only needs to judge whether that rebuttal holds water. The human doesn't have to verify everything themselves. Greg Brockman sees these techniques as keeping humans in the driver's seat and calls it an underrated direction in AI safety alignment.

— Greg Brockman
10:18

Math yields to AI while biology doesn't—the difference is data

Greg Brockman points out that AI's breakthroughs in math and theoretical physics aren't about smarter models—they come from how much data these fields generate. In deep learning, compute plus data plus algorithms equals capability, and that's a physical law. Biology is the opposite: a classic data bottleneck. Neural networks have no innate reason to be better at language than at biology; the advances we see in language should apply equally to biology if we could get the data. He admits this problem remains unsolved: whether we can get enough biological data will determine how fast we move forward.

— Greg Brockman
13:35

Humans are actually terrible at drug development

A drug takes roughly 15 years to develop and costs about $1.5 billion, with ~$50 million spent in preclinical stages like mouse testing and over $1.4 billion and above in clinical trials. Greg Brockman's reasoning: humans are naturally adapted to language but are complete foreigners to biology. Neural networks have no reason to be innately better at language than at biology, so breakthroughs in language models should apply equally to cracking the language of biology. That's the first-principles logic for why he puts drug development as AI's top target for transforming science.

— Greg Brockman
17:47

Dots changed how Greg Brockman himself works every day

Dots is an always-on proactive agent that keeps working even when its user stops. Every morning Greg wakes up to a to-do list it has generated itself—often flagging things he never asked it to notice, like schedule conflicts or ‘you need to leave this meeting early to make the next one’. It's connected to his email, Slack, and calendar; it knows what projects he's working on and what questions he's been asking lately. Greg says Dots represents a shift not in model capability but in form—the same frontier-grade model, wrapped in a proactive agent shell, is a game changer in the workplace.

— Greg Brockman
23:06

The best interface is no interface at all

Greg Brockman sees AI products converging on zero UI—you can talk to it, message it on Slack, send it an iMessage, and reach it that way. Dots works like this now. But enterprise and personal use diverge here: companies have admins, security teams, and other stakeholders, so features need toggles before they launch. That creates a new problem: how do you tell users that a feature is turned off for them if there's no UI to show it? Then there's generative UI on the flip side—ask an AI how to assemble a bike, and it generates a custom app on the spot to walk you through it, something humans would never spend a month building.

— Greg Brockman
27:14

The window for defenders to stay ahead is closing

Greg Brockman introduces the concept of a ‘defender's window’: frontier AI capabilities can help the defense side patch systems more safely right now, but these capabilities haven't spread widely to attackers yet. Meanwhile, the offensive hacking tools available to everyone keep getting stronger with each model release. He points to Korean banks hit by attacks powered by different models as evidence. The internet was built for resilience, not security, and AI is now exposing those historical debts. OpenAI launched a $1 billion Daybreak initiative to arm frontline defenders with state-of-the-art models so they can find and patch vulnerabilities.

— Greg Brockman
33:29

Security spending should vastly exceed software development costs

As AI makes software development radically cheaper, what you actually want isn't just software—it's reliable, secure, trustworthy software. So security spending should be orders of magnitude larger than software spending itself. He backs this up with OpenAI's own practice: the company once took 25% of its production engineers off all projects to spend full-time using the best available model to hunt for flaws across every bit of infrastructure. After they fixed them, they codified the tools, and every new model release triggers the same hunt-fix-saturate cycle all over again. This loop will run forever.

— Greg Brockman

Glossary

Dots
An always-on proactive agent used internally at OpenAI that continuously tracks tasks and schedules; it keeps working even when the user has stopped.
Daybreak
OpenAI's program that grants network defenders access to cutting-edge model capabilities, backed by $1 billion in funding.
defender's window
A finite window of time before elite AI capabilities are widely available to attackers; currently, defenders still have the edge.
debate / iterative amplification
A technique using an auxiliary AI to identify errors and help humans oversee AI outputs that exceed their direct verification ability.
generative UI
Custom interfaces generated by AI on-demand for the task at hand, replacing fixed menus and buttons.
