Diffusion Isn't Just for Images — It's Going to Win at Language Model Inference

Autoregressive inference has to generate one token at a time, sequentially, and it's a memory-bandwidth bottleneck; diffusion models process many tokens in parallel at once, and the inference workload looks more like training — that's why Inception is betting on it.

3:10

Diffusion models started because GANs were too unstable

Ermon says that back when he was working on image generation he was very dissatisfied with GANs: they worked, but training was extremely unstable and results were hard to reproduce. So in 2019 he and his PhD students turned to score-based generative models, with the idea of training a neural network to denoise — if it can denoise, that means it understands image structure, and it can then work in reverse to progressively refine a clean image out of pure noise. This is the underlying technology of modern diffusion models. He stresses that today's best image, video, some music, and a large number of protein models are all based on diffusion.

— Stefano Ermon
5:11

The 2024 paper moved diffusion onto text

Ermon's team published a paper in 2024 that, for the first time, got a diffusion model to match autoregressive model quality at GPT-2 scale (under a billion parameters): the same perplexity, the same degree of data fit, but roughly 10x faster generation, because diffusion outputs multiple tokens at once. He says this was still fairly academic, but it was enough to make him decide to start a company and scale the technology to commercial scale.

— Stefano Ermon
8:14

Autoregressive inference is a memory-bandwidth bottleneck

Ermon analogises the 2017 switch from RNNs to transformers to the inference stage: during training, transformers won by processing multiple tokens in parallel, but at inference time autoregressive models are still sequential — before generating the 10th token you must first generate all the tokens before it. This workload is extremely memory-bandwidth-bound; most of the time is spent moving weights between levels of the memory hierarchy, with very little arithmetic, which is unfriendly to GPUs. Diffusion models' inference workload looks more like training — processing multiple tokens in parallel at once — so it naturally matches the compute pattern GPUs are good at.

— Stefano Ermon
10:16

Inference efficiency also caps RL post-training

Ermon points out that economics are determined by how much intelligence you get per watt and per dollar. A great deal of progress in reasoning models comes from test-time compute scaling, and one of the bottlenecks in RL post-training is generating rollouts — letting the model explore, scoring trajectories, then improving the model. So inference efficiency directly gates post-training efficiency: a model that scales better at inference time will automatically also scale better at post-training time. This is the core logic behind his bet on diffusion LLMs, and behind his claim that ‘the more parallel approach will eventually win’.

— Stefano Ermon
12:19

Mercury is benchmarked against Haiku, Flash, and the mini series

Ermon says Inception's Mercury model is benchmarked against speed-optimised models like Haiku, Flash, and mini nano, with comparable quality but significantly faster, and it is already serving customers in production. He stresses that you cannot just run a diffusion LLM on vLLM or SGLang — you have to build your own serving engine to handle the complexity of real production workloads. This is both a barrier and a moat: even if someone else trains a diffusion LLM, without a corresponding serving engine they can't put it to use.

— Stefano Ermon
17:21

A voice customer switched from custom chips back to Nvidia GPUs

Ermon gives the example of voice agent company OpenCall: the voice pipeline includes ASR, an inference LLM for tool calling, and TTS, and speed is critical. OpenCall originally served its LLM on Cerebras custom chips to reach the required speed, then switched to Inception's diffusion LLM, because it could get equivalent speed on Nvidia GPUs — higher availability, lower cost, better quality. Ermon says once you get used to a fast model you can't go back, like broadband.

— Stefano Ermon
25:26

Diffusion models are inherently easier to control than autoregressive ones

Ermon says diffusion models are generally easier to control than autoregressive models: with autoregressive you have to wait until the entire object is generated before you know whether it satisfies constraints, whether it's aligned, what score a reward function gives it; diffusion is coarse-to-fine generation, so from early on you can tell whether the direction is right, and you can use external reward functions or constraint sets to guide generation. There is a fair amount of evidence in the academic literature supporting this, and some guidance methods simply can't be done on autoregressive models at all. He sees this as the differentiation space for future product experiences.

— Stefano Ermon
29:29

20% to 30% of tasks are extremely latency-sensitive

Ermon estimates, referencing OpenRouter's task categories (research, chat, coding, software engineering, log processing, etc.), that roughly 20% to 30% of those tasks care a great deal about latency. As a lower bound, that share of the workload can be served by whichever model offers the highest quality within a given latency budget. He also acknowledges that we are not yet at frontier intelligence, and much of the workload still needs frontier-level intelligence, so the addressable market for diffusion models is layered.

— Stefano Ermon
35:36

Flash Attention and DPO both came out of his lab

Ermon responds to the scepticism that ‘academia can't do large models’: the early diffusion work beat GANs at academic scale and was more stable, and later grew into Stable Diffusion and Midjourney; he also co-advised Flash Attention, and DPO originated from a project in his group and is now widely used to align LLMs and diffusion models. He argues academia's advantage is being able to make counterintuitive bets, having excellent students, and people not being afraid to take risks — many important AI ideas originated in academia.

— Stefano Ermon

Glossary

diffusion model
A generative model that starts from pure noise and progressively denoises, iteratively refining a result.
autoregressive model
A model that predicts the next token one at a time, generating sequentially from left to right.
perplexity
A metric measuring how well a language model recognises structure in data; lower is better.
serving engine
The software stack that deploys a trained model as an online service and handles production requests.
RL post-training
Continuing to optimise a model after pretraining with reinforcement learning, often requiring large numbers of rollouts.
