What Solo Founders Lack Isn't Hands, It's Someone Who Makes Them Tell the Truth

Julian's diagnosis of five solo founders points at almost the same thing every time: without a co-founder, you lose the person who forces you to face what you actually did today.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:04

Hundreds of paying users means you haven't given them something worth paying for

Utkar's Game Native has 1.25 million users, but only a few hundred pay $5 to $10 a month for a Discord role, priority support and early builds. Julian's read is blunt: all of that is bonus, not core value — it's essentially a Patreon-style fan club, where people pay to support you, not because it's genuinely a good deal. His standard: a few hundred paying out of 1.25 million is precisely the proof that you haven't yet offered something truly worth paying for. And don't charge by taking existing features away — create something new in the future that users haven't used yet.

— Julian
11:08

A solo founder has no accountability partner, and that's the deadliest part

Julian says solo founding is hardest from zero to one because there's no accountability partner. He uses the gym as an analogy: if you've already agreed to train with someone every day, you're more likely to actually show up. Early on, with no customers, everything is fuzzy, and you're the only one judging whether you're spending your time right. His antidote: be a little kinder to yourself, while staying honest about what you're actually doing each day. He also names a default trap — writing code is the addictive default option, because it always feels like progress, but the only thing that truly settles the question is paying customers.

— Julian
18:13

Let the lab see its own gap first, then sell the solution

Ryan sells RL environments and benchmarks to frontier labs. Julian endorses his path: don't start by selling generic data — first use a public benchmark to get the lab to admit for itself that it has this gap, then offer that you can fill it. Ryan says this is already standard practice among frontier-lab data vendors: show that some model fails at some capability, and a few months later the research lead comes to you asking whether they can train on your data. Ryan's benchmark is a bunch of frontier models playing Werewolf against each other, testing not who wins but reasoning ability, who lies best, and who lies most.

— Julian
21:16

You don't need to be the best, you need to be the only

Ryan worries that being solo will make labs not take him seriously. Julian says the worry is partly valid, but only if you're competing head-on with others. He quotes Kevin Kelly: you don't want to be the best, you want to be the only. So the question isn't "how does this team look" but "is this the only person who can help us solve this problem." He also says being solo is "more in your head than in anyone else's" — you can absolutely answer: I'm just starting out, I only work with a few people, I'll be hiring, and I have AI leverage.

— Julian
26:20

Users don't want features, they want a button they can press

Charlie is torn between building the workflow features customers ask for and going straight for the AI brain that would eliminate all workflows. Julian tells the Magic School story: founder Adil was a former high school principal who wanted teachers to use ChatGPT, ran a whole-school training, and nobody actually used it. He judged that the blocker wasn't AI being too weak but a blank chat box being too scary — it can do anything, and therefore feels like it can do nothing. So Adil built dozens of "micro-apps," each doing one specific thing. First week: 100 users. Second week: 1,000. Julian's conclusion: the feature requests users make are really them telling you their jobs to be done.

— Julian
31:24

A slow deal may not be an efficiency problem but an incentive problem

Charlie says both sides of a wholesale trade know exactly what they want, yet it takes two or three weeks of emails and CSVs to close. Julian doesn't give a direct answer; instead he asks a counterintuitive question: if everyone wants to compress the time, why hasn't it been compressed? Could some incentive be deliberately stretching it out? He says sometimes things drag on for a reason — just not the reason everyone assumes. He also suggests using frequency times severity to decide which problem to tackle first.

— Julian
39:28

Build the brain first, don't start with the body

Andy wants to build "describe a robot, get a design and a 3D-printed kit," and is torn about whether to bring someone in to split software and hardware. Julian turns it around: if you were given a full-time person and enough money, what would you do differently? Andy says he'd put it all into 3D printing and hardware testing. So Julian suggests: could you skip hardware for now, run the character on a phone, put it on a desk as a base — the character can't move, but it exists; then the robot's first boot is releasing the character from the phone into the physical world. He uses dementia as an analogy: what makes a person a person isn't an exoskeleton, it's memory, a way of communicating, the look in their eyes when they recognise you. So early on, the body may matter less than the mind.

— Julian
43:35

Different characters move differently, and that's exactly the hardware problem

Andy explains why he can't let go of physicality: Pikachu, Snoopy, a rover — they move through physical space in completely different ways. If you want to bring Pikachu or an owl into the real world as a robot, you have to solve for different characters moving in different ways. That's why he weights physicality so heavily. Julian concedes the point holds, and says if he built an owl it would be "a real hoot." This is the only moment in the session where a founder pushes back on Julian's advice head-on.

— Andy
