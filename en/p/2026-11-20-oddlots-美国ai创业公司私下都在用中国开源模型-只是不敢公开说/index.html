American AI startups are using Chinese open-weight models in private, they just won't say it publicly

The real moat is not choosing a foundation model—it's fine-tuning open-weight models with proprietary data to build specialized solutions cheaper and more accurate than a giant's single API call.

Use closed-weight flagship models to build, switch to fine-tuned open-weight for production

Minerva's approach: use cutting-edge closed-weight models like Claude and Astra during development—developer time costs more than tokens, don't waste cycles on loops and errors. Once a task becomes repeatable and needs only "customer satisfaction" quality, switch to a framework with completely different economics: stop measuring by price per million tokens, measure by cost per completed task. That's when open-weight models become your workhorse. This means closed and open aren't substitutes but serve two different economic stages.

9:18

Open-weight models themselves are worthless—the value is in fine-tuning

According to Daniel, application-layer companies get real value from open-weight models through fine-tuning, not the models themselves. Labs rely on bought massive datasets; startups can't replicate that scale, so they fine-tune on thousands of their own customer data points to specialize a general model. Example: converting user natural-language requests to SQL using OpenAI models was slow and expensive; after fine-tuning Quen, it's faster and more accurate, giving the company a "small advantage"—the logic Harvey and Cognition use when fine-tuning Kimi with proprietary data to build differentiation.

— Daniel Saidi
12:20

Cost is the first reason for choosing open-weight, but not the only one

Daniel ranks enterprise motivations for choosing open-weight models by importance: first is cost—verify that upfront and operating expenses are lower than OpenAI or Anthropic bills. Second is data sovereignty and control: enterprises send data to closed-weight model APIs and get answers, but the model doesn't improve for your use case. Fine-tuning lets the model continuously improve with your data. Third is negotiating power: once you hold "models trained on our own data," you can pressure closed-weight vendors to price by value, not lock you to a single supplier.

— Daniel Saidi
17:39

Fine-tuning infrastructure is cheap now, but data quality moats remain

A year ago, fine-tuning and self-hosting a model needed a full inference engineering team. Now services like Fireworks and Modal let you do it for about 7 to 9 dollars per million tokens in and out; some achieved solid final training for just 90 dollars. Daniel notes Harvey spent roughly 6 million dollars in compute to build its legal Tenant model; now similar work costs "two or three orders of magnitude less." But what hasn't been commodified is data and evaluation system quality—that's the difference between good and bad fine-tuning, and why data companies like Mercor and Surge can charge labs enormous fees.

— Daniel Saidi
23:44

The real risk from open-weight models is obliterated models, not data theft

When preparing this episode, Daniel tested a DeepSeek version with the "refusal" neurons turned off—an obliterated model. It performed near Astra from February this year but costs about 1/100th of Fable. Labs don't actually worry about "China stealing your data." The real threat is anyone tweaking a few parameters to run thousands of PhD-level, zero-guardrail "hacker clones" in the cloud simultaneously—a real security risk happening now, and Daniel admits he hasn't seen a convincing policy solution yet.

— Daniel Saidi
27:47

Distillation exists, but Chinese open-weight leadership comes from more than copying

To the charge that Chinese open-weight models secretly distill from Claude, Daniel offers a counterexample: Reflection AI is a US open-source company backed by Nvidia, running tens of thousands of Blackwell GPUs, strictly following terms of service—yet its flagship model still lags Chinese open-weight models by three to six months. This shows that even with compute and talent and no rule-breaking distillation, you can't keep up with Chinese labs' pace, suggesting distillation isn't the whole story. Chinese labs have made real innovations in sparse model architectures and open-sourced inference frameworks that leading labs have adopted.

— Daniel Saidi
31:49

Skip benchmarks—judge open-weight models by whether they handle your daily work

According to Daniel, benchmarks are almost useless now. A higher score on some benchmark doesn't tell you if it's actually better in real work. His test: can you drop Kimi straight into your existing coding or customer-service workflow and get equal performance? Half-joking, he says the standard is "how often I swear at my terminal"—if that frequency hasn't dropped, the model hasn't caught up yet.

— Daniel Saidi
38:56

Every knowledge worker using AI agents will need dedicated GPU compute

When debating whether compute is enough, Daniel applies first principles: the US has deployed roughly 5 to 10 million top-tier Blackwell GPUs, while knowledge workers number about 100 million. If every knowledge worker gets a full-time agent running 24/7, we'd need far more compute than deployed today. That's why Musk and Huang reportedly found each other at the White House to discuss compute swaps: even the labs themselves face chronic shortages.

— Daniel Saidi

Glossary

open weight
Publishing final model parameters without training code, data, or methods—different from full open source.
obliterated model
A version with internal "refusal" parameters disabled, producing outputs without guardrails or boundaries.
RL environment
A simulation of real tasks where AI trains repeatedly through trial and error.
