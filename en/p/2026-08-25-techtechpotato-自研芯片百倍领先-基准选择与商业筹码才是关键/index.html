OpenAI's In-House Chip 100x Ahead? That's a Self-Selected Benchmark on a Bespoke Stack

OpenAI's first in-house chip, Jalapeno, beats GB200 by 100x on a self-selected benchmark — but that's the result of a bespoke chip, a bespoke model and a bespoke software stack; the real moat is system integration and pricing power.

This episode isn't a neutral review but a page-by-page teardown of the Hot Chips presentation: it tells you which numbers to trust, which are selection bias, and what an in-house chip means commercially for OpenAI.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:11

The 9 months start from RTL, not from zero

Most media describe Jalapeno as a "clean sheet design," but OpenAI's own timeline shows: architecture concept from a blank page only in October 2024, a codec CLI only in April 2025, tape-out in November 2025, codecs running in May 2026, and ChatGPT on the chip only in July. The host questions the definition of "9 months" throughout: the starting point here is RTL rather than product definition, and layers like EDA and verification in chip design were compressed or parallelised away. So the 9 months is real, but it does not mean anyone else can replicate a chip in 9 months.

5:13

The benchmark's rules themselves favour whoever set the questions

OpenAI chose SemiAnalysis's Inference X open-model benchmark, on the grounds that open models let others run the same set of models on their own hardware. But the host points to two amplifiers: first, it explicitly says "open to hardware specific optimization," which together with hardware-aware training/fine-tuning lets every vendor tune the model into the shape most favourable to itself; second, Jalapeno's results use only single token prediction, while the selected baselines include multi-token prediction. Add the non-goals that exclude time to first token and single-chip throughput, and the whole set of numbers has a strong policy tilt.

7:14

Speculative decoding saves serial steps, not compute

Jalapeno's slides put speculative decoding very near the front. The mechanism is like CPU branch prediction: rather than predicting one token at a time, first do seven mini passes to generate draft tokens, then use one "batched large trunk pass" to verify the first seven and predict the eighth at the same time; as long as the drafts are right, the serial cost drops from eight steps to one. The host compares it to super sampling/frame generation in games. But note: these official benchmarks do not enable speculative decoding, which means the 100x numbers still leave performance headroom, and also means the real full-load figures have to be calculated separately.

10:17

The 100x gap only holds at speeds humans can't use

On GPT-OSS 120B, Jalapeno versus Nvidia GB200 (NVL72): about 100x higher performance at 500 tokens/s, and 53.7x at another 500 tokens/s point; end-to-end time to last token is 4.2x lower at long context, with the lowest latency reduction close to half. The host notes that 500 tokens/s is meaningless for human reading, but meaningful for agent-to-agent chat and batch scenarios. On the larger 670B open model the numbers are pushed down, though the peak still reaches 104x. All comparisons are power-normalised, Jalapeno at 700W and GB200 at about 1.2kW.

16:18

What decides utilisation is memory latency, not bandwidth

The example OpenAI gives: a 128-chip HBM4 system, 1PB/s bandwidth divided by 0.5TB of parameters, could theoretically read the full model weights 2000 times a second, but in practice it never gets close. Latency comes from memory, congested paths, cores out of sync and global synchronisation. Jalapeno's answer is to "co-design the architecture to address the bottleneck" — the host says outright that he has heard this line on fifty pieces of hardware, and what matters is not the slogan but how it puts compute locally and only touches global memory when necessary. In the previous generation's hardware story, the boundary of that "necessary" is where the gap comes from.

18:18

The in-house advantage is network topology, not the chip

Jalapeno is not a retail chip but a rack-scale system. OpenAI uses Broadcom Tomahawk 6 for a two-tier network: a 128-chip scale-up domain and a 48-chip scale-out domain. A large scale-up means large models can take fewer hops; a large scale-out means tensor parallelism has looser bandwidth constraints. The full system is 448 chips, 27 exaflops (4-bit matrix), 432TB HBM, 32PB/s. Because OpenAI only runs its own workloads, it can aim the network topology precisely at its own models' communication patterns — this is the shared logic of hyperscaler in-house chips, and the biggest difference from general-purpose GPUs.

20:20

What AI saves is not engineers but iteration count

OpenAI says it used AI tools to improve every design stage: 10% better matrix unit area, 8% better SIMD unit area, and that is against an "optimised human baseline." The host connects this to what Synopsys/Cadence say: AI solutions can beat human teams, for example doing a 50-person team's six months of work in a week. This does not mean engineers lose their jobs, but that the same people can iterate more versions in half a year. The final judgement deserves to be underlined on its own: humans handle abstraction, frontier AI handles mapping, and that is how the hierarchy grows.

27:31

The return on an in-house chip is negotiating leverage, not savings

OpenAI will not stop buying Nvidia because it has Jalapeno, nor will it drop partnerships like Cerebras; the motive for in-house silicon is optionality, leverage, and the ability to combine across different models, performance tiers and price points. Enterprise customers pick a model and stick with it for 3 to 24 months, and what OpenAI wants is to offer an option for every need that isn't held hostage by a bundle. The host also distinguishes the roles: Richard's job is to make the chip good, pricing is the product team's job. This explains why a chip that only runs internally still has strategic value.

Glossary

speculative decoding
Generating several draft tokens in parallel first, then verifying them in one large batch, reducing the number of serial inference steps.
multi-token prediction (MTP)
A model predicting several subsequent tokens at once rather than one by one; optional in Jalapeno's baselines, and not used in the official results.
time to last token (TTLT)
The end-to-end latency from request to the generation of the last token, OpenAI's primary optimisation metric.
MXFP4
A 4-bit floating-point matrix compute format; Jalapeno reports 13.4 PFLOPS in it.
scale-up / scale-out domain
The two interconnect tiers of 128 chips and 48 chips in the Jalapeno system, serving large models and bandwidth constraints respectively.
Tomahawk 6
A Broadcom switch chip, used by OpenAI to connect Jalapeno's scale-up and scale-out networks.
