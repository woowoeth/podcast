Nvidia tears off the band-aid: admits price hikes, gross margin falls to 72%-73%

Huang chose to disclose cost increases and gross margin declines all at once this quarter to end Wall Street's sustained anxiety about Nvidia's margins.

NvidiaComputeChip pricingGross marginAI infrastructureEmployment and AI
This episode provides concrete numbers on sold-out chips, pricing increases, and capital flows to AI labs, rather than routine earnings summaries.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

1:07

The bottleneck is compute capacity, not customer demand

Huang argues that what constrains AI lab growth is no longer the number of customers or technology, but compute supply itself—token generation is already profitable, yet customers cannot buy all the compute they want. He emphasizes that AI extends far beyond a handful of leading labs: enterprises, sovereign clouds, and neoclouds are all ramping purchases. This portion of the business typically remains invisible to outsiders.

— Jensen Huang
3:09

Next year's chips are already sold out a year early

Huang says customers must now plan at least a year in advance because they're not buying computers but factories—each representing $500 billion to $600 billion in total capital investment. Every chip Nvidia can manufacture next year is already pre-sold. Revenue is forecast to grow 70% year-over-year, but this 70% is constrained by the supply chain, not demand. When the host suggested next year's revenue would exceed $1 trillion, Huang did not confirm, only stating that actual demand exceeds 70%.

— Jensen Huang
5:12

Price increases are strategically announced, not reluctant admissions

After memory chip costs surged, Nvidia chose this quarter to proactively disclose price increases and gross margin declines, absorbing cost pressures and reflecting them in new pricing. Huang says this was to eliminate investor anxiety about gross margins once and for all; gross margin will fall from 75% to 72%-73% next year. He describes this as "ripping off the band-aid"—ending the uncertainty and leaving only execution.

— Jensen Huang
7:22

Amazon orders two million more GPUs, but revenue is highly concentrated

Amazon Web Services announced an additional order for two million GPUs, with delivery planned for 2027-2028. However, Huang also admits that half of Nvidia's revenue comes from six hyperscalers, and which is largest changes quarter to quarter. These companies are both customers and partners reselling compute to developers globally—meaning Nvidia's revenue structure depends heavily on the purchasing timing of a handful of giants.

— Jensen Huang
9:26

Nvidia is not just a chip vendor but also backing AI labs

Responding to claims that Nvidia is becoming "the banker of the AI boom, dangerously exposed," Huang makes two arguments: First, Nvidia invests like a venture firm in companies using its technology—SpaceX's IPO has already returned $20 billion to $30 billion. Second, Nvidia provides a backstop on compute purchases for AI labs lacking investment-grade credit ratings and unable to access cheap funding while pre-IPO, specifically in two critical labs that are not yet investment-grade.

— Jensen Huang
12:34

Huang argues against Bill Gates's proposal to tax token generation

Bill Gates proposed that unemployment is severe enough to warrant taxing token generation. Huang publicly disagreed with Gates's reasoning. He argues that more productive companies typically hire more people, not fewer, because they have growth ambitions; meanwhile chip foundries, packaging plants, system integrators, and AI data center construction are already creating skilled jobs. AI will net-create employment overall. Huang is not against taxation itself, just disagrees with Gates's framing of the problem.

— Jensen Huang
