Nvidia Has No Five-Year Plan: Jensen Huang Built the Company Into a GPU

Jensen Huang's eight counterintuitive management rules aren't scattered points — they're a distributed operating system: 60 executives reporting directly to him, pay identical to the cent, no one-on-ones, no plans. The org's communication structure determines the product architecture.

4:13

60 direct reports, because the word "manage" is already the wrong word

The normal management span is a dozen or so people; 60 people reporting directly to the CEO looks impossible. Wang Yajun's breakdown: the problem is the word "manage" — managing means giving orders to be executed. Jensen Huang's position is that these people don't need managing; each of them does their own thing exceptionally well, and all they need is for you to create opportunities for them. These are two different management logics. Wang Yajun went through a phase at Longfor where roughly 50 people reported directly to the CEO with no layer in between, so he says he wasn't surprised at all. But he also admits this does create a real problem: what happens to developing people.

— Wang Yajun
7:53

60 people paid the same, down to the cent

Jensen Huang's analogy: in an Excel spreadsheet there's a number representing a person's pay; you click the bottom-right corner and drag it straight down to everyone — these 60 people's pay is the same, accurate to the dollar or the cent. Wang Yajun sees this as a match between a sense of equality and the value produced: anyone who can be placed in a role reporting directly to the CEO is a star who can run their own show, different roles but the same magnitude, so they should share a sense of equality. More crucially, when they interact no one is above or below anyone else, and when they discuss a problem they deal with the problem itself, rather than "you're more senior, you decide."

— Wang Yajun
9:43

No one-on-ones — feedback happens in the room

Many management scholars stress that bosses need to learn to have one-on-one conversations, because feedback and emotional issues are best handled in private. Jensen Huang does the opposite: he gives feedback in the room. When a meeting surfaces a disagreement or a way of thinking that has a problem, he won't pull that person aside; he'll say it right there, so everyone hears it. His logic is that this isn't criticism, it's learning — besides learning from your own mistakes, the best thing is to learn from other people's mistakes. If another 10 or 20 people hear it at the same time, the speed and frequency of learning is much higher. Wang Yajun says the precondition is that people aren't constantly thinking about face.

— Wang Yajun
12:19

The org is like a GPU: Conway's Law, hardware edition

One commenter online said Nvidia's organization sounds kind of like a GPU, and the comment got a lot of likes. Wang Yajun says the metaphor is exactly right. He learned from an AMD executive that AMD's chips are a chiplet-style modular design rather than one monolithic design; a GPU goes further — a large number of weaker cores plus enormous inter-core transport channels and bandwidth. Abstracted into software, that's Conway's Law: an organization's communication structure determines its product architecture. So talking purely about "what organizational form is good" is meaningless; the organizational form, the product form and market demand have to reach a continuously evolving best match.

— Wang Yajun
18:36

The most common mistake in learning from Nvidia is copying the motions

Wang Yajun gives the example of Youzan founder Bai Ya (白鸦): Bai Ya visited Nvidia, saw a large venue, learned it was where Jensen Huang holds all-hands meetings every month, and reflected that once a company gets big, processes, systems and institutions really aren't much use — the top person still has to personally attend to the key things. The practice he drew from it was to go past his direct reports and look at what the layer below is doing. Wang Yajun thinks this leans toward control, whereas what Jensen Huang does at an all-hands is provide the information needed for decisions, similar to what Zhang Yiming calls context not control. The same all-hands format — talking about performance metrics versus talking about how to make decisions — is a completely different thing.

— Wang Yajun
35:27

No documents — he reads over a hundred employee emails a day

Jensen Huang doesn't encourage writing documents internally; instead he encourages employees to email him the five most important things, and he spends a stretch every morning reading over a hundred emails from employees at every level — anyone can message him. Wang Yajun guesses what he wants is a sense of the company's reality, the company's pulse — what is actually happening. But he stresses that not everything should be sent: telling him the actual information is very valuable, telling the boss the conclusion is unnecessary. He mentions Ren Zhengfei saying that a frontline employee who writes the chairman a ten-thousand-word article on company strategy is the kind of person the company cannot keep.

— Wang Yajun
38:12

No layoffs only works if you believe people can grow

Without layoffs, how do you weed out employees who don't push themselves? Wang Yajun says he was pretty shocked when he heard it too, but some formidable companies really don't lay people off proactively — Toyota, for instance. He lays out several preconditions: first, you have to believe that both people and companies grow continuously, that people aren't rigid and fixed; second, you have to be more careful in hiring, with growth potential perhaps the first factor and professional expertise second; third, you can't chase short-cycle opportunities to make quick money — hiring in a rush when demand surges and then having no choice but to cut when demand falls. Jensen Huang's line is I will torture you into greatness — I will torture you into greatness.

— Wang Yajun
42:48

Ignore market share — only do what no one else can do

Jensen Huang only does what others can't do; he doesn't look at Market Share, he goes after a Zero Billion Dollar Market — a market that doesn't exist yet, where he wants to create a new market. His exact words are Market Share is for the losers. Wang Yajun connects this to strategic choice: if you copy others and rush to imitate, it's easy to end up with everyone adding capacity at once, capacity coming in extremely fast, the point where capacity and demand match arriving very quickly, and then you have to cut people fast. He gives real estate as an example: many companies died at around the three-year mark, when industry-wide demand surged, everyone pushed scale in, and when it came down it simply didn't work.

— Wang Yajun
45:10

No plans, because you fundamentally can't predict

Jensen Huang doesn't make five-year plans, three-year plans, or even one-year plans. Wang Yajun says he wasn't surprised at all, because that's how he operated before. He went to Longfor in 2012 and found that the real estate industry faces extremely high uncertainty — market, environment, regulatory policy, financial policy — making it impossible to infer how big the market will be in three years; and with the market highly fragmented, talking about market share is meaningless. Later the whole company reached a consensus: we can't predict what the future will be, but we can respond quickly. So there was no five-year strategy, no three-year strategy, only a one-year operating plan. He says Longfor's strategy at the time was a single page, used for several years, fine-tuned each year by half a sentence or a third of a sentence.

— Wang Yajun
48:52

Without love there is no innovation — fear is the enemy of innovation

In his earlier interview with the Stripe founder, Jensen Huang said one of the company's values is love and care. Wang Yajun says this isn't in conflict with rationality: innovation means facing the unknown and uncertainty, and it will fail many times before it succeeds; if innovation is a company's core capability, you have to know how to manage failure, how to accept failure, and the cost of failure can't be too high. He uses the analogy of a child holding a cup: you can give them an unbreakable cup, or you can give them a breakable one so they learn, but if they break three or five cups you'll be exhausted — without love you can't do this. Many companies manage through fear, adding a bit of care only when filling out an ESG form, and the underlying logic never connects.

— Wang Yajun
1:11:45

A company isn't a barrel — it's a bamboo forest

Wang Yajun proposes an alternative to the barrel theory: the bamboo forest. Bamboo is segmented both above and below ground, but the roots underneath are all connected; a patch of bamboo you see might be the life of a single bamboo that grew dozens of stalks. He has personally experienced trying to dig out a small patch of bamboo — you can only do it with a shovel, and it's agony. Mapped onto an organization: 60 people reporting directly to the CEO, and the horizontal alignment between teams is a bamboo-forest-style organic connection, not a barrel — where each function is a stave, hooked together with a few hoops, with a big board at the bottom of the barrel as the CEO. He says horizontal alignment has a methodology, and it can be taught, learned, and grown sustainably.

— Wang Yajun
