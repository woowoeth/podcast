Black Holes Have Only Two Parameters, but How They Form Is Still a Mystery

Gravitational waves and the EHT confirm that black holes really do have only two parameters, but how a 120-solar-mass black hole forms, whether dark matter is made of primordial black holes, and how the last parsec gets crossed all remain open questions.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

3:07

Observers and theorists don't mean the same black hole

The same black hole: observers treat it as just a heavy, dark point mass, used to explain gravitational interactions with nearby matter; theorists treat it as a special geometry in which spacetime curves so much that time stops at the event horizon. The different definitions lead to opposite research methods — observers extrapolate, theorists zoom in to small-scale structure. This determines every later argument about ‘how do we prove what we're seeing is a black hole’.

— Vitor Cardoso
10:47

A trillion black holes across the universe differ by only two parameters

A stationary Kerr black hole in vacuum is determined by only two parameters, mass and spin, and all trillion black holes in the universe are the same; charge exists in theory but the universe neutralises it. But the theorem relies on two assumptions, vacuum and stationarity, while the real universe has matter and changes. Cardoso's work is about breaking those assumptions to see whether a black hole's radiation and dynamics change as a result.

— Vitor Cardoso
19:34

The singularity is always hidden by a horizon — still only a conjecture

If the Earth suddenly collapsed into a black hole, it would spin too fast to form a horizon at all; the equations give a naked singularity, and we have no theory for collapse unshielded by a horizon. Cosmic censorship claims singularities are always hidden by a horizon, but that is only a conjecture — in gravitational theories in higher-dimensional spacetime it already fails. Physicists still don't know whether it is correct.

— Vitor Cardoso
20:52

A 120-solar-mass black hole shouldn't exist

Gravitational-wave observations have seen black holes up to 120 solar masses, but that mass range is hard to form: stars above 80 solar masses become unstable and tend to split apart rather than collapse; the path of merging first and growing later is also blocked because the black holes are too small and rarely meet. Meanwhile every galaxy centre hosts a supermassive black hole of millions to billions of solar masses, but for intermediate-mass black holes in between there is only scattered evidence.

— Vitor Cardoso
28:59

Explaining dark matter with black holes is too good to be true

Explaining dark matter with black holes requires specifying a mass first. Too heavy and they would be exposed by microlensing, which observations don't see; too light and they evaporate through Hawking radiation and don't survive to today. Microlensing has already excluded most of the parameter space, leaving only a narrow corner. Cardoso's judgement: possible, but unlikely; he calls the explanation ‘too good to be true, a bit of a Hail Mary’.

— Vitor Cardoso
37:15

Ninety percent of that EHT image isn't measured

What the Event Horizon Telescope photographed is not the event horizon but the ‘light ring’ of the innermost photon orbit. More importantly, the images of M87 and the Galactic Centre are extremely blurry: actual measurements account for only about 10%, and the other 90% comes from prior knowledge based on tens of thousands of simulations of the matter around the black hole. Cardoso thinks the ideal future is an instrument with enough resolution that prior-based reconstruction is no longer needed.

— Vitor Cardoso
42:38

Seeing lower-frequency signals means stretching the interferometer arms to 1 million km

After LIGO, KAGRA is already running, and around 2030 India will join; Europe is planning the Einstein Telescope (ground-based, an upgraded LIGO), and Europe and the US are pushing LISA into space. LISA stretches the interferometer arms from LIGO's 4 km to about 1 million km, probing lower-frequency signals beyond 20 Hz to 1 kHz, with the goal of supermassive black hole mergers and even the inhomogeneities of the early universe.

— Vitor Cardoso
1:00:37

A hydrogen atom may grow around a spinning black hole

A spinning black hole can amplify low-frequency incoming waves and bounce them back; if the wave has mass, escape fails, it falls back and is amplified again, forming a ‘black hole bomb’. Axions have mass and are very light, so theory predicts: if dark matter is axions, a cloud of axions will condense around a spinning black hole, with a structure like a hydrogen atom — the nucleus is the spinning black hole, the cloud is the axions. This model recurs again and again in Cardoso's calculations.

— Vitor Cardoso

Glossary

no-hair theorem
A stationary black hole in vacuum is determined only by mass, spin (and charge), with no other degrees of freedom.
Kerr metric
The exact general-relativity solution describing the spacetime outside a rotating black hole.
light ring
The innermost orbit on which photons can circle a black hole, which sets the shape of the shadow.
cosmic censorship
The conjecture that physical singularities are always hidden by an event horizon, so naked singularities never occur in nature.
matched filtering
Comparing theoretical waveform templates against detector output to extract a gravitational-wave signal from noise.
last parsec problem
Once two supermassive black holes get within about 1 parsec, there is no mechanism to keep them merging — which contradicts observations.
