The In-Between Tech and Trust Podcast 2026-08-27

Open-source models pass through five or six intermediaries, who fills the accountability gap in the EU AI Act?

Open weights do not equal open trust; after passing through five or six intermediaries, responsibility for open-source models becomes blurred, and the EU AI Act's exemption design may leave a structural accountability gap. Europe needs layered responsibility and patient capital.

This episode breaks 'openness' back into mechanisms: the chain of five or six intermediaries, the loopholes in the AI Act's exemptions, Europe's three timelines, and the double meaning of 'agency'—all are rare analytical frameworks.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

2:01

Don't ask the public to trust more; demand technology be more trustworthy

She first breaks down 'trust' in the technical context into four meanings: engineering reliability and dependability; the zero-trust paradigm in cryptography—'never trust, always verify'; institutional trust, i.e., trusting a company's policies, incentives, and accountability mechanisms; and epistemic trust, i.e., whether what technology tells us is true. Wikipedia has verifiability norms, journalism has source checking, academia has citation systems, but AI is challenging all these mechanisms simultaneously. Her conclusion: rather than asking the public to trust technology more, we should demand that technology and its underlying institutions be more trustworthy.

— Sebnem Erener
4:08

Openness is a spectrum, not a switch, but exemptions are designed as a switch

The EU AI Act sets tiered obligations for general-purpose AI models but carves out exceptions for open-source models: as long as they are released under an open license, not commercialized, and weights are public, some obligations are waived. The surface logic holds—if weights are public, piling on compliance burdens seems pointless. But she points out: openness is a spectrum, not a switch, and 'not commercialized' is ambiguous in the open-source ecosystem. More critically, the law assumes a single responsible party—'one company placing a model on the market'—which does not match the reality of the open-source ecosystem.

— Sebnem Erener
5:11

Open-source models pass through five or six parties, but the law recognizes only one

After an open-source model is uploaded to Hugging Face, it gets served by inference platforms, routed by aggregators, fine-tuned on third-party infrastructure, and by the time it reaches end users, five or six intermediaries have touched it and exerted some form of control. Legal systems generally assume a model is placed on the market by a single company, which does not hold at all along this chain. The core of her research is identifying the gaps in the downstream chain of responsibility among these intermediaries. This is also a concrete example of her view that the deeper the openness, the higher the governance complexity.

— Sebnem Erener
9:36

Openness without auditing reduces trust, not increases it

Her response is not to put all responsibility on the original developer but to layer it: developers bear primary responsibility for documentation, risk assessment, and known misuse risks; hosting platforms, fine-tuners, deployers, and even end users who intentionally misuse each bear their own responsibility. She also emphasizes that openness is a mechanism for distributing access and participation rights, not an automatic guarantee of transparency or fairness; responsible openness also requires independent auditing, staged release, incident reporting, and community governance. Without these, open source can actually reduce trust, because no one knows who to hold accountable after a model spreads, whereas closed-source models at least have clear responsibility.

— Sebnem Erener
14:53

European tech sovereignty cannot be judged on a single timeline

The European tech sovereignty package contains several timelines with very different speeds: legal sovereignty advances fastest—the Cloud and AI Development Act already requires data to be located in the EU, owned and controlled by the EU; model sovereignty is medium-speed—Open Euro LLM, with 20 institutions, releases its first industry model next month, and Europa, led by Italian company Domin, aims to challenge the world's strongest; hardware sovereignty is slowest—the Chips Act invests 200 billion euros only by 2035. The biggest risk is bundling everything into one timeline, generating uniform optimism or pessimism. We should be decisive on fast-moving things and patient on slow ones.

— Sebnem Erener
18:00

Open models win on quantity, but governance difficulty doubles

She originally envisioned a future where 'the model layer is open and the agent layer is closed,' but reality is different. Open-weight models dominate in quantity: about 60% of tokens on the largest model router come from Chinese open models, and four of the five most-used models globally are Chinese open models; US closed-source labs do not compete with open source but instead segment the market with different value capture strategies. The mixed landscape means both governance systems must be done right: the open-source side needs audit infrastructure, explainability, and incident response; the closed-source side needs clear responsible entities—this is the most complex governance scenario.

— Sebnem Erener
22:05

European companies are not unable to build; they are acquired too early

Asked what Europe needs most, her answer is direct and somewhat counterintuitive: capital. Specifically, growth-stage capital—patient, risk-tolerant, able to stay until later stages. Europe does not lack innovation; it lacks patient capital that prevents companies from being bought out too early. European companies are not unable to build; they are acquired too early. She hopes for investors who believe in Europe's future and in European founders. This point pulls the tech sovereignty discussion back from models and chips to financial infrastructure, a layer that is easily overlooked.

— Sebnem Erener
23:10

The more autonomous the system, the heavier—not lighter—human responsibility

She worries that the word 'agency' is being conflated in AI discussions. In agentic AI, agency refers to a system's ability to autonomously execute tasks, which AI is rapidly acquiring; but in the legal sense, agency refers to an entity with intent, the ability to understand consequences, and the capacity to be held accountable—this is exclusive to natural or legal persons. A person can be sued, fined, and held responsible; an AI model, no matter how autonomous, cannot. The more autonomous the system, the heavier the responsibility on humans and companies, not transferred to the model. Linguistic confusion can imply that models can bear responsibility that should be borne by humans.

— Sebnem Erener

Glossary

zero trust
A security paradigm: trust no entity by default, verify every access request.
open washing
Claiming open source merely by releasing weights, without disclosing key information such as training data.
general purpose AI models
A category in the EU AI Act for AI models covering a broad range of tasks, the main body of frontier models.
layered responsibility
Allocating responsibility along the value chain: developers, hosts, fine-tuners, deployers, and users each bear their own responsibility.
