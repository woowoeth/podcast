Before Your Teen's First Party, Agree on a Code Word and an Exit Plan

Research shows the earlier kids start drinking, the more likely they are to move toward heavy use — so "I drink, you drink, call me anytime" is the wrong move. The right one is a clear prohibition plus a zero-punishment code word and pickup plan.

1:00

Fix yourself first, then fix the kid

The episode opens by asking parents to deal with their own fear: this really is frightening, but there are also reasons to be excited for your kid. You have to tell apart healthy vigilance and fear-based parenting. At the same time, judge two things — are you ready, and is your kid ready, and who needs to be ready first. The order matters, because if you go into the talk carrying fear, the conversation turns into an interrogation instead of groundwork.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
2:00

Don't have the talk in the car on the way

The conversation should happen early and in scattered pieces, like seasoning — values and expectations seeping in a little at a time in ordinary moments, not suddenly delivered on the drive to the party. What to ask: what do you want to get out of this party, and out of parties in general? Where does your picture of a party come from, and how far off is what you see in movies from reality? Staying curious gets you far more information than lecturing. She also notes: even though we were young once, we don't actually remember what it felt like.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
3:00

Even at 13, you have to talk about alcohol and drugs

Even if your kid is only 13 and this is just a first party, you still have to talk about alcohol and drugs, because something could be handed to him and he'd freeze on the spot. Two things need to be present at once in that talk: a clear, firm boundary (my expectation is that you won't touch it), and clear, firm grace (whatever happens, you are the person he comes to). But this main line often gets misread as "so be his buddy and give him a drink before the party" — a mistake that keeps recurring.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
4:04

Early exposure doesn't train moderation

She explicitly opposes the "give him a little so he can practice" approach, because the research points the other way: the earlier substance use starts, the more likely it is to move toward heavier use, and by college, binge drinking becomes more likely. Early exposure also raises the risk of later substance use disorders and psychotic disorders. It's worse in the short term too — drinking may relax him in the moment, but the next day he pays for choices he wouldn't normally make, and he's more likely to fall apart. So the tone isn't "I drink, you drink, call me anytime" — it's "my expectation is that you won't drink and won't use, because your safety matters most to me."

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
6:12

Agree on the code word and exit plan in advance

The concrete move is to agree in advance on an emoji or code word: if your kid sends it, that means "emergency, come get me," and the parent goes to pick him up without asking a single question. Triggers include the party spinning out of control beyond what he's ready for, and also his own accidental misstep — both cases run through the same zero-shame, zero-punishment process. Once he's in the car, don't say "what happened, tell me everything" — just say "I'm glad you contacted me, let's go home." She stresses that both things can hold at once: holding the expectation that he won't touch this stuff, and providing a punishment-free exit.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
9:16

An Airbnb party means no adults

Ask clearly who's going, whether any parent will be present, and where it is. She personally insists another adult must be there, because some cities have seen a trend of parties in rented Airbnbs or other places that don't belong to any parent — kids go to a random location with no adults and no supervision, and this gets treated as a given. Kids will say "everyone's going there" and "you're invading my privacy." Her response: I won't read your diary, but your phone, your social media, and which party you go to are my business, because my business is keeping you safe and letting you earn more and more freedom as you grow.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
14:28

"All the parents said yes" is a lie

When a kid says "every parent agreed, only you're crazy," she offers a way to disprove it: of the parents who come to her, how many are saying "my kid says I'm the only one who said no." She's heard that line herself as a parent, and she's also talking to other parents, so she knows it isn't true. Teens who badly want your yes are smart enough to gauge whether saying it will get to you; once they see you have adult confidence, they either stop saying it or keep saying it while you're no longer affected, because you don't need to prove yourself to anyone.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
24:58

When your kid brings back a secret, don't go tell on him

If your kid or his friend tells you something in the car and says "you can't tell anyone," then your kid's trust in you matters more than anything — don't immediately notify the other parent. Tell apart "life-threatening" from "merely against your values": if someone had one beer and you're against beer, you don't need to be the one who reports it; but if it's a drug where one try could kill, or any form of assault occurred, then work out with your kid how to tell the other parent, pulling him into the decision instead of executing policy behind his back. She also notes that many parents actually don't want to know information that would require them to act, because that means conflict with their kid.

— Dr. Aliza Pressman
