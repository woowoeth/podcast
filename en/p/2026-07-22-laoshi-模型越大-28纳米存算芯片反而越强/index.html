A 28nm Chip Beats by 1000x: The Compute-in-Memory Black Magic That Needs No EUV

Zhicun Technology turns 28nm Flash into a compute-in-memory chip, exploiting the physical property that efficiency rises with array size, so efficiency improves as models grow — and Transformers are pure upside for it.

A rare episode that lays out compute-in-memory's physics, process choices and four years of tape-out blood and tears, with unusual candor from a founder's point of view.

The argument · tap a timestamp to hear it

4:00

Before he founded the company, nobody in the industry believed in compute-in-memory

Wang Shaodi wanted to build compute-in-memory chips while doing his PhD. Before graduating in 2016 he approached four or five leading chip companies in China and the US; none had heard of the technology, and none would open a new direction for a fresh PhD graduate. Academia was a dead end too — a Peking University professor asked him point-blank where the funding would come from, and at the time very few chip grants nationwide exceeded a million yuan, not even enough to pay for a tape-out. With both paths blocked, he had no choice but to start his own company.

— Wang Shaodi
7:01

The bigger the model, the faster the read

In a conventional von Neumann architecture, matrix multiplication requires reading data out of memory cells one by one and sending it to the arithmetic unit; the larger the memory array, the slower the readout — a law of physics. Compute-in-memory instead turns each memory cell itself into a compute node in the matrix multiplication, so a single activation completes an entire matrix computation without row-by-row, column-by-column readout. That means scaling an algorithm from a few rows to ten thousand rows still takes only one activation, and effective bandwidth and compute actually grow as the model grows — the exact opposite of the von Neumann rule.

— Wang Shaodi
8:02

Ohm's law is the principle behind the black magic

At the core of compute-in-memory is using Ohm's law — voltage times conductance equals current — to implement multiplication directly: apply a voltage along the rows of the memory, each memory cell is itself a conductance value, and the current that naturally flows out along the columns is the product of voltage and conductance; currents from multiple rows in the same column sum together, completing addition physically. The whole matrix multiplication is finished in a single read, with no need at all for the adders and multipliers of digital chips — it is essentially using the most basic electrical relationships from middle-school physics to do AI computation.

— Wang Shaodi
13:02

Why bet on the most ordinary Flash memory

Faced with SRAM, non-volatile Flash, the RRAM much discussed in academia and other storage media, Zhicun chose the seemingly most unremarkable NOR Flash, because the compute current Flash needs for analog multiplication is the lowest of any memory, and compute-in-memory requires activating as many cells at once as possible to process as much in parallel as possible — lower current means a larger array can be turned on and effective compute is stronger; Flash is also the most mature memory process, making tape-out and debugging fastest. The price was a hard fight on precision: it took several hundred people at the company seven or eight years to get precision to the level applications required.

— Wang Shaodi
16:04

Transformers are pure upside for compute-in-memory

Moving from deep learning to Transformers gave compute-in-memory two dividends: first, deep learning often reads a parameter once to compute tens of thousands of times, so memory bandwidth pressure is modest; a Transformer reads each parameter once and computes once, so the read-to-compute ratio is inherently 1:1, whereas architectures like GPUs typically run 64:1 to 128:1 and the worst can reach 1000:1 — a perfect match for compute-in-memory's 1:1 storage-to-compute ratio. Second, Transformer matrices are 1000 to 10,000 times larger than those in deep learning, and compute-in-memory finishes the whole matrix in one go, so the larger the matrix the higher the compute efficiency — again by 1000 to 10,000 times. On top of that, large models need only INT4's 4-bit precision, far below the 12 to 16 bits the deep learning era demanded, which sharply lowers the bar to deployment.

— Wang Shaodi
21:05

Six tape-outs in four years, and powering up is when the nightmare begins

There are no ready-made EDA tools for compute-in-memory, and each chip that comes back from tape-out may hide dozens of problems — for instance, a design targeting 8-bit precision that in fact achieves only 3 bits. The team has to locate them one by one: spending more than a month slicing the chip open and running experiment after experiment to pin down a single problem, only to find precision barely improves after the fix, because dozens of other problems are still dragging it down — so they redesign the experiment, slice the chip again, and hunt the next problem. For an ordinary chip, powering up means the job is done; for compute-in-memory, powering up is when debugging begins. That cycle lasted four years and six major failures, and the company has now taped out close to thirty times in total.

— Wang Shaodi
32:10

Anker's earbuds: from earbuds to a Guinness World Record

After ChatGPT made generative models hot in 2023, Anker's algorithm team came to Zhicun wanting to run a generative audio algorithm in earbuds for call noise reduction — but that requires a chip with strong compute, large capacity, low power, small size and controllable cost all at once, nearly impossible with a von Neumann architecture. Zhicun took on a Transformer model with several million parameters (earbud chips had previously fit generally under 500,000 parameters) using a 28nm NOR Flash compute-in-memory chip, spent nearly three years cracking it, and ultimately won the Guinness World Record for the highest voice restoration in call noise reduction, far ahead of second place — which Wang Shaodi calls a dimensional strike against conventional earbud audio chips.

— Wang Shaodi
41:17

China doesn't need EUV, but compute-in-memory needs China speed

Wang Shaodi believes compute-in-memory's long-term advantage lies in China's manufacturing and iteration speed: China's consumer electronics and new energy vehicle supply chains can compress the entire chain from materials and components to products into a very short cycle of rapid iteration, which overseas players cannot do, and compute-in-memory precisely needs the shortest-distance interaction and feedback among materials and equipment, foundries, design companies and product applications. He judges that compute-in-memory has a chance to grow from a coprocessor in niche scenarios like earbuds into a universal memory that unifies the various dedicated algorithms on the edge — just as the market for this technology did not exist at all before it was actually built.

— Wang Shaodi

Glossary

Von Neumann architecture
The conventional chip architecture that separates memory from compute and requires data to be shuttled back and forth
NOR Flash
The non-volatile memory medium Zhicun chose, whose compute current is far lower than other memories
NAND Flash
Three-dimensionally stacked high-density flash, slower to read and write but with enormous capacity, suited to storing large models
EDA
The software toolchain for chip design and verification, currently almost a blank in compute-in-memory
INT4
The low-precision format commonly used for large-model inference, a far lower bar for compute-in-memory than early deep learning
Universal Memory
The ideal memory concept that is large in capacity, fast, and able to serve multiple scenarios at once
