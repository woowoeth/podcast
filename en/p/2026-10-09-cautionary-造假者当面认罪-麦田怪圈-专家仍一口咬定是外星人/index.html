Hoaxers confess the circles were fake; researchers insist the aliens were real

2:20

A three-thousand-pound prize proved humans could fake crop circles overnight

To test whether humans could create crop circles or if something more exotic was needed, organizers divided a farm into 12 sections. Each team had to create a complex pattern—concentric circles, triangles, various lines—between 10 p.m. and 3 a.m., unseen. At dawn, a helicopter flew over to photograph the results, and judges scored them. The winning team took home £3,000 (about $15,000 today). The real purpose wasn't the prize but a controlled experiment: Could humans actually do this?

8:36

Fourteen nights of alarm clocks at 4:15 a.m. decoded a message from space

Colin marked each crop-circle location on a map in his garden shed with colored pins, but he could never find a pattern—until his carefully installed security alarm went off 14 consecutive nights at precisely 4:15 a.m., driving his neighbors to complain. After many checks with no malfunction found, he stared at the map one day and realized the pins formed an equilateral triangle with 41.5-mile sides—and 4, 1, 5. After that day, the alarm never rang at 4:15 a.m. again. He believed he'd received a message.

— Colin Andrews
12:44

A drunken wager turned into a decade-long hoax by two middle-aged men

Two men named Doug and Dave, regulars at their local pub, were discussing a UFO report one evening when Doug suggested they flatten a nearby wheat field into a circle to see if anyone would think a flying saucer had landed. One night they snuck into the field, spent half an hour with an iron rod compressing the wheat into a circle about 30 feet across. At first, no one noticed. But they became addicted to it, upgrading to wooden boards tied with rope to work faster, choosing slopes and roadside locations for visibility, making designs increasingly complex.

18:54

Confessed hoaxers' in-person demonstration failed to convince the experts of the truth

After years of hoaxing, Doug and Dave reached out to a newspaper, then performed in front of journalists, creating a pattern and inviting crop-circle researcher Pat Delgado to inspect it. The moment Delgado stepped into the field, he declared ‘no human being could do this,’ then the journalist introduced him to the two hoaxers themselves. Delgado collapsed emotionally. But the next day, when Doug and Dave publicly demonstrated their technique before the media, Delgado and his partner Colin fought back, claiming what they'd just seen was obviously fake—crude workmanship that proved nothing except that two fit sixty-year-olds could do it.

20:03

False evidence retracted later, but people keep believing their original conclusion anyway

Psychologist Craig Anderson had students analyze personality-test results for two firefighters. One group was told excellent firefighters take risks; the other was told they're cautious. After turning in their answers, Anderson revealed the test data was completely fabricated. But when he asked ‘Do risk-takers make better firefighters?’ both groups still tended to agree with their original assignment—despite knowing the evidence was fake. Psychologists call this belief perseverance.

26:18

A competition proved ordinary people could replicate every feature experts claimed needed aliens

Of the 12 teams in the competition, all but one (which deliberately wasn't trying) successfully created complex patterns—concentric circles, triangles. Aerial photos showed clean lines and sharp edges. The winning team, three young engineers from a helicopter company, used two lightweight ladders and a plank to move undetectably, extendable PVC tubing to measure concentric circles, and a roller made from PVC pipe wrapped with rope to press down the crop. Organizer Rupert Sheldrake admitted the experiment's conclusion was clear: the most sophisticated crop circles of that era could be replicated entirely by humans, with no need to invoke aliens.

— Rupert Sheldrake
34:45

Proving eighty percent were human-made earned researchers hate mail instead of vindication

In 1999, Colin secured funding for a two-year study. He hired private investigators to infiltrate hoaxing groups and learn the tricks, then used magnetometers and aerial analysis to hunt for human traces in the fields. In 2000, he published: over the past two years, 80% of England's crop circles were human-made, but the remaining 20% showed no evidence of human involvement. He expected the focus to shift to that mysterious 20%. Instead, he received hate mail from crop-circle enthusiasts calling him a ‘propagandist’ taking CIA or Rockefeller money to suppress the alien truth.

— Colin Andrews
