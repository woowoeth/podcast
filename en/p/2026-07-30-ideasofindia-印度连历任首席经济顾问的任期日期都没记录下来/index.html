India has no record of when its chief economic advisors served

Without institutional archives or official transition records, researchers could confirm this history only by achieving four-out-of-five agreement among those who lived it.

2:07

Two-thirds of Indians have no memory of the license permit system

Kadambari realized that Prakhar and Shreyas, the most knowledgeable young people in policy circles, couldn't explain how the 1991 reforms happened. This convinced her that almost no one truly knew this history. Over 70% of Indians were born after her, meaning nearly two-thirds never lived under the license permit regime. The project started as an effort to fill this historical gap.

— Kadambari Shah
16:34

Four matching accounts out of five became the standard for a historical fact

Shruti described how many reform details were never written into memoirs or academic papers, only accessible through direct interviews with those who lived them. If four out of five people told the same story, the team treated it as the mainstream account. This is why the project insisted on direct oral histories rather than second and third-hand accounts that lose accuracy.

— Shruti Rajagopalan
18:41

No handover tradition meant even chief advisors' tenures went unrecorded

When Shankar Acharya took office as Chief Economic Advisor, his office had no plaque recording the tenures of his predecessors. He was the tenth; the previous nine had incomplete records, some missing even the exact month. Shreyas realized that even core technical positions in the Finance Ministry had no institutional memory, making oral history not supplementary but essential—the only way to reconstruct this history.

— Shreyas Narla
38:21

Indian economic thought survived in obscure committee reports, not university journals

Niranjan Rajadhyaksha once told Shruti something that never left her: India's university culture and academic journals were weak then, so many officials' real ideas after completing their doctorates never became papers—they stayed scattered across expert committee reports. The team made collecting, scanning, and digitizing these reports core work, even traveling to Nehru Library to photocopy the PC Alexander report. Later they found that even well-known scholar Arvind Panagaria didn't have a copy.

— Shreyas Narla
58:11

Six sectors had to move together or the reforms would fail

Shruti explained why the M document Montek Singh Ahluwalia led was crucial: it didn't handle problems separately, but placed currency, tariffs, license systems, price controls, and fiscal deficits on a single map for coordinated reform. Loosening one constraint made another the new bottleneck. Today's piecemeal state-level land and labor reforms show no results because no one conducts this kind of systems diagnosis anymore.

— Shruti Rajagopalan
1:07:40

One missing photo led to the discovery of three forgotten women officials

The team found no women in reform-era photographs. Kadambari pursued this gap, finding Bandana Agarwal, Renuka Vishwanathan, and Sindhu Shrikhar in scattered footnotes and memoirs—female IAS officers who drafted industrial policy, managed fiscal expenditure, and advised Chidambaram at the Finance Ministry. Yet literature had almost never systematically recorded their roles.

— Kadambari Shah
1:14:47

Major outlets used the project's research without attribution after Singh's death

Manmohan Singh refused oral history while alive, fearing his memory would become fiction. After his death, media focused on his premiership's core deals and rural employment law, relegating 1991 reforms to history. But those who lived through the reforms told Shruti that the project had made it "sexy" again. Multiple mainstream outlets borrowed the team's material without acknowledgment.

— Shruti Rajagopalan
1:34:33

Twenty years of state-level reform talent shortage led the project to build supply

Shruti had heard this complaint in policy circles for two decades: everyone agreed the next reforms must happen at the state level (land, agriculture, energy), yet insisted ‘there are no competent people.’ The team responded by launching the 1991 Fellowship covering ten states with twelve early-career researchers, funded for three years, directly cultivating this previously non-existent talent pool.

— Shruti Rajagopalan

Glossary

license permit raj
India's pre-1991 regulated system requiring government approval for each enterprise investment and production decision.
M document
Montek Singh Ahluwalia's reform blueprint proposing simultaneous change across six economic sectors.
Fabian socialists
British socialist thinkers advocating gradual reform whose ideas influenced early Indian reformers.
Chatham House Rule
A confidentiality protocol permitting discussion content to be quoted without attributing speakers by name.
