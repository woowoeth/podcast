90 meters from breaking the record, yet she chose retreat over summit

Ada's first speed-climbing attempt had the team announcing her success in advance—teammates told friends before she summited that she had made it. Near 8,000 meters, weather turned. Blizzard wind left her unable to open her eyes; snowdrift reached her thighs. The summit was nearly in sight, 90 meters away from breaking the world record. She and the Sherpa locked eyes and decided together to descend. Later, she explained: even a 1 percent risk that someone would never leave the mountain was not acceptable. Five or six days later, the same team set out again, successfully summited, and set the new record.

Two years of failure on five mountains before one success

Before formally attempting Everest, Ada spent two full years on five or six peaks without a single summit. On Siguniang Mountain's second peak, heavy snow stopped her at 4,800 meters. The third peak's snow was so thick she couldn't reach base camp. One mountain saw her stalled 100 meters from the top for an hour and a half by high winds. She didn't blame the team or weather. Instead, she explained each failure to her students—this was the five-year pact she'd made with them: the process mattered more than the summit.

22:12

The earthquake turned the tent into a clothes washer with her inside it

April 25, 2015, midday. Ada was at Everest base camp when the Nepal earthquake triggered an avalanche. Decades of accumulated snow from the opposite peak rushed toward camp like a bomb. She ran into the dining tent; the avalanche's force picked it up and it tumbled repeatedly, with knives and cups inside. She described herself being spun like clothes in a washing machine. Her head was split open with a scar 6 centimeters long that remains today; doctors found she had lost approximately 2 liters of blood. Teammates sent her family messages that night saying they should prepare for the worst. She lost all memory of the event, yet woke herself up from her sleeping bag the next morning.

30:13

Emotion and sleep, not exercise, are what recovery actually requires

During recovery, Ada refused to make any decisions while her body or mind was unstable. She focused on one goal: complete healing. Her doctor noted she never took out her phone, returning to the hotel to sleep after each treatment, then cycling the next day. She proposed a formula: physical health equals exercise plus diet plus sleep plus emotion—but emotion and sleep are the real keys, accounting for roughly 80 percent; exercise gets overestimated only because it's easiest to measure and do. Three or four months after injury, she rode round trip to Wuling at 4,000-plus meters. Before a year passed, she completed the Hong Kong 100—100 kilometers in just over 13 hours—placing 10th overall, 1st among Hong Kong runners.

37:04

School forced her to resign; it redefined what being a teacher means

After Ada's second failed Everest attempt, her school demanded she resign if she wanted to try again, citing repeated absences that undermined teaching. She said this was the one thing she hadn't anticipated in her climbing plan—the hardest thing to overcome. Teaching wasn't just a job; it was the foundation of all her values. Her mother supported her: ‘You're only leaving the job. You're still a teacher.’ Later, the Hong Kong government wanted to give Ada the education sector's annual leadership award. She initially refused, saying she was no longer a teacher. They replied: education doesn't have to happen in a classroom.

50:34

She turned down all sponsorships to avoid being frozen at the peak

After summiting Everest and setting the speed record, brands approached Ada with sponsorship offers, usually requiring her to promote them on social media. She refused all of them. The reason: sponsors will shape you into a ‘world champion’ image, pin you permanently to a peak narrative, and never let you come down. What she wanted was to come down from the summit and do the next thing. She's never posted about the summit on any social media, never reads comments about herself.

— Ada Zeng Yanhong
55:36

Building a women's climbing team proved harder than climbing the mountain

In 2019, Ada led three Chinese women to summit Everest. But she's candid: organizing this team was far harder than her own summit. Domestic society doesn't embrace women climbing Everest—some see it as recklessness. Sponsors don't believe a women's climbing team has commercial value. One teammate nearly quit because she couldn't raise funds. Ada ended up lending her own money to fill the gap and complete the team before departure.

— Ada Zeng Yanhong
1:01:39

To start mountaineering, first test whether you can sleep outdoors for days

For people with no background wanting to approach snow mountains, Ada's advice: mountains welcome all body types, genders, and backgrounds; success doesn't mean running fastest or being strongest. Two things matter. First: basic comfort with snow and rock—learn rock climbing, ice climbing, skiing first. Second: can you sleep continuously in a tent outdoors for two or three nights? Real mountaineering can mean spending a month and a half in the wilderness. Start with peaks like Siguniang Big Peak in Sichuan—5,000-meter peaks where you can gauge your mental state and energy expenditure at altitude before deciding whether to go higher.
