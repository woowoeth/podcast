To Judge a Consumer AI Product, Don't Ask About Retention — Ask What Users Stopped Doing

A consumer product has to clear four gates: make people curious, actually get them to switch, make them want to spread it, and keep them for the long haul. The sharpest test is a single question — once you started using it, what did you stop doing? If there's no answer, it hasn't squeezed into anyone's life yet.

Early stage is about utility, mainstreaming is about trust

Josh Elman's judgment: in the early-adoption phase, utility tends to beat trust — people are willing to try new things and to experiment with them. But if a product wants to go from early users to a mainstream market of hundreds of millions or even billions, trust eventually becomes the most important thing. The dividing line he draws is how a company handles data — whether it uses your data to make itself stronger and then trades on it, or only uses it to give you a better experience. He himself stopped using Google search long ago; when he needs to describe what he wants, he goes straight to Claude, GPT, Gemini. He thinks the basic way people search online has changed permanently.

— Josh Elman
20:19

Microdrama isn't about output volume, it's about creator identity

Josh invested in Musical.ly while at Greylock, and his post-mortem is this: Instagram is the "rear camera" — look at what a great place I'm in, what a great latte I'm drinking. Musical.ly was "I want to create something" — even with a pile of unwashed laundry behind you, with dance and music it becomes a work worth sharing. Short video made discovery easy, but after watching you want something deeper, you want a more complete story — that's the entry point for microdrama. AI lets people without a studio, actors or sets make the good story in their head, and the opportunity lies in finding those creators, giving them tools, and pushing them in front of more people; an entirely new story market could emerge. But he stresses it isn't about dumping content into every category — what actually holds up this wave of UGC companies is the relationship between users and creators.

— Josh Elman
30:30

Today's AI is still a single-player game

His observation: the vast majority of AI use right now is single-player mode — I have the AI handle things in my own life, but it isn't plugged into my group, it doesn't understand the shorthand of our crowd. Pull it into a group chat and it only helps me with one thing; it can't let me and the same group of people do other things together. Yet group chat is itself the social form that has genuinely grown over these past few years — he's in far more local group chats now than a decade ago. The next step he imagines is agent-to-agent conversation: I ask mine, yours is being asked too, and when the two match up they bring the people together. He describes a very specific awkwardness — you want to make weekend plans with someone, but you're afraid that sending it out will just get silence; that kind of unaskable moment is exactly right to hand off to an agent to arrange.

— Josh Elman
35:44

To build a big company, first earn the right to do one small thing

He doesn't believe in doing it all at once: to become a very big company, you can't do everything from the start — you have to do a few things extremely well first, then step by step earn the right to do more. Discord is the sample — it first earned the position of "voice chat while gaming," then people started chatting there, chatting more and more, until even people who don't game came to use it, and only then did it slowly become a place you can rely on for more things. The counterexample is Siri: it once did a good job earning the right to make calls, set alarms, set reminders and send texts, but when you want it to do a little more, it often can't. He says the chances for people to say "try one more thing, try five more things" are limited.

— Josh Elman
37:44

Voice is lossy, so a pure-voice agent doesn't hold up

Josh likes voice because he thinks while speaking and talks faster than he types. But he points out an easily overlooked property: voice is lossy — you ramble, you go off topic, whereas typing forces you to tighten your thinking before you send it. So he deliberately thinks about where voice sits in the whole spectrum of communication: an agent should let you ramble at length, then help you distill what you actually mean and remember the core. He doesn't believe we'll turn everything over to voice; voice will always be a balance, and the concept of a pure-voice agent doesn't really hold up. In quiet settings, or when you want to polish a thought first, he'd rather type. He thinks we do far too little with voice today.

— Josh Elman
41:49

Inference cost is back, and consumer payment doesn't come up front

He walks through the history of cost: in the early days, building a website meant buying servers, renting racks in a data center, hiring people to maintain them. In his first week at LinkedIn, a dozen-odd people across the whole company were called in to move machines from one rack to another. With the cloud and AWS era, that upfront heavy asset disappeared and the product itself became more important. Now cost is back, and it's variable cost — inference cost, not fixed infrastructure. The hard part is consumer distribution: willingness to pay doesn't always show up at the start. Enterprise customers can expense it to the company; consumers pay out of their own pocket. The good news is that mobile games and subscriptions have cultivated paying habits, and with open-source models and the ability to switch models at any time, costs are trending down. He bets the two curves cross in the next few years.

— Josh Elman
45:57

Four gates: fresh, switch, spread, stay

The order in which he looks at a product is four steps. First, can it be explained in one sentence — the thing it gives that didn't exist before — such that it raises an eyebrow? AI has made this step easier. Second, once people actually come and try it, is the experience good, can they figure out how it fits into their life, and it usually has to replace something they were already doing elsewhere — which is why he especially loves to ask: what did you stop doing after you started using this? A good answer means it can really be adopted. Third, will users spread it for you on their own — sometimes it happens naturally through network effects, sometimes it spreads as fast as ChatGPT did, because it gives answers directly and you no longer need to Google. Fourth, will people stay; only with network effects, with a history that gets harder to move away from the more you use it, or with a creator ecosystem, have you truly caught something.

— Josh Elman
49:05

Features can be copied in days, direction can't

He acknowledges that in today's competitive environment, good ideas get chased fast, whether the rival is another startup or a big company. His dividing line: others can copy what you've already built, but they can't copy where you're going, and they can't figure out where you're going before you do — so the best thing a founder can do is stay on their own path and live out that vision. He goes further: building an AI product today isn't just building a capable tool, it also needs personality, a relationship, an identity users are willing to identify with, a set of ways of interacting with you that becomes more personal over time, and a point of view of its own. Users will say: I tried that one, but it's missing something that's genuinely special.

— Josh Elman

Glossary

earn the right to do more
Do one small thing extremely well and build trust first; only then will users authorize the product to take over more of their behavior.
inference cost
The variable cost incurred each time a model is called to generate a result, unlike the fixed server overhead of traditional cloud services.
microdrama
Extremely short serialized episodes distributed on platforms like TikTok and YouTube, whose production barrier AI is lowering.
personal agent
AI that handles a user's private affairs — schedule, health, finances — as opposed to efficiency agents in work settings.
