Meta's $18 Billion Settlement: Buying Industry-Wide Default Limits

The settlement amount is just the surface: what's really valuable is the two-hour time lock, nighttime ban, and age assurance becoming defaults, plus the $18B-vs-$12B gap that pressures TikTok and YouTube to join.

Meta settled not proactively but because evidence was overwhelming

After the federal case went to trial, several former Meta employees testified, and plaintiffs cited internal documents alleging Meta knew its products harmed minors. Instagram head Adam Mosseri testified early and admitted some safety measures were not rolled out fast enough. Mark Zuckerberg was scheduled to testify the next day. By Megan's assessment, this was hardly a sure win for Meta.

— Megan Borowski
6:57

Two consecutive jury losses killed the fight-to-the-end strategy

Early in the year, Meta's strategy was to fight rather than settle, but in the spring it lost two jury verdicts in New Mexico and Los Angeles. This Oakland federal case was larger; Meta itself said a loss could expose it to over $1 trillion in damages, nearly its entire market cap. CFO Susan Li also acknowledged on the earnings call that these legal threats could materially affect the business. In the prior quarter, Meta had already spent $2.4 billion on legal costs.

— Megan Borowski
10:00

If rivals don't follow, Meta's payout automatically discounts

The settlement is written as $18 billion, but it's not unconditional: Meta pays the full amount only if TikTok and YouTube agree to a default one-hour daily limit and pay states $5.3 billion; if they don't join, Meta pays only about $12 billion. Meta publicly urged peers to follow, arguing that teens restricted on one app will just move to another. As of production, TikTok and YouTube had not responded.

— Megan Borowski
13:54

Even when you think you'll win, settle: kids can't wait for appeals

Colorado Attorney General Phil Weiser was one of the lead negotiators. He admitted the states believed they would win and that the evidence was very strong, but they still settled because going through a full trial and appeals could drag on for years, leaving kids unprotected in the meantime. The settlement delivers not soft promises but court-enforced requirements, with an independent auditor overseeing Meta's compliance.

— Phil Weiser
15:00

The real value isn't $18 billion; it's that defaults changed

Weiser's blunt assessment: $18 billion paid over ten years is not really much for a company like Meta. He said the settlement is one of the largest consumer protection settlements in U.S. history. More importantly, product restrictions shift from 'optional' to 'default': Meta must set a two-hour time lock on every under-18 account, disable notifications at night and during school hours, and complete the changes within six months.

— Phil Weiser
16:33

Lying about age to get through is exactly where Meta broke the law

When pressed on what happens if kids lie about their age at signup, Weiser said that's precisely where past violations occurred—a child who is really 11 or 12 and registers truthfully gets rejected, but the same user, known to Meta, can add ten years to their age and get an account. The settlement requires Meta to develop and run age assurance technology that uses signals like usage patterns and facial geometry to estimate true age, and to remove any user found to be under 13.

— Phil Weiser
20:00

Settling with states leaves thousands of lawsuits pending

Weiser made clear this is not the end; other companies still face ongoing litigation and investigations. For Meta, most state legal challenges are over, but thousands of lawsuits from school districts and individuals remain, one of which goes to trial in October. Megan then pulls back to the core question: will these restrictions just be wishful thinking, since users can always find workarounds?

— Phil Weiser
21:54

Writing rules into a settlement doesn't change teen behavior

Megan says we are entering a 'post-social-media world' we've never seen: max two hours a day, no use at night, no push notifications during school. But the underlying question is whether these rules actually have teeth—if teens can always find a way around them, the pressure falls back on Meta: it must use technology to continuously purge under-13 accounts and ensure under-18s only use compliant accounts. What she most wants to watch is whether enforcement actually works.

— Megan Borowski

Glossary

COPPA
U.S. law requiring online platforms to obtain parental consent before collecting data from children under 13.
age assurance
Using signals like usage patterns and facial geometry to estimate true age, rather than relying on self-reported age.
algorithmic recommendations
Mechanisms by which platforms push content based on user behavior, cited as driving addictive use among teens.
