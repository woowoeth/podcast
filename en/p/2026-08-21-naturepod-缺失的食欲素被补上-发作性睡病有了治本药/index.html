Filling in the Missing Orexin: Narcolepsy Gets a Root-Cause Drug, and Maybe a Brain Version of GLP-1

The FDA has approved the first orexin agonist, replacing the orexin that type 1 narcolepsy is missing rather than just treating symptoms; it could become a "brain version of GLP-1." Meanwhile, a brain organoid has lived past five years for the first time, opening late-onset brain disease research.

0:00

Sleeping through the day and not at night is one disease

Narcolepsy comes in type 1 and type 2. The core is excessive daytime sleepiness: patients can drop into brief deep sleep with no warning, while at night they instead suffer insomnia, and may have nightmares, sleep paralysis and hallucinations. Type 1 patients suddenly lose muscle tone under strong emotion such as laughter, which is cataplexy. Existing drugs only address symptoms: stimulants by day, sleeping pills by night, neither touching the root cause.

1:36

Stimulants plus sleeping pills never treated the cause

Treatment has only managed symptoms: stimulants to stay awake in the day, sleeping pills to sleep at night. The host gives the example of a friend who fell asleep in the brief gap at a red light, which became the clue that led to diagnosis. The newly FDA-approved drug is the first to target the root cause of narcolepsy, and diagnosed patients in the US can get it by prescription.

2:38

Type 1 is missing orexin; type 2 works differently

Most scientists believe the root cause of type 1 is a lack of a peptide in the brain called orexin, which regulates the sleep-wake cycle. The new drug is an orexin agonist: it essentially mimics orexin's function, replacing the missing signal. The discussion draws an analogy to GLP-1: GLP-1 agonists mimic a gut hormone to regulate satiety, while this drug mimics a missing molecule in the brain. Type 2 has a different mechanism, possibly involving other neurons and pathways, so orexin is not the only root cause.

— Mar
4:28

On the drug, sleep cycles are indistinguishable from normal

In clinical trials the drug was highly effective; the patient interviewed said their life was completely changed, and the scientist involved in the research said that after taking it, patients' sleep cycles are essentially indistinguishable from those of "normal" people. Millions of patients stand to benefit. The key point is that it could become a "brain version of GLP-1": GLP-1 expanded from type 2 diabetes to weight loss, and this orexin agonist may act on other neural pathways too — researchers want to try it for ADHD symptoms and for fatigue associated with multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease.

— Mar
9:53

Brain organoids can live five years and still follow human brain timing

By optimizing culture conditions, swapping media partway through and continuously promoting new neurons, researchers kept a brain organoid alive for more than five years, where earlier organoids died within months. It follows a developmental trajectory similar to the human brain: gene expression at 15 days to 2 months resembles an early-gestation fetus, 3 to 6 months resembles mid-gestation, and beyond 12 months resembles an infant brain. These are long-term changes that were previously impossible to see.

— Mar
11:03

Neurons remember their age, so you can bank them by age

Many brain diseases emerge later in development, schizophrenia for instance, and existing organoids do not survive to that stage. Five-year organoids let researchers observe these late-onset diseases. More importantly, neurons seem to "remember their age": mix old cells with new ones and the old neurons still behave according to their older developmental stage. That means you can bank them by age, freeze them and take them out as needed, creating libraries of neurons at different developmental stages. The team's next step is to add sensory inputs such as vision and smell; if signs of consciousness ever appeared, it would raise ethical questions.

Glossary

narcolepsy
A sleep disorder whose main feature is excessive daytime sleepiness, divided into type 1 and type 2.
cataplexy
Sudden loss of muscle tone under strong emotion; the hallmark of type 1 narcolepsy.
orexin
A neuropeptide secreted by the hypothalamus that regulates the sleep-wake cycle; missing in type 1 patients.
orexin agonist
A drug that mimics orexin's function to replace the missing signal; the mechanism of the newly approved narcolepsy drug.
brain organoid
A simplified, miniature three-dimensional brain structure grown from stem cells, without consciousness, used to study development and disease.
GLP1
Glucagon-like peptide-1, a gut hormone whose agonists are used for diabetes and weight loss; here used as the analogy for a "brain version of GLP-1."
