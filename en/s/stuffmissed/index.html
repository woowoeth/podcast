An explorer died for a city that never existed, conjured by a political forgery

The city of Z that Fawcett spent his life hunting comes out of a fragmentary eighteenth-century manuscript of unexplained provenance, suspected of being a political forgery made after Brazil's independence; his final coordinates differ from the published version by about 200 miles, and there is still no hard evidence about his disappearance.

An explorer died for a city that never existed, and the source was a political forgery

Percy Fawcett's Amazon expeditions are usually told as adventure legend, but the real starting point was a border dispute set off by the rubber trade; the story of his "respect" for Indigenous people is badly oversimplified, and the City of Z that consumed his life may rest on a forged manuscript.

Stuff You Missed in History Class 0829

New York's Poison Milk, 1858: The Evidence Was Overwhelming; the City Ruled It Harmless

New York milk adulterated in 1858 was blamed for killing infants; after a reporter exposed it, the official hearing ruled the milk harmless. The affair eventually produced a state law in 1862 and the federal Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906.

Stuff You Missed in History Class 0822

Food Safety Standards Born of Space Food: Testing the Finished Product Is Never Enough

HACCP, the core of the global food safety system, was born out of NASA's space food program: testing the finished product is never enough, and the whole process from ingredient to person has to be controlled; a 1971 botulism case and the 1993 Jack in the Box outbreak turned it into a mandatory global standard.

Stuff You Missed in History Class 0819

The Illuminati Died of Infighting; the Conspiracy Theory Was Invented Later

The Illuminati was an anti-clerical order founded in 1776 by a professor and five students, peaking at roughly two to three thousand members; it died of infighting and narcissism, and today's conspiratorial image comes from later books and hoaxes.

Stuff You Missed in History Class 0817

The sultan came to her: Tétouan's woman governor was no pirate

Sayyida al-Hurra governed Tétouan for more than twenty years, built shipyards and worked with Ottoman privateers against Spain and Portugal; European sources call her a pirate, while recent scholarship treats her as an example of early decolonial practice.

Stuff You Missed in History Class 0812

The father of linoleum never trademarked it, and even the name became public property

Frederick Walton invented linoleum but lost the name because he never registered it as a trademark; the process that actually set the industry standard came from a man on his payroll, C.F. Leak.

Stuff You Missed in History Class 0810

Smashing threshing machines bought 252 death sentences, and the workers still lost

Nineteenth-century English farm laborers protested unemployment by destroying threshing machines. What they got was 252 death sentences, 481 or 505 people transported to Australia, and the limited parliamentary reform of 1832 — the laborers' fortunes did not change, but the political map did.
